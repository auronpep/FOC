### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**D is correct.** The merger doctrine bars use of an assaultive felony as the predicate for felony murder when the assault is the very act that caused the death. The predicate felony must have an independent felonious purpose — a criminal objective collateral to the killing. Here, Martha's only criminal conduct was the scalding-soup assault that directly caused Mary's death; the assault is not collateral to the homicide, it is the homicide. Allowing the assault to bootstrap itself into felony murder would convert every assaultive killing into felony murder, eliminating the meaningful distinction between murder and felony murder.

**Gold Key:** When the only "predicate" is the very assault that caused the death, the felony merges with the homicide and cannot independently support felony murder.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations (one per distractor)

**C — The dominant trap.** "Felony + death = felony murder" is the surface reading of the rule and what makes C so attractive: a felony happened, a death happened, the rule seems to fit. But the felony-murder rule has the merger limitation built in. C collapses the rule to its surface and ignores the independent-felonious-purpose requirement. The merger doctrine exists precisely to prevent this collapse — without it, every assaultive killing would automatically become felony murder.

**B — The separate-intent fallacy.** B feels like a careful answer: it isolates "intent to assault" from "intent to kill" and treats that distinction as decisive. The move is plausible because intent matters elsewhere in homicide doctrine (it separates murder from manslaughter, for example). But the merger doctrine does not turn on a different mental state; it turns on whether the predicate felony has an independent felonious purpose. A different intent inside the same assaultive act does not give the felony a collateral purpose — the purpose is still the assault itself, which is the killing. B confuses the role of mens rea in homicide grading with the role of independent purpose in merger analysis.

**A — Right result, wrong doctrine.** A reaches "yes, dismiss" but grounds that result in the inherent-dangerousness limitation, not the merger doctrine. Felonious assault is generally considered inherently dangerous to life, so the inherent-dangerousness argument is weak on the merits, and — more importantly — it identifies the wrong limitation. The merger doctrine is the load-bearing rule on these facts: Martha's only criminal act was the assault that directly caused the death. Reaching the right answer through the wrong legal theory costs the point on an MBE-style question. **Gold Key:** The merger doctrine is the right tool when the predicate felony is the lethal act itself; the inherent-dangerousness limitation targets felonies whose abstract elements do not endanger life (e.g., forgery, statutory rape).

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**Governing rule (merger doctrine / independent felony limitation).** A defendant can be convicted of felony murder only if the killing occurs during the commission, or attempted commission, of a felony that is independent of — collateral to — the homicide itself. When the predicate felony is itself the assaultive act that caused the death, the felony "merges" into the homicide and cannot serve as the predicate.

**Authority floor.** The seminal authority is *People v. Ireland*, 7 Cal. 3d 244 (1969), which held that assaultive felonies whose purpose is the injury causing death cannot support a felony-murder conviction. The doctrine is widely followed; see, e.g., *State v. Ulland*, 24 Kan. App. 2d 249 (1997) (lethal act itself cannot serve as the independent collateral felony), and *State v. Prouse*, 244 Kan. 292 (1989) (cited in Ulland). The independent-felonious-purpose test is the controlling analytical tool: a predicate felony is valid only if its criminal objective is separate from inflicting injury or causing death. Robbery, burglary, arson, and kidnapping are paradigmatic independent-purpose felonies; aggravated assault, battery, and child abuse (in the lethal-variety) are paradigmatic merging felonies.

#### choice_by_choice_walkthrough

**A — "Yes, because throwing a pot of soup is not an inherently dangerous felony…"**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Misfit doctrine. The inherent-dangerousness limitation targets abstract felonies whose elements do not endanger life (forgery, statutory rape, bigamy). A felonious assaultive act that caused death is not that — the assault is, by definition, dangerous to life. The right tool here is the merger doctrine, not inherent dangerousness.
2. **Student label:** "Right result, wrong doctrine."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Yes, because the assault is the very act that caused the death, so the felony merges with the homicide." That is the merger-doctrine answer (D).
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** *People v. Ireland*, 7 Cal. 3d 244 (1969) bars felony-murder liability when the predicate felony is itself the lethal assaultive act. The inherent-dangerousness limitation is a separate gating rule and is not the rule that defeats the charge on these facts.

**B — "No, because Martha had the intent to commit the assault, which is a separate intent from the intent required for murder."**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Half-truth. The merger doctrine does not turn on a different mental state; it turns on whether the predicate felony has an independent felonious purpose. A separate intent inside the same assaultive act does not give the felony a collateral purpose — the purpose is still the assault itself, which is the killing. Intent distinguishes murder from manslaughter, but it does not save a merging predicate.
2. **Student label:** "Separate-intent fallacy."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "No, because the predicate felony has a purpose collateral to the killing" — e.g., Martha was simultaneously committing a robbery and the assault was incidental. But the stem says she was engaged in no other crime.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Merger asks about the predicate's purpose, not the defendant's mens rea as between homicide grades. A different mental state inside the same act does not create an independent felonious purpose.

**C — "No, because the assault was a felony and death resulted from it, satisfying all elements of felony murder."**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Surface read. The felony-murder rule, on its face, looks like "felony + death = murder." But the rule carries the merger limitation as a built-in exception. C treats the rule's surface as the whole rule and ignores the limitation. The merger doctrine is exactly the carve-out that prevents the surface reading from controlling.
2. **Student label:** "Surface read of the felony-murder rule."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "No, because the predicate felony (e.g., robbery) is independent of the homicide" — the predicate has a purpose beyond the killing.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Without the merger limitation, every assaultive killing would become felony murder, eliminating the meaningful distinction between murder and felony murder. *People v. Ireland* rejected the surface read; merger is part of the rule, not a peripheral gloss.

**D — "Yes, because the assault merges into the homicide, leaving no independent predicate felony to support the felony murder charge."**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** True and responsive. Martha's only criminal conduct was the scalding-soup assault that directly caused Mary's death. The assault is not collateral to the homicide — it is the homicide. Under the merger doctrine, the assault cannot serve as an independent predicate for felony murder. The motion to dismiss should be sustained.
2. **Student label:** "Merger doctrine — no independent felonious purpose."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This is the credited answer. No alternative.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** *People v. Ireland*, 7 Cal. 3d 244 (1969); the independent-felonious-purpose test requires the predicate felony to have a criminal objective collateral to the killing. The lethal act itself cannot serve as the independent collateral felony. *State v. Ulland*, 24 Kan. App. 2d 249 (1997); *State v. Prouse*, 244 Kan. 292 (1989).

#### residual_answer
