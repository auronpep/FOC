Question: Abraham contracted with Sarah to buy Sarah's bakery. As part of the price, Abraham promised in the written contract to pay Mary $20,000 so Mary could open a small food cart. Sarah promptly sent Mary a copy of the contract. Relying on Abraham's promise, Mary sold her delivery cart and signed a lease for a food-cart space. One week later, before Abraham paid Mary, Abraham and Sarah agreed to remove the payment-to-Mary clause. If Mary sues Abraham for the $20,000, what is the likely result?
Answer A: Mary can enforce the promise because her rights vested when she materially changed position in reasonable reliance on the promised payment.
Answer B: Mary cannot enforce the promise because Abraham and Sarah remained free to modify the contract until Abraham actually paid Mary.
Answer C: Mary cannot enforce the promise because she gave no consideration to Abraham.
Answer D: Mary can enforce only if Sarah formally assigned her own contract rights to Mary.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Mary was an intended beneficiary because the contract directed payment to her, and her rights vested when she materially relied on the promise by selling her cart and leasing space. After vesting, Abraham and Sarah could not eliminate Mary's right without her consent.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because the original parties' power to modify ends once the intended beneficiary's rights vest. C: C is wrong because an intended beneficiary need not supply consideration to enforce the promise. D: D is wrong because Mary's route is third-party beneficiary status, not assignment.
lb_rule_reference: Common law third-party beneficiary — vesting by reliance
