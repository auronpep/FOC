Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Right to Jury Trial
Question: Odette was charged with misdemeanor shoplifting, which carried a maximum sentence of six months' imprisonment and a $1,000 fine. She demanded a jury trial. The court denied her demand, reasoning that the offense was petty. Odette argued that any potential imprisonment, even six months, constitutes a serious deprivation of liberty that entitles her to a jury. The judge proceeded with a bench trial and convicted her; she was sentenced to forty-five days. Was the court correct to deny the jury trial demand?
Answer A: No, because any potential term of imprisonment, however short, triggers the constitutional right to a jury trial
Answer B: No, because the combination of imprisonment and a $1,000 fine makes this a serious offense
Answer C: Yes, because an offense punishable by no more than six months' imprisonment is a petty offense with no constitutional right to jury trial
Answer D: Yes, but only because the actual sentence imposed — forty-five days — was less than six months, so no serious deprivation occurred
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Baldwin v. New York* and *Blanton v. City of North Las Vegas*, the constitutional dividing line between serious and petty offenses is six months of potential imprisonment. An offense with a maximum authorized sentence of six months or less is a petty offense, and the defendant has no Sixth Amendment right to jury trial. The court correctly denied the jury demand and conducted a bench trial.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Supreme Court has drawn the line at six months. Offenses carrying a maximum of six months are classified as petty offenses, and there is no constitutional right to jury trial for such offenses. The Sixth Amendment jury right attaches only for serious offenses — those with a potential sentence exceeding six months. B: The jury trial threshold is determined primarily by the authorized term of imprisonment, not by the combination with fines. A fine, even a substantial one, does not convert a petty offense into a serious offense for Sixth Amendment jury trial purposes when the maximum imprisonment does not exceed six months. D: The jury trial right is determined by the maximum authorized sentence, not by the sentence actually imposed. Classification occurs at charging, based on the statutory maximum. Actual sentence length is irrelevant to whether the defendant had a constitutional right to demand a jury.
lb_rule_reference: A defendant has a Sixth Amendment right to jury trial for serious offenses — those carrying more than six months' potential imprisonment; offenses punishable by six months or less are petty offenses with no jury right
