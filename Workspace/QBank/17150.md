Question: A state constitution is amended by popular referendum to prohibit any state or local government entity from enacting, enforcing, or maintaining any law or policy that provides special protection to persons based on sexual orientation. The amendment repeals an existing municipal ordinance barring discrimination against gay and lesbian individuals in housing, employment, and public accommodations, and prohibits any future similar protection. A gay resident challenges the amendment under the Equal Protection Clause, arguing that it singles out gay and lesbian persons and bars them from seeking antidiscrimination protection through the normal political process. Under Equal Protection analysis, how should the court evaluate the constitutional amendment targeting protections for gay and lesbian individuals?
Answer A: The amendment is subject to strict scrutiny because sexual orientation is a suspect classification
Answer B: The amendment survives rational basis review because the political process is available to gay persons on equal terms, and states may decide as a democratic matter not to extend anti-discrimination protection to any particular group
Answer C: The amendment likely fails rational basis review because it singles out gay and lesbian persons, strips them of specific protections available to others, and reflects bare animus toward an identifiable group rather than a legitimate government interest
Answer D: The amendment is unconstitutional per se because it restructures the political process to place a special burden on gay and lesbian persons seeking legislation, triggering Hunter/Seattle doctrine strict scrutiny
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The Supreme Court has applied rational basis review to strike down laws that single out gay or lesbian persons for disadvantageous treatment when the record shows the law's sole purpose is to reflect animus. An amendment that repeals existing anti-discrimination protections specifically as applied to sexual orientation — while leaving other groups' protections intact — and prohibits any future protection for that group embodies the same animus the Court has found insufficient to survive even rational basis review. Bare desire to harm or disadvantage a politically unpopular group is not a legitimate government interest.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Supreme Court has not declared sexual orientation a suspect or quasi-suspect classification that triggers intermediate or strict scrutiny. The Court has instead struck down laws targeting gay persons by applying rational basis review and finding the laws failed even that minimal standard because they were motivated by bare animus rather than a legitimate interest. B: A law that specifically strips a group of the ability to seek legal protection through the political process — while leaving other groups free to seek protection — raises the specific concern the Supreme Court addressed: the amendment does not just fail to help a group, it restructures the political process to disadvantage one group. When the only identifiable purpose is to make gay persons unequal before the law, even rational basis is not satisfied. D: The Supreme Court has not extended the Hunter/Seattle political process doctrine to sexual orientation. The doctrine has been applied in the racial context. While the political process concern informs the animus analysis, the Court has struck down such laws on rational basis grounds rather than on a free-standing political process restructuring theory applied to sexual orientation.
lb_rule_reference: Laws that reflect bare animus against gay or lesbian persons fail even rational basis review under the Equal Protection Clause; the Court has applied meaningful rational basis review to strike down laws whose only discernible purpose was disadvantaging a disfavored group.
