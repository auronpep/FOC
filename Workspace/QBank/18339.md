Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Federal-Question Jurisdiction
Question: An employee files a federal-question action against her employer under the Family and Medical Leave Act, seeking damages for the employer's refusal to grant medical leave. In the same complaint, the employee adds a state-law breach-of-contract claim against the same employer arising from a real estate transaction that occurred two years earlier and had nothing to do with the employee's employment. The state claim is for $30,000. The employer moves to dismiss the state-law breach-of-contract claim for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. The governing forum is federal court under the MBE baseline. Should the court dismiss the state-law breach-of-contract claim?
Answer A: No, because both claims involve the same parties, and supplemental jurisdiction allows any claim between the same parties to be litigated together in federal court
Answer B: Yes, because the court should dismiss the state-law breach-of-contract claim for lack of supplemental jurisdiction — it shares no common nucleus of operative fact with the FMLA anchor claim
Answer C: No, because Rule 18 permits a plaintiff to join any claims against the same defendant in a single complaint, which independently satisfies subject matter jurisdiction
Answer D: No, because a $30,000 state claim falls within supplemental jurisdiction when paired with any federal claim of greater value, satisfying a proportionality test
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Under § 1367(a), supplemental jurisdiction extends to claims that form part of the same case or controversy as the anchor claim — which requires a common nucleus of operative fact. The FMLA claim concerns the employer's denial of medical leave; the state-law contract claim concerns an entirely unrelated real estate transaction from two years prior. They share no common facts. The court must dismiss the breach-of-contract claim for lack of supplemental jurisdiction. The claim cannot support independent diversity jurisdiction either, since the amount is only $30,000.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Section 1367(a) requires the supplemental claim to form part of the same case or controversy as the anchor claim — meaning they must share a common nucleus of operative fact. The FMLA claim arises from the refusal to grant medical leave; the contract claim arises from an unrelated real estate transaction two years earlier. They share no operative facts. Same parties alone does not satisfy § 1367(a). C: Rule 18 governs whether claims may be joined in a single pleading — it is a procedural rule about permissive joinder. But Rule 18 does not create or confer subject matter jurisdiction. Federal courts must independently have jurisdiction over each claim. An unrelated state claim joined under Rule 18 that lacks both federal-question and diversity jurisdiction, and that shares no common nucleus with the anchor claim, cannot be heard in federal court. D: Supplemental jurisdiction has no proportionality test. There is no rule that smaller state claims automatically tag along with larger federal claims. The only test under § 1367(a) is common nucleus of operative fact. A $30,000 claim entirely unrelated to the federal anchor claim has no supplemental jurisdictional basis regardless of the relative sizes of the claims.
lb_rule_reference: 28 U.S.C. § 1367(a) — supplemental jurisdiction requires the state claim to be part of the same case or controversy as the anchor claim; unrelated claims between the same parties do not share a common nucleus and fall outside § 1367(a).
