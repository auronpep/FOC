Question: A credit card company obtained and properly filed a judgment against a man after he failed to pay a $10,000 debt. A statute in the jurisdiction provides as follows: "Any judgment properly filed shall, for 10 years from filing, be a lien on the real property then owned or subsequently acquired by any person against whom the judgment is rendered." Two years later, the man purchased land for $200,000. He made a down payment of $20,000 and borrowed the remaining $180,000 from a bank. The bank loan was secured by a mortgage on the land. Immediately after the closing, the deed to the man was recorded first, and the bank's mortgage was recorded second. Five months later, the man defaulted on the mortgage loan and the bank initiated judicial foreclosure proceedings. After receiving notice of the proceedings, the credit card company filed a motion to have its judgment lien declared to be the first lien on the land. Is the credit card company's motion likely to be granted?
Answer A: A: No, because the bank's mortgage secured a loan used to purchase the land.
Answer B: B: No, because the man's down payment exceeded the amount of his debt to the credit card company.
Answer C: C: Yes, because the bank had constructive notice of the judgment lien.
Answer D: D: Yes, because the bank is a third-party lender and not the seller of the land.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A is correct. The bank's mortgage is a purchase-money mortgage, which is considered senior to a previously-filed judgment lien such as the credit card company's in this case.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B is incorrect. The relative amounts of the down payment and the credit card debt are irrelevant. C is incorrect. Despite the bank having had constructive notice of the credit card company's properly filed judgment lien, the fact that the bank's mortgage was used as purchase money renders the credit card company's lien second priority. D is incorrect. Although a purchase-money mortgage may be granted by a seller, it may also be granted by a third party (here, the bank), whose interest will still be senior to previous judgment liens.
