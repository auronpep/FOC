Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Before signing a search warrant, the magistrate told officers, "I am part of your team on this operation," crossed out limiting language in the warrant, and told them to "search wherever you think useful." The affidavit was thin, and the warrant was used to search every room and container in a suspect's house. The suspect moves to suppress. Should the good-faith exception apply?
Answer A: Yes, because the officers obtained a signed warrant
Answer B: Yes, because the officers did not personally force the magistrate to act that way
Answer C: No, because the magistrate abandoned the neutral judicial role and the warrant could not support objectively reasonable reliance
Answer D: No, because good faith is unavailable whenever an affidavit is thin
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The magistrate's statements and edits made her a law-enforcement participant rather than a neutral decisionmaker. Good faith does not apply.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Reliance is not objectively reasonable when the magistrate abandons neutrality and acts as part of the police effort. B: Good faith asks objective reasonable reliance. A reasonable officer would know this was not neutral judicial authorization. D: Not every weak affidavit defeats good faith. The decisive fact here is abandonment of neutrality.
lb_rule_reference: The good-faith exception does not apply when the issuing magistrate acts as a rubber stamp or member of the law-enforcement team.
