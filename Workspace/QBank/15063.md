Question: An owner conveyed Blackacre to a buyer by a warranty deed. The buyer recorded the deed four days later. After the conveyance but prior to the buyer's recording of the deed, a lender properly filed a judgment against the owner. The two pertinent statutes in the jurisdiction provide the following: 1) any judgment properly filed shall, for 10 years from filing, be a lien on the real property then owned or subsequently acquired by any person against whom the judgment is rendered; and 2) no conveyance or mortgage of real property shall be good against subsequent purchasers for value and without notice unless the same be recorded according to law. The recording act has no provision for a grace period. The lender joined both the owner and the buyer in an appropriate action to foreclose the judgment lien against Blackacre. If the lender is unsuccessful, it will be because
Answer A: A: the owner's warranty of title to the buyer defeats the lender's claim.
Answer B: B: the lender is not a purchaser for value.
Answer C: C: any deed is superior to a judgment lien.
Answer D: D: four days is not an unreasonable delay in recording a deed.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: B is correct. The recording statute identified in the facts is a notice act. In this case, the lender's interest arises from a judgment lien against the owner rather than payment of valuable consideration. Therefore, the lender is not protected by the recording statute. Because the buyer is protected by the statute, the owner no longer holds an interest in Blackacre, and there is no property for the lien to attach to.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A is incorrect. Under a notice statute, the lender will prevail over the buyer, assuming the lender is a BFP (a purchaser for value) because the buyer is a grantee who failed to record before the judgment lien was properly filed. C is incorrect. A deed is not always superior to a judgment lien. D is incorrect. This is a correct statement but is irrelevant to why the lender will be unsuccessful.
