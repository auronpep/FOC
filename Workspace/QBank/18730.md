MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Intentional Torts
Outline: Battery
Question: During a recreational soccer game, a defender legally bumped an opponent while both chased the ball. After the referee blew the whistle and play stopped, the opponent walked back to the defender and deliberately punched him in the ribs for "playing too hard." The defender sues for battery. Can the opponent defeat the defender's battery claim by relying on consent to sports contact?
Answer A: Yes, because soccer players consent to all physical contact during a game
Answer B: No, because the deliberate after-whistle punch exceeded the scope of any sports consent
Answer C: Yes, because the defender first bumped the opponent
Answer D: No, because consent is never a defense to battery
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The defender consented to ordinary soccer contact, not a retaliatory punch after play stopped. The consent defense fails.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Sports consent is limited to contacts within the ordinary scope of the game. A deliberate after-whistle punch is outside that scope. C: Lawful in-play contact does not consent to later intentional retaliation outside the game's scope. D: Consent can be a valid defense to battery when within scope. The problem is that the opponent exceeded the scope.
lb_rule_reference: Consent to ordinary contacts within a sport does not extend to deliberate contacts outside the rules or scope of play.
