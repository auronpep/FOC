Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Rights during Sentencing
Question: A federal defendant pleads guilty to bank fraud and admits only the facts in the indictment. Under the Federal Sentencing Guidelines, the base offense level for bank fraud yields an advisory range of 18 to 24 months. The probation officer recommends a six-level upward adjustment for "sophisticated means" of fraud—a factual finding not admitted by the defendant and not found by any jury. Treating the Guidelines as advisory, the district court makes the sophisticated-means finding by a preponderance of the evidence, applies the upward adjustment, and sentences the defendant to 36 months. The defendant argues the sentence violates Apprendi and Blakely. Is the defendant's argument correct?
Answer A: Yes, because any judicial fact-finding at sentencing that increases the sentence above the base offense level is unconstitutional under Apprendi regardless of whether the guidelines are advisory or mandatory
Answer B: No, because after Booker the Federal Sentencing Guidelines are advisory, so judicial fact-finding that increases the guidelines calculation does not violate Apprendi as long as the final sentence falls within the statutory maximum for the offense of conviction
Answer C: Yes, because any upward departure from a calculated guidelines range must be based on facts admitted by the defendant or found by a jury, even under an advisory system
Answer D: No, because Booker applies only to cases where the judge imposes a sentence above the otherwise-applicable statutory maximum; here the guidelines range, not the statute, was exceeded
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Blakely v. Washington held that mandatory sentencing schemes violate Apprendi when judges find facts that increase the mandatory minimum or maximum above what the jury's verdict alone would authorize. United States v. Booker applied Blakely to the federal guidelines and held the mandatory-application provision unconstitutional, but remedied it by making the guidelines advisory. Under an advisory scheme, the Apprendi analysis asks only whether the final sentence exceeds the statutory maximum for the offense of conviction—not whether it exceeds the advisory range. Here, the defendant pleaded guilty to bank fraud; the district court found sophisticated means as an advisory adjustment; and the 36-month sentence must be compared against the statutory maximum for bank fraud (30 years), not the 24-month advisory ceiling. The sentence does not violate Apprendi.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: United States v. Booker (2005) remedied the Blakely violation in the federal guidelines by making them advisory rather than mandatory. Under an advisory guidelines system, the judge may make factual findings that increase the calculated range because the guidelines do not set a mandatory maximum—the judge retains discretion to sentence anywhere within the statutory range authorized by the conviction. The Apprendi ceiling is the statutory maximum set by the offense of conviction, not the advisory guidelines range. Advisory guidelines do not violate Apprendi. C: This accurately describes the rule for mandatory guidelines (Blakely), not advisory ones (Booker). After Booker made the guidelines advisory, the constitutional constraint is that the final sentence must not exceed the statutory maximum for the offense. Within that ceiling, the judge may make guideline calculations based on preponderance findings without a jury. D: The answer arrives at the correct result (no violation) but for confused reasons. It frames Booker as applying only when the statutory maximum is exceeded, which is circular. The real point is that after Booker, advisory guidelines calculations do not raise Apprendi issues at all, even when they increase the recommended range—because the statutory maximum, not the advisory range, is the relevant ceiling.
