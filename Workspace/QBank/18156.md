Outline: False pretenses
Question: Harold approaches an elderly woman, Helen, and tells her: "I will fix your roof next week for $2,000." Helen pays Harold $2,000 in cash upfront. Harold has no intention of doing the work and never does. He spends the money immediately. There was no misrepresentation about Harold's current skill, licensure, or resources — only the promise of future work. Is Harold guilty of false pretenses at common law?
Answer A: Yes, because Harold made a representation he knew was false when he made it
Answer B: Yes, because by accepting payment, Harold implicitly represented that he currently intended to perform
Answer C: No, because at common law, a false promise about future conduct does not satisfy the present-fact requirement for false pretenses
Answer D: No, because Helen should have required a written contract before paying
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Common-law false pretenses requires a knowingly false representation of a presently existing material fact. Harold's representation — that he would fix Helen's roof next week — is a promise about future conduct, not a statement about present reality. The common law has consistently held that false future promises, even when made with no intention of keeping them, do not satisfy the present-fact element. Harold may be liable for breach of contract and potentially prosecuted under modern theft-by-deception statutes, but he is not guilty of common-law false pretenses.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: False pretenses at common law requires a false statement of a presently existing fact, not a false promise about future conduct. Harold promised to do work in the future. Even though Harold subjectively knew he would not perform, his promise was about a future event, not a present state of affairs. The common law traditionally does not treat false future promises as false representations of present fact for purposes of false pretenses liability. B: The majority common-law rule holds that a false promise, even one reflecting a current fraudulent intent, is a future promise and does not satisfy the present-fact requirement. The argument that "I currently intend to perform" is a present fact has been accepted in some jurisdictions but is not the traditional common-law rule. On an MBE question applying common law, the traditional rule controls: Harold's misrepresentation was about future conduct, not a present fact. D: The victim's failure to take precautions is entirely irrelevant to the defendant's criminal liability. Criminal law does not apportion fault to victims for being deceived. The reason Harold is not guilty of common-law false pretenses is the legal element analysis — future promise vs. present fact — not any conduct by Helen.
lb_rule_reference: False pretenses requires a false representation of a presently existing material fact. A false promise about future conduct — even one the defendant intends not to keep — is not a false representation of present fact and does not satisfy this element at common law.
