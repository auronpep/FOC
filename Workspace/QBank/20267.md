Outline: Constitutional limitations and justiciability
Question: Congress enacts a statute prohibiting the transfer of certain defense articles to any foreign state without a 30-day notice to Congress. The President transfers equipment without giving notice, citing urgent foreign-policy needs. A contractor denied payment under the transfer contract sues, asking the court to decide whether the statutory notice requirement was violated. Is the case automatically barred as a political question?
Answer A: Yes, because every foreign-affairs dispute is textually committed to the President
Answer B: No, because the court can apply the statute's notice requirement without deciding an unmanageable policy question
Answer C: Yes, because any separation-of-powers dispute between Congress and the President is nonjusticiable
Answer D: No, but only because contractors always have taxpayer standing
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The court is asked to apply a statutory notice rule, a manageable legal standard. The foreign-affairs context does not itself make the case nonjusticiable.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Courts can decide legal questions about statutory limits in foreign-affairs settings when manageable standards exist. C: Courts regularly resolve statutory and constitutional separation-of-powers questions when legal standards are available. D: The issue is political-question reviewability, not taxpayer standing.
lb_rule_reference: Foreign-affairs context does not automatically create a political question; courts can decide whether executive action violates a statutory limit using manageable legal standards.
