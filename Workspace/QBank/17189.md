Outline: Strict scrutiny (suspect) classifications
Question: A city rezones a neighborhood, changing the designation from multifamily residential to single-family residential. The rezoning prevents a nonprofit housing developer from completing a mixed-income apartment complex that would have served predominantly low-income Black residents. Statistical analysis shows that 82% of the residents who would have occupied the apartments are Black. The nonprofit and prospective residents sue under the Equal Protection Clause, citing the racial composition of those harmed. The city council's meeting minutes reflect purely fiscal and urban-planning justifications. No council member made racially charged statements. However, the plaintiffs introduce evidence that the rezoning was initiated after a neighborhood association meeting in which residents stated concerns about "property values" and "the character of the neighborhood." To trigger strict scrutiny under the Equal Protection Clause on a racial discrimination theory, what must the plaintiffs establish?
Answer A: Plaintiffs establish a prima facie violation by showing the rezoning had a racially disparate impact — that 82% of those harmed are Black
Answer B: Plaintiffs establish strict scrutiny by showing the formal legislative record contains no racial justification — the absence of a legitimate non-racial rationale triggers constitutional suspicion
Answer C: Plaintiffs must prove that racial discrimination was a motivating factor in the rezoning decision; evidence they may rely on includes statistical patterns, the sequence and context of events, departures from normal procedures, and legislative history including community meeting context
Answer D: Plaintiffs establish a constitutional violation by showing the rezoning had a disparate impact combined with a foreseeable racially disparate effect — foreseeability substitutes for proof of discriminatory intent
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under Village of Arlington Heights, strict scrutiny is triggered for a facially neutral law only if the plaintiff proves discriminatory purpose was a motivating factor. Proof may be drawn from statistical evidence, the historical background of the decision, the sequence of events leading up to it, departures from normal procedures, and legislative or administrative history. The neighborhood meeting statements about "property values" and "neighborhood character," combined with statistical disparity, are probative evidence — though ultimately the trier of fact determines whether they establish motivating racial purpose.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Under Washington v. Davis and Village of Arlington Heights, a facially neutral government action does not violate the Equal Protection Clause merely because of racially disparate impact. The plaintiff must prove discriminatory purpose — that race was a motivating factor in the decision. Disparate impact is evidence, but is not sufficient standing alone. B: Arlington Heights does not require plaintiffs to merely negate non-racial explanations. Plaintiffs must affirmatively prove that discriminatory purpose was a motivating factor — not merely that the legislative record is uninformative about racial motivation. A neutral legislative record does not alone establish unconstitutional purpose. D: The Equal Protection Clause requires actual discriminatory purpose, not merely foreseeability of disparate effect. Foreseeability is relevant to some tort analyses, but under Arlington Heights the government does not violate Equal Protection merely because a facially neutral decision predictably disadvantages a racial group. Actual discriminatory motivation must be shown.
