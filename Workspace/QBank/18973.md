Outline: Statute of Frauds
Question: Mary orally promises to provide John with monthly consulting advice “for the rest of John's life” in exchange for a fixed monthly fee. John dies eight months later, after Mary has performed. John's estate refuses to pay the last two months, arguing that a lifetime contract must be in writing because it could have lasted more than one year. How should the court rule on the one-year Statute of Frauds defense?
Answer A: The defense should fail because the contract could have been fully performed within one year if John died within that year.
Answer B: The defense should succeed because lifetime contracts always require a writing.
Answer C: The defense should succeed because the parties probably expected John to live longer than a year.
Answer D: The defense should fail only if Mary was a merchant.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The one-year category applies only if the contract cannot be fully performed within one year by its terms. A lifetime promise can be fully performed within one year if the person dies within that year, so it is not within the one-year SOF merely because it may last longer.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because lifetime contracts are not automatically within the one-year provision. C: C is wrong because probable duration is not the test. D: D is wrong because merchant status is irrelevant to common-law one-year analysis.
lb_rule_reference: Common-law SOF — one-year provision
