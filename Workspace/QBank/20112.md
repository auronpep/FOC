Outline: Double Jeopardy
Question: The state prosecuted Walt for simple assault arising from an incident in which he struck and injured a victim. Walt was acquitted. The state then charged Walt with aggravated battery based on the same incident, arguing that the new charge is distinct because it requires proof of serious bodily injury — an element not required for simple assault. Walt moves to bar the aggravated battery prosecution on double jeopardy grounds. Under the *Blockburger* same-offense test, is the successive aggravated battery prosecution barred?
Answer A: Yes, because the charges arise from the same conduct, and the Double Jeopardy Clause bars all successive prosecutions based on the same incident
Answer B: No, because aggravated battery requires proof of serious bodily injury, an element not required for simple assault, so they are different offenses under *Blockburger*
Answer C: Yes, because simple assault is a lesser included offense of aggravated battery — every element of simple assault is contained within aggravated battery — and acquittal on the lesser bars prosecution for the greater
Answer D: No, because Walt was acquitted rather than convicted, and the Double Jeopardy Clause's bar applies only after conviction
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Blockburger*, two offenses are "the same" if one is a lesser included offense of the other — that is, if conviction for the greater necessarily proves all elements of the lesser. Simple assault is typically a lesser included offense of aggravated battery (assault is subsumed within the battery, with serious bodily injury as the aggravating element). After acquittal on simple assault, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars prosecution for aggravated battery arising from the same incident because the prosecution would have to relitigate the assault elements already resolved by acquittal. The bar is absolute after acquittal.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar all successive prosecutions arising from the same conduct. The *Blockburger* test focuses on the elements of the offenses, not the underlying facts. Two charges from the same incident may be prosecuted successively if each requires an element the other does not. B: The *Blockburger* test requires that each offense have an element the other does not. While aggravated battery requires serious bodily injury (which simple assault does not require), the analysis must also ask whether simple assault requires any element that aggravated battery does not. If simple assault is a lesser included offense of aggravated battery — meaning every element of simple assault is subsumed within aggravated battery — then they are the same offense for double jeopardy. The acquittal of assault bars prosecution for the lesser-included-within-greater assault element of aggravated battery. Courts would need to examine the specific statutory elements. D: The Double Jeopardy Clause's bar on successive prosecution is, if anything, stronger after an acquittal than after a conviction. An acquittal is an absolute bar to retrial for the same offense — even if the acquittal was based on legal error, even if the evidence overwhelmingly supported conviction. The finality of acquittals is a core feature of double jeopardy doctrine.
lb_rule_reference: Under *Blockburger v. United States* (1932), two offenses are the same offense for double jeopardy purposes unless each requires proof of an element that the other does not; if one offense is a lesser included offense of the other, they are the same offense and successive prosecution is barred.
