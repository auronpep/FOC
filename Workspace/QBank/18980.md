MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Right to Jury Trial
Question: During the defendant's felony fraud trial, the jury sends a note to the judge asking for clarification on the definition of "intent to defraud." The judge, without notifying defense counsel or the defendant, convenes in chambers with the prosecutor and provides a written supplemental instruction that is then delivered to the jury. The defendant is not present and is not informed until after the jury returns a guilty verdict. On appeal, the defendant argues that the in-chambers conference and supplemental instruction violated her right to be present at a critical stage. Is the defendant's right-to-be-present argument correct?
Answer A: No, because the right to be present attaches only to jury selection and the initial charge conference; supplemental jury instructions are administrative and not a critical stage
Answer B: No, because the defendant waived the right to be present by not affirmatively requesting to attend all jury communications
Answer C: Yes, because the in-chambers supplemental instruction to the jury on an element of the offense is a critical stage, and the defendant had no opportunity to be present or to object to the content of the instruction
Answer D: Yes, and the error automatically requires reversal because ex parte judicial communications with the prosecution are a structural defect
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The Due Process Clause and the Sixth Amendment guarantee a defendant the right to be present at all stages of trial at which presence has a reasonably substantial relation to the fullness of the opportunity to defend. Supplemental jury instructions addressing the meaning of an element of the offense during deliberations are a critical stage—the defendant and defense counsel had a right to be informed of the jury's question, to propose an accurate instruction, and to object to a legally incorrect one. The ex parte communication between the judge and the prosecutor (without defense counsel present) also implicates the defendant's right to counsel. This is not a ministerial matter like scheduling. The error is not structural, so it is subject to harmless-error review.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A supplemental instruction responding to a jury question about an element of the offense directly affects the jury's deliberative process and the defendant's understanding of what must be proved. The Supreme Court has held that proceedings where the jury receives additional instructions or information require the defendant's presence because presence bears a reasonably substantial relation to the defendant's ability to defend. This is not a ministerial proceeding. B: The right to be present at critical stages cannot be waived by the defendant's failure to demand it. The obligation falls on the court to ensure the defendant's presence. A valid waiver of the presence right must be knowing, voluntary, and intentional—it is not satisfied by passive failure to object to an exclusion the defendant did not know was occurring. D: The presence violation and ex parte contact are constitutional errors subject to harmless-error review, not structural errors. Structural errors are confined to the narrow category of defects that render a criminal trial fundamentally unfair in a systemic way (complete denial of counsel, biased judge, denial of public trial, etc.). A supplemental instruction given ex parte is serious, but courts analyze whether it prejudiced the verdict. The correct analysis is Chapman harmless-error, not automatic reversal.
lb_rule_reference: The Sixth Amendment and Due Process Clause guarantee a defendant the right to be present at all stages of trial where the defendant's presence has a reasonably substantial relation to the fullness of the opportunity to defend; this includes jury selection, witness examination, and in-trial conferences with the jury; purely administrative proceedings outside the defendant's presence are permissible
