Outline: Taxing and spending power
Question: Congress imposes a large "tax" on anyone who knowingly sells a prohibited device. The amount is many times the device's value, applies only to intentional violations, and is enforced with criminal prosecution and imprisonment for nonpayment. What is the strongest argument that the exaction is not a valid tax?
Answer A: The exaction is labeled a tax, so it must be sustained under the taxing power
Answer B: The exaction has punitive features, including scienter and criminal-style sanctions, so it looks like a regulatory penalty rather than a tax
Answer C: The exaction fails because taxes can never discourage conduct
Answer D: The exaction is valid because Congress can attach imprisonment to any tax
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The facts show punishment and prohibition rather than a revenue-raising tax.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Labels do not control; punitive function matters. C: Taxes may influence behavior; the issue is the punitive, criminal-like structure. D: Criminal-style sanctions are a reason to view the exaction as punitive.
lb_rule_reference: An exaction is less likely to be sustained as a tax when it is punitive, prohibitory, tied to scienter, or enforced through criminal-style sanctions rather than functioning as a revenue measure.
