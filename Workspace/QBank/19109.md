Subtopic: Presentation of Evidence
Outline: Trial judge’s discretion to control order and presentation of evidence
Question: In a federal accounting trial, Deborah asks Lydia to explain how a monthly invoice system works. The judge allows Lydia to give a brief narrative because the process is technical. When Lydia begins discussing unrelated office gossip, the judge stops her and directs Deborah to ask focused questions. Was the judge's handling proper?
Answer A: No, because once narrative testimony is allowed, the witness may complete the narrative without interruption.
Answer B: Yes, because the court may allow limited narrative testimony when useful and then control it when it becomes inefficient or irrelevant.
Answer C: No, because all technical subjects must be presented only through yes-or-no questions.
Answer D: Yes, but only because office gossip is always privileged.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The court may reasonably control the mode of testimony. A judge can allow a limited narrative to explain a process and then narrow the testimony when it becomes inefficient or unrelated.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because allowing some narrative testimony does not surrender trial control. C: C is wrong because technical subjects need not be reduced to yes-or-no answers. D: D is wrong because the ruling rests on presentation control, not privilege.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 611(a) / controlled narrative permitted
