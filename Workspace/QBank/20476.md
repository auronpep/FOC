MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: An officer conducted a lawful Terry stop of a suspect near a known drug corner. During a pat-down for weapons, the officer felt a small, hard lump in the suspect's jacket pocket. The officer knew it was not a weapon. Curious, he continued to manipulate the lump through the fabric for several seconds until he determined it felt like a small rock of crack cocaine, at which point he reached in and seized it. The suspect moves to suppress. Was the seizure of the crack cocaine lawful under the plain feel doctrine?
Answer A: Yes, because the officer was conducting a lawful Terry frisk and anything discovered during the frisk is admissible
Answer B: Yes, because the officer had reasonable suspicion that the suspect was involved in drug activity, justifying a search for drugs
Answer C: No, because the officer's continued manipulation of the object to identify it as cocaine exceeded the lawful scope of a Terry frisk; the incriminating character was not immediately apparent
Answer D: No, because the officer should have obtained a warrant before reaching into the pocket once he determined the object was not a weapon
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Minnesota v. Dickerson recognized a plain feel corollary to plain view: an officer may seize non-weapon contraband felt during a lawful frisk if its identity as contraband is immediately apparent. Here, the officer knew the object was not a weapon and continued to manipulate it to identify it. That additional manipulation was an independent search requiring its own justification. Because the crack cocaine's identity was not immediately apparent and the officer exceeded the scope of the authorized frisk, the seizure was unconstitutional. Suppression is required.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Minnesota v. Dickerson's plain feel doctrine limits what may be seized during a Terry frisk. The frisk is authorized only to find weapons. Non-weapon objects may be seized only if their identity as contraband is immediately apparent by touch — without further manipulation. The doctrine does not authorize open-ended tactile exploration. B: Reasonable suspicion justifies a Terry stop and a frisk for weapons. It does not authorize a full search for drugs. A search for drugs requires either a warrant, probable cause plus an exception, or consent. Reasonable suspicion is a lower standard that authorizes only the narrow weapons pat-down. D: This choice gets the result right but for an incomplete reason. The problem is the unlawful manipulation before reaching into the pocket — that manipulation was itself an unconstitutional search. Additionally, if the crack's identity had been immediately apparent by touch (without extra manipulation), no warrant would have been needed. The defect is the manipulation, not merely reaching into the pocket.
lb_rule_reference: An officer conducting a lawful Terry frisk may seize an item under plain feel only if its identity as contraband is immediately apparent by touch; further manipulation beyond what is necessary to determine if the object is a weapon is unconstitutional (Minnesota v. Dickerson)
