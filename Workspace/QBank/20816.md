Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: During the defendant's trial for aggravated battery, the trial court erroneously admitted into evidence a statement the defendant made during a custodial interrogation after he had invoked his right to counsel—a clear Miranda violation. Defense counsel timely objected. The prosecution's case also included three eyewitness identifications, surveillance video showing the defendant at the scene, and a credible alibi rebuttal. The jury convicts. The defendant appeals, arguing the admission of the post-invocation statement violated his Fifth Amendment rights and requires automatic reversal. Which of the following best describes the correct standard of appellate review and its likely outcome?
Answer A: The defendant is correct; admission of a statement obtained in violation of Miranda is a structural error that requires automatic reversal without harmless-error analysis
Answer B: The defendant bears the burden of showing the error was not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt because he must prove the statement actually affected the verdict
Answer C: The court applies Chapman harmless-error review; the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict, and given the strong independent evidence, the error is likely harmless
Answer D: The court applies plain-error review because Miranda is a prophylactic rule, not a constitutional right, so the error is treated the same as a non-constitutional evidentiary error
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Chapman v. California (1967) established that a constitutional trial error that is properly preserved at trial requires the government on appeal to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the conviction. The error here—admission of a post-invocation statement in violation of Miranda—is a constitutional trial error, not a structural error. The trial court's admission was properly objected to, so plain error does not apply. Under Chapman, courts assess whether the jury would have convicted absent the error. Given three eyewitness identifications, surveillance video, and a rebutted alibi, a reviewing court would likely find that the admitted statement did not contribute to the verdict and that the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Miranda violations are not structural errors. The Supreme Court has held that structural errors are confined to a narrow category: complete denial of counsel, biased judge, defective reasonable-doubt instruction, denial of public trial, racial discrimination in jury selection, and denial of self-representation. Miranda violations, like most trial errors, are subject to Chapman harmless-error review on direct appeal. B: Under Chapman v. California, when a constitutional error has been preserved at trial and raised on appeal, the burden falls on the government (not the defendant) to show that the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. This is different from plain-error review, where the defendant must show a reasonable probability that the error affected the outcome. Because defense counsel timely objected, Chapman applies and the government bears the burden. D: Even if Miranda is described as prophylactic, violations of Miranda's protections are treated as constitutional errors for purposes of appellate review when properly preserved. Because defense counsel objected at trial, the error is preserved and Chapman—not plain-error—review applies. Plain error is triggered only when the defendant failed to object below, regardless of whether the error is constitutional or non-constitutional.
lb_rule_reference: Under Chapman v. California, a constitutional trial error requires reversal unless the government proves beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict; structural errors require automatic reversal without harmless-error analysis; unpreserved errors are reviewed for plain error (defendant's burden to show prejudice affecting substantial rights)
