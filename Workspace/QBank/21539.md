Subtopic: Inchoate Crimes
Outline: Conspiracy
Question: Alice and Bob conspired to commit armed robbery. They carried out the robbery as planned, stealing $20,000 from a jewelry store. Both are convicted of armed robbery. The prosecutor also seeks convictions for conspiracy to commit armed robbery based on their pre-crime agreement. Defense counsel argues that because the conspiracy fully ripened into the completed robbery, the conspiracy charge should merge and be dismissed. Is defense counsel's merger argument correct under common law?
Answer A: Yes, because inchoate offenses always merge into the completed crime
Answer B: Yes, because the conspiracy's purpose was fully achieved, leaving nothing for a separate conviction to punish
Answer C: No, because conspiracy does not merge into the completed crime at common law
Answer D: No, but only because the robbery involved violence, which creates an exception to the merger rule
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The common-law rule is clear and unambiguous: conspiracy does not merge into the substantive offense that is its object. Alice and Bob may be convicted and separately punished for both the conspiracy and the completed armed robbery. The agreement is a standalone offense. This distinguishes conspiracy from attempt (which merges into the completed crime) and from solicitation (which merges into either the conspiracy when agreed or the completed crime). Defense counsel's merger argument is legally incorrect.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The merger principle does not apply uniformly to all inchoate offenses. Specifically, conspiracy is a well-established exception. Common law has long held that conspiracy does not merge into the completed crime because the conspiracy — the dangerous agreement itself — is an independent evil that justifies separate punishment. B: The rationale for conspiracy's non-merger rule is precisely that the agreement itself is a distinct social harm — it creates an organized criminal unit, it increases the likelihood of crime, it tends to produce more sophisticated criminal activity, and it is harder to detect. Success of the plan does not erase this independent harm. Courts have consistently rejected the "nothing left to punish" argument. D: The non-merger rule for conspiracy is categorical — it applies regardless of whether the completed crime was violent or non-violent. There is no "violence exception" that explains the rule. The correct reason conspiracy does not merge is simply the longstanding common-law rule based on the independent harm of the conspiratorial agreement.
lb_rule_reference: Conspiracy does not merge into the completed crime; a defendant who agrees to commit a crime and then completes it may be convicted and punished separately for both conspiracy and the substantive offense
