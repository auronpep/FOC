Question: A city council enacts an ordinance prohibiting any residence within city limits from housing more than three unrelated persons, but explicitly exempts "traditional families," defined as groups of persons related by blood, marriage, or adoption of any number. The ordinance's stated purpose is "preserving neighborhood character." Legislative history includes public hearing testimony in which council members referred to "those communal living arrangements" as "not normal households" and expressed concern about "that kind of group" moving into residential areas. A tenant advocacy group challenges the ordinance as applied to unrelated groups of four or more adult residents, arguing that the exclusion of unrelated cohabitants serves no legitimate purpose other than disfavor for communal-living lifestyles. Applying Equal Protection rational basis review, how should a court rule on the ordinance?
Answer A: The ordinance survives because controlling residential density is a conceivable legitimate government interest that rationally supports the three-unrelated-persons limit
Answer B: The ordinance is subject to strict scrutiny because it targets a discrete and insular group of non-traditional household members
Answer C: The ordinance likely fails because the unlimited family-size exemption, combined with evidence of council hostility to communal living, suggests the sole purpose is animus toward an identifiable group, which is not a legitimate government interest
Answer D: The ordinance fails because lifestyle choices of cohabitation are a fundamental right subject to strict scrutiny
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Even under rational basis review, a law fails if its only conceivable purpose is a bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group. Here, the ordinance's unlimited exemption for related households undermines any density-based justification, and the legislative record reflects explicit hostility to communal living as a lifestyle. Under the rational basis with bite analysis the Supreme Court has applied in analogous contexts, bare animus is not a legitimate government interest.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The ordinance exempts related households of any size — so the density concern cannot be the actual or conceivable purpose driving the unrelated-persons limit. Where the legislative record reveals that the sole operative purpose is animus toward an identifiable lifestyle group, courts applying rational basis will look beyond surface rationales. A conceivable but factually contradicted justification does not save a law driven purely by bare animus. B: Communal-living residents are not a racial, national-origin, or other suspect class. The Supreme Court has not recognized lifestyle-based classifications as suspect. The proper analysis remains rational basis. However, even rational basis review can invalidate a law when the record shows the law was motivated by bare animus rather than any legitimate purpose. D: The Supreme Court has not recognized a fundamental right to live with a chosen group of unrelated persons as a fundamental right triggering strict scrutiny. The relevant analytical path is rational basis, where the law fails on animus grounds — not because the classification impairs a fundamental right.
lb_rule_reference: A law fails even rational basis review if its sole conceivable purpose is to disadvantage a politically unpopular group; bare animus toward a group is not a legitimate government interest.
