MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Negligence
Outline: Proximate (legal) cause
Question: A contractor negligently left a stack of lumber unsecured beside a quiet residential sidewalk. Before any wind or pedestrian contact occurred, a stranger used the lumber to build a barricade, set it on fire for amusement, and intentionally pushed it into a parked car. Is the stranger's conduct likely a superseding cause of the car damage?
Answer A: Yes, because the intentional arson and pushing were extraordinary risks different from the unsecured-lumber risk
Answer B: No, because every criminal act is foreseeable as a matter of law
Answer C: No, because the contractor's negligence created the lumber's physical existence
Answer D: Yes, because all intervening acts automatically cut off liability
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The criminal act is not the kind of harm that made leaving lumber unsecured negligent.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Criminal intervention can be superseding when it is extraordinary and outside the risk. C: But-for involvement is not enough for proximate cause. D: Only superseding intervening acts cut off proximate cause.
lb_rule_reference: An extraordinary intentional or criminal act can be a superseding cause when it is not among the risks that made the defendant's conduct negligent.
