Question: Simon was indicted for aggravated assault after allegedly stabbing a victim during a street fight. An attorney was appointed. While in custody awaiting trial, a detective gave Simon Miranda warnings and asked him about the same stabbing incident but framed the questioning around whether the victim might die — i.e., about a potential murder charge — rather than about the assault charge. Simon, believing the questions were only about a hypothetical future charge, waived his Miranda rights and made statements. The victim later died, and Simon was charged with murder. He moved to suppress his statements. Are Simon's statements admissible in the murder prosecution?
Answer A: No, because the Sixth Amendment right attached to the assault charge and the statements arose from the same criminal episode
Answer B: No, because police deliberately framed questions about the same incident to circumvent the Sixth Amendment
Answer C: Yes, the statements are admissible, because murder was a separate uncharged offense and counsel had not attached to it
Answer D: Yes, the statements are admissible, because Simon waived his Sixth Amendment right by answering after Miranda warnings
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under the *McNeil* offense-specific rule, the Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches charge by charge. At the time of questioning, Simon had been charged with aggravated assault, not murder. Murder is a legally distinct offense. Therefore, the Sixth Amendment right had not attached to murder. Because Simon received Miranda warnings and validly waived, the statements are admissible in the murder prosecution. The factual overlap between the two crimes does not merge them for Sixth Amendment purposes.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Sixth Amendment is offense-specific and turns on the legal definition of the offense, not the factual transaction. Murder and aggravated assault are distinct offenses under the relevant statute. Counsel for the assault does not automatically cover questioning about a potential murder arising from the same event. B: Courts apply the offense-specific rule based on the legal charge, not police intent to circumvent the Sixth Amendment. Because murder had not been charged and is a legally distinct offense from assault, the Sixth Amendment right had not attached to it regardless of investigative strategy. D: The Sixth Amendment right had not attached to murder at all, so there was no Sixth Amendment right to waive with respect to that charge. The correct analysis is non-attachment, not waiver. Miranda waiver governs; the Sixth Amendment is simply inapplicable.
lb_rule_reference: The Sixth Amendment offense-specific rule turns on legal identity of offenses, not factual overlap; a different statutory offense is a different offense even if it arises from the same events
