Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: A state prisoner was convicted of possession with intent to distribute cocaine. At trial, the state introduced cocaine seized during a warrantless vehicle search. Before trial, the defendant moved to suppress, arguing the search violated the Fourth Amendment. The state trial court held a full suppression hearing, found the search reasonable under the automobile exception, and denied the motion. The defendant appealed; the state court of appeals affirmed the denial after briefing and argument. The defendant did not raise additional suppression arguments in the state supreme court. The defendant now files a federal habeas petition under § 2254, arguing the cocaine should have been suppressed and that its admission at trial constitutes constitutional error. Should the federal court hear the merits of the defendant's Fourth Amendment habeas claim?
Answer A: Yes, because Fourth Amendment violations are constitutional claims and federal courts must review all constitutional claims raised in federal habeas petitions
Answer B: No, because Stone v. Powell bars Fourth Amendment suppression claims in federal habeas when the state provided a full and fair opportunity to litigate the claim, and that condition is satisfied here
Answer C: No, but only because the defendant failed to raise the Fourth Amendment claim in the state supreme court, making it unexhausted and procedurally defaulted
Answer D: Yes, because Stone v. Powell applies only when the state court actually suppressed evidence; when the state court denied suppression, the federal court must independently review the Fourth Amendment merits
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Stone v. Powell held that where a state prisoner has had an opportunity for full and fair litigation of a Fourth Amendment suppression claim in state court, the federal courts will not reconsider the merits of that claim on habeas review. The key inquiry is whether the state provided a meaningful opportunity to litigate—not whether the state courts reached the correct answer. Here, the defendant received a full suppression hearing at trial and appellate review. The state process was full and fair. The federal court should decline to reach the merits of the Fourth Amendment claim under Stone v. Powell.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Stone v. Powell (1976) created a categorical exception specifically for Fourth Amendment suppression claims. Where the state has provided an opportunity for full and fair litigation of a Fourth Amendment claim, a federal habeas court may not reconsider the merits of that claim. The Court reasoned that the exclusionary rule's deterrence purpose is adequately served by the opportunity to litigate in state court, and that the costs of relitigation outweigh the marginal benefit. C: The correct basis for denying the habeas claim is Stone v. Powell, not procedural default. The defendant did present the suppression issue to the trial court and the intermediate appellate court—that constitutes full and fair litigation for Stone v. Powell purposes. Procedural default would apply if the defendant failed to raise the claim at all in state court through proper procedure, but that is not the situation here. Stone v. Powell is the dispositive doctrine. D: Stone v. Powell bars Fourth Amendment habeas claims based on whether the state provided an opportunity for full and fair litigation—not based on whether the state court suppressed or denied suppression. The bar applies regardless of whether the state court ruled for or against the defendant on the merits of the Fourth Amendment claim. The federal court does not step in as a corrective mechanism for incorrect state Fourth Amendment rulings.
lb_rule_reference: Stone v. Powell (1976) held that a state prisoner may not obtain federal habeas corpus relief on the ground that evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment was introduced at trial, if the state provided an opportunity for full and fair litigation of the Fourth Amendment claim in state court; this bar applies regardless of the merits of the underlying Fourth Amendment claim
