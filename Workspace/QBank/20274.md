Question: Paul signed and delivered a deed granting a utility company “a perpetual right to install and maintain electric lines across Paul's land.” The utility company did not own any neighboring parcel that benefited from the lines. Paul later argued that the grant failed because every easement must benefit a dominant estate. What is the best result?
Answer A: The grant can create a valid easement in gross because a utility easement may benefit the holder personally or commercially without a dominant parcel.
Answer B: The grant fails because all easements require a dominant estate.
Answer C: The grant creates a profit because electric lines remove resources from Paul's land.
Answer D: The grant creates only a revocable license because the utility company owns no neighboring land.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: An easement in gross benefits a person or entity rather than a dominant parcel. Commercial and utility easements in gross are valid when created by proper grant. The absence of a neighboring dominant parcel does not defeat the utility company's express easement.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because that rule applies to appurtenant easements, not easements in gross. C: C is wrong because the utility is not removing natural resources. D: D is wrong because a written perpetual utility grant is a property interest, not merely revocable permission.
lb_rule_reference: Commercial easement in gross / no dominant parcel required
