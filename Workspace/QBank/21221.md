Outline: Authentication and Identification
Question: In a federal fraud trial, the prosecutor calls Abigail to testify that she received a phone call from someone who said, “This is Moses. I need the account numbers tonight.” Abigail had never spoken with Moses before, did not recognize the voice, and the call came from an unknown number. The prosecutor offers the call to prove that Moses was the caller. How should the court rule on Moses's authentication objection?
Answer A: Sustain the objection, because the caller's self-identification alone is insufficient on these facts.
Answer B: Overrule the objection, because a caller's statement of his own name is always enough to authenticate the call.
Answer C: Overrule the objection only if Abigail testifies that the statement was not offered for its truth.
Answer D: Sustain the objection because telephone calls can be authenticated only by expert voice analysis.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A phone call must be connected to the alleged speaker by sufficient identifying circumstances. Here, Abigail had no voice familiarity, no assigned-number evidence, no distinctive knowledge, and only the caller's self-identification. That is not enough to support a finding that Moses was the caller.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because self-identification alone is not always enough, especially for an incoming call from an unknown number with no corroborating circumstances. C: C is wrong because the issue is caller identity, not hearsay purpose. D: D is wrong because expert voice analysis is not required; circumstantial or lay voice identification can authenticate a call.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 901(b)(5)-(6)
lb_student_diagnostic: Self-identification trap: student treats a caller's name claim as automatic authentication.
