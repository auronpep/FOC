Question: Nolan walked into a police station and said he wanted to confess to a bank robbery. Officers gave Miranda warnings, Nolan said he understood, and he calmly described the robbery. Later, a psychiatrist concluded that Nolan was experiencing delusions that made him feel compelled to confess. The officers had not threatened, tricked, or pressured him. Is Nolan's confession involuntary under due process?
Answer A: Yes, because Nolan's mental illness made his confession irrational
Answer B: Yes, because police must refuse to hear any confession from a mentally ill person
Answer C: No, because there was no coercive police conduct that overbore Nolan's will
Answer D: No, because Miranda warnings eliminate all voluntariness objections
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Nolan initiated the contact, received warnings, and confessed without police threats, promises, or pressure. Mental illness alone does not make the confession involuntary under due process.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Due process involuntariness requires coercive police conduct. Nolan's internal mental condition alone does not supply state coercion. B: There is no categorical rule barring statements by mentally ill suspects. The totality must include coercive police conduct. D: Miranda warnings do not automatically cure coercion. The confession is admissible because no coercive police conduct occurred.
lb_rule_reference: Due process involuntariness requires coercive state action that overbears the will; mental illness alone is not enough.
