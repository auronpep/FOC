Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Supplemental Jurisdiction
Question: A plaintiff files a complaint in federal court asserting a federal civil rights claim under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 and a related state-law negligence claim against a county sheriff. The two claims arise from the same incident. After discovery, the defendant moves for summary judgment on the § 1983 claim, which the court grants. The plaintiff's state-law negligence claim remains pending. The plaintiff argues that the court must retain and decide the state-law claim. The defendant argues that the court must dismiss it now that the federal claim is gone. The governing forum is federal court under the MBE baseline. What should the court do with the state-law negligence claim?
Answer A: Dismiss the state-law claim because the court's supplemental jurisdiction automatically terminates when the anchor federal claim is dismissed
Answer B: Retain the state-law claim because the court must exercise supplemental jurisdiction over any claim that was properly joined and shared a common nucleus with the original federal claim
Answer C: Exercise its discretion under § 1367(c)(3) to decide whether to retain or dismiss the state-law claim, considering factors such as judicial economy, fairness, convenience, and comity
Answer D: Dismiss the state-law claim because a federal court cannot adjudicate state-law claims in the absence of an independent basis for federal jurisdiction
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under § 1367(c)(3), when all claims over which the court had original jurisdiction have been dismissed, the court may — in its discretion — decline to exercise supplemental jurisdiction over the remaining state claims. The court is not required to dismiss nor required to retain the claim. The Supreme Court has identified relevant factors: judicial economy, convenience, fairness, and comity toward state courts. Typically, when no federal claim survives to trial, courts dismiss the state claims without prejudice, but this is a discretionary determination, not an automatic rule.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Under § 1367(c)(3), when all claims over which the court had original jurisdiction have been dismissed, the court MAY decline to exercise supplemental jurisdiction — it does not automatically lose that jurisdiction. The original grant of supplemental jurisdiction under § 1367(a) does not evaporate; the court retains discretion to keep or dismiss the state claim, weighing factors including judicial economy, fairness, convenience, and comity. B: Section 1367(a) grants supplemental jurisdiction; § 1367(c) provides grounds for declining it. Both provisions co-exist. After all federal claims are dismissed, the court's discretion under § 1367(c)(3) comes into play. Retaining the state claim may be appropriate in some circumstances, but the court is not compelled to do so. D: A federal court that has already acquired supplemental jurisdiction over a state claim retains that jurisdiction even after the anchor claim is dismissed — unless it exercises its discretion under § 1367(c) to decline. The statement that an "independent basis" is always required is wrong for supplemental claims. Section 1367(a) itself grants jurisdiction without requiring an independent basis.
lb_rule_reference: 28 U.S.C. § 1367(c)(3) — a court may decline to exercise supplemental jurisdiction over a state claim if the claim substantially predominates over the federal claim or all claims over which the court had original jurisdiction have been dismissed.
