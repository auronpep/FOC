MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Officers responded to a 911 call reporting screaming from inside a residence. Through a window, they observed an adult bleeding from the head and a person standing over him. Without obtaining a warrant, officers entered the home. While rendering aid to the injured person, an officer observed, in plain view on the kitchen counter, a bag of cocaine and a scale. The officer seized both items. The resident moves to suppress. Were the officers' entry and the seizure of the cocaine and scale constitutional?
Answer A: No, because the officers entered without a warrant and no recognized exception applies to the home
Answer B: Yes for the entry, but no for the seizure, because once the emergency was addressed, officers needed a warrant to seize anything
Answer C: Yes, because the entry was justified by the emergency aid exigency, and the cocaine and scale were lawfully seized under plain view during the officers' authorized presence
Answer D: No, because the 911 call was anonymous and could not provide the probable cause needed to justify entering the home
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The officers had an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone was seriously injured inside — screaming plus visible head wound. Under Brigham City v. Stuart, the emergency aid exception authorized warrantless entry. While rendering aid, officers were lawfully present in the kitchen. The cocaine and scale were in plain view, and their incriminating character (drug paraphernalia) was immediately apparent. All three plain view conditions are satisfied: lawful vantage point, immediately apparent incriminating nature, and lawful access. Both the entry and the seizure are constitutional.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The emergency aid exception is a well-established exigent-circumstances doctrine. In Brigham City v. Stuart and Michigan v. Fisher, the Supreme Court held that officers may enter a home without a warrant when they have an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone inside is seriously injured or faces imminent danger. Screaming and visible bleeding plainly satisfy this standard. B: This overstates the required warrant step. While officers may not extend their presence beyond what the emergency justifies, plain view allows seizure of items whose incriminating character is immediately apparent without any additional search. Officers lawfully in the kitchen while rendering aid were in a lawful position. The cocaine and scale were immediately apparent as contraband. Plain view authorizes the seizure. D: The emergency aid exception does not require probable cause — it requires an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone inside needs help. Moreover, the officers did not rely solely on the 911 call; they personally observed through the window an adult bleeding and another person standing over him. Direct sensory observation by responding officers independently satisfied the emergency aid standard.
lb_rule_reference: The emergency aid exception permits warrantless entry when officers have an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone inside is seriously injured or in imminent danger; evidence found in plain view during the emergency response is admissible (Brigham City v. Stuart; Michigan v. Fisher)
