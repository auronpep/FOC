Subtopic: Inchoate Crimes
Outline: Conspiracy
Question: Alex and Brenda, both married to other people, agreed with each other to engage in an ongoing adulterous relationship. Adultery is a crime in the jurisdiction. The prosecutor charges both Alex and Brenda with (1) adultery and (2) conspiracy to commit adultery. No third party was involved in the planning or commission of the relationship. Under common-law Wharton's Rule, may Alex and Brenda be convicted of both adultery and conspiracy to commit adultery?
Answer A: Yes, because conspiracy and the completed crime are separate offenses that do not merge
Answer B: No, because Wharton's Rule bars conspiracy charges when the target crime necessarily requires two participants and no third party is involved
Answer C: No, because conspiracy merges into the completed crime of adultery
Answer D: Yes, but only if the prosecutor can show they planned the relationship before it began
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Wharton's Rule prevents a conspiracy charge from being added on top of a substantive crime whose very definition requires the participation of two persons. Adultery, by definition, requires two parties. The rationale is that the "agreement" to commit such a crime is already baked into the substantive offense — punishing the agreement separately would amount to double punishment. Because no third party was involved, the exception to Wharton's Rule (which applies when a third outsider participates) does not apply. Both may be convicted of adultery but not also of conspiracy to commit adultery.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The non-merger rule for conspiracy is correct in general, but Wharton's Rule is an exception to the ability to charge conspiracy in the first place. For crimes that structurally require two parties, the conspiracy charge is barred unless a third party is involved. Non-merger is a separate issue. C: Conspiracy does not merge into the completed crime as a general matter. Wharton's Rule bars the conspiracy charge here, but for a specific, different reason: the structural two-party requirement of the target offense. Citing merger is a doctrinal misstatement. D: Pre-planning is a generic feature of conspiracy, not a special requirement that overcomes Wharton's Rule. Even with extensive pre-planning, if the offense structurally requires two parties and no third party exists, the conspiracy charge is barred.
lb_rule_reference: Wharton's Rule: when the definition of a substantive offense necessarily requires the participation of two or more persons (e.g., adultery, bigamy, incest, dueling), they cannot be charged with conspiracy to commit that offense unless a third party is also involved
