Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Authentication and Identification
Question: Ruth sues Jacob for breach of a delivery contract. Ruth testifies that she called the phone number Jacob had listed on the contract. A man answered, said, “This is Jacob,” and discussed the specific delivery order described in the contract. Ruth offers testimony about the call to show that Jacob agreed to delay delivery. Jacob objects that Ruth has not authenticated the speaker. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection because a person can never authenticate a phone call without recognizing the speaker's voice.
Answer B: Overrule the objection because the call was made to Jacob's assigned number and the conversation included identifying circumstances.
Answer C: Sustain the objection because Ruth must produce the phone company's complete call records.
Answer D: Overrule the objection only if Jacob later admits that he answered the call.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: A call made to a number assigned to a person may be authenticated when circumstances, including self-identification and discussion of the relevant transaction, show that the person answering was the one called. Ruth's testimony is enough for threshold authentication.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because voice recognition is not the only method of authenticating a telephone conversation. C: C is wrong because phone company records are not categorically required. D: D is wrong because a party admission is not required for authentication.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 901(b)(6)
lb_student_diagnostic: Proof-channel over-demand: student requires call records or voice recognition despite assigned-number plus transaction circumstances.
