Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Federal agents suspected a defendant of tax fraud. Without obtaining a warrant or subpoena, agents contacted the defendant's bank and requested copies of the defendant's checking account statements, deposit records, and canceled checks for the past two years. The bank complied. The records showed large cash deposits inconsistent with the defendant's reported income. The defendant moves to suppress the bank records, arguing the government violated his Fourth Amendment rights. Should the court grant the motion to suppress?
Answer A: Yes, because the defendant had a reasonable expectation that his bank records would remain private
Answer B: Yes, because the government failed to obtain a subpoena before demanding the records
Answer C: No, because the defendant voluntarily disclosed the information to the bank, eliminating any Fourth Amendment protection under the third-party doctrine
Answer D: No, because financial records are excluded from the Fourth Amendment by statute
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under United States v. Miller and the third-party doctrine, a depositor has no protected Fourth Amendment interest in financial records held by a bank because the records were voluntarily conveyed to the bank in the ordinary course of the banking relationship. By sharing transaction details with the bank, the defendant assumed the risk of disclosure to the government. No warrant or subpoena is constitutionally required. Suppression is denied.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: United States v. Miller held that bank records are not protected by the Fourth Amendment because the customer voluntarily conveyed the information to the bank when making deposits and transactions. By sharing the records with the bank, the customer assumes the risk that the bank will disclose them to the government. The subjective expectation of privacy is not objectively reasonable given the voluntary disclosure. B: The Fourth Amendment does not require a warrant or subpoena for bank records because the defendant had no Fourth Amendment interest in them. Statutory frameworks may impose procedural requirements, but a Fourth Amendment suppression motion cannot succeed where no constitutional right was implicated. D: The correct basis is the constitutional third-party doctrine under Miller, not statutory exclusion. Misidentifying the source of the rule is a significant error because it gets the analytical framework wrong and will not survive a follow-up question.
lb_rule_reference: When a person voluntarily conveys information to a third party, they assume the risk that the third party will disclose it to the government; no warrant is needed to obtain those records (Smith v. Maryland; United States v. Miller)
