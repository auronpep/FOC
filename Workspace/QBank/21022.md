Question: Volkov was charged in federal court with leading a racketeering enterprise involving multiple murders and drug trafficking. At the detention hearing, the government argued that Volkov posed an extreme danger to the community and that no combination of conditions could reasonably assure public safety. The magistrate reviewed detailed evidence of Volkov's alleged role in ordering murders of witnesses and held Volkov without bail pending trial. Volkov challenged the detention order, arguing that the Eighth Amendment prohibits pretrial detention without bail. Is the federal court's pretrial detention of Volkov without bail constitutional?
Answer A: No, because the Eighth Amendment categorically prohibits denying bail before a conviction
Answer B: No, because the government need only show probable cause that the defendant committed the charged offense
Answer C: Yes, because under *United States v. Salerno* the federal Bail Reform Act permits pretrial detention when the government proves by clear and convincing evidence that no condition will assure community safety
Answer D: Yes, but only if the charge is a capital offense
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Salerno* (1987) upheld the Bail Reform Act's preventive detention provisions against Eighth Amendment and due process challenges. A federal magistrate may detain a defendant without bail when, after a hearing with adequate procedural protections, the government establishes by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant poses a danger to the community and no conditions can adequately mitigate that danger. The facts here satisfy those requirements.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: In *United States v. Salerno* (1987), the Supreme Court upheld the federal Bail Reform Act's preventive detention provisions. The Court held that pretrial detention for dangerousness does not violate the Eighth Amendment or due process when the government demonstrates the requisite level of danger and procedural safeguards are present. B: Under the federal Bail Reform Act, preventive detention for dangerousness requires the government to prove by clear and convincing evidence that no condition or combination of conditions will reasonably protect the community — a far higher standard than probable cause. D: The Bail Reform Act permits pretrial detention for a wider category of serious offenses and for dangerousness, not only capital charges. The dangerousness prong has no capital-offense requirement.
lb_rule_reference: The federal Bail Reform Act and *United States v. Salerno* permit pretrial detention without bail when the government proves by clear and convincing evidence that no condition or combination of conditions will reasonably assure the safety of any other person or the community
