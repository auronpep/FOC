MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Civil Procedure
Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Consent
Question: Pelletier Imports LLC was sued in a Harwick federal court. Pelletier's attorney filed an answer that included the following language: "Defendant denies the allegations of the complaint. Defendant reserves all defenses, including but not limited to jurisdictional defenses." Three months later, Pelletier participated in a scheduling conference, produced documents in discovery, and took two depositions. Before the close of discovery, Pelletier filed a motion to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction. The plaintiff argues the motion is untimely because Pelletier waived personal jurisdiction by engaging in discovery. Has Pelletier waived its personal jurisdiction defense?
Answer A: No, because Pelletier's answer expressly reserved "all defenses, including jurisdictional defenses," which preserved the PJ objection indefinitely
Answer B: Yes, because Pelletier engaged in discovery — production and depositions — which constitutes a general appearance and waives personal jurisdiction
Answer C: Yes, because although the answer referenced jurisdictional defenses generically, the combination of a non-specific reservation clause and three months of active merits participation — scheduling, discovery production, and depositions — constitutes waiver of the personal jurisdiction defense
Answer D: No, because the motion was filed before the close of discovery, so Pelletier preserved the jurisdictional challenge by raising it at the earliest practicable opportunity
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Rule 12(h)(1) requires that the PJ defense be specifically raised in the first responsive pleading. A vague reservation of "all defenses including jurisdictional defenses" is likely insufficient as a specific assertion of personal jurisdiction objection. Even if it were sufficient, three months of scheduling-conference participation, document production, and two depositions without pressing the PJ defense represents extensive merits engagement that courts treat as implied consent to jurisdiction. The combination of an inadequately specific pleading defense and extensive subsequent merits conduct establishes waiver.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A generic reservation clause in an answer does not preserve a personal jurisdiction defense indefinitely. Under FRCP 12, the question is whether the defense was specifically raised in the first responsive pleading — not whether it was mentioned generically. More critically, under *Insurance Corp. of Ireland*, extensive post-answer litigation conduct without pressing the PJ objection can itself constitute waiver through conduct. The facts require determining whether the answer's language was sufficiently specific and whether subsequent conduct constituted implied consent. B: This is the right analysis framework but an overbroad application. Under FRCP 12, what matters is whether the PJ defense was raised in the answer or first pre-answer motion. Here, Pelletier's answer included a reference to jurisdictional defenses and did not make a pre-answer motion. If the PJ defense was sufficiently raised in the answer — which requires factual analysis of whether the language was specific enough — subsequent discovery participation may not automatically waive it. Courts split on whether generic answer language plus continued litigation constitutes waiver; the outcome depends on the specificity of the answer's defense and the extent of merits engagement. D: The timing of a PJ motion is measured from the answer and the Rule 12 motion-practice rules — not from the close of discovery or trial. Rule 12(h)(1)(B) requires that defenses not raised in the first responsive pleading are waived. The question is whether the answer adequately raised PJ, and whether subsequent conduct waived whatever protection the answer provided — not whether the motion came before some later procedural milestone. "Before discovery closed" is not a recognized safe harbor for PJ motions.
lb_rule_reference: A defendant who voluntarily appears in court and litigates the merits without timely challenging personal jurisdiction waives (consents to) that court's jurisdiction. Under FRCP 12, the PJ defense must be raised in the first pre-answer motion or the first responsive pleading; failure to do so waives it. A defendant may enter a special appearance to contest jurisdiction only without waiving the objection. *Insurance Corp. of Ireland v. Compagnie des Bauxites de Guinée* (1982).
