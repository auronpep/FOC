


BARMATRIX Q#: 22865
Outline_code: 94010000
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Civil Procedure
Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Federal Subject-Matter Jurisdiction
Question: A manufacturer, a State A corporation, entered into an agreement with a retailer, a State B corporation, to transfer its business in certain patented devices in exchange for a portion of the retailer's profits. The contract was made and substantially performed in State A. The contract did not contain a choice of law clause. Several years later, the manufacturer sued the retailer in federal district court in State B based on diversity jurisdiction. After a jury trial, the manufacturer was awarded $1 million in damages. The manufacturer then moved to have interest added to the award pursuant to State A law. Under State B law and federal common law, no interest could be added to the award. Can the manufacturer have interest added to the award?
Answer A: A: Yes, because the contract was made and substantially performed in State A, and there was no choice of law clause.
Answer B: B: Yes, because the contract was made and substantially performed in State A, and State B courts would be obliged to give effect to State A laws under the Full Faith and Credit Clause.
Answer C: C: No, because choice of law rules are substantive.
Answer D: D: No, because federal common law did not allow interest to be added to the award.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: When exercising diversity jurisdiction, a federal court has to apply the substantive law of the state in which it sits. Erie Railroad v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938). The United States Supreme Court has established that choice of law rules are substantive. Klaxon v. Stentor Electric Manufacturing Co., 313 U.S. 487 (1941). Consequently, the State B federal court should apply State B law and not allow interest to be added to the award. Therefore, A, B, and D are incorrect.
