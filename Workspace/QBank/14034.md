


BARMATRIX Q#: 14034
Outline_code: 94010100
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Civil Procedure
Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Diversity Jurisdiction
Question: An investor from State A sued a State A company in State A court, asserting two claims: (i) a violation of federal securities laws, over which state and federal courts have concurrent subject-matter jurisdiction; and (ii) fraud under State A law. The company filed a notice of removal in the local federal district court. Several months later, after seeking the advice of counsel, the investor decided to voluntarily dismiss his federal claim and moved to remand the case to state court. May the court grant the investor's motion to remand?
Answer A: A: Yes, because if the federal court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction at any time before final judgment, the case must be remanded to state court.
Answer B: B: Yes, because the federal court has dismissed all claims over which there existed an independent basis of jurisdiction and may use its discretion to remand.
Answer C: C: No, because the federal court still has subject-matter jurisdiction, even after dismissal of the federal claim, and is therefore obligated to hear the case.
Answer D: D: No, because the motion to remand was made more than 30 days after the filing of the notice of removal and is therefore untimely.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: B is correct. The court initially exerted supplemental jurisdiction over the investor's state law claim under federal question jurisdiction, and the dismissal of the federal claim allows the court to exercise its discretion to decline to hear the remaining claim without an independent basis for jurisdiction.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A is incorrect. The court should grant the motion to remand, but not because it must do so based on lack of subject- matter jurisdiction. The investor's dismissal of his federal question claim did not automatically oust the court of jurisdiction. Rather, the court may exercise its discretion to remand because it dismissed the federal question claim and no other independent basis for jurisdiction exists. C is incorrect. Although the federal court could decide to hear the remaining state law claim, it is not obligated to adjudicate every claim within their supplemental jurisdiction, especially after the federal claim was dismissed. D is incorrect. The investor's motion to remand was not untimely, as it was made promptly after the federal question claim was eliminated from the case.
additional_info: Supplemental jurisdiction allows a federal court with original subject-matter jurisdiction to hear other claims arising out of the same transaction or occurrence as the original claim, even if they do not have independent grounds for federal jurisdiction. 28 U.S.C. §1367. The underlying basis for subject-matter jurisdiction may be either federal question or diversity. When the original claim is based on federal question jurisdiction, § 1367 allows the federal court to assert jurisdiction over similar and related state law claims. However, federal courts are not required to hear every claim falling under supplemental jurisdiction. A federal court may decline to exercise supplemental jurisdiction when it has dismissed all claims over which it had original jurisdiction. The later a federal court dismisses an original jurisdiction claim, the less likely it is to decline to hear the remaining supplemental claim(s). A federal court may simply decline to exercise supplemental jurisdiction if: (i) the [supplemental] claim raises a novel or complex issue of state law; (ii) the claim substantially predominates over the claim which the district court has original jurisdiction; OR (iii) any other exceptional or compelling reason for declining jurisdiction exists. See 28 U.S.C. § 1367(c). B is correct. The court initially exerted supplemental jurisdiction over the investor's state law claim because the court had federal question jurisdiction over the investor's federal securities law claim. Because the federal claim was dismissed, § 1367 allows the court to decline to hear the remaining state law claim. Therefore, without an independent basis for jurisdiction, the federal court may remand the state law claim. A is incorrect. This answer choice states the correct conclusion with the incorrect legal reasoning. The federal district court did not automatically lose subject-matter jurisdiction over the state claim when the federal question claim was dismissed. Its decision to remand may be based on its discretion, but it is not required to do so. Under § 1367, the federal court still has supplemental jurisdiction over the state law claim, but without an independent basis for jurisdiction, it may decline to hear it and remand. C is incorrect. Neither maintaining nor losing supplemental jurisdiction requires an "automatic" decision around adjudication in federal court. There is no obligation for a federal court to hear every claim falling under supplemental jurisdiction. As explained above, § 1367 allows this court to decline to exercise supplemental jurisdiction. D is incorrect. This choice contains an incorrect application of the law. The investor's motion to remand was made promptly after the federal question claim was eliminated from the case. The motion was therefore not untimely, so it is not a basis to deny the remand.
