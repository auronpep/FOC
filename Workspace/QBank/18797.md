internal_id: CP-037

MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Civil Procedure
Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Federal-Question Jurisdiction
Question: A tenant sues her landlord in federal court for breach of a residential lease. The complaint recites state-law breach of contract as the cause of action. Anticipating an expected defense, the tenant also alleges in the complaint: "The defendant will assert that the federal Fair Housing Act preempts the tenant's state remedy, but that defense is without merit because the Fair Housing Act does not preempt state contract remedies." The landlord moves to dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. The governing forum is federal court under the MBE baseline. Should the court dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction?
Answer A: No, because the complaint expressly references a federal statute, giving the court a sufficient basis to exercise § 1331 jurisdiction
Answer B: No, because the landlord's anticipated defense will require construction of the Fair Housing Act, creating a federal issue
Answer C: Yes, because the complaint's only cause of action is state breach of contract and the federal reference goes only to an anticipated defense
Answer D: Yes, but only because the tenant failed to file the complaint in state court first
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under the well-pleaded complaint rule, a plaintiff cannot bootstrap federal jurisdiction by anticipating and preemptively refuting a federal defense. The court looks only at the plaintiff's cause of action — here, state breach of contract. Neither the mention of the Fair Housing Act nor the anticipation of preemption transforms the state claim into a federal cause of action. Dismissal for lack of subject matter jurisdiction is required.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Merely mentioning a federal statute does not create federal-question jurisdiction. The question is whether federal law creates the plaintiff's cause of action or is a necessary element of the state cause of action. Here the cause of action is state breach of contract; the Fair Housing Act reference appears only to negate an anticipated defense. That is insufficient. B: The well-pleaded complaint rule requires courts to ignore anticipated defenses when assessing jurisdiction — even when the plaintiff has helpfully flagged those defenses in the complaint. A plaintiff cannot manufacture jurisdiction by pleading that a federal defense exists and is wrong; the rule does not permit that maneuver. D: There is no exhaustion requirement that forces a plaintiff to file in state court before filing in federal court. Jurisdiction is assessed by the nature of the claim, not by filing sequence. The dismissal here is for lack of federal subject matter jurisdiction over a state-law claim, not because of a procedural sequencing error.
