Subtopic: Separation of Powers
Outline: Treaties and foreign affairs
Question: To settle claims between American businesses and a foreign government, the President enters an executive agreement with that government. The agreement assigns covered claims to a federal claims process and bars state-court litigation over those claims. A state statute later directs state courts to hear the same claims because the legislature thinks the federal settlement is unfair. How should a court most likely treat the state statute?
Answer A: The state statute controls because only Article II treaties can preempt state law
Answer B: The state statute is likely preempted because it conflicts with a valid federal executive agreement resolving foreign claims
Answer C: The state statute controls because state courts have general jurisdiction over private lawsuits
Answer D: The executive agreement is valid only if Congress reenacts it after the state statute passes
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The agreement resolves foreign claims through a federal mechanism. A state law requiring courts to hear the same barred claims conflicts with that federal foreign-affairs arrangement and is likely preempted.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Valid executive agreements can preempt conflicting state law in appropriate foreign-affairs settings. Treaty form is not the only route to federal preemption. C: General jurisdiction does not defeat federal preemption when a valid federal agreement bars the claims from state-court litigation. D: A valid executive agreement need not be reenacted each time a state passes a conflicting law.
lb_rule_reference: A valid executive agreement within the President's foreign-affairs authority can preempt conflicting state law, even though it is not an Article II treaty.
