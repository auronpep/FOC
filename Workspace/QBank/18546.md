MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Civil Procedure
Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Diversity Jurisdiction
Question: A plaintiff, then a citizen of Georgia, files a diversity action against a defendant, a citizen of Florida, seeking $90,000. The amount in controversy is satisfied. Three months after filing, the plaintiff moves permanently to Florida, where she intends to remain indefinitely. The defendant moves to dismiss for lack of diversity jurisdiction, arguing that the plaintiff is now a Florida citizen and the parties share the same citizenship. The governing forum is federal court under the MBE baseline. Should the court dismiss for lack of diversity jurisdiction?
Answer A: Yes, because the court must continuously assess diversity throughout the case, and the parties now share Florida citizenship
Answer B: No, because diversity of citizenship is assessed at the time the complaint is filed, and at that time the parties were diverse
Answer C: Yes, because diversity jurisdiction requires the parties to remain citizens of different states for the entire duration of the litigation in order for the court to retain power over the case
Answer D: No, but the plaintiff should be required to amend the complaint to reallege diversity based on her prior citizenship, because the complaint as filed now reflects inaccurate citizenship information
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Under the snapshot rule, diversity of citizenship under § 1332 is assessed at the time the complaint is filed. At the time of filing, the plaintiff was a citizen of Georgia and the defendant was a citizen of Florida — the parties were completely diverse and the amount in controversy exceeded $75,000. The plaintiff's subsequent relocation to Florida after filing does not retroactively destroy jurisdiction that was properly established at filing. The motion to dismiss should be denied.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Federal courts assess diversity at the time of filing. Once diversity jurisdiction is properly established at the time the complaint is filed, subsequent changes in citizenship do not destroy it. The plaintiff was a Georgia citizen when she filed; the defendant was a Florida citizen; complete diversity existed at filing. The post-filing move does not disturb that. C: For diversity cases, the required ongoing basis is not continuous diversity — it is continuous Article III controversy (a live dispute between adverse parties). The citizenship diversity requirement is only a § 1332 statutory requirement, assessed at filing. Changes to citizenship after filing do not affect the court's Article III or § 1332 authority over a case properly filed. D: There is no requirement that a party amend her complaint to update her post-filing citizenship. The complaint stated the citizenship at the time of filing — which is the relevant time — and that information was accurate. Post-filing changes to citizenship do not require any amendment because they are legally irrelevant to jurisdiction.
lb_rule_reference: Diversity of citizenship for § 1332 purposes is determined at the time the complaint is filed; later changes in citizenship do not affect jurisdiction already (or not yet) established.
