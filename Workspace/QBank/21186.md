Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Specific and General Jurisdiction
Question: Nexfort Life Insurance Co. is incorporated in Delaware with its principal place of business in Indianapolis, Indiana. Nexfort sells life insurance policies directly to residents of the State of Palladin through a mass-mail marketing campaign, targeted at Palladin ZIP codes. Nexfort issues the policies from Indianapolis and mails them to Palladin customers. Palladin policyholders send premium payments to Indianapolis. Nexfort is licensed by the Palladin Insurance Commissioner and maintains a registered agent in Palladin as required by Palladin law. Harriet, a Palladin resident, purchased a Nexfort policy. After Harriet died, her estate filed a claim. Nexfort denied the claim, alleging a misrepresentation in the application. Harriet's estate sued Nexfort in Palladin federal court for breach of contract and bad-faith denial. Nexfort moves to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction, arguing that the policy was issued in Indiana and all its processing occurs there. Does the Palladin court have specific personal jurisdiction over Nexfort?
Answer A: No, because the insurance contract was formed in Indiana — Nexfort issued the policy from Indianapolis — so all relevant contractual conduct occurred outside Palladin
Answer B: No, because the premium payments flowed from Palladin to Indiana, establishing Indiana as the locus of the financial relationship — Nexfort therefore had contacts with Indiana, not Palladin
Answer C: Yes, because Nexfort deliberately targeted Palladin residents through ZIP-code marketing, issued coverage to a Palladin resident, maintained a Palladin registered agent, and holds a Palladin insurance license — all deliberate acts of purposeful availment giving rise to this claim
Answer D: Yes, because Nexfort is licensed and has a registered agent in Palladin, and licensing constitutes general consent to jurisdiction under Mallory
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Nexfort's Palladin contacts are its own deliberate acts: targeted ZIP-code marketing to Palladin addresses, issuance of coverage to Palladin residents, state licensing in Palladin, and maintenance of a Palladin registered agent. Under *McGee*, even a single insurance policy sold to a forum resident provides sufficient contacts for specific jurisdiction when the claim arises from that policy. Here, Nexfort's contacts are more extensive. The breach-of-contract and bad-faith claim arises directly from Nexfort's Palladin-targeted policy. Both the statutory step (licensed in Palladin; registered agent for service) and due-process step (purposeful availment, relatedness) are satisfied.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.* (1957) held that a California court had personal jurisdiction over a Texas insurer that had issued a single policy to a California resident by mail, even though the company had no other California contacts. The Court emphasized that the insurance contract had a substantial connection with the forum state — it was sold to, and covered the life of, a California resident. Here, Nexfort specifically targeted Palladin through ZIP-code marketing, issued policies to Palladin residents, and is licensed in Palladin. Where the policy was processed internally is not the dispositive contact — the deliberate marketing to and coverage of forum-state residents is. B: Premium payments flowing from the insured's forum to the insurer's state are the insured's payments — they reflect the insured's act, not the insurer's contact with the forum. The relevant contacts for Nexfort's purposeful availment of Palladin are Nexfort's acts: ZIP-code-targeted marketing, issuing coverage to Palladin residents, accepting applications from Palladin, and maintaining a licensed Palladin registered agent. These are Nexfort's deliberate, forum-directed acts. D: The specific-jurisdiction analysis directly resolves this case — the claim arises from Nexfort's Palladin-directed policy, and purposeful availment is clear. Whether the registration also constitutes consent to general jurisdiction is a separate (and post-*Mallory* contested) question. The cleanest and most straightforward basis for PJ here is specific jurisdiction under *McGee*. Using general-jurisdiction consent theory when specific jurisdiction is directly available is an unnecessary analytical detour and conflates two distinct jurisdictional theories.
lb_rule_reference: An insurer that sells policies to forum-state residents through licensed agents or direct marketing in the forum, knowing the insured is in the forum, and providing coverage for forum-state risks, has purposefully availed itself of the forum. Claims arising from such policies satisfy the relatedness prong of specific jurisdiction. *McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.* (1957).
