Subtopic: Strict Liability and Products Liability
Outline: Strict Products Liability
Question: A hardware retailer sold a new rolling tool cart to a contractor. A wheel bracket had cracked during manufacturing before the cart reached the retailer. While the contractor pushed the loaded cart through a hallway in ordinary use, the bracket snapped and the cart tipped onto a passerby, injuring the passerby's leg. The passerby did not buy the cart and had no contract with the retailer. The passerby sues the retailer under strict products liability. Is the passerby likely to prevail on a strict products liability claim against the retailer?
Answer A: No, because the passerby was not in privity with the retailer
Answer B: Yes, because the retailer was a commercial seller and the defect caused personal injury to a foreseeable bystander
Answer C: No, because only the contractor who bought the cart may sue for product defects
Answer D: Yes, because the passerby is a third-party beneficiary of the sales contract
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The retailer was in the commercial distribution chain, the defect existed before sale, and the defect caused personal injury. Privity is not required for the passerby's strict products claim.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Strict products liability is a tort theory and does not require privity. A foreseeable bystander injured by a defective product may sue a commercial supplier. C: Strict products liability protects more than direct purchasers. Foreseeable users and bystanders may recover for tort-type injury caused by a defect. D: The passerby need not rely on third-party-beneficiary contract status. The proper theory is strict products liability against a commercial seller.
lb_rule_reference: Strict products liability does not require privity; a foreseeable bystander injured by a defective product may sue a commercial supplier.
