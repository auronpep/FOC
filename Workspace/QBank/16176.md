


BARMATRIX Q#: 16176
Outline_code: 75080400
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Double Jeopardy
Question: Four hours into a defendant's assault trial, the lawyers gathered in the judge's chambers to discuss an evidentiary issue. While there, the judge received a phone call from his wife telling him that her mother had suddenly died. Without asking the lawyers what they wanted to do, the judge brought the lawyers back into the courtroom, declared a mistrial, excused the jury, and rushed home to his wife. A new jury was impaneled the next day before a second judge. The defendant has objected to the second trial on double jeopardy grounds. Would the second trial violate the prohibition against double jeopardy?
Answer A: A: No, because the first judge acted in good faith in declaring a mistrial.
Answer B: B: No, because the first trial did not produce a verdict.
Answer C: C: Yes, because the second judge's evidentiary rulings might be inconsistent with those of the first judge.
Answer D: D: Yes, because there was no manifest necessity for a mistrial.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: (D)is the best response, because the trial judge failed to consider an alternative to a mistrial (like a continuance), and thus failed to establish that there was manifest necessity for the mistrial. For centuries, the Supreme Court has held that once a jury trial has begun, the trial judge should declare a mistrial only if there is a “manifest necessity” for this drastic step (and if there is, then a retrial is ordinarily permitted despite the Double Jeopardy Clause). See U.S. v. Perez, 22 U.S. 579 (1824) (trial court must take “all the circumstances into consideration” in determining “whether there is a manifest necessity for [the mistrial], or the ends of public justice would otherwise be defeated”). In a modern-era case, U.S. v. Jorn, 400 U.S. 470 (1971), the Court indicated that the manifest-necessity standard will not be found to be satisfied unless the trial judge has considered whether the circumstances are such that the drastic step of a mistrial can be avoided by use of some alternative, including, especially, a continuance (postponement) of the trial. Furthermore, Jorn suggests that a judge who is considering a mistrial should give lawyers for both sides the chance to object to the mistrial and/or to suggest an alternative like a continuance. Here, the first judge did neither of these things: (1) there is no sign that he even considered whether a brief continuance until the next day (to allow him to visit with his wife and console her, then resume the trial himself the next day) would be a sensible alternative to a mistrial; and (2) he declared the mistrial “[w]ithout asking the lawyers what they wanted to do[.]” Given the defendant's strong double jeopardy-derived interest in not being forced unnecessarily to go through a second jury selection and the early part of a trial, the absence of facts demonstrating that the mistrial was “manifestly necessary” would lead the appellate court to hold that the defendant's double jeopardy rights would be violated by subjecting him to a retrial on the same charges. (A)is not the best response, because, given the manifest-necessity standard, the first judge's “good faith” is not sufficient to overcome the double jeopardy problem. As we discuss in Choice (D), the second trial will violate the prohibition against double jeopardy unless there was a manifest necessity for a mistrial, which there was not. Given that fact, even though the judge may have acted “in good faith” in declaring a mistrial (something we don't even really know to be true based on the facts we're told), the retrial will still violate the prohibition against double jeopardy. (B)is not the best response, because a retrial can (and in this case does) violate the Double Jeopardy Clause even though the first trial did not produce a verdict. This choice assumes that the Double Jeopardy Clause cannot be violated unless the first trial produced a verdict. But this assumption is flatly incorrect as a matter of law: once jeopardy “attaches” (which in a jury trial happens once the entire jury has been selected and sworn in), then a mistrial will typically violate the prohibition on double jeopardy even though no verdict has been reached. This choice not only starts from an incorrect legal principle, but it also produces the wrong outcome: the retrial would violate the defendant's double jeopardy rights. (C)is not the best response, because the retrial would violate the defendant's double jeopardy rights whether or not the second judge's evidentiary rulings would or might be inconsistent with those of the first judge. This choice is nonsensical. It seems to be based on the idea that in deciding whether a retrial would violate the Double Jeopardy Clause, the possibility — triggered by the change of judge — that the retrial might produce different evidentiary rulings from those that actually occurred, or might have occurred, in the first trial somehow makes a difference. But the possible change in evidentiary rulings between the first and second trial is completely irrelevant to whether the second trial violates the defendant's double jeopardy rights. Once the jury has been sworn, then a mistrial for which there was no manifest necessity will violate the defendant's rights even if the same judge conducts the retrial. (The principle behind the Double Jeopardy Clause is that the defendant ought not be required, unless for good reason, to suffer through the risks and stress of a second jury selection process, calling of witnesses, etc., whether or not there is a change of judge between the first and second trial.) Therefore, although this choice reaches the correct result — that a retrial would violate double jeopardy — the choice does so for a completely nonsensical reason.
