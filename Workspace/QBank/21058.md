Question: During a bank robbery, Kofi grabbed a customer named Sylvia by the arm and forced her at gunpoint from her position near the front door to a back office approximately fifteen feet away. Kofi used Sylvia as a shield while he demanded that the manager open the safe. After retrieving the money, Kofi fled alone. Kofi is charged with kidnapping in addition to robbery. Under common law, did Kofi's movement of Sylvia satisfy the asportation element of kidnapping?
Answer A: No, because fifteen feet is too short a distance to constitute meaningful asportation for kidnapping
Answer B: No, because the movement was incidental to the robbery and lacked an independent criminal purpose
Answer C: Yes, because common law requires only some movement of the victim and does not impose a minimum distance requirement
Answer D: Yes, but only because Kofi used the movement to gain an advantage over law enforcement
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Common-law kidnapping consists of (1) the unlawful (2) confinement of another (3) that involves asportation — movement of the victim from one place to another. At common law, the asportation requirement is satisfied by any movement, however minimal. Forcing Sylvia fifteen feet from the front of the bank to a back office constitutes movement and therefore satisfies asportation. The distance is irrelevant; the fact of movement is all that is required.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: At common law, any movement of the victim — however short — satisfies the asportation element. There is no minimum distance requirement. Some modern statutes have added a "substantial distance" or "significant movement" requirement, but the traditional common-law rule requires only some movement. Fifteen feet more than satisfies common-law asportation. B: The incidental-movement limitation is a modern statutory construction adopted by some jurisdictions — particularly California — to prevent kidnapping from being charged in every robbery involving movement of a victim. It is not a traditional common-law rule. At common law, movement used as a means to commit another crime still satisfies asportation. The question tests common law. D: Asportation at common law is satisfied by the fact of movement, not the purpose behind it. While using someone as a shield might support an aggravated kidnapping charge in some jurisdictions (e.g., for purposes of avoiding detection), it is not what makes the asportation element satisfied. Basic kidnapping asportation requires movement, period — no specific purpose behind that movement is needed.
lb_rule_reference: Common-law kidnapping requires asportation — some movement of the victim; even slight or minimal movement satisfies the asportation element; there is no minimum distance requirement at common law
