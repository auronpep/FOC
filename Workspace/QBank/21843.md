MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Police received a tip and conducted a warrantless entry into an apartment rented by the host. Inside, they found two visitors bagging cocaine on the kitchen table. The visitors had arrived two hours earlier purely to conduct the drug-bagging operation; they had never stayed overnight, had no belongings at the apartment, and had no social relationship with the host. The visitors move to suppress, arguing the warrantless entry violated their Fourth Amendment rights. Do the visitors have standing to challenge the warrantless entry?
Answer A: Yes, because any person present in a home when it is unlawfully searched has standing to challenge that search
Answer B: Yes, because the visitors were on private residential property, which always affords Fourth Amendment protection to those inside
Answer C: No, because the visitors were present solely for a brief commercial purpose, had no property interest or social connection to the apartment, and thus lacked a reasonable expectation of privacy sufficient for standing
Answer D: No, because the visitors were engaged in illegal activity, and persons engaged in illegal activity in another's home have no Fourth Amendment rights
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Minnesota v. Carter held that persons present in a third party's home solely to conduct a commercial transaction have a diminished expectation of privacy that is not one society recognizes as reasonable. The relevant factors are: purely commercial (not social) purpose, very short duration, no overnight stay, no connection to the property. All four factors apply here. The visitors lack standing to challenge the warrantless entry, and their suppression motion fails on standing grounds — the court need not reach the merits of the search's constitutionality.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Minnesota v. Carter squarely rejected this principle. Mere presence in a home does not establish a Fourth Amendment interest. The Court held that commercial visitors who use a third party's residence for a business transaction, have no connection to the property, and are present briefly have at most a minimal expectation of privacy that society is not prepared to recognize as reasonable. B: The strength of Fourth Amendment protection in a home is personal to those with a genuine connection to the home. It does not extend automatically to all persons physically present. Carter established that temporary commercial visitors receive reduced — and potentially no — Fourth Amendment protection in a third party's home. D: Fourth Amendment standing does not depend on the lawfulness of the activity. The correct basis for denying standing here is the nature of the visit under Carter — commercial, brief, and without property interest — not the illegality of the conduct. An overnight guest engaged in illegal activity would still have standing; a commercial visitor engaged in lawful activity would still lack it.
lb_rule_reference: An overnight guest has a sufficient interest to assert Fourth Amendment rights in the host's home; a person present only for a brief commercial transaction has a significantly diminished expectation and may lack standing (Minnesota v. Olson; Minnesota v. Carter)
