Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Police received a domestic violence call. When they arrived, a woman named Carol met them outside. Carol told officers that she shared the apartment with her boyfriend, the suspect. She produced a key and opened the door. Officers entered and found methamphetamine in plain view. At a suppression hearing, it emerged that Carol had moved out of the apartment three months earlier, had no possessory interest in the unit, and the key was an old copy the suspect had not reclaimed. Officers had no reason to doubt her claim of co-residency at the time they entered. Was the officers' warrantless entry based on Carol's consent constitutional?
Answer A: No, because Carol lacked actual authority over the apartment, making any consent she gave legally ineffective
Answer B: No, because the suspect did not consent and was not present to object
Answer C: Yes, because officers reasonably believed Carol had authority over the apartment based on the information available at the time of entry
Answer D: Yes, because domestic violence exigency authorized the entry regardless of consent
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under Illinois v. Rodriguez, apparent authority consent validates a warrantless entry when officers reasonably believe the consenting party has authority. At the time of entry, Carol had a key, claimed co-residency, and nothing in the officers' observations contradicted her claim. Their belief was objectively reasonable. The fact that Carol had actually moved out does not retroactively invalidate the consent because the constitutional standard is objective reasonableness at the time of action, not hindsight accuracy. The methamphetamine found in plain view is admissible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Illinois v. Rodriguez established the apparent authority doctrine: consent is valid if the officers reasonably believed, based on the facts available to them, that the consenting party had authority over the premises. Officers need not be certain; they need only act reasonably on the information presented. Here, Carol presented a key, claimed co-residency, and nothing indicated otherwise. B: Randolph's rule requires the objecting co-occupant to be physically present and to expressly object. An absent resident cannot veto consent given by a co-occupant or apparent co-occupant. The suspect was not present to object, so Randolph does not apply. D: The exigency exception requires specific, articulable facts suggesting an emergency requiring immediate action. The facts here describe Carol calmly meeting officers outside and providing consent — not an ongoing emergency inside. The correct basis for the valid entry is apparent-authority consent, not exigency.
lb_rule_reference: Third-party consent is valid if the consenting party has actual authority over the area searched, or if officers reasonably believe — based on the facts presented — that the third party has authority (apparent authority) (Illinois v. Rodriguez; United States v. Matlock)
