Question: Sarah signed and delivered to Rebekah a document headed “General Warranty Deed.” The body stated, “Sarah grants Blueacre to Rebekah and warrants title only against claims arising by, through, or under Sarah.” Rebekah accepted. A title dispute later turns on the scope of Sarah's warranty. Which statement is best?
Answer A: The heading alone makes Sarah liable as a general warrantor against all title defects, regardless of the body.
Answer B: The body's limited warranty language controls the deed-type lock; Sarah gave a special warranty rather than a general warranty on these facts.
Answer C: The inconsistency between heading and body makes the entire deed void.
Answer D: Sarah gave no warranty at all because only a quitclaim deed may limit warranty scope.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The title source is a deed, and the decisive deed-type lock is the operative language, not the form heading alone. The body limits the warranty to claims arising by, through, or under Sarah. That is special-warranty language, so the heading does not convert the instrument into an unlimited general warranty.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because a heading does not override the express operative limitation. C: C is wrong because inconsistent labels do not automatically void an otherwise operative deed. D: D is wrong because a deed may convey title with limited warranty covenants.
lb_rule_reference: Operative deed language controls warranty scope despite an inconsistent heading
