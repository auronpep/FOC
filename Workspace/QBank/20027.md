Question: A hospital contracted with a cleaning company to clean all operating rooms each night. The contract stated that cleaning would be done “to promote sanitary conditions for patients and staff.” Mary later developed an infection after a room was not cleaned properly. Mary sued the cleaning company for breach of the hospital-cleaning contract as a third-party beneficiary. Is Mary likely to recover on the contract theory?
Answer A: Yes, because patients were expected to benefit from clean rooms.
Answer B: Yes, because Mary was physically harmed by poor performance.
Answer C: No, because a patient can never sue a cleaning company under any theory.
Answer D: No, because the contract's general patient-safety purpose does not necessarily show intent to give each patient an enforceable contract right.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: Mary is likely an incidental beneficiary on the contract. A general purpose to promote patient safety usually does not show that the hospital and cleaning company intended to give every patient a direct right to enforce the cleaning contract.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because expected benefit is not enough. B: B is wrong because injury may support another theory but does not establish contract standing. C: C is wrong because the question asks only contract enforcement; other theories are not decided.
