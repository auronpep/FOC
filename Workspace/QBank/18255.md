Question: Naomi's winery contracted to sell Isaac “all output of Vineyard Lot 7” for the harvest season, with an estimate of 4,000 cases of wine. When market prices doubled, Naomi transferred the Lot 7 grapes to an affiliated winery that sold the wine at a higher price, and Naomi tendered zero cases to Isaac. Naomi argues that an output contract permits her actual output to be zero. Can Isaac recover?
Answer A: No, because an output seller may always choose to produce zero units.
Answer B: No, because the 4,000-case estimate is not an exact quantity term.
Answer C: Yes, because an output term measures actual good-faith output and does not allow the seller to divert covered production to avoid the contract.
Answer D: Yes, but only if Isaac proves a prior oral promise that Lot 7 would produce at least 4,000 cases.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: An output contract measures quantity by the seller's actual good-faith output within the agreement's scope, subject to estimates and comparable output. The trigger facts are the specified source “Vineyard Lot 7,” the market-price increase, the transfer to an affiliate, and the zero tender after covered grapes were diverted. The decisive lock is good faith: zero output can be permissible after a genuine shutdown, but not when the seller diverts covered production to avoid the bargain.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because it overstates output flexibility and ignores good faith. B: B is wrong because the estimate is not a fixed quantity, but the output term still binds covered good-faith output. D: D is wrong because Isaac's strongest route is the written output term plus bad-faith diversion, not a prior oral promise.
lb_rule_reference: UCC 2-306(1) output quantity; good faith and contract-scope diversion
