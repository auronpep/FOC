


BARMATRIX Q#: 16186
Outline_code: 75070202
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Exceptions to warrant requirement
Question: Police officers had probable cause to believe that drug dealing was routinely taking place in a particular room at a local motel. The motel manager authorized the officers to enter the room and provided them with a passkey. Without obtaining a warrant, the officers knocked on the room's door, announced their presence, and told the occupants that they would like to speak with them. The officers then heard yelling and repeated flushing of the toilet. They then used the passkey and entered the room, where they saw the occupants dumping drugs into the toilet. The occupants of the room were charged with drug dealing and have moved to suppress the drugs. Should the court grant the motion to suppress?
Answer A: A: No, because exigent circumstances justified the officers' entry.
Answer B: B: No, because the motel manager consented to the officers' entry.
Answer C: C: Yes, because exigent circumstances cannot excuse the lack of a warrant.
Answer D: D: Yes, because the officers cannot benefit from exigent circumstances that they created.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: (A)is the best response, because the yelling and repeated flushing gave the officers reason to believe that evidence was being destroyed, thereby creating exigent circumstances. To begin the analysis, the occupants of the motel room had a reasonable expectation of privacy while they were in the room, and the motel manager had no authority to consent to the police's warrantless entry into the room. (For more about this aspect, see the discussion of Choice (C) below.) So the legality of the officers' entry boils down to this: just prior to the officers' entry via the passkey, did the officers face exigent circumstances justifying their warrantless entry? The brief answer to this issue is “yes.” The facts here are quite similar to those of Kentucky v. King, 563 U.S. 452 (2011), except that in King, the police did not even have probable cause (merely reasonable suspicion) to believe that contraband would be found when, without a warrant in hand, they arrived at D's dwelling. Similarly to the facts here, in King the officers knocked, announced themselves, got no response, heard sounds that “things were being moved inside,” and broke down the door. The Supreme Court assumed (without deciding) that these facts gave the officers reason to believe that contraband inside would be destroyed if they took the time to get a warrant; the Court then made the following statement about whether and why the exigent circumstances exception to the requirement of a search warrant would apply: “When law enforcement officers who are not armed with a warrant knock on a door, they do no more than any private citizen might do. And whether the person who knocks on the door and requests the opportunity to speak is a police officer or a private citizen, the occupant has no obligation to … open the door or to speak. … Occupants who choose not to stand on their constitutional rights but instead elect to attempt to destroy evidence have only themselves to blame for the warrantless exigent-circumstances search that may ensue.” The Court in King then concluded that the police's warrantless entry was justified by the exigent-circumstances exception to the usual requirement of probable cause and a search warrant. There is one more fact present in our question that might make a difference: the occupants could plausibly argue that because the officers had probable cause (and time) to procure a warrant, they should not be able to (as Choice (D) puts it) “benefit from exigent circumstances that they created.” But the Supreme Court has rejected exactly such an argument, as we detail in our discussion of Choice (D). (B)is not the best response, because the motel manager did not have authority to consent to the officers' entry. The Supreme Court has squarely rejected the theory asserted by this choice. In Stoner v. California, 376 U.S. 483 (1964), police acting without a warrant persuaded a hotel manager to let them into the hotel room in which D was living (though D was not present at that moment). The Court rejected the prosecution's argument that the manager's approval was sufficient, saying “No less than a tenant of a house, or the occupant of a room in a boarding house, a guest in a hotel room is entitled to constitutional protection against unreasonable searches and seizures. … That protection would disappear if it were left to depend upon the unfettered discretion of an employee of the hotel.” Therefore, although this choice correctly states the outcome, it does so based on reasoning expressly rejected by the Supreme Court in Stoner. (C)is not the best response, because exigent circumstances can excuse the lack of a warrant, and did so here. The existence of exigent circumstances — particularly situations in which the police reasonably fear that contraband or evidence of crime will be destroyed unless they can make immediate entry into premises — can, and often does, excuse the lack of a warrant. As we describe in greater detail in Choice (A), that's the case here, since the sounds of “yelling and repeated flushing of the toilet” justified the officers' belief that if they did not enter immediately, the drugs that they had probable cause to believe were there would be destroyed. (D)is not the best response, because the Supreme Court has rejected, for most purposes, the argument that the police “cannot benefit from exigent circumstances that they created.” The Supreme Court has squarely rejected a “police-created exigency exception to the exigent-circumstances exception,” as this choice contemplates. The argument in favor of such an exception-to-the-exception is based on the idea that where the police have time (and probable cause) to get a search warrant, allowing them to decline to get the warrant and then rely on exigent circumstances would allow them “routinely to dishonor the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement in drug cases” (as the dissent argued in Kentucky v. King, discussed immediately infra). But the Court in Kentucky v. King, 563 U.S. 452 (2011) (also discussed in Choice (A) above) rejected such a police-created exigency exception, except in the rare case in which the police “gain entry to premises by means of an actual or threatened violation of the Fourth Amendment.” (Here's how the exception for “actual or threatened Fourth Amendment violation” might work: Suppose the police here had falsely yelled, “Open up, because we have a search warrant.” The police would not be able to use the exigent circumstances exception, according to King: the police's failure to possess the warrant they claimed to have would make their claim a threat to violate the Fourth Amendment.) But where, as here, the police merely announce themselves and ask the occupants to answer the door (so there's no actual or threatened Fourth Amendment violation), King allows the police to then use the exigent-circumstances exception to, say, break down the door if the occupants seem to be responding to the knock-and-announce by starting to destroy evidence. And that's true even though the police arguably “created the exigent circumstances” by failing to procure a search warrant that they had time and probable cause to procure.
