Question: A man entered the police station and announced that he wanted to confess to a murder. The police advised the man of his Miranda rights, and the man signed a written waiver. The man described the murder in detail and pinpointed the location where a murder victim had been found a few weeks before. Later, a court-appointed psychiatrist determined that the man was suffering from a serious mental illness that interfered with his ability to make rational choices and to understand his rights and that the psychosis had induced his confession. The man's confession is
Answer A: A: admissible, because there was no coercive police conduct in obtaining the man's statement.
Answer B: B: admissible, because the man was not in custody.
Answer C: C: inadmissible, because the man's confession was a product of his mental illness and was therefore involuntary.
Answer D: D: inadmissible, because under these circumstances, there was no valid waiver of Miranda warnings.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A is correct. The statement was not the product of any police coercion, and under the law, a statement merely being the result of a mental disease does not render it involuntary under the Fourteenth Amendment.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B is incorrect. This ignores the non-coercive effect of the police conduct, and the man may well have been in custody at the time of the interrogation. C is incorrect. The scope of the exclusionary rule does not include the right of the defendant to confess to a crime only when totally rational and properly motivated. There must be some type of coercive police activity to find that the man's confession was not voluntary and inadmissible. D is incorrect. Miranda was not violated because there was no police coercion, no inducement of the confession, and the man's mental illness is not grounds for excluding his confession under the Fourteenth Amendment.
