Outline: Responsibility
Question: Marcus has a severe impulse-control disorder recognized by psychiatrists as a legitimate mental disease. He has been examined and found to understand fully that stealing is illegal and wrong. Despite this knowledge, he experiences overwhelming uncontrollable urges to steal whenever he enters a store. He steals repeatedly and is charged with multiple counts of theft. The jurisdiction uses both M'Naghten and the irresistible-impulse supplement. Marcus's expert testifies that his urges are genuinely uncontrollable due to his mental disease, and that he cannot conform his conduct to the law. Which of the following best states Marcus's insanity claim?
Answer A: Marcus has no insanity defense because he knew stealing was wrong
Answer B: Marcus has an insanity defense under M'Naghten because the impulse overrides his cognition
Answer C: Marcus may have an insanity defense under the irresistible-impulse supplement because he could not conform his conduct to the law despite knowing it was wrong
Answer D: Marcus has an insanity defense because he has a recognized mental disease
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The irresistible-impulse supplement to M'Naghten provides that a defendant is not criminally responsible if, due to mental disease, he was unable to control his conduct even though he knew it was wrong. The supplement adds a volitional prong that M'Naghten lacks. Marcus clearly knew stealing was wrong — M'Naghten's cognitive test is not satisfied. But his expert's testimony establishes that he genuinely could not control his conduct due to a recognized mental disease. In a jurisdiction using the irresistible-impulse supplement, Marcus has a viable insanity claim under the volitional prong.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The question specifies a jurisdiction that uses M'Naghten plus the irresistible-impulse supplement. That supplement specifically addresses the volitional prong: even if the defendant knew the act was wrong, he may be excused if he could not control his conduct due to mental disease. Marcus's awareness of wrongfulness defeats M'Naghten but does not end the analysis in this jurisdiction. B: The facts state Marcus knew stealing was wrong. M'Naghten's "wrongfulness" prong is a cognitive test about knowledge, not a test about the strength of competing desires. Overwhelming desire does not negate cognitive knowledge of wrongfulness. M'Naghten is not satisfied; only the irresistible-impulse supplement is. D: Mental disease is a threshold prerequisite for the insanity defense, not a sufficient condition by itself. Both M'Naghten and the irresistible-impulse test require the mental disease to have caused a specific legal incapacity (failure to know nature/wrongfulness, or failure to control conduct). A diagnosis alone is insufficient. Many defendants with recognized mental diseases do not meet the legal insanity standard.
lb_rule_reference: The irresistible-impulse test supplements M'Naghten by excusing a defendant who, due to mental disease, knew the act was wrong but was unable to control his conduct; M'Naghten alone does not recognize the volitional prong
