Question: A state bars licensed pharmacies from dispensing a medication to customers unless the customer appears in person. An online pharmacy loses sales and sues, arguing that the law violates its customers' constitutional right to access the medication. Customers are unlikely to sue because the medical condition is private and short-term. The state argues the pharmacy cannot assert customer rights. What is the pharmacy's strongest response?
Answer A: The pharmacy can never assert customer rights because constitutional rights are always personal
Answer B: The pharmacy may assert the customers' rights because it has its own economic injury, a close provider-customer relationship, and a practical hindrance to customer suits
Answer C: The pharmacy may assert customer rights solely because it disagrees with the law
Answer D: The pharmacy may assert customer rights only if every customer signs a written assignment
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The pharmacy alleges lost sales, has a direct relationship with customers seeking the medication, and identifies privacy and timing barriers that hinder customer litigation.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Third-party standing can exist where the litigant has its own injury, a close relationship, and a hindrance to the right-holder's suit. C: Third-party standing needs the pharmacy's own injury plus relationship and hindrance, not mere disagreement. D: Third-party standing doctrine does not require a written assignment when close relationship and hindrance are shown.
lb_rule_reference: A litigant may sometimes assert third-party rights if the litigant has its own injury, a close relationship with the right-holder, and a hindrance to the right-holder's ability to sue.
