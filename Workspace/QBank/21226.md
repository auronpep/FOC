Question: A supplier threatens to stop sending bricks to a subcontractor who is building a warehouse for Solomon. If the bricks stop, Solomon's project will fail and Solomon will lose a profitable lease. Solomon orally tells the supplier, “Keep sending bricks for my warehouse project, and if the subcontractor does not pay, I will.” The supplier continues deliveries, the subcontractor defaults, and the supplier sues Solomon. Is Solomon's oral promise barred by the suretyship Statute of Frauds?
Answer A: Yes, because every promise to pay another's debt requires a writing.
Answer B: Yes, because the supplier continued performance for the subcontractor rather than Solomon.
Answer C: No, but only because the bricks are goods under the UCC.
Answer D: No, if Solomon's main purpose was to secure his own economic advantage in keeping the warehouse project alive.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: The main-purpose exception removes a collateral promise from the suretyship writing requirement when the promisor's leading object is to serve the promisor's own economic interest. Solomon's purpose was to protect his own warehouse project and lease.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because it ignores the main-purpose exception. B: B is wrong because the focus is Solomon's leading object, not the formal identity of the original debtor. C: C is wrong because this question turns on the suretyship exception, not the UCC goods SOF.
