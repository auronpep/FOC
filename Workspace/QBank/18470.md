Question: A state enacts a statute restricting the sale of homemade baked goods to face-to-face transactions at farmers' markets or residential homes; online sales and mail-order delivery of homemade baked goods are prohibited. A home baker challenges the statute on Equal Protection grounds, arguing that the legislature's only stated purpose in the committee hearing transcripts was "supporting local community events," and that the mail-order ban does nothing to promote community events. The state defends the statute on appeal, for the first time offering the rationale that the prohibition ensures consumers can inspect home kitchens before purchase, reducing foodborne illness risk. Under Equal Protection rational basis review, how should the court evaluate the home-baker statute?
Answer A: The statute is unconstitutional because the state's newly offered rationale was not articulated during the legislative process
Answer B: The statute is unconstitutional because there is no rational basis for treating internet home-bakers differently from brick-and-mortar bakeries, which may sell online
Answer C: The statute survives because a court may hypothesize that ensuring consumers can inspect uninspected home kitchens before purchase is a conceivable legitimate interest rationally served by restricting remote sales
Answer D: The statute must be evaluated under intermediate scrutiny because it disadvantages home-based small businesses, which are a politically powerless group
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under rational basis review, the court need not find the stated legislative purpose; it may hypothesize any conceivable legitimate purpose. Protecting consumers from foodborne illness by limiting home-baked-good sales to face-to-face transactions — where the buyer can assess the kitchen — is a plausible health-and-safety rationale. Consumer health protection is a paradigmatic legitimate state interest. The connection between limiting mail-order sales and ensuring kitchen inspection is at least rational.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Under rational basis, a court is not limited to the legislature's stated purpose. A court will uphold a statute if any conceivable legitimate purpose exists, regardless of whether the legislature ever articulated it. The state is even allowed to offer new rationales on appeal, and the court may supply its own hypothetical rationale. B: Rational basis review is extremely deferential. A legislature may draw imperfect lines as long as the line rationally advances a conceivable legitimate interest. Distinguishing home kitchens — which are not routinely inspected — from commercial kitchens subject to health-code regulation is a rational basis for the different treatment. The line need not be perfectly drawn. D: Intermediate scrutiny under Equal Protection applies to quasi-suspect classifications: sex, legitimacy, and certain alienage categories. Economic groupings — even small businesses or home workers — are not quasi-suspect classifications. The appropriate standard for economic regulation that does not involve a suspect classification or fundamental right is rational basis.
lb_rule_reference: Under rational basis, courts will uphold a law based on any conceivable legitimate purpose — not merely the purpose actually stated by the legislature — and the challenger bears the burden to negate every conceivable basis.
