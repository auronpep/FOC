MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Other Crimes
Outline: Robbery
Question: Officers arrested Owen and gave him Miranda warnings. Owen said, "I want a lawyer before I answer any questions." The officers stopped the interview. Two hours later, different officers came to Owen's cell to question him about an unrelated armed robbery that had just been solved by a co-conspirator's tip. Without counsel present, the officers told Owen they wanted to discuss a "completely different matter" and began asking about the robbery. Owen made incriminating admissions. He moves to suppress all statements made after his request for counsel. Should Owen's statements about the robbery be suppressed?
Answer A: Yes, because once a suspect invokes the right to counsel, all questioning must cease until counsel is present, regardless of the offense being investigated
Answer B: No, because the right to counsel invocation applies only to the offense for which the suspect was arrested, not to a different crime
Answer C: No, because two hours had elapsed and different officers came to question him, which constituted a break sufficient to allow re-initiation
Answer D: No, because the officers informed Owen they were asking about a different matter, which satisfied the requirement to give fresh warnings before re-initiation
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Edwards v. Arizona* (1981) and *McNeil v. Wisconsin* (1991), once a suspect unambiguously invokes the Sixth Amendment right to counsel or the Miranda Fifth Amendment right to counsel, all questioning must cease until counsel is present. This prohibition applies to questioning about any offense, not just the original offense. The right-to-counsel invocation is broader and more durable than the right-to-silence invocation: police cannot re-initiate on any topic. Owen's invocation barred the robbery questioning, and his statements must be suppressed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: The *Edwards v. Arizona* prohibition is not offense-specific. Once a suspect invokes the right to counsel in the Miranda context, that invocation protects against all police-initiated questioning about any offense until counsel is present. This contrasts with the Sixth Amendment right to counsel, which is offense-specific. The Miranda right-to-counsel rule is broader. C: The right-to-silence rule (from *Michigan v. Mosley*) allows re-initiation after a significant time with fresh warnings and a different crime. The right-to-counsel rule under *Edwards* does not have a time-lapse exception. Once counsel is invoked, police may not re-initiate regardless of how much time passes or which officers are involved, until counsel is present or the suspect spontaneously re-initiates. The two-hour gap and different officers are legally irrelevant here. D: There is no "different matter" exception to *Edwards v. Arizona*. Informing the suspect that the topic has changed does not satisfy the right-to-counsel rule. The only way to validly resume questioning after a counsel invocation is if the suspect spontaneously and voluntarily re-initiates communication with police, or if counsel is present. Neither occurred here.
lb_rule_reference: Once a suspect unambiguously invokes the right to counsel, all questioning must cease until counsel is present; unlike the right to silence, police may not re-initiate questioning about any offense until counsel is provided.
