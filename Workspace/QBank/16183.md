


BARMATRIX Q#: 16183
Outline_code: 75120000
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Other Considerations
Question: A defendant was charged with and tried for murder. At trial, the defendant testified that he had acted in self-defense. The trial court instructed the jury that the defendant had the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that he had acted in self-defense. The defendant objected, arguing that this instruction violated the constitutional requirement that the prosecution prove the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. Did the trial court's instruction violate the defendant's federal constitutional rights?
Answer A: A: No, because due process does not preclude requiring a defendant to prove self-defense by a preponderance of the evidence.
Answer B: B: No, because due process does not regulate the burdens of proof in criminal cases.
Answer C: C: Yes, because due process precludes placing any evidentiary burden on a criminal defendant.
Answer D: D: Yes, because due process precludes requiring a criminal defendant to bear the burden on an issue that would make an act lawful.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: (A)is the best response, because due process requires that the burden of persuasion beyond a reasonable doubt be placed on the prosecution only as to each element of the crime, not as to “affirmative defenses.” The Due Process Clause (of both the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments) protects a criminal defendant against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to establish each element of the crime. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970). But the clause does not apply in the same way to “affirmative defenses.” That is, suppose that the state or federal statute defining the crime does not include absence of a particular fact as an element of the crime, but the government chooses to allow the presence of that fact to be a defense (a so-called “affirmative” defense). In this situation, due process is not violated by the government's placing on the defendant (1) the burden of coming forward with evidence of the fact(s) establishing the defense, or (2) the burden of persuading the jury of the fact(s) by a preponderance of the evidence. See Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197 (1977), articulating this as a general principle. Self-defense is just such an affirmative defense. Thus in Martin v. Ohio, 480 U.S. 228 (1987), an Ohio statute did what the statute here does: it recognized self-defense as a complete defense to murder, but imposed on the defendant the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that when she killed, she did so in self-defense. The Court concluded that this arrangement did not violate the defendant's due process rights, since the state was still required to prove each element of murder (as the state chose to define the crime) beyond a reasonable doubt. (B)is not the best response, because due process does regulate the burdens of proof in criminal cases, but only with respect to elements of the crime, not elements of affirmative defenses. As we discuss in the treatment of Choice (A), the Due Process Clause does require that the government bear the burden of proving, beyond a reasonable doubt, each element of the crime. For instance, in the murder prosecution here, it would violate the defendant's right to due process if the state were to allow a conviction based on the jury's finding, by a mere preponderance of the evidence, that the defendant brought about the death of the victim (since murder is defined so that the defendant's causing the death of another is an element of the crime). Therefore, this choice — by saying that due process does not regulate the burdens of proof in criminal cases — is legally incorrect. (C)is not the best response, because due process does not preclude placing an evidentiary burden on a criminal defendant, where the burden relates to an affirmative defense. As we discuss in the treatment of Choice (A), due process does preclude the government from placing any evidentiary burden on a criminal defendant with respect to an element of the crime. But the same is not true of affirmative defenses: the government does not violate due process by imposing on the defendant the burden of coming forward with evidence of, and establishing by a preponderance of the evidence, facts needed to constitute an affirmative defense. Therefore, this choice overstates the limits of how and when the government may put an evidentiary burden on a criminal defendant (and it also reaches the incorrect outcome). (D)is not the best response, because it overstates the limits that due process places on the government's right to impose an evidentiary burden on issues. As we discuss in Choice (A), due process does not prevent the government from requiring a criminal defendant to bear the burden of persuasion (as well as the burden of production) on an issue that is not defined to be part of the crime, but that is instead defined as an affirmative defense. So, here, the state has chosen to define “acting in self-defense” as (in the words of Choice (D)) “an issue that would make [the] act [of killing] lawful,” i.e., to define self-defense as an affirmative defense. Therefore, even though under the state's statutory setup the presence or absence of self-defense is an “issue that would make an act lawful,” this choice incorrectly asserts that the state's setup violates due process.
