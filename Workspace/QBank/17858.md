Question: A city ordinance prohibits the use of amplified sound equipment in any city park between 8 p.m. and 8 a.m. The ordinance is enforced uniformly against all groups regardless of the content of their message. A civic advocacy group wishes to hold a candlelight rally in a downtown park at 9 p.m. to protest a proposed city budget. The group argues it needs amplified sound to reach the anticipated crowd of 500 people and that the ordinance unconstitutionally restricts its speech. The city defends the ordinance as necessary to protect nearby residential neighborhoods from noise during evening hours. Which of the following most accurately states how a court should analyze the ordinance?
Answer A: The ordinance is content-based because it targets the political advocacy group's ability to communicate its message effectively during peak assembly hours
Answer B: The ordinance is constitutional because the city may ban all expression in parks during evening hours to protect residential quiet
Answer C: The ordinance is likely constitutional as a content-neutral TPM restriction if noise control near residences is a significant interest and unamplified speech, daytime amplification, and adjacent venues remain available
Answer D: The ordinance is unconstitutional because the ample-alternatives requirement means the city must provide an equally effective channel, and no alternative matches amplified sound for a crowd of 500
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Downtown parks are traditional public forums. The ordinance is content-neutral on its face (no message-based distinctions) and in purpose (noise reduction). Residential noise abatement is a significant government interest. The ordinance is limited in scope — hours-based, not venue-based — and unamplified speech, daytime events, and alternative locations remain available. If those alternatives are genuinely accessible, the ample-alternatives prong is satisfied. The ordinance passes the three-part test under Ward v. Rock Against Racism.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Content-neutrality is determined by the face of the law and the government's stated justification, not by whether a specific speaker is burdened. The ordinance applies to all amplified sound regardless of message. The government's stated purpose is noise reduction in residential areas — a content-neutral concern. An incidental burden on a particular group does not transform a content-neutral law into a content-based one. B: Even a valid government interest does not allow a blanket ban on all expressive activity in a traditional public forum. Parks are paradigm traditional public forums, and the government may not entirely suppress a mode of communication — amplified sound — during specific hours without showing the restriction is narrowly tailored and that ample alternatives exist. The ordinance must survive all three prongs. D: The ample-alternatives requirement does not demand an equally effective substitute — only a genuine opportunity to communicate. The Supreme Court has held that alternatives need not be perfectly equivalent, only real and accessible. Unamplified speech or daytime events may be less effective but are still legally "ample." The inquiry focuses on whether alternatives exist, not whether they are equally potent.
lb_rule_reference: In a traditional public forum, content-neutral TPM restrictions must be (1) content-neutral, (2) narrowly tailored to a significant government interest, and (3) leave open ample alternative channels of communication.
