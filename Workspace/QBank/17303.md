MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Negligence
Outline: Proximate (legal) cause
Question: A driver negligently ran a stop sign and broke a pedestrian's leg. At the hospital, a nurse carelessly administered the wrong dose of a pain medication, causing the pedestrian to suffer additional kidney injury. The medication error was negligent, but it was the kind of treatment error that can occur during medical care after a traumatic injury. In the pedestrian's negligence claim against the driver, is the driver potentially liable for the kidney injury?
Answer A: Yes, because negligent medical treatment after an accident is generally foreseeable
Answer B: Yes, but only because the driver intended the pedestrian to receive medical treatment
Answer C: No, because any later negligence by a medical provider is a superseding cause
Answer D: No, because the driver caused only a leg injury and never touched the pedestrian's kidneys
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The driver's negligence required medical treatment, and ordinary negligent treatment is a foreseeable intervening act. It usually does not supersede the original tortfeasor's liability for enhanced harm.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Negligence proximate cause does not require intent. The relevant question is whether the medical intervention and resulting harm were within the foreseeable risk chain. C: Foreseeable medical negligence is a classic intervening act that generally does not supersede the original defendant's negligence. D: Proximate cause can include foreseeable complications of medical treatment required by the original injury. Direct contact with the later organ injury is not required.
lb_rule_reference: Ordinary medical negligence in treating an injury caused by the defendant is generally a foreseeable intervening act and does not supersede the defendant's liability for enhanced harm.
