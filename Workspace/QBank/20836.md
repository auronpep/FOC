Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: A state statute authorized officers to inspect pawnshop records without a warrant. Officers followed the statute exactly, inspected a shop ledger, and found entries linking Maya to stolen jewelry. Months later, the statute was held unconstitutional. Maya moves to suppress the ledger evidence, and there is no evidence that officers acted dishonestly or outside the statute. What is the best result?
Answer A: Suppress the evidence because any unconstitutional search requires exclusion
Answer B: Admit the evidence because officers reasonably relied on the statute when they conducted the search
Answer C: Suppress the evidence unless the officers also obtained a warrant
Answer D: Admit the evidence because statutes can never violate the Fourth Amendment
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Suppression would not meaningfully deter police misconduct when officers followed then-valid law in objective good faith. The later invalidation does not automatically require exclusion.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The exclusionary rule is not automatic. Objectively reasonable reliance on a statute later invalidated may make suppression inappropriate. C: The good-faith exception can apply to reasonable reliance on law, not only to warrants. D: Statutes can be unconstitutional. The evidence is admitted because of objective good faith, not because the statute was immune from review.
lb_rule_reference: Suppression is not required when officers act in objectively reasonable reliance on binding authority or a statute later invalidated, absent culpable police misconduct.
