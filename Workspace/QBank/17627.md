MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Negligence
Outline: Proximate (legal) cause
Question: A repair shop negligently stored oily rags in an open bin next to a hot compressor. The rags ignited, and firefighters used large amounts of water to keep the fire from spreading to neighboring businesses. The water seeped through a shared wall and damaged a bookstore's inventory. The bookstore sues the repair shop for negligence. Is the bookstore likely to prevail in a negligence claim against the repair shop for the water damage?
Answer A: Yes, because fire-suppression damage is a foreseeable consequence of negligently creating a fire hazard
Answer B: Yes, but only because the firefighters were agents of the repair shop
Answer C: No, because the firefighters' water was an intervening act that automatically superseded the shop's negligence
Answer D: No, because the bookstore suffered only economic loss
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The repair shop's negligence created a fire risk. Ordinary firefighting efforts and resulting water damage to nearby property are foreseeable consequences of that risk and do not supersede liability.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: The firefighters are not the shop's agents. Liability turns on proximate cause: emergency response damage is within the foreseeable risk created by the fire hazard. C: Foreseeable emergency response is not automatically superseding. It is a normal consequence of the danger the defendant created. D: Damaged inventory is property damage, not pure economic loss. The relevant issue is whether the water damage was within the foreseeable risk chain.
lb_rule_reference: Harm caused by ordinary emergency response to a fire or hazard created by the defendant's negligence is generally within the foreseeable scope of risk.
