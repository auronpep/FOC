Subtopic: Separation of Powers
Outline: Pardon
Question: A former federal contractor is under investigation for a federal false-statements offense allegedly committed last year. No indictment has been filed. The President signs a pardon covering "any federal false-statements offense committed by the contractor before today's date." Prosecutors argue the pardon is invalid because there has been no conviction. Is the timing objection likely correct?
Answer A: Yes, because a presidential pardon can be issued only after conviction and sentencing
Answer B: No, because the alleged federal offense was already committed before the pardon
Answer C: Yes, because a pardon before conviction violates Article III by deciding guilt
Answer D: No, because the President may also pardon any similar federal offense the contractor commits next year
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The pardon covers federal offenses committed before the date of the pardon. The lack of indictment or conviction does not by itself make the pardon invalid.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A pardon may cover a federal offense after the offense has been committed, even before indictment or conviction. C: A pardon is an executive act of clemency, not an Article III judgment of guilt. Its validity does not depend on a prior judicial conviction. D: The pardon power does not authorize prospective permission to commit future federal crimes.
lb_rule_reference: The President may pardon a federal offense after it has been committed, even before conviction, but may not issue a prospective pardon for future offenses.
