Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Federal-Question Jurisdiction
Question: A drug manufacturer files an action in federal court seeking a declaratory judgment that it does not infringe a patent held by a competitor. No federal statute explicitly creates a cause of action for a non-infringement declaration by a patent holder's competitor. The patent holder had sent the manufacturer a cease-and-desist letter threatening to sue for patent infringement under 35 U.S.C. § 271. The patent holder moves to dismiss the declaratory judgment action for lack of § 1331 federal-question jurisdiction, arguing that the manufacturer's declaratory judgment claim is not a federal cause of action. The governing forum is federal court under the MBE baseline. Does § 1331 federal-question jurisdiction exist over the declaratory judgment action?
Answer A: No, because the manufacturer's declaratory judgment claim is a state-created procedural mechanism, not a federal cause of action, and declaratory judgments cannot independently establish § 1331 jurisdiction
Answer B: Yes, because the dispute involves patent law, which is exclusively federal, and the coercive action the patent holder would bring — patent infringement — arises under federal law
Answer C: No, because the manufacturer is the plaintiff and did not plead a federal cause of action; the patent holder's threatened federal claim does not appear in the manufacturer's complaint
Answer D: Yes, because both parties are litigating about patent rights, and any dispute involving a federal patent is automatically a federal case
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The Declaratory Judgment Act does not by itself create federal subject matter jurisdiction, but it does not prevent it either. In declaratory judgment actions, courts apply the well-pleaded complaint rule by asking what the coercive action would be — the suit the defendant would have brought had the plaintiff not filed for a declaration. Here, the patent holder's threatened action is for patent infringement under 35 U.S.C. § 271, a federal cause of action arising under federal patent law. Because that coercive action would support § 1331, the declaratory judgment action also falls within federal-question jurisdiction.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The well-pleaded complaint rule in declaratory judgment cases looks at the "coercive" action: what claim would the defendant (patent holder) have brought if the plaintiff had not sought a declaration? Here, the patent holder would have sued the manufacturer for federal patent infringement under 35 U.S.C. § 271 — a federal cause of action. Because the coercive action would arise under federal law, § 1331 jurisdiction exists over the declaratory judgment action. C: The well-pleaded complaint rule is modified in declaratory judgment actions: courts look at what the coercive action would be, not just at the face of the declaratory judgment complaint. Otherwise, patent holders could avoid federal court by always waiting and forcing the accused infringer to seek a declaration, even though the underlying dispute is purely federal. D: This answer reaches the right outcome but for an overbroad reason. Not every dispute tangentially touching a federal patent is automatically within § 1331. For example, a state contract dispute over a patent licensing royalty might not be a federal case. The correct basis is the coercive-action test showing that the underlying infringement claim arises under federal patent law — not the fact of patent subject matter alone.
lb_rule_reference: In declaratory judgment actions, § 1331 jurisdiction exists if the "coercive" action — the suit the defendant could have brought had the plaintiff not sought a declaration — would arise under federal law.
