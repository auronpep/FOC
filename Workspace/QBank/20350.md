Question: Reeva abducted eight-year-old Miles from a park by luring him into her vehicle and transporting him to a remote cabin. Once at the cabin, Reeva drafted a written note demanding $50,000 from Miles's parents and threatening harm if the demand was not met within 48 hours. Before Reeva could arrange to transmit the note, law enforcement located the cabin based on witness descriptions and arrested Reeva. Miles was unharmed. The note was found in the cabin but had never been sent. Under common law principles, which charge best describes Reeva's criminal liability for the taking of Miles?
Answer A: Simple kidnapping only, because the ransom demand was never communicated to Miles's parents
Answer B: Simple kidnapping only, because Miles was not physically harmed during the confinement
Answer C: Aggravated kidnapping, because the abduction of a child combined with the intent to hold for ransom satisfies multiple aggravating factors recognized at common law
Answer D: Aggravated kidnapping, but only because Miles was a minor — the ransom note is irrelevant because it was never sent
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Aggravated kidnapping at common law (and in most statutory frameworks tracing common-law principles) is established when kidnapping is accompanied by one or more enumerated aggravating factors. Two independent aggravating factors are present here: (1) the victim is a minor child, which most jurisdictions treat as a per se aggravating circumstance; and (2) Reeva drafted a demand for $50,000, evidencing the intent to hold for ransom. The ransom trigger does not require that the demand be transmitted or received. The confinement element is satisfied by transporting Miles to the cabin.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The aggravated kidnapping trigger of "holding for ransom" does not require that the demand be received, accepted, or acted upon. The intent to hold for ransom — evidenced by the drafted note — is sufficient to establish the aggravating purpose. Aggravated kidnapping is complete upon confinement with the intent to extort ransom, not upon successful communication of the demand. B: Physical harm to the victim is not required for aggravated kidnapping. Aggravating factors are typically enumerated and include: holding for ransom, using the victim as a shield, taking the victim across state lines, or taking a child. Ransom — not injury — is the trigger here, and injury is a separate and independent aggravating factor in jurisdictions that recognize it. D: While the minor-victim factor alone may be sufficient in many jurisdictions, this answer incorrectly dismisses the ransom note as legally irrelevant. An undelivered ransom note evidencing intent to hold for ransom is legally operative for the ransom aggravation — it is not irrelevant. Choosing only one valid aggravating factor while incorrectly discarding another valid one reflects an incomplete legal analysis.
lb_rule_reference: Aggravated kidnapping requires confinement plus one of several enumerated aggravating factors; holding for ransom — a demand for money or benefit as a condition for release — is the paradigmatic trigger; the demand need not be received or paid
