Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Specific and General Jurisdiction
Question: Halverson Parts GmbH is a German company that manufactures brake-caliper housings. Halverson sells these components exclusively to TruDrive Inc., a Delaware-incorporated automobile manufacturer with factories in Ohio and Tennessee. Halverson has no U.S. offices, employees, or distributors, and it negotiates all contracts with TruDrive at TruDrive's Ohio headquarters. TruDrive installs Halverson housings in vehicles assembled at its Tennessee plant and sells those vehicles through dealerships in all 50 states, including the State of Marlowe. A Marlowe resident purchased a TruDrive vehicle at a Marlowe dealership. The vehicle's brake caliper failed, causing a collision in which the resident was injured. The resident filed suit against Halverson in Marlowe federal court. Marlowe's long-arm statute reaches any foreign defendant whose product causes injury in the state. Halverson moves to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction. Under prevailing federal due-process doctrine for specific jurisdiction, how should the court rule?
Answer A: Deny the motion, because Halverson placed its components into the stream of commerce knowing they would enter vehicles sold nationwide, including in Marlowe
Answer B: Grant the motion, because Halverson's only contact with the United States is through TruDrive, and Halverson cannot be haled into any U.S. court
Answer C: Grant the motion, because Halverson did not purposefully avail itself of Marlowe — it sold only to TruDrive in Ohio without additional forum-targeting conduct directed at Marlowe
Answer D: Deny the motion, because the long-arm statute is satisfied and long-arm satisfaction alone establishes personal jurisdiction
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The long-arm statute is satisfied (product caused injury in Marlowe), but the due-process step fails under the prevailing purposeful-availment standard. Halverson sold exclusively to TruDrive in Ohio, negotiated contracts there, and took no acts specifically targeting Marlowe — no Marlowe advertising, no Marlowe distribution network, no design modifications for Marlowe's market. Under the O'Connor plurality's "additional conduct" requirement, mere awareness that TruDrive would sell nationwide does not constitute purposeful availment of Marlowe. The motion should be granted on constitutional grounds.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Under the O'Connor plurality — applied by many lower courts as the prevailing standard — awareness that a product may reach the forum is not enough without additional conduct targeting the forum (advertising there, designing for that market, establishing distribution there, etc.). Halverson sells only to TruDrive, does not market in Marlowe, and has no U.S. presence. Stream-of-commerce awareness alone, without more, falls short of purposeful availment under the dominant framework. B: Asahi* reached its result on reasonableness/fairness grounds after finding even minimum contacts was contested — it did not create a categorical rule that indirect sales through intermediaries can never establish PJ. Whether Halverson is reachable depends on the specific facts; the question is whether there are sufficient contacts with Marlowe, not whether indirect sales are categorically excluded. D: Long-arm statute satisfaction is a necessary but not sufficient condition for personal jurisdiction. The court must also determine that exercising jurisdiction complies with the Due Process Clause — i.e., that the defendant has minimum contacts with the forum. A statute cannot expand jurisdiction beyond constitutional limits. Here, even if Marlowe's long-arm statute reaches Halverson, the due-process analysis must still be conducted and, on these facts, comes out in Halverson's favor.
lb_rule_reference: Specific PJ over a component manufacturer requires that it purposefully directed its product toward the forum; placing goods into a distribution chain without additional forum-targeting acts may not suffice under the plurality in *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court* (1987). The majority rule requires awareness plus additional conduct (*O'Connor plurality*) or at minimum awareness that the product will reach the forum (*Brennan concurrence*). Questions must resolve under the majority/prevailing standard.
