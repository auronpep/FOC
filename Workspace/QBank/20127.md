Question: Officers arrested Victor for robbery and questioned him without Miranda warnings. Victor voluntarily said, "The stolen watch is in my locker." Officers obtained a valid search warrant for the locker based on the statement and found the watch. The prosecution does not offer Victor's statement, only the watch. Should the watch be suppressed as fruit of the Miranda violation?
Answer A: Yes, because all evidence derived from an unwarned statement is fruit of the poisonous tree
Answer B: Yes, because getting a warrant after the statement cannot cure any constitutional violation
Answer C: No, because physical evidence derived from a voluntary unwarned statement need not be suppressed
Answer D: No, because Victor's voluntary statement means there was no Miranda violation at all
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Victor's statement may be excluded from the case-in-chief, but the watch is physical evidence found through a voluntary unwarned statement. Miranda does not require suppressing that physical evidence.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Physical fruits of a voluntary unwarned statement are generally not suppressed for a Miranda violation. Miranda primarily excludes testimonial use of the unwarned statement. B: The key point is that physical evidence derived from a voluntary Miranda violation is not excluded on that basis. The later warrant is not the central doctrine. D: A statement can be voluntary yet still obtained in violation of Miranda. The remedy differs, but the warning violation is not erased.
lb_rule_reference: A Miranda violation does not require suppression of physical evidence discovered through an unwarned but voluntary statement.
