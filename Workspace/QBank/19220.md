Question: John contracted with Miriam to design a lobby mural for John's hotel. The written services agreement stated that Miriam would receive the final installment “upon the hotel architect's written approval of the completed mural.” The agreement also set a completion date. Miriam finished on time, but John told the architect to withhold approval because John had decided to replace the lobby with digital screens. John argues that the missing written approval defeats final payment. Which statement is least likely to be accepted?
Answer A: The approval language can be treated as a condition to final payment.
Answer B: The completion date does not by itself erase the separate approval condition.
Answer C: John may be unable to rely on nonapproval if he wrongfully prevented the approval from occurring.
Answer D: The approval condition is invalid because the architect did not separately sign a Statute of Frauds memorandum.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that approval language can operate as a condition, but a party who wrongfully prevents a condition may be unable to rely on its nonoccurrence. The trigger facts are the written approval clause, the separate completion date, and John's instruction to withhold approval for reasons unrelated to the mural. The decisive lock is term status and legal effect. D is least likely because the architect's separate Statute-of-Frauds signature is not required to make written approval a contractual condition.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is not least likely because “upon written approval” is condition language. B is not least likely because a completion date and an approval condition can coexist.
