Question: Walter owns a house that he has insured for $300,000. The house has significant structural problems and is worth far less than the insurance policy. Walter deliberately sets fire to the house at 2:00 a.m. while his family is away, burns it to the ground, and files a fraudulent insurance claim. The house is Walter's own property; he is the sole owner. Is Walter guilty of common-law arson?
Answer A: Yes, because Walter maliciously burned a dwelling at nighttime
Answer B: Yes, because Walter intended to defraud his insurance company, making the burning malicious
Answer C: No, because common-law arson requires burning the dwelling of another, and Walter burned his own property
Answer D: No, because the burning occurred at 2:00 a.m. when no one was inside, so there was no danger to human life
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Common-law arson is the malicious burning of the dwelling house of another. Walter owned the house outright — it was his property. One cannot commit common-law arson by burning one's own dwelling, regardless of the motive. Walter may be guilty of insurance fraud, wire fraud, or arson under modern statutes that eliminate the "of another" requirement, but he is not guilty of common-law arson. The traditional rule is a categorical exception for one's own property.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Common-law arson requires burning the dwelling of "another." Walter burned his own home. The "of another" element requires that the structure belong to someone other than the defendant. Because Walter owned the home, the common-law arson element of "another's dwelling" is not satisfied. B: "Malice" in arson means intentional or reckless disregard for the plain possibility that a dwelling would be burned. Walter clearly had the requisite mental state. But the mental-state element is not the problem here — the property element is. Common law requires burning of another's dwelling. Walter's fraudulent intent does not convert his own property into someone else's. The malice element is satisfied; the "of another" element is not. D: Common-law arson does not require that anyone be present or endangered. It is a property crime at its core, focused on the dwelling itself. The presence or absence of occupants is irrelevant to the elements of the offense. The dispositive reason Walter is not guilty of common-law arson is that the dwelling was his own, not that it was unoccupied.
lb_rule_reference: Common-law arson requires malicious burning of the dwelling house of another. A homeowner who burns his own dwelling cannot be convicted of common-law arson, even if done for insurance fraud purposes, because the property is his own.
