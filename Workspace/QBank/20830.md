Outline: Lay opinion testimony
Question: In a federal narcotics trial, Amos, a police officer, testifies that he watched Eve exchange a sealed package for cash. The prosecutor then asks Amos, “Based on your years in narcotics investigations, was Eve acting as a wholesale drug courier?” Eve objects that the question calls for expert testimony although Amos has not been qualified as an expert. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Overrule the objection because Amos personally watched the exchange.
Answer B: Overrule the objection because police officers may always give lay opinions based on investigative experience.
Answer C: Sustain the objection because the question asks for an opinion based on specialized law-enforcement knowledge rather than ordinary perception.
Answer D: Sustain the objection only because the question goes to the ultimate issue.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: A lay witness may testify from ordinary perception, such as what Amos saw. But the proposed opinion depends on specialized law-enforcement experience about narcotics roles. That type of testimony must satisfy the expert route; it cannot be admitted as lay opinion merely by calling the witness an observer.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because Amos's observation supports testimony about what he saw, not a specialized drug-courier classification. B: B is wrong because police officers do not have a blanket lay-opinion exception for expertise-based conclusions. D: D is wrong because the decisive defect is specialized knowledge, not merely ultimate-issue status.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 701(c) / specialized law-enforcement opinion requires expert route
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests expert-disguise and specialized-knowledge boundary.
