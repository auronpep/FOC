MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Carolyn was the victim of a street mugging. Without any police prompting, Carolyn searched social media on her own, found a profile she believed matched the mugger, and texted the photo to the lead detective, saying "I'm sure this is him." Police confirmed the name was Patrick. They then held a proper, neutral six-person photo array — Patrick's photo in position three, varied clothing and lighting, administered by an officer who did not know which photo depicted the suspect. Carolyn again picked Patrick. Patrick moved to suppress the photo array identification on grounds that Carolyn's prior social-media identification made the formal photo array impermissibly suggestive. Should the court suppress the photo array identification on due process grounds?
Answer A: Yes, because Carolyn's prior exposure to Patrick's photo made the subsequent photo array impermissibly suggestive regardless of how the array was conducted
Answer B: Yes, because any pretrial identification tainted by prior exposure to the suspect's image must be suppressed to protect the integrity of the trial
Answer C: No, because the due process challenge requires police-arranged suggestiveness, and the formal photo array was conducted in a neutral, non-suggestive manner
Answer D: No, because photo arrays are not subject to due process scrutiny at any stage
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The Supreme Court's due process identification framework — *Stovall v. Denno*, *Neil v. Biggers*, *Manson v. Brathwaite*, and confirmed in *Perry v. New Hampshire* (2012) — applies only when police arrange or are responsible for the suggestive procedure. Where suggestiveness arises from the witness's own independent actions rather than state conduct, there is no due process violation. Here, Carolyn's self-directed social-media search was not police-orchestrated, and the formal photo array was neutral and properly administered. The motion to suppress should be denied. The prior exposure is a credibility issue for the jury.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The due process protection against suggestive identification procedures is triggered only by state-created, police-arranged suggestiveness. Carolyn's independent social-media research was not orchestrated by or attributable to law enforcement. Because the formal photo array itself was properly conducted, there is no constitutional violation. The prior self-initiated exposure may affect the weight a jury assigns to the identification but does not provide grounds for constitutional suppression. B: There is no categorical constitutional rule requiring suppression whenever a witness has seen the suspect's image before a formal identification procedure. The due process clause targets state-engineered suggestiveness. Where the prior exposure is the result of the witness's independent conduct, no state action has created the impermissible suggestion. The formal procedure here was properly conducted. D: The *Ash* holding addresses only the Sixth Amendment right to counsel; it does not exempt photo arrays from due process analysis. Photo arrays are subject to due process scrutiny under the *Biggers/Brathwaite* framework if police arrange them in an impermissibly suggestive manner. The correct answer here turns on the absence of police-created suggestiveness, not an exemption from constitutional review.
lb_rule_reference: The due process protection against suggestive identification procedures applies only when police arrange or create the suggestive circumstances; independent suggestiveness arising from the witness's own actions or prior encounters does not implicate the Constitution.
