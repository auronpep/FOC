Question: A crane operator negligently swung a steel beam within inches of a pedestrian standing on the sidewalk. The pedestrian was not touched, but reasonably feared immediate impact and developed documented tremors and insomnia from the fright. The jurisdiction follows the zone-of-danger approach to standalone NIED. May the pedestrian recover for emotional distress?
Answer A: No, because emotional distress damages require physical impact in every negligence case
Answer B: No, because only bystanders to family injury may recover NIED
Answer C: Yes, because the pedestrian was personally endangered and suffered physical symptoms from the fright
Answer D: Yes, because any upset witness may recover emotional distress damages
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The facts satisfy the stated zone-of-danger NIED requirements.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Under the stated zone-of-danger approach, impact is not required when personal danger and physical symptoms are shown. B: This is a direct zone-of-danger claim, not a bystander claim. D: Recovery depends on a recognized NIED category; general upset alone is not enough.
lb_rule_reference: In a zone-of-danger NIED frame, a plaintiff who was personally endangered and suffered physical symptoms from fright may recover emotional-distress damages.
