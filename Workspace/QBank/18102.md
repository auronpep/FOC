Outline: Solicitation
Question: During a heated argument at a poker game, Pete slammed his fist on the table and shouted at his friend Ross: "You've been cheating all night — somebody ought to kill you!" Pete and Ross had been friends for fifteen years and regularly insulted each other during games. Pete did not follow up on the statement, and both men continued playing cards. Ross reported the comment to police. Pete is charged with solicitation to commit murder. Is Pete guilty of solicitation to commit murder?
Answer A: Yes, because Pete verbally communicated a request for another person to commit murder
Answer B: Yes, because the statement was made in the presence of others who could have heard it
Answer C: No, because the statement lacked specific intent that anyone actually commit murder
Answer D: No, because no specific individual was solicited to commit the murder
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Solicitation is a specific-intent crime: the defendant must genuinely intend that the solicited person commit the target offense. Pete's outburst during a card game among longtime friends, with no follow-up and in a context of habitual ribbing, is most naturally characterized as venting frustration or hyperbole rather than a genuine request for murder. The prosecution would be unable to prove that Pete actually wanted someone to kill Ross. Without that specific intent, the solicitation charge fails.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Solicitation requires specific intent that the solicited person actually commit the crime. Courts examine whether the speaker genuinely meant the request. Statements made in anger, as hyperbole, or in jest are not solicitations because they lack the required specific intent. Pete's context — a long friendship, a heated game, no follow-up — points strongly against genuine intent. B: The presence of others affects nothing about the specific-intent requirement. Whether one or ten people heard the statement, the prosecution must still prove Pete genuinely intended for someone to murder Ross. Publicity of a statement is not a substitute for intent. D: Solicitation does not require that a specific, identified individual be solicited. A general request to whoever might act is still solicitation if made with genuine intent. The correct basis for acquittal here is lack of specific intent, not failure to identify a specific target.
lb_rule_reference: Criminal solicitation requires that the defendant specifically intend that the person solicited commit the crime; a request made without genuine intent that the crime be carried out is not solicitation
