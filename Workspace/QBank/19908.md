MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: Mia was arrested and given Miranda warnings. She said, "I want a lawyer." Questioning ceased. Three hours later, Mia asked the guard, "Can I talk to the detectives? I want to clear some things up." The guard notified the detectives, who returned to the interview room, re-administered Miranda warnings, and asked if Mia still wanted to talk. Mia said yes, was advised of her rights again, signed a waiver form, and then gave a detailed confession. She now moves to suppress the confession. Is Mia's confession admissible?
Answer A: No, because once a suspect invokes the right to counsel, no subsequent waiver is valid until counsel has been present at a prior session
Answer B: No, because the detectives should not have accepted Mia's request to speak without first providing her with counsel
Answer C: Yes, because Mia re-initiated contact with police, and after fresh Miranda warnings she validly waived her rights
Answer D: Yes, because three hours is a sufficient time lapse after invocation to permit police to re-approach the suspect
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Oregon v. Bradshaw* (1983), a suspect who has invoked the right to counsel may voluntarily waive Miranda rights if the suspect — not police — spontaneously re-initiates communication relating to the investigation. Here, Mia asked to speak to the detectives herself. The detectives properly re-administered Miranda warnings, Mia expressly waived her rights in writing, and then confessed. Each step of the *Bradshaw* framework was satisfied. The confession is admissible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Edwards v. Arizona* bars police-initiated questioning after a counsel invocation — it does not prevent a suspect from voluntarily re-initiating contact. When the suspect spontaneously re-initiates communication about the investigation, police may question the suspect after re-administering Miranda warnings. This is the *Oregon v. Bradshaw* (1983) rule. Mia initiated contact; the detectives then followed proper procedure. B: The Miranda right to counsel is the right to have counsel present during custodial interrogation — it is not an obligation on police to provide counsel before a willing suspect may speak. If a suspect changes her mind and voluntarily re-initiates communication, police may resume questioning with fresh warnings. There is no duty to force a willing suspect to wait for counsel. D: Time lapse is the operative factor for re-initiating questioning after a right-to-silence invocation. For a right-to-counsel invocation, time lapse alone does not permit police to re-initiate — only the suspect may re-initiate. The correct basis for admissibility is Mia's spontaneous re-initiation, not the three-hour gap. Police could not have lawfully returned to question Mia based on the time lapse alone.
lb_rule_reference: After invoking the right to counsel, a suspect may validly waive Miranda rights if the suspect — not police — spontaneously re-initiates communication about the investigation; police may then question after re-administering warnings.
