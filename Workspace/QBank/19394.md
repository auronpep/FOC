MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Civil Procedure
Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Federal Subject-Matter Jurisdiction
Question: A plaintiff files a diversity action in federal court against a defendant for breach of a commercial contract. The contract contains no fee-shifting provision, and no statute applicable to this type of commercial contract dispute authorizes attorney's fee awards. The plaintiff's compensatory damages are $60,000. The plaintiff adds $20,000 in anticipated attorney's fees to the demand, claiming a total of $80,000 and arguing that this satisfies the § 1332 AIC threshold. The defendant moves to dismiss for failure to satisfy the $75,000 AIC requirement. The governing forum is federal court under the MBE baseline. Does the amount in controversy satisfy § 1332?
Answer A: Yes, because litigation is expensive, and attorney's fees are a foreseeable and actual cost of any lawsuit; including them in the AIC calculation reflects the true value of the dispute
Answer B: No, because the applicable law has no fee-shifting statute or contract provision, so attorney's fees are not legally recoverable and must be excluded from the AIC, leaving only $60,000 — below the threshold
Answer C: Yes, because any anticipated costs of the litigation, including fees, may be included in a plaintiff's good-faith demand to satisfy the AIC
Answer D: No, but the plaintiff may amend the complaint to add a breach-of-implied-covenant claim, which under some states authorizes fees, curing the AIC deficiency before the court rules
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Under the American Rule, each party pays its own attorney's fees unless a statute or contract provides otherwise. Here, neither the contract nor any applicable statute authorizes fee-shifting. Because attorney's fees are legally unavailable to the plaintiff in this action, they cannot be included in the AIC. Under the legal certainty test, it is legally certain that the plaintiff cannot recover the $20,000 in fees. Excluding them, only $60,000 in compensatory damages remains — below the $75,000 threshold. The court must dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Under federal law, attorney's fees are included in the AIC only when they are authorized by the applicable substantive law — by statute or contract. Here, neither applies. Under the American Rule, each party bears its own fees in the absence of a fee-shifting statute or agreement. Fees that are not legally recoverable are legally certain not to be awarded and therefore cannot be included in the AIC. C: The good-faith demand rule operates within the constraint of the legal certainty test. A plaintiff cannot include components in the AIC that are legally unavailable. Because no law authorizes fee recovery here, a demand for fees is not a good-faith demand under § 1332 — it is legally certain to fail. D: This answer does not respond to the question asked — whether the current complaint satisfies the AIC. It also misstates the amendment option: amending to add a new theory after filing to try to manufacture fee-shifting creates its own jurisdictional questions and is not a reliable cure. More fundamentally, the question asks about the current complaint; on those facts, the AIC is not satisfied.
lb_rule_reference: Attorney's fees are included in the § 1332 AIC only when they are recoverable as of right under the applicable substantive law (statute or contract); fees not authorized by law or contract are excluded under the American Rule.
