Question: Noah agreed in a signed land-sale contract to buy Leah's vacant parcel. The contract set a closing date and price, and stated, “Closing is subject to county approval of the lot-split application.” The county denied the application before closing. Leah sued Noah for refusing to close, arguing that the closing date and price were unconditional promises and that the approval language was only background planning language. What is Noah's best Content/Meaning defense?
Answer A: The approval language created a condition precedent to Noah's duty to close, so the duty did not mature when county approval was denied.
Answer B: The approval language is unenforceable because the county was not a party to the land-sale contract.
Answer C: The Statute of Frauds bars Noah from relying on any approval condition connected to a land sale.
Answer D: The approval language is inadmissible because it is collateral to the written closing date.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that condition language can qualify when a duty becomes enforceable. The trigger facts are the signed writing's “subject to county approval” phrase and the county's denial before closing. The decisive lock is term status: the condition appears in the written agreement, so Noah is not trying to add an outside term. If the approval condition failed, Noah's duty to close did not mature.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because a third party can be the event whose approval triggers a condition; the county need not be a contracting party. C: C is wrong because the condition is in the land-sale writing, so this is not a writing-sufficiency objection. D: D is wrong because the approval language is written content that qualifies the closing duty, not a collateral oral understanding.
