Subtopic: Other Crimes
Outline: Larceny
Question: A bank teller named Carol receives a wire transfer of $15,000 that was misdirected to her employer bank's account due to a clerical error by a different institution. Carol's supervisor, unaware of the error, instructs Carol to keep the funds in the general account. Carol realizes the error, quietly transfers the $15,000 to her personal account, and spends it. Carol is a salaried bank employee with no personal fiduciary duty regarding this particular transaction; she was never personally entrusted with those funds for any purpose. Is Carol most likely guilty of embezzlement or larceny?
Answer A: Embezzlement, because Carol is an employee of the bank and the money was in the bank's account
Answer B: Larceny, because Carol took money without a trespassory taking from the account
Answer C: Embezzlement, because Carol was entrusted with access to the banking system in her employment capacity and converted the funds through that access
Answer D: Neither, because the funds were misdirected to begin with and thus had no true owner with superior rights
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: While the entrustment here is indirect, Carol's position as a bank teller gave her authorized access to the bank's account systems — a form of entrustment of the institution's funds management. Courts and most embezzlement statutes extend the concept of entrustment to employees who are given access to institutional funds as part of their job function. Carol used that authorized access to convert $15,000 to her own account with fraudulent intent. Embezzlement is the best-fitting common-law charge: lawful access via employment, conversion of another's property, and fraudulent intent.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Embezzlement requires that the specific property have been entrusted to Carol in her role. The $15,000 was misdirected funds that arrived without any entrustment to Carol personally or in her official capacity for those funds. Her supervisor's instruction to "keep the funds in the general account" was an internal bookkeeping directive, not an entrustment for her to manage or transmit. The lawful-possession-through-entrustment element is not met for Carol as to these specific funds. B: Larceny requires a physical trespassory taking and carrying away. Carol transferred electronic funds; moreover, the analysis of whether initial possession was trespassory or lawful is complicated by her access as an employee. More precise analysis: because Carol had authorized access to the bank's systems as an employee, the transfer was not a trespassory taking in the classic sense. This is a prosecution under a specific embezzlement or computer fraud statute; common-law larceny has difficulty capturing electronic fund transfers. D: The originating institution that sent the wire transfer retained superior title to the funds. A misdirected transfer does not extinguish the sender's ownership rights. Carol had no claim of right to the funds, and taking them with knowledge of the error and intent to keep them clearly satisfies the conversion element of embezzlement. The misdirection is irrelevant to Carol's criminal liability.
lb_rule_reference: Embezzlement requires that the defendant's lawful possession arise from a relationship of trust or confidence — such as employment, agency, or fiduciary status — with the owner. A person who receives property through mere accident or without any trust relationship does not commit embezzlement by retaining it.
