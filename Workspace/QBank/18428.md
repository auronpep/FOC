Question: Rachel hired a builder to construct a small house for Rachel's mother, Hannah. The contract stated that the builder would deliver the finished house to Hannah. Rachel repeatedly refused to give the builder access to the land, making construction impossible. Hannah then sued the builder as the intended beneficiary of the promise. What is the builder's best defense?
Answer A: Hannah's beneficiary status gives her rights greater than Rachel's, so Rachel's breach is irrelevant.
Answer B: Hannah may recover because the builder promised performance directly to her.
Answer C: The builder has no defense unless Hannah personally breached the contract.
Answer D: The builder may assert Rachel's material breach because an intended beneficiary's rights are subject to defenses the promisor has against the promisee.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: An intended third-party beneficiary generally takes subject to defenses that the promisor could assert against the promisee. Rachel's refusal to provide access materially prevented the builder's performance. Hannah does not receive greater rights than Rachel had under the contract.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because a beneficiary's rights are derivative in this respect. B: B is wrong because a promised benefit does not erase defenses against the promisee. C: C is wrong because the relevant breach can be Rachel's breach, not only Hannah's.
lb_rule_reference: Third-party beneficiary derivative defense / constructive condition
