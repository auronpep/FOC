Subtopic: Other Torts
Outline: Defamation
Question: A former supervisor received a confidential reference request from a clinic considering a nurse for employment. The supervisor knew the nurse had resigned after a scheduling dispute, not theft. Angry about the resignation, the supervisor wrote that the nurse "was fired for stealing medication" and copied the statement to a local hospital managers' listserv that had not requested a reference. The nurse sued the supervisor for defamation. Is the supervisor likely protected by a defamation qualified privilege for the job reference?
Answer A: Yes, because all job references are absolutely privileged
Answer B: No, because any false statement automatically defeats every privilege
Answer C: No, because the supervisor likely lost any qualified privilege through knowing falsity, spite, and excessive publication
Answer D: Yes, because the clinic had an interest in medication safety
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The reference setting may create a qualified privilege, but the supervisor knowingly made a false accusation and copied people outside the need-to-know reference context, which supports loss of privilege.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Job references are typically qualified, not absolute. The privilege can be lost through knowing falsity, malice, or excessive publication. B: Falsity alone does not defeat all privileges. The key here is knowing falsity, improper motive, and publication beyond the reference request. D: A proper reference purpose does not protect knowingly false statements or excessive publication beyond that purpose.
lb_rule_reference: A job reference is commonly protected by a qualified privilege, but the privilege can be lost by malice, knowing falsity, excessive publication, or use outside the privileged purpose.
