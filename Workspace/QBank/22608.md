Question: A man was running for state senate in State A. His opponent found out that the man had been a dues- paying member of a student activist group that destroyed several government buildings 10 years ago. After the destruction of the final building, the state legislature had officially declared the group a terrorist organization and passed a law banning the group from the state and banning any member of the group from holding elected office. Although the group disbanded naturally, the law was never repealed. The man's opponent petitioned the state to remove the man from his campaign based on the old law. The state agreed and told the man that he could not be seated in state government based on his past membership in the banned group. The man sued, arguing that the law was unconstitutional. Is the court likely to find the law constitutional?
Answer A: A: Yes, because the state legislature officially declared the group a terrorist organization.
Answer B: B: Yes, because the state legislature can create rational qualifications for its members.
Answer C: C: No, because the law is a bill of attainder.
Answer D: D: No, because there is no rational basis for banning group members from public office.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: A law is an unconstitutional bill of attainder if it punishes named or ascertainable members of a group without judicial process. Here, the law specifically names the student group, so it's an unconstitutional bill of attainder. A is incorrect because the fact that the group was officially declared a terrorist organization does not change the analysis. B is incorrect because the law did not simply state qualifications for office, it specifically punished a particular group. D is incorrect because the law would not be judged with rational basis analysis.
