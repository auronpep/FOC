Outline: Burglary
Question: After voluntarily drinking heavily, Theo mistakenly enters a neighbor's unlocked garage, believing it is his own. He lies down on a couch and falls asleep. The jurisdiction defines burglary as breaking and entering a building with intent to commit a felony inside. Theo is charged with burglary. How does Theo's voluntary intoxication affect the burglary charge?
Answer A: It may negate burglary if it prevented Theo from forming the intent to commit a felony inside
Answer B: It is no defense because voluntary intoxication is never relevant to mens rea
Answer C: It automatically acquits Theo of every offense arising from the entry
Answer D: It works only if Theo was intoxicated involuntarily
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Burglary requires a specific intent at the time of entry: intent to commit a felony inside. Voluntary intoxication can be used to show the defendant lacked that specific intent. If Theo drunkenly believed he was entering his own garage to sleep, he lacked the required burglarious objective.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: The categorical bar applies to general-intent, malice, recklessness, negligence, and strict-liability crimes. Specific-intent crimes are different. C: Voluntary intoxication negates only the specific mental element it actually prevents. It does not automatically defeat trespass or other general-intent offenses. D: Involuntary intoxication can be broader, but voluntary intoxication still may negate specific intent. Burglary contains that specific intent requirement.
lb_rule_reference: Voluntary intoxication may negate specific intent, including the intent to commit a crime inside required for burglary, but it does not excuse general intent crimes
