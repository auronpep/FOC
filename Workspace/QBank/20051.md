


BARMATRIX Q#: 20051
Outline_code: 41030401
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Constitutional Law
Subtopic: Relations Between Federal and State Governments
Outline: Tenth Amendment
Question: After examining studies indicating that chewing gum was directly related to the incidence of tooth decay, the state legislature enacted a law prohibiting the advertising of chewing gum in all media. Which of the following is the clearest reason for holding the law to be unconstitutional?
Answer A: A: A state may not interfere with commercial speech.
Answer B: B: The sale of chewing gum frequently involves interstate commerce.
Answer C: C: The law imposes a prior restraint on publication.
Answer D: D: There are less restrictive ways of protecting the public against tooth decay that would be equally effective.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: Although the state may interfere with commercial speech to serve a substantial governmental interest, it must not do so in a way that is unnecessarily restrictive. Thus, even though the state may have a substantial interest in protecting the public against tooth decay, the law prohibiting the advertising of chewing gum would be constitutionally invalid if there are less restrictive ways of accomplishing the same objective (e.g., by requiring a warning). Although it is not certain that the argument in D would result in a finding that the law is invalid, it is the only one listed that could possibly support such a finding.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A is an incorrect statement of law since freedom of expression is not absolute and may be interfered with to serve a substantial government interest. The mere fact that a state law will have an effect on interstate commerce is not enough to make that law invalid unless it imposes an unreasonable burden on interstate commerce. B is therefore incorrect. Although the United States Supreme Court is wary of laws that impose prior restraint on publication, it is far less concerned when those laws affect commercial speech only. C is incorrect because the speech involved is commercial, and the fact that the law imposes a prior restraint is therefore not sufficient alone to render it constitutionally invalid.
