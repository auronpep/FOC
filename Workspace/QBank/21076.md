Subtopic: General Principles
Outline: Defense of property
Question: Harold owns a remote cabin that has been burglarized repeatedly. Frustrated, Harold installs a spring-loaded shotgun rigged to fire at anyone who opens the cabin door without first pressing a hidden button. Harold posts no warning signs. One night, a trespasser named Lena, who intends to steal tools from the cabin, opens the door and is shot, sustaining serious injuries. Harold argues he had the right to protect his property just as he would have if he had been standing there himself. Which of the following best states Harold's criminal liability?
Answer A: Harold is not liable because property owners may use any means necessary to protect their homes
Answer B: Harold is not liable because Lena was committing a burglary and assumed the risk
Answer C: Harold is criminally liable because using a deadly spring gun to protect property alone is never justified
Answer D: Harold is criminally liable only if he knew a human being would trigger the device
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The spring-gun rule is a direct application of the prohibition on deadly force to protect property. Courts treat a mechanical device as the functional equivalent of the owner personally using force. Because Harold could not have lawfully shot Lena himself solely to protect property (absent a threat to persons), Harold cannot lawfully set a device to do so in his absence. Harold is liable for the battery (and potentially attempted murder or assault) resulting from Lena's injuries. The leading case, *Katko v. Briney* (civil), and corresponding criminal doctrine both reach this result. Harold's intent to deter burglary does not create a justification that does not otherwise exist.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The castle doctrine gives extra rights when the owner is present and facing a threat — it does not authorize booby traps in the owner's absence. The prohibition on deadly force to protect property extends to mechanical devices regardless of whether they are in a home or cabin. The law does not distinguish whether the deadly force was applied personally or mechanically. B: Assumption of risk is a civil tort doctrine and does not provide a criminal defense. More importantly, the rule against deadly force to protect property applies regardless of what offense the victim was committing. A burglar does not forfeit all legal protection against deadly force simply by trespassing, absent a threat to persons. D: Harold set a device designed to fire at anyone opening the door. Intent to set a deadly trap that is triggered by a human is sufficient — the law does not require certainty about a specific victim. Moreover, the core issue is not subjective intent but the categorical rule that deadly force cannot be used to protect property alone. Harold's knowledge that the device could seriously injure any entrant is sufficient mens rea.
lb_rule_reference: An owner may not use a deadly mechanical device (spring gun, trap) to protect property from trespassers or burglars; the device is treated as though the owner pulled the trigger, and deadly force to protect property alone is never justified
