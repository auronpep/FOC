Question: John hired Naomi's data company to prepare monthly compliance reports. The signed services agreement stated that “late reports qualify for service credits,” but it did not say whether the credits were the exclusive remedy. The agreement contained a merger clause. After several late reports, John sued for additional losses. Naomi offered drafting correspondence showing that both sides used “service credits” to mean the exclusive remedy for late reports. Should the court admit the correspondence for that purpose?
Answer A: Yes, if the service-credit language is ambiguous and the correspondence is offered to explain its meaning.
Answer B: No, because remedy questions are never part of Contract Content and Meaning.
Answer C: No, because the merger clause bars all prior drafting correspondence for every purpose.
Answer D: Yes, but only as proof that John suffered no actual loss.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that a remedy clause may need interpretation before the remedies issue can be decided, and a merger clause does not bar evidence offered to explain ambiguous language. The trigger facts are the undefined service-credit clause, the merger clause, and the offered purpose of explaining exclusivity. The decisive lock is purpose: Naomi offers the correspondence to interpret the remedy term, not to add an unrelated term or prove damages. The evidence may be admitted for that limited meaning purpose if the clause is reasonably susceptible to that meaning.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because remedy labels can depend on contract-content meaning. C: C is wrong because merger clauses do not automatically bar ambiguity explanation. D: D is wrong because the correspondence is not offered to prove the amount of loss; it is offered to define the remedy term.
lb_rule_reference: Remedy-clause ambiguity / PER purpose split
