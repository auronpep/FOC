MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Other Torts
Outline: Independent contractor and nondelegable duties
Question: A music hall employed a door attendant to check tickets and remove disruptive patrons. During a crowded show, a patron argued with the attendant after being told to leave a blocked exit area. The attendant shoved the patron harder than necessary, causing the patron to fall and break a wrist. The patron sues the music hall for vicarious liability. The hall argues that it cannot be liable because the attendant committed an intentional battery. Is the patron likely to prevail on a vicarious liability claim against the music hall under respondeat superior?
Answer A: No, because intentional torts are always outside the scope of employment
Answer B: Yes, because the shove arose from the attendant's assigned crowd-control duties and foreseeable friction with patrons
Answer C: Yes, because the music hall is directly liable for battery whenever an employee touches a customer
Answer D: No, because the attendant violated the hall's policy requiring reasonable restraint
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The attendant was doing assigned work at the hall, during the show, while handling a patron dispute of the kind the job predictably creates. Excessive force can still be within scope for vicarious liability.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Intentional torts are not automatically outside scope. Force used by an employee hired to control entry and remove disruptive patrons can be closely connected to assigned duties and foreseeable job friction. C: The question asks for vicarious liability, not whether the hall itself committed battery. The hall's liability depends on the employee's scope of employment, not on treating the hall as the direct actor. D: An employee can act within scope even while performing assigned duties in a forbidden or excessive way. The issue is whether the conduct was connected to the job, not whether it complied with every instruction.
lb_rule_reference: An intentional tort may fall within scope when it is closely connected to assigned duties or foreseeable job friction, especially for security-type work.
