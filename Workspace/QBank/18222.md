Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Definition
Question: In a federal negligence trial, Sarah claims that Jonah ran a red light because he was impaired. Sarah offers testimony from a paramedic that Jonah smelled strongly of alcohol and stumbled when he got out of his car ten minutes after the crash. Jonah objects that the testimony does not prove he was impaired at the moment of impact. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the testimony because Sarah must first prove that Jonah was impaired at the exact moment of impact.
Answer B: Exclude the testimony because later observations are never relevant to a driver's condition at an earlier time.
Answer C: Admit the testimony because observations close in time to the crash have some tendency to make impairment at the time of the crash more probable.
Answer D: Admit the testimony only if Sarah also offers a blood-alcohol test.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under FRE 401, evidence is relevant if it has any tendency to make a fact of consequence more or less probable. Jonah's condition ten minutes after the crash tends to make impairment at the time of the crash more probable. The timing weakness goes to weight.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because relevance does not require proof of the exact ultimate fact before admission. B: B is wrong because later observations may support an inference about an earlier condition when close in time. D: D is wrong because a blood test is not required for threshold relevance.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401 / intoxication evidence and timing
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests the low-threshold relevance of close-in-time condition evidence and the mistake of demanding conclusive proof.
