Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: Detectives read Miranda warnings to Mina and handed her a waiver form. Before she signed, a detective said, "If you do not sign and confess, we will make sure your sister is charged too." Mina signed and confessed. The threat had no factual basis. Is the signed waiver form enough to make Mina's confession admissible?
Answer A: Yes, because a signed Miranda waiver conclusively proves voluntariness
Answer B: Yes, because police may threaten relatives during interrogation
Answer C: No, because the baseless threat undermined voluntary waiver and confession despite the signed form
Answer D: No, but only because Mina invoked her right to silence
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The state must prove voluntary waiver and confession. The threat to charge Mina's sister if Mina did not confess is coercive and defeats reliance on the signed form.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A waiver form is evidence, not conclusive proof. Coercive threats can make waiver and confession involuntary. B: Baseless threats to charge a family member can overbear the suspect's will and defeat voluntariness. D: Mina did not invoke silence. The defect is coercion affecting waiver and confession.
lb_rule_reference: Miranda waiver must be knowing, voluntary, and intelligent; police coercion can invalidate waiver and also make the confession involuntary.
