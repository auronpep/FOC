MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Intentional Torts
Outline: Privileges
Question: At a park, a runner saw a stranger raise a fist and rush toward a child. The runner reasonably believed the stranger was about to strike the child and used a moderate push to stop him. The stranger was actually the child's uncle playing a rough game, but the runner had no way to know that. The uncle sues the runner for battery. Can the runner assert defense of others?
Answer A: Yes, because the runner reasonably believed the child faced imminent contact and used proportionate force
Answer B: No, because the child was not actually in danger
Answer C: No, because the runner was defending a stranger rather than a family member
Answer D: Yes, but only because the runner used no force at all
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The privilege turns on reasonable apparent danger and proportionate force, not perfect accuracy.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: A reasonable mistake can still support defense of others. C: Defense of others is not limited to family members. D: The runner did use force. The question is whether the moderate force was privileged.
lb_rule_reference: Defense of others can be privileged when the defendant reasonably believes another person faces imminent harmful or offensive contact and uses proportionate force.
