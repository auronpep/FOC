Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: Ned confronted his neighbor Ola in her driveway and said, "If you file that noise complaint, I will come back next week and break your nose." Ola was frightened by the statement and believed Ned was serious. Ned made no physical move toward Ola and left immediately after speaking. A week passed and Ned did not return. Was Ned's statement sufficient to constitute common-law assault?
Answer A: Yes, because Ola genuinely feared that Ned would carry out the threat
Answer B: Yes, because Ned had the apparent ability to carry out the threat
Answer C: No, because the threat was conditioned on a future event and referred to harm next week, not imminent harm
Answer D: No, because Ned left the scene without making any physical movement toward Ola
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Common-law assault requires that the defendant place the victim in reasonable apprehension of an imminent battery. "Imminent" means about to happen now, not at some future point. Ned's statement was doubly deficient: it was conditioned on Ola filing a complaint (a future contingency), and it expressly set the time for next week. Courts uniformly hold that conditional threats of future violence do not constitute assault because they lack the imminence element. The fear Ola experienced, while genuine, was fear of a future event — not of a battery that was about to happen.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Assault requires apprehension of imminent battery, not merely genuine fear of future harm. Ned's threat was conditioned on Ola filing a complaint and expressly referenced next week. Neither the condition nor the timeframe satisfies imminence. Subjective fear of a future event is insufficient. B: Apparent ability is a necessary but not sufficient element. The threat must also place the victim in apprehension of imminent battery. Ned's threat was explicitly for next week, conditioned on a future act. There was no imminent battery threatened. D: While the result is correct, the reasoning is wrong. Assault does not always require physical movement — a present oral threat alone, without movement, can constitute assault if the victim is placed in apprehension of imminent battery. The reason there is no assault here is the lack of imminence, not the lack of physical movement.
lb_rule_reference: Assault requires that the victim be placed in reasonable apprehension of an imminent battery; a threat of future harm, or a threat conditioned on a future event, does not satisfy the imminence requirement
