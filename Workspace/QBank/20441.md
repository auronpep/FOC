Question: Noah hired Samuel to manage advertising for a garden center. The signed services agreement was headed “Client: Noah Garden LLC.” In the body, the agreement sometimes referred to “the Client” and sometimes to “Noah.” The signature line read only “Noah, owner,” and the agreement contained a merger clause. After Noah Garden LLC failed to pay, Samuel sued Noah personally. Noah offered negotiation emails stating that Samuel agreed to contract only with the LLC. Which statement is least likely to be accepted?
Answer A: The merger clause automatically bars all evidence about whether Noah signed personally or only for the LLC.
Answer B: The inconsistent heading, body language, and signature line can make party capacity ambiguous.
Answer C: If the writing is reasonably susceptible to an LLC-only reading, the emails may be considered to explain party identity.
Answer D: If the court finds Noah signed only as the LLC's agent, the signature alone does not establish personal liability.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that a merger clause bars added or inconsistent obligations, but it does not automatically bar evidence offered to resolve ambiguity about party identity or signing capacity. The trigger facts are the LLC heading, mixed references to “Noah,” and unclear signature line. The decisive lock is term status: the identity of the contracting party is ambiguous despite integration. A is least likely because it treats a merger clause as an absolute four-corners bar.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is not least likely because those facts create the ambiguity. C is not least likely because the emails are offered to explain who is bound, not to add a new duty. D is not least likely because an agent who signs only for a disclosed entity is not personally liable merely because his name appears on an ambiguous signature line.
