MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Civil Procedure
Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Minimum contacts test
Question: Petra lives and works in the State of Elston, where she owns a software consulting firm. While attending a three-day industry conference in the State of Norvale, she was personally served with a summons and complaint in a civil lawsuit filed by Randall, a former Norvale client, who claims Petra breached a services contract and owes him $90,000. Petra's only connection to Norvale is the conference; she has never marketed to Norvale residents, maintained an office there, or performed any work there except for the project Randall commissioned — which Petra performed remotely from Elston. Norvale's long-arm statute authorizes jurisdiction over persons served with process within the state. Petra moves to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction, arguing that her brief presence at a conference does not create minimum contacts with Norvale. Should the court grant Petra's motion to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction?
Answer A: Yes, because Petra's three-day conference attendance does not create minimum contacts with Norvale sufficient to support specific jurisdiction over a contract claim
Answer B: Yes, because Petra's work for Randall was performed in Elston, so Norvale lacks specific jurisdiction over the contract claim even under a minimum-contacts analysis
Answer C: No, because Petra was personally served with process while physically present in Norvale, and in-state service on an individual is a traditional basis for personal jurisdiction that does not require minimum contacts
Answer D: No, because Petra performed work for Randall, a Norvale citizen, which satisfies purposeful availment of the Norvale forum
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The long-arm statute authorizes jurisdiction over persons served within the state, satisfying the statutory step. Under *Burnham v. Superior Court*, personal service on an individual physically present in the forum state is a constitutionally valid basis for personal jurisdiction — a rule rooted in traditional notions of territorial sovereignty that predates *International Shoe*. Minimum contacts are not required when the defendant is served while in-state. Petra's motion should be denied.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Transient jurisdiction does not require minimum contacts. Personal service on a defendant physically present in the forum is an independent, traditional basis for jurisdiction recognized by the Supreme Court in *Burnham v. Superior Court*. Where an individual is validly served within the state, the minimum-contacts test is not the applicable framework — the Court upheld tag jurisdiction as consistent with due process without applying *International Shoe*. B: Again, this answer applies the wrong framework. Once the defendant is personally served within the state, the traditional tag rule applies and no minimum-contacts or relatedness analysis is needed. The where-the-work-was-done question is irrelevant to tag jurisdiction. D: Performing work remotely in Elston for a Norvale client does not clearly satisfy purposeful availment of Norvale as a forum. The correct basis for denying the motion is tag jurisdiction — in-state service — not specific PJ through purposeful availment. An answer that denies the motion for the wrong reason would mislead students about the applicable doctrine.
lb_rule_reference: An individual physically present in the forum state when process is served is subject to personal jurisdiction in that state based on transient presence (tag jurisdiction), regardless of how briefly the defendant was present and without requiring minimum contacts. *Burnham v. Superior Court* (1990) (plurality and concurrence reaching the same result on different grounds).
