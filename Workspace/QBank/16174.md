


BARMATRIX Q#: 16174
Outline_code: 73020200
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Other Crimes
Outline: Assault
Question: A state statute provides that the crime of assault is generally punishable by a maximum of five years in prison. It further provides that the maximum punishment increases to 10 years in prison if the defendant possessed a deadly weapon during the assault. The statute designates the deadly weapon element as a sentencing factor. A defendant charged with assault has pleaded not guilty and has requested a jury trial. The prosecutor has announced that she will be seeking a 10-year sentence based on the defendant's alleged possession of a deadly weapon. What finding regarding the defendant's possession of a deadly weapon is necessary for the court to impose a 10-year sentence?
Answer A: A: A judicial finding by a preponderance of the evidence.
Answer B: B: A jury finding by a preponderance of the evidence.
Answer C: C: A judicial finding beyond a reasonable doubt.
Answer D: D: A jury finding beyond a reasonable doubt.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: (D)is the best response, because the legislature has in effect defined an additional crime of “assault while in possession of a deadly weapon,” and the Sixth Amendment right to jury trial entitles the defendant not to be convicted unless the jury finds every element of this additional crime beyond a reasonable doubt. The “deadly weapon” portion of the statute increases the maximum penalty for assault from the five years it would be without possession of a deadly weapon during the assault to 10 years with possession. You can think of this provision as defining a new crime of “assault while in possession of a deadly weapon,” where the new crime has a higher maximum penalty than garden-variety assault does. In this “maximum-penalty-enhancement” situation, the Supreme Court has held that any fact that is needed to trigger the enhancement falls within the general rule that under the Sixth Amendment, any element of the crime must be submitted to the jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt. The key Supreme Court ruling is Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), where the Court said, “Other than the fact of a prior conviction, any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury, and proved beyond a reasonable doubt.” The facts here fall within the heart of this Apprendi principle: the fact that the defendant possessed a deadly weapon while committing the assault causes the maximum penalty for assault to rise to 10 years from the five year maximum that would otherwise exist, so deadly-weapon possession is a fact that “must be submitted to a jury, and proved beyond a reasonable doubt.” Neither (A), nor (B), nor (C) is the best response, because each is logically inconsistent with the correct Choice (D), which says that possession of the deadly weapon is a fact that must be found by the jury to have existed beyond a reasonable doubt.
