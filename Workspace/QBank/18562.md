Question: In a federal premises-liability trial, Ezra, a security supervisor, is asked, “Why did Naomi slip?” Ezra answers that he did not see the fall, did not inspect the aisle, and only read an incident report prepared by another employee. The plaintiff then asks Ezra to testify that Naomi slipped because oil was on the floor. The defendant objects. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection because Ezra lacks personal knowledge of the cause of the fall and is merely relying on another employee's report.
Answer B: Overrule the objection because Ezra supervises security and may repeat any report kept in the ordinary course of business.
Answer C: Overrule the objection if the incident report was made near the time of the fall.
Answer D: Overrule the objection because causation in a slip-and-fall case never requires expert testimony.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Ezra did not perceive the fall or inspect the aisle and therefore lacks personal knowledge of the cause. A possible business-record route for the report itself does not permit Ezra to testify as though he personally observed the event. The proponent must use an admissible proof channel for the report or another witness with perception.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B and C are wrong because recordkeeping facts do not give Ezra personal knowledge of the accident. D is wrong because the issue is not whether expert testimony is required; it is whether this witness has a personal basis for the fact testimony.
