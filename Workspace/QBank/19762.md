Question: Willa had been publicly mocked by her coworker, Dex, for months — he would imitate her speech in team meetings and post unflattering photos of her in the break room. One afternoon, Dex loudly called Willa a derogatory name in front of twenty colleagues. Willa, humiliated and enraged, went to her car, retrieved a tire iron, returned, and struck Dex twice in the head. Dex died from the blows. Is Willa guilty of murder or voluntary manslaughter?
Answer A: Voluntary manslaughter, because Dex's sustained harassment gave Willa adequate provocation to lose her self-control
Answer B: Voluntary manslaughter, because Willa was subjectively provoked and acted in the heat of passion
Answer C: Murder, because verbal insults and mockery do not constitute adequate provocation at common law
Answer D: Murder, but only because Willa retrieved the tire iron from her car, which demonstrates premeditation
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Voluntary manslaughter requires: (1) adequate provocation, (2) actual provocation (defendant was in fact provoked), (3) no cooling-off period, and (4) a causal connection between the provocation and the killing. The critical element missing here is adequate provocation. At common law, words alone — including insults, mockery, and verbal abuse, however sustained — are not legally sufficient to constitute adequate provocation. The conduct must be of a type that would cause a reasonable person to lose self-control. Willa's killing is murder because the provocation is legally insufficient.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The adequate provocation standard is objective — would the provocation cause a reasonable person to lose self-control to the point of killing? Verbal abuse, mockery, name-calling, and insults, however sustained or humiliating, do not satisfy the objective reasonable person standard for adequate provocation at common law. Words alone are generally insufficient to reduce murder to manslaughter. B: Voluntary manslaughter uses an objective, not subjective, standard for adequate provocation. The question is whether a reasonable person would have been provoked to the point of losing self-control, not whether this particular defendant was. Willa's subjective emotional state, however severe, does not substitute for objectively adequate provocation. D: The correct reason for murder is that verbal abuse is insufficient provocation, making the manslaughter reduction unavailable. Whether the car retrieval constitutes premeditation for first-degree murder is a separate (and harder) question — it might, but D's framing is both incomplete (ignoring the provocation analysis) and potentially overbroad. The primary murder theory here rests on inadequate provocation, not definitively on premeditation.
lb_rule_reference: Voluntary manslaughter requires adequate provocation that would cause a reasonable person to lose self-control; the standard is objective (reasonable person), not purely subjective; minor insults or petty slights are legally insufficient
