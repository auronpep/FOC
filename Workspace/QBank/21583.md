MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Homicide
Outline: Murder
Question: During Bruno's murder trial, after the jury was sworn and two witnesses had testified, a juror told the judge she had seen news coverage of the case and discussed it with her husband. Defense counsel moved to excuse the juror and continue with eleven jurors, which was permitted under state law. The prosecutor opposed this proposal and moved for a mistrial. The judge granted the mistrial over Bruno's objection, reasoning that the trial was easier to redo than to fix. Bruno moves to bar retrial on double jeopardy grounds. Is retrial barred?
Answer A: No, because any juror misconduct that threatens the fairness of a trial constitutes manifest necessity for a mistrial
Answer B: No, because jeopardy had not yet attached when the mistrial was declared since only two witnesses had testified
Answer C: Yes, because the court declared a mistrial over the defendant's objection without manifest necessity — a viable alternative (proceeding with eleven jurors) existed and was not adequately considered
Answer D: Yes, because jeopardy attached when the jury was sworn, and any subsequent mistrial over the defendant's objection automatically bars retrial regardless of the reason
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Jeopardy attached when the jury was sworn. Once jeopardy attaches, a mistrial declared over the defendant's objection bars retrial unless there was manifest necessity. Manifest necessity requires the judge to seriously consider all reasonable alternatives before ending the trial. Here, state law permitted proceeding with eleven jurors, and defense counsel affirmatively proposed that solution. The judge's stated reason — that retrial was easier — does not satisfy the demanding manifest necessity standard. Retrial is barred.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Manifest necessity is a demanding standard. Juror misconduct may constitute manifest necessity in some circumstances, but the court must first consider reasonable alternatives before declaring a mistrial. Here, a viable alternative existed: excusing the juror and proceeding with eleven jurors (permitted under state law). If the court failed to consider that alternative, declaring a mistrial was not manifestly necessary, and retrial is barred. B: In a jury trial, jeopardy attaches when the jury is sworn — not when witnesses begin testifying. The problem states the jury was sworn before the misconduct arose. Jeopardy had already attached before any witnesses testified, so the attachment question is resolved in Bruno's favor. The issue is whether the mistrial was supported by manifest necessity. D: A mistrial declared over the defendant's objection bars retrial only when manifest necessity was absent. If manifest necessity existed (e.g., a juror became genuinely incapacitated with no viable alternatives), retrial is permitted even if the defendant objected. The result here is correct, but the reasoning that any mistrial over objection bars retrial is an overstatement that would generate wrong answers on other facts.
lb_rule_reference: When a judge declares a mistrial over the defendant's objection, retrial is permitted only if there was manifest necessity for the mistrial; absent manifest necessity, retrial is barred by double jeopardy.
