Question: A buyer agreed in writing to purchase a car from a seller for $15,000, with the price to be paid on a specified date at the seller's home. The contract provided, and both parties intended, that time was of the essence. Before the specified date, however, the seller sold the car to a third party for $18,000. On the specified date, the buyer arrived at the seller's home prepared to tender payment. The seller was not there, so the buyer called the seller to ask where he was. The seller then told the buyer that he had sold the car to the third party. If the buyer sues the seller for breach of contract, will the buyer be likely to prevail?
Answer A: A: No, because the contractual obligations were discharged on the ground of impossibility.
Answer B: B: No, because the buyer did not tender her performance on the specified date
Answer C: C: Yes, because the seller did not inform the buyer of his repudiation.
Answer D: D: Yes, because the seller anticipatorily repudiated the contract when he sold the car to the third party.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: D is correct. Selling the car to the third party was an action that made the seller's performance under the contract impossible, which amounted to an implied anticipatory repudiation, which gives the buyer an immediate claim for breach of contract. A
Wrong Answer Explanations: A is incorrect. Impossibility is unavailable to the seller as a defense here, as his actions were his own doing, not the result of some unforeseen outside occurrence that rendered him unable to perform. He made his own ability to perform impossible by selling the car to a third party, in breach of the contract with the buyer. B is incorrect. The buyer's duty to perform was discharged when the seller anticipatorily repudiated. C is incorrect. The seller's failure to inform the buyer is irrelevant; the act of selling the car alone constituted an implied anticipatory repudiation, which is why the buyer is entitled to sue for breach.
