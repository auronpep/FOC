Outline: Executive immunity
Question: While in office, the President directs an agency head to remove a federal appointee from a policy role. The appointee sues the President personally for money damages, alleging that the removal was motivated by political retaliation. The President asserts absolute immunity from the civil damages claim. How is the immunity claim most likely resolved?
Answer A: The President is likely immune because the challenged act was within the outer perimeter of official presidential duties
Answer B: The President is not immune because allegations of bad motive always defeat official-act immunity
Answer C: The President is immune only if Congress has enacted a statute granting immunity
Answer D: The President is not immune because agency heads, rather than the President, usually carry out removals
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Directing executive personnel decisions is an official presidential function. Absolute immunity protects the President from civil damages liability for official acts, even when the plaintiff alleges improper motive.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Official-act immunity would be undermined if plaintiffs could defeat it merely by alleging improper motive. The threshold is whether the conduct falls within official responsibility. C: Presidential official-act civil immunity is a constitutional separation-of-powers doctrine, not dependent on a specific immunity statute. D: Directing executive personnel decisions remains within the outer perimeter of presidential responsibility.
lb_rule_reference: The President has absolute immunity from civil damages liability for official acts within the outer perimeter of presidential responsibility.
