


BARMATRIX Q#: 14277
Outline_code: 44040502
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Constitutional Law
Subtopic: Individual Rights
Outline: Freedom of speech and expression
Question: A group of students at a state university's law school wished to debate the future of affirmative action in that state and at that law school. For this debate, they requested the use of a meeting room in the law school that is available on a first-come, first-served basis for extracurricular student use. Speakers presenting all sides of the issue were scheduled to participate. The law school administration denied the use of its meeting room for this purpose solely because it believed that “such a debate, even if balanced, would have a negative effect on the morale of the law school community and might cause friction among the students that would disrupt the institution's educational mission.” Is the law school's denial of the use of its meeting room for this purpose constitutional?
Answer A: A: No, because the law school cannot demonstrate that its action was necessary to serve a compelling state interest.
Answer B: B: No, because the law school cannot demonstrate that its action was rationally related to a legitimate state interest.
Answer C: C: Yes, because the law school's only concern was the adverse effect of such a discussion of affirmative action on the immediate audience and the mission of the institution.
Answer D: D: Yes, because the law students do not have a right to use a state-owned law school facility for a meeting that is not organized and sponsored by the law school itself.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A is correct. As a threshold determination, the law school's regulation is content-based because it is aimed at the communicative impact of the speech. This means it must pass strict scrutiny and will be presumptively unconstitutional. The law school would need to prove that its denial was necessary to serve a compelling governmental interest, which it could not achieve here because its goal was merely to avoid "friction" and disrupting the mission.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B is incorrect. As stated above, because the denial of the use of the room was content-based, it is subject to strict scrutiny. Satisfying rational basis scrutiny, as stated in this choice, would be insufficient to justify the school's regulation. C is incorrect. This concern by the law school regarding the effect of the debate on the audience and mission is not, in fact, a sufficient basis for upholding the regulation. Rather, it is not a compelling interest that would withstand the applicable standard of strict scrutiny, as stated above. D is incorrect. This choice references the analysis of whether the law school facility is a limited public forum, which is not necessary to determine and is also a misstatement of the law. A limited public forum may, in fact, be government property that is opened up for a designated purpose, even if organized by an outside, non-government entity. Nevertheless, this analysis is unnecessary because, as explained above, the restriction is content-based, triggering strict scrutiny.
additional_info: On the MBE, when a government restriction on the time, place, and manner of speech is at issue, it is necessary to FIRST make a threshold determination of whether the regulation is content-based or content-neutral. There may be facts in the question related to whether the location would be considered a "public forum," a "limited public forum," or a "non-public forum." However, your analysis will not need to go that far if the regulation is content-based. A regulation is content-based if the government is aiming at the "communicative impact" of the expression, and if it is, it does not matter whether the expression takes place in a public forum or not; in either event, the governmental regulation is presumptively invalid, unless the expression falls in a pre-defined "unprotected category" (which includes, but is not limited to, speech that creates a clear and present danger, constitutes "fighting words," is obscene, defamatory, and so on). Content-based regulation of speech is subject to strict scrutiny, meaning the government bears the burden of showing that the regulation is necessary to serve a compelling state interest and is narrowly drawn to achieve this end. By contrast, if the government is aiming at something other than the communicative impact, the action is considered "content-neutral," even if it has the effect of burdening expression. Content-neutral regulations require a subsequent determination of whether the forum is public, limited public, or non-public. A is correct. In this case, the law school denied the use of its meeting room solely because it believed that "such a debate, even if balanced, would have a negative effect on the morale of the law school community and might cause friction among the students that would disrupt the institution's educational mission." Applying the definition of "content- based," the school was aiming at the "communicative impact" of the expression. This means that the regulation is content-based, which triggers strict scrutiny and will be presumptively invalid. To satisfy strict scrutiny, the law school would have to prove that its denial was necessary to serve a compelling governmental interest. It is unusual for the courts to uphold content-based speech restrictions under strict scrutiny, and the law school's concerns about causing "friction" and disrupting the mission are clearly insufficient to meet that test. As such, the law school's denial of the meeting room to the student group violates the Free Speech Clause of the First Amendment. B is incorrect. This answer reaches the correct answer with the wrong reasoning. The law school's action is unconstitutional because it is content-based, as discussed above, and is presumptively unconstitutional. It is an incorrect application of the law to assert that the school need only satisfy rational basis review, which is the standard laid out in this answer choice. C is incorrect. The fact that the law school's only concern was the adverse effect of the discussion of affirmative action on the immediate audience and the mission of the institution is NOT a sufficient basis for finding the regulation constitutional. On the contrary, this stated interest is why the regulation is presumptively unconstitutional. As explained above, because the law school's denial of the room was based on the content of the student group's expression, the denial is a presumptive violation of the Free Speech Clause, and it must pass strict scrutiny. This rather weak interest is not enough to be considered "compelling" under strict scrutiny. D is incorrect. This answer choice is related to whether the students' intended use of the law school facility would be considered a limited public forum. First, it is a misstatement of the scope of a limited public forum. The usage of a limited public forum does not necessitate collaboration with the government entity who owns it. Limited public forums may be government property opened up for a specific purpose, but this specific purpose does not need to be organized by the government who owns the property. Second, as explained above, it is not necessary for the analysis to reach whether the forum is limited public or not; rather, the fact that the regulation is content-based triggers strict scrutiny, which is not met here.
