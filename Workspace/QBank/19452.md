Outline: Freedom of speech and expression
Question: A city school district opens its middle-school cafeterias on weekday evenings for use by "community groups engaged in educational programming for school-age youth." The district has approved scouts, tutoring organizations, arts groups, and after-school academic clubs. A local church applies to use a cafeteria for an after-school program that teaches character formation and ethical decision-making through Biblical stories. The district denies the application on the ground that the program "advances religious viewpoints and is therefore inappropriate for a school facility." The district continues to allow secular character-education programs that teach ethics without any religious content. Is the district's denial of the church's application constitutional?
Answer A: Yes, because school property is a nonpublic forum and the district may restrict use to nonreligious groups on any rational basis
Answer B: Yes, because the Establishment Clause requires the district to exclude religious organizations from school facilities
Answer C: No, because the district's denial discriminates against the church's religious viewpoint while permitting secular programs on the same educational topic
Answer D: No, because the district's cafeteria qualifies as a traditional public forum and strict scrutiny invalidates the religion-based exclusion
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The district created a limited public forum for community educational programming for youth. Character formation and ethical decision-making are within the forum's permitted scope. Permitting secular ethics programs while barring the same topic taught from a religious viewpoint is classic viewpoint discrimination under Good News Club v. Milford Central School. Viewpoint discrimination is unconstitutional in any forum, including a limited public forum.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The district opened the cafeteria to a broad class of community groups for educational youth programming. That deliberate opening creates a limited public forum. In a limited forum, the government may set subject-matter limits consistent with the forum's purpose, but it cannot engage in viewpoint discrimination. Allowing secular ethics instruction while banning identical instruction from a religious perspective is viewpoint discrimination, not a subject-matter restriction. B: The Supreme Court has held that permitting religious groups to use government facilities on the same terms as secular groups does not violate the Establishment Clause when the government is not actively endorsing religion and access is neutral. Denying religious groups equal access to a limited public forum to avoid Establishment Clause problems would itself violate the Free Speech Clause when secular equivalents are allowed. D: Traditional public forums are places with a long history of public assembly and debate — streets, parks, and similar spaces. A school cafeteria opened by administrative policy is a limited public forum. The result (unconstitutional) is correct, but the reasoning (wrong forum type) is not. On the bar exam, an answer that reaches the right outcome for the wrong doctrinal reason is incorrect.
lb_rule_reference: A limited public forum is government property opened for a specific purpose or class of speakers. Subject-matter restrictions consistent with the forum's purpose are constitutional; viewpoint-based restrictions are not, even within a limited forum.
