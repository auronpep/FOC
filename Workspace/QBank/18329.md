Question: Felix was arrested as a suspect in a forgery scheme. A grand jury issued a subpoena requiring Felix to (1) provide a handwriting exemplar, (2) give a blood sample for DNA testing, and (3) appear before the grand jury and answer questions about his knowledge of the forged documents. Felix refuses all three demands, claiming his Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination protects him from each. The government moves to compel. Which of Felix's refusals is protected by the Fifth Amendment?
Answer A: All three refusals are protected because each disclosure would incriminate Felix
Answer B: Only the handwriting exemplar is protected because handwriting is a form of personal expression
Answer C: Only the obligation to answer grand jury questions about his knowledge is protected by the Fifth Amendment, because that demands testimonial/communicative responses; the handwriting exemplar and blood sample are physical evidence not covered by the privilege
Answer D: The blood sample is protected because it is drawn from Felix's body without his consent, violating his bodily autonomy and the Fifth Amendment
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination applies only to testimonial or communicative evidence — that is, evidence that reveals the contents of the suspect's mind. Under *Schmerber v. California* (1966), blood samples are physical evidence not protected by the Fifth Amendment. Under *Gilbert v. California* (1967), handwriting exemplars are physical characteristics not testimonial in nature. In contrast, compelling Felix to testify before the grand jury about his knowledge directly compels testimonial evidence. That demand is protected by the Fifth Amendment (absent a grant of immunity). The government may compel the exemplar and blood sample but not compelled testimony.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Fifth Amendment protects only testimonial or communicative evidence — the expression of the contents of the mind. It does not protect against compelled production of physical characteristics or biological samples. Handwriting exemplars and blood samples are physical evidence, not testimonial acts. Only the grand jury testimony implicates the Fifth Amendment. B: The Supreme Court in *Gilbert v. California* (1967) held that handwriting exemplars are not testimonial for Fifth Amendment purposes. The government is not compelling Felix to convey what is in his mind — it is requiring him to produce a physical characteristic (his handwriting) for comparison. The communicative content of the exemplar (what it says) is irrelevant; the physical characteristic of the strokes is what is tested. Handwriting exemplars are not protected. D: Schmerber v. California* (1966) squarely held that a compelled blood draw does not violate the Fifth Amendment because blood is physical, not testimonial, evidence. Bodily integrity concerns may implicate the Fourth Amendment (search and seizure analysis), but the Fifth Amendment privilege does not extend to physical characteristics extracted from the body. The two amendments address different interests.
lb_rule_reference: The Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination protects only testimonial or communicative evidence — the expression of the contents of the mind; it does not protect against compelled production of physical or biological evidence such as blood samples, handwriting exemplars, voice samples, or DNA.
