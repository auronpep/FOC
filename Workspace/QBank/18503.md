Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Discretionary exclusion (undue prejudice, confusion, waste of time)
Question: Aaron is charged in federal court with unlawful possession of a firearm by a person previously convicted of a felony. Aaron offers to stipulate that he has a qualifying prior felony conviction. The prosecutor instead seeks to introduce a certified judgment showing that Aaron's prior conviction was for armed robbery, with details of the sentence. How should the court rule on the judgment details?
Answer A: Admit the full judgment because the prosecutor is always entitled to prove every element in the most dramatic way.
Answer B: Exclude the nature and details of the prior conviction if the stipulation supplies the status element and the remaining probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice.
Answer C: Admit the judgment only to prove Aaron's bad character.
Answer D: Exclude all evidence of the prior conviction, including the fact of a qualifying conviction, because prior convictions are never relevant.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: When the only disputed point is status as a person with a qualifying conviction and the defendant offers to stipulate, the name and details of the prior offense may add little probative value while creating a serious risk of unfair prejudice. The court may require a less prejudicial means of proving the status element.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because Rule 403 permits the court to consider probative need and unfair prejudice. C: C is wrong because bad-character proof is not the permissible use here. D: D is wrong because the fact of a qualifying conviction remains relevant to the status element.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 403
lb_student_diagnostic: Student ignores how a stipulation can reduce probative need in 403 balancing.
