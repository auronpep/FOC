Question: Sarah signed a one-year agreement with a glass supplier to buy “all tempered glass required for Sarah's storefront division,” with an estimate of 18,000 panes. Four months later, a new safety regulation made the storefront division unprofitable, and Sarah permanently closed that division. She bought no tempered glass from any other supplier for storefront work. The supplier sued for lost profit on the remaining estimated panes. Which statement is most accurate?
Answer A: The supplier wins because the estimate is treated as a minimum quantity promise.
Answer B: Sarah may have zero requirements if she closed the covered division in good faith and did not shift the same requirements elsewhere.
Answer C: The supplier wins unless Sarah proves impossibility or frustration of purpose.
Answer D: The court may not consider Sarah's shutdown evidence because it varies the written estimate under the parol evidence rule.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: A requirements quantity is measured by the buyer's actual good-faith requirements, subject to stated estimates and normal or comparable requirements. The trigger facts are the stated estimate, the division-specific scope, the permanent good-faith shutdown, and the absence of substitute purchases from another supplier. The decisive lock is quantity/good faith: the estimate is not automatically a minimum promise, and a good-faith shutdown can reduce actual requirements to zero.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because an estimate helps police disproportionate quantity changes but is not automatically a minimum purchase promise. C: C is wrong because the supplier's claim turns on the requirements term itself, not on excuse doctrines. D: D is wrong because the shutdown occurred after signing and measures performance under the requirements term; it does not vary the estimate as parol evidence.
lb_rule_reference: UCC 2-306(1) / requirements quantity; good-faith zero requirements and stated estimate
