Question: A restaurant supplier sent a letter to a regular customer offering to sell the customer an industrial freezer for $10,000. Two days later, the customer responded with a letter that stated: "I accept your offer on the condition that you provide me with a warranty that the freezer is merchantable." In response to the customer's letter, the supplier called the customer and stated that the offer was no longer open. The supplier promptly sold the freezer to another buyer for $11,000. If the customer sues the supplier for breach of contract, is the customer likely to prevail?
Answer A: A: No, because the customer's letter added a term, making it a counteroffer.
Answer B: B: No, because the subsequent sale to a bona fide purchaser for value cut off the claims of the customer.
Answer C: C: Yes, because the customer's letter was an acceptance of the supplier's offer, since the warranty of merchantability was already implied in the sale.
Answer D: D: Yes, because the supplier's letter was a firm offer that could not be revoked for a reasonable time.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: C is correct. An enforceable contract was formed when the customer mailed the acceptance to the supplier. The customer's "condition" of a warranty of merchantability was not an additional or different term that turned the acceptance into a counteroffer because that warranty is implied in every contract for the sale of goods by a merchant seller of that kind of good. The supplier had no right to call the customer and revoke the offer that had already been accepted. A
Wrong Answer Explanations: A is incorrect. The customer's letter did not add a term because the "conditional" term was already implied in the offer under UCC § 2-314, and thus the acceptance letter formed a valid contract. B is incorrect. The subsequent sale to the bona fide purchaser (BFP) would have to bring a separate action in order to contest the customer's claim of right to the freezer. This question is asking about the suit between the customer and the supplier, so the BFP's rights are not at issue. D is incorrect. The supplier's letter did not create a firm offer because it failed to give assurance that the offer would be held open, a principal requirement of a firm offer under UCC § 2-205.
