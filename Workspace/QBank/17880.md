Question: A runaway train is barreling toward five workers trapped on the main track. Conductor Phoebe discovers she can divert the train to a side track, but a single maintenance worker, Leo, is on that track and would certainly be killed. Phoebe cannot stop the train in time. She diverts it, killing Leo and saving five lives. Phoebe is charged with murder. Under the common law, is necessity a valid defense to Phoebe's murder charge?
Answer A: Yes, because five lives saved outweigh one life lost — classic lesser evil
Answer B: Yes, because Phoebe did not intend to kill Leo — she intended to save five lives
Answer C: No, because necessity is not a defense to intentional homicide at common law, even to save more lives
Answer D: No, because Phoebe had a duty as conductor to stop the train, which she failed to discharge
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Common law imposes a categorical bar: necessity cannot justify the intentional killing of an innocent person. The law does not allow defendants — even well-intentioned ones — to deliberately take one innocent life to preserve others. The doctrine holds that courts cannot sanction the sacrifice of a person who has done nothing to create the danger. Phoebe knowingly caused Leo's death by her deliberate act. The defense of necessity fails, and she is guilty of murder under the common law. Note: the MPC takes a different approach and might permit such a defense under the choice-of-evils provision depending on jurisdiction; this question applies common law.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The common law refuses to permit necessity as a defense to the intentional killing of an innocent person, regardless of the arithmetic of lives. Necessity requires that the harm caused be lesser than the harm avoided, but it does not authorize the deliberate sacrifice of an innocent life. The rule is categorical, not subject to case-by-case utilitarian balancing. B: Phoebe knew with certainty that diverting the train would kill Leo. Under common-law murder doctrine, knowing (virtually certain) causation of death satisfies the intent element — it does not matter that her primary goal was to save others. The killing was intentional in the legally relevant sense. The necessity bar on homicide applies. D: The failure-of-duty analysis is not the reason necessity fails here. The necessity defense is defeated by the categorical bar on homicide, not by a separate negligence or duty theory. Framing liability in terms of a duty breach is a distraction from the controlling necessity doctrine.
lb_rule_reference: Necessity does not justify intentional homicide at common law; the doctrine requires that the harm caused be less than the harm avoided, but the law does not permit deliberately killing an innocent person even to save multiple lives
