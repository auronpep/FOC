Outline: Materiality and probative value
Question: Ruth sues David after a delivery truck struck her bicycle. Ruth claims the collision caused persistent wrist pain. Ruth offers testimony from her tennis partner, Naomi, that Ruth played tennis twice a week for years before the collision and never complained of wrist pain. David objects that Ruth may have injured her wrist later while gardening. Assuming Naomi has personal knowledge of Ruth's tennis activity, how should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the testimony because Naomi is not a physician.
Answer B: Exclude the testimony because the later gardening possibility makes the tennis testimony speculative.
Answer C: Admit the testimony because Ruth's lack of wrist complaints before the collision has some tendency to support causation; the later gardening theory goes to weight.
Answer D: Admit the testimony only if Ruth first proves by a preponderance that she did not injure her wrist while gardening.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Ruth's prior condition is a fact that can make collision causation more probable. The testimony does not have to rule out every later cause. Alternative-cause arguments affect weight and sufficiency, not the threshold relevance of the before-condition evidence.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because lay testimony from personal knowledge of Ruth's prior condition can be relevant even if medical causation ultimately requires expert proof. B: B is wrong because a possible later cause weakens the inference but does not eliminate it. D: D is wrong because relevance does not require Ruth to disprove every alternative cause before admission.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401 / FRE 402
lb_student_diagnostic: Treating alternative cause as a relevance bar instead of a weight argument.
