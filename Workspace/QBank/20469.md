Outline: Expectation damages
Question: Peter hired Sarah to build a booth for a fair. At the time of contracting, Peter told Sarah that he planned to use the booth to launch a new product and hoped to make “a large profit.” Sarah breached. Peter proves that Sarah knew profits were possible, but Peter offers no sales projections, prior sales, comparable market data, customer orders, or reliable estimate of net profit. May Peter recover lost profits?
Answer A: No, because Peter has not proven the lost profits with reasonable certainty.
Answer B: Yes, because Sarah knew at contracting that Peter hoped to profit.
Answer C: Yes, because foreseeability is the only limit on consequential lost profits.
Answer D: No, because lost profits are unavailable unless the breaching party acted in bad faith.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Lost profits require more than foreseeability. Peter has no reliable evidence of amount. Even if Sarah knew profits were possible, Peter still must prove the amount with reasonable certainty.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because foreseeability does not prove amount. C: C is wrong because lost profits must also satisfy certainty, causation, and mitigation limits. D: D is wrong because bad faith is not a general prerequisite to recovering lost profits.
lb_rule_reference: Lost profits / certainty gate distinct from foreseeability
