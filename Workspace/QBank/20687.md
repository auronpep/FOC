Question: Nolan has a diagnosed mental disease that contributed to his decision to steal office equipment. He knew the equipment belonged to his employer and knew stealing it was illegal. The jurisdiction applies only M'Naghten. Nolan argues that his theft was a "product" of mental disease under the Durham test. How should the court treat Nolan's Durham argument?
Answer A: Accept it, because Durham is available whenever mental disease causes crime
Answer B: Accept it, because Durham is broader than M'Naghten
Answer C: Reject it, because M'Naghten is the supplied test and Nolan knew the nature and wrongfulness of the theft
Answer D: Reject it because insanity is unavailable for theft
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The jurisdiction applies only M'Naghten. Nolan knew he was taking another's property and knew the act was illegal. Durham's product-of-mental-disease formulation is unavailable, so the insanity defense fails.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A defendant cannot select an insanity test not adopted by the jurisdiction. M'Naghten controls here. B: Broadness is irrelevant if the jurisdiction has not adopted the test. D: Insanity can apply to theft if the jurisdictional test is met. Nolan loses because the supplied M'Naghten test is not satisfied.
lb_rule_reference: The insanity test is jurisdiction-specific; Durham/product language does not apply unless the jurisdiction adopts it
