Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: FBI agents investigating a string of robberies subpoenaed MetroPCS — without a warrant — for 127 days of historical cell-site location information (CSLI) for a suspect's cell phone. The records showed the suspect's phone near the scene of each robbery and were introduced at trial. The suspect argued the warrantless acquisition of his CSLI violated the Fourth Amendment. Did the warrantless acquisition of 127 days of CSLI violate the suspect's Fourth Amendment rights?
Answer A: No, because the suspect voluntarily disclosed his location data to the cell phone carrier, triggering the third-party doctrine
Answer B: No, because historical records — as opposed to real-time tracking — do not implicate Fourth Amendment privacy interests
Answer C: Yes, because comprehensive CSLI spanning 127 days reveals a detailed chronicle of the suspect's movements and requires a warrant under Carpenter v. United States
Answer D: Yes, because any use of electronic surveillance requires a warrant under the Fourth Amendment
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Carpenter v. United States held that the government violates the Fourth Amendment when it accesses historical CSLI covering seven or more days without a warrant. Unlike bank records or pen registers, CSLI provides an all-encompassing record of the user's movements. The Court declined to extend the third-party doctrine to this category of digital information. 127 days of CSLI far exceeds the Carpenter threshold, so a warrant was required. The warrantless subpoena was unconstitutional, and the CSLI is subject to suppression.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Carpenter v. United States held that comprehensive CSLI is categorically different from ordinary third-party records. Because cell phones are virtually indispensable and a person does not meaningfully "voluntarily" share detailed location information, the third-party doctrine does not apply. 127 days of CSLI provides a near-perfect chronicle of movements and implicates core privacy interests that the third-party doctrine was never designed to eliminate. B: Carpenter did not limit its holding to real-time tracking. The Court expressly addressed historical CSLI and held that 7 or more days of records constitute a Fourth Amendment search. The 127-day record here is squarely within Carpenter. D: Carpenter's holding is narrow. The Court explicitly preserved Smith v. Maryland (pen registers), United States v. Miller (bank records), and left open shorter-duration CSLI queries. Not all electronic surveillance requires a warrant; the rule applies specifically to comprehensive CSLI of 7+ days.
lb_rule_reference: The government must obtain a warrant to access historical cell-site location information (CSLI) covering seven or more days because it reveals a comprehensive chronicle of the user's movements (Carpenter v. United States)
