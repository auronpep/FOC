Question: A state enacts a statute requiring all voters to present a government-issued photo ID at the polls. Residents without a qualifying ID must obtain a free state voter ID, but the nearest office issuing such IDs is located more than 40 miles away, accessible only during business hours on weekdays. Statistical evidence shows that low-income urban residents — who are disproportionately minorities — are substantially less likely to possess qualifying IDs and substantially less able to travel to the issuing office during business hours. A civil rights organization challenges the voter ID requirement under the Equal Protection Clause. Which Equal Protection framework should a court apply to the voter ID requirement, and what factors are relevant to the analysis?
Answer A: Rational basis, because states have inherent authority to regulate their own elections, and preventing voter fraud is a legitimate state interest that rationally justifies any identification requirement
Answer B: Intermediate scrutiny, because the voter ID law has a disparate impact on racial minority voters but does not classify expressly by race
Answer C: Strict scrutiny, because the right to vote is a fundamental right; the state must show a compelling interest and that the voter ID requirement is narrowly tailored, which requires evaluating whether the burden imposed on non-ID voters is genuinely minimized
Answer D: Strict scrutiny applies, and the voter ID law automatically fails because any restriction on voting that imposes a cost — monetary or logistical — on exercising the franchise is per se unconstitutional
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The right to vote is fundamental under the Equal Protection Clause. A law that substantially burdens the exercise of that right triggers strict scrutiny: the state must show a compelling interest (such as election integrity) and narrow tailoring — meaning the law must minimize the burden on voters who lack IDs. The 40-mile, weekday-only restriction on free ID availability is relevant to whether the law is genuinely narrowly tailored or merely nominally free.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The right to vote is a fundamental right under the Equal Protection Clause. Laws that substantially burden the right to vote trigger strict scrutiny, not rational basis — regardless of the states' traditional authority over elections. The Court has held that the strength of the state's interest in voter integrity must be weighed against the magnitude of the burden imposed on voters. B: The applicable scrutiny tier is determined by the right at stake (voting, a fundamental right) — not by the demographic impact of the law. Because voting is fundamental, strict scrutiny applies regardless of whether the law expressly classifies by race. Intermediate scrutiny does not apply here. D: The Court has not adopted a per se rule that any logistical burden on voting is unconstitutional. Strict scrutiny is applied and the outcome depends on the weight of the state's interest relative to the magnitude of the burden. Some voter ID requirements have been upheld; the analysis is contextual, not categorical.
lb_rule_reference: The right to vote is a fundamental right under the Equal Protection Clause. Laws that impose substantial burdens on the right to vote trigger strict scrutiny, requiring a compelling interest and narrow tailoring.
