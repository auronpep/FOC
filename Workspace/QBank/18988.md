Subtopic: Inchoate Crimes
Outline: Attempt
Question: Nina intended to steal a wallet from the coat pocket of Victor, a man sleeping on a park bench. She reached into Victor's coat pocket and felt around thoroughly, but the pocket was empty — Victor had left his wallet at home that day. Nina did not know the pocket was empty when she reached in. Nina was observed by an off-duty officer and arrested. Nina is charged with attempted theft. She argues that because Victor had no wallet, she could not have completed the theft. What is the most likely result?
Answer A: Nina is not guilty because it was impossible to steal a wallet that did not exist
Answer B: Nina is not guilty because she did not reach the last proximate act
Answer C: Nina is guilty because factual impossibility is not a defense to attempt
Answer D: Nina is guilty only if the prosecution proves she knew the pocket might be empty
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Factual impossibility means the crime was physically impossible due to a circumstance the defendant did not know about at the time of the act. Courts universally hold it is no defense, because the defendant's intent and conduct were exactly what they would have been had the circumstances been as she believed. Nina intended to steal a wallet, performed the acts of stealing, and only failed because of an unknown fact. She is guilty of attempted theft.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Nina's situation is factual impossibility: her plan would have been criminal but for an unknown factual condition (the empty pocket). Factual impossibility is universally rejected as a defense to attempt. Her culpability is the same as if the wallet had been present. B: Even under a narrow last-proximate-act standard, inserting her hand into the pocket and feeling around is the last act before removing the wallet. Regardless, the question turns on impossibility doctrine, not the test for preparation vs. attempt. D: The defense of factual impossibility does not turn on what the defendant knew about whether success was possible. Attempt requires intent to commit the crime, not knowledge that the crime would succeed. Injecting a knowledge-of-impossibility element conflates two separate issues.
lb_rule_reference: Factual impossibility — where the defendant's intended crime is impossible only because of an unknown circumstance of fact — is not a defense to attempt at common law
