Question: In a federal civil fraud action, a party calls the opposing party's current spouse to testify that she saw boxes of inventory moved from one warehouse to another. The spouse refuses to testify, invoking the federal adverse spousal testimonial privilege. No confidential marital statement is involved. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the refusal because current spouses may never testify adversely to each other.
Answer B: Sustain the refusal because any observation made during marriage is privileged.
Answer C: Reject the refusal because the federal adverse spousal testimonial privilege is a criminal-case privilege and the testimony concerns observed conduct, not a marital communication.
Answer D: Reject the refusal only if the parties are legally separated.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The federal adverse spousal testimonial privilege applies in criminal cases. This is a civil action, and the proposed testimony concerns observed conduct rather than a confidential marital communication. The decisive axes are proceeding type and protected object.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because This overstates the testimonial privilege and ignores the criminal-case limitation. B: B is wrong because Observation is not communication. The marital communications privilege protects confidential communications, not everything a spouse sees. D: A is wrong because This overstates the testimonial privilege and ignores the criminal-case limitation. B is wrong because Observation is not communication. The marital communications privilege protects confidential communications, not everything a spouse sees. C is wrong because Correct answer D is wrong because Separation is not the decisive fact in this civil observed-conduct stem.
