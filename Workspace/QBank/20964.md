Question: Mary hired a software developer to build a custom scheduling app. The signed services agreement stated, “Developer may not assign this Agreement without Mary's consent.” The developer later assigned only its right to receive the final payment to a lender, while keeping all coding and support duties. Mary argues that the assignment violated the anti-assignment clause. The lender wants to strengthen its Content/Meaning argument that the clause bars transfer of performance obligations but not assignment of payment rights. Which additional fact best supports the lender's interpretation?
Answer A: The developer told Mary before signing that it sometimes sold invoices to lenders, but the final agreement did not mention invoice sales.
Answer B: The agreement's definitions section stated that “assignment of this Agreement” means transfer of performance duties, while “payment rights may be assigned separately.”
Answer C: Mary would suffer no additional damages if payment were made to the lender instead of the developer.
Answer D: The lender did not sign the services agreement.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that the meaning of an assignment clause must be determined from the agreement before third-party-rights consequences are decided. The trigger facts are the anti-assignment language, the transfer of only payment rights, and the proposed definitions section. The decisive lock is term status: an incorporated or operative definition in the contract directly defines what the clause covers. B most directly supports the lender's meaning route.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is tempting because it concerns negotiations, but an unincorporated prior statement is weaker and may be barred or limited. C is wrong because lack of damages does not define the clause. D is wrong because the lender's signature status does not answer what Mary and the developer's clause means.
