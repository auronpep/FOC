Question: A fire chief reasonably concluded that a fast-moving wildfire would reach a neighborhood unless an abandoned shed at the edge of the woods was destroyed to create a firebreak. The chief ordered firefighters to demolish the shed. The firebreak stopped the fire from spreading to dozens of homes. The shed owner sues the fire chief for trespass and conversion. What is the likely result?
Answer A: The chief is liable because the shed owner did not consent
Answer B: The chief is liable for actual damage because necessity always requires payment
Answer C: The chief is not liable because public necessity privileged the reasonable firebreak action
Answer D: The chief is not liable only because the shed was abandoned
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The chief reasonably destroyed the shed to stop a wildfire threatening a neighborhood. Public necessity supplies a complete privilege under these facts.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Public necessity can privilege reasonable destruction to protect the public from disaster. B: Public necessity is generally a complete privilege when reasonably invoked to avert public disaster. D: The controlling doctrine is public necessity, not merely the shed's low value or abandonment.
lb_rule_reference: Public necessity can provide a complete privilege for reasonable destruction or entry undertaken to avert a public disaster.
