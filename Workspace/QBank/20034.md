Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Under a state statute, a local justice of the peace was paid a $5 fee for each warrant he issued but received nothing for denying warrant applications. Police applied to this justice for a warrant to search a suspect's home for stolen electronics. The justice issued the warrant; police found the electronics. The suspect moves to suppress on the grounds that the issuing magistrate was not neutral and detached. Should the court grant the motion to suppress?
Answer A: No, because the justice of the peace was a judicial officer with authority to issue warrants under state law
Answer B: No, because the $5 fee was a trivial amount that could not realistically influence a judicial officer's decision
Answer C: Yes, because the fee arrangement gave the magistrate a direct financial incentive to issue rather than deny warrants, violating the neutral and detached requirement
Answer D: Yes, because only an Article III federal judge may issue a constitutionally valid warrant
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Connally v. Georgia held that a justice of the peace who receives a fee only upon issuing a warrant does not satisfy the Fourth Amendment's requirement that warrants be issued by a neutral and detached magistrate. The financial incentive — however small — creates a structural bias toward issuance. The warrant here was therefore constitutionally invalid, and the evidence seized under it is subject to suppression (subject to Leon good-faith analysis if applicable on follow-up).
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Statutory authority is not the same as constitutional neutrality. In Connally v. Georgia, the Supreme Court invalidated the same fee arrangement — a justice paid only when he issued a warrant. The financial incentive destroys the neutrality the Fourth Amendment demands, regardless of statutory authorization. B: The Supreme Court in Connally expressly rejected this argument. The constitutional requirement of neutrality is categorical, not proportional. Any direct financial incentive tied to the grant of a warrant — regardless of the dollar amount — is constitutionally impermissible. D: State judicial officers, magistrates, and justices of the peace may issue warrants so long as they are neutral and detached. The Fourth Amendment does not require Article III status. The defect in Connally was the fee arrangement, not the magistrate's judicial rank.
lb_rule_reference: A warrant must be issued by a neutral and detached magistrate; a magistrate who receives a fee only when a warrant is issued has an unconstitutional financial incentive that disqualifies him (Connally v. Georgia)
