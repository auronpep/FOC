Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Authentication and Identification
Question: In a federal extortion case, Sarah received a call from an unknown number. The caller did not state his name, but he knew the exact amount hidden in Sarah's locked chest, the name of her guard, and the private phrase Sarah had used in a prior note to Isaac. The prosecutor claims Isaac made the call. Isaac objects that the call is unauthenticated. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection because the caller did not say he was Isaac.
Answer B: Overrule the objection because the caller's distinctive knowledge and the surrounding circumstances can support a finding that Isaac was the caller.
Answer C: Sustain the objection because a caller's identity can be proved only by phone records.
Answer D: Overrule the objection only if Sarah had previously spoken with Isaac on the phone.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: A call may be authenticated through distinctive characteristics and surrounding circumstances. The caller's knowledge of private details connected to Isaac is circumstantial evidence from which a jury could find identity.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because self-identification is not required if other identifying circumstances exist. C: C is wrong because phone records are not the exclusive method of authentication. D: D is wrong because prior voice familiarity is one route, not the only route.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 901(b)(4)-(6)
lb_student_diagnostic: Single-method trap: student treats one authentication route as mandatory and ignores distinctive knowledge.
