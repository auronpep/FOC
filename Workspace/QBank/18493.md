Question: Ortega was charged with burglary. At the plea hearing, the judge confirmed that Ortega had discussed the plea with counsel, that no promises other than the stated plea bargain had been made, and that Ortega was entering the plea freely. The judge did not inform Ortega that by pleading guilty he was waiving his right to a jury trial, his privilege against self-incrimination, and his right to confront witnesses. Ortega pleaded guilty. On appeal, he argues his plea was invalid. Was Ortega's guilty plea constitutionally valid?
Answer A: Yes, because the judge confirmed the plea was free and voluntary, which satisfies due process
Answer B: Yes, because defense counsel's presence at the plea hearing satisfies the knowing and intelligent requirement
Answer C: No, because the judge failed to advise Ortega on the record of his right to a jury trial, his privilege against self-incrimination, and his right to confront witnesses, as required by *Boykin v. Alabama*
Answer D: No, because the plea was not made in open court and thus lacked the necessary record
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Boykin v. Alabama* (1969) held that a guilty plea constitutes the simultaneous waiver of three fundamental constitutional rights: the right to jury trial, the privilege against self-incrimination, and the right to confront adverse witnesses. For a plea to be constitutionally valid, the record must affirmatively show that the defendant was informed of and understood these specific rights. The judge's generic inquiry into voluntariness, without the required Boykin advisements, renders the plea defective.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Boykin v. Alabama* (1969) requires more than a general voluntariness inquiry. The Court mandated that the record affirmatively show the defendant's knowing waiver of three specific constitutional rights: jury trial, self-incrimination, and confrontation. A general inquiry into voluntariness does not substitute for the Boykin advisements. B: Counsel's presence may satisfy the knowing and intelligent prong in some contexts, but *Boykin* requires an affirmative, on-the-record demonstration that the defendant understood the three rights being waived. The court — not defense counsel — must establish this on the record. Counsel's participation does not substitute for the judge's colloquy. D: The plea was apparently made in open court. The problem is not the setting but the omission of the three specific Boykin advisements. Even a plea taken in open court is invalid if the judge fails to explain the rights being waived.
lb_rule_reference: Under *Boykin v. Alabama*, a valid guilty plea requires the defendant to knowingly, voluntarily, and intelligently waive three constitutional rights: the right to trial by jury, the privilege against self-incrimination, and the right to confront witnesses; the court must make an affirmative showing of these waivers on the record
