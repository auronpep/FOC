Question: In a federal drug prosecution, the government offers a sealed vial. The arresting officer testifies that he sealed the vial and placed his initials on the seal. The lab analyst testifies that she received a sealed vial bearing the officer's initials and that the seal was intact. The custody log is missing one transfer signature. The defendant objects that the missing signature makes the vial inadmissible. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the vial because every link in the custody log must be proved by testimony.
Answer B: Exclude the vial unless the government proves beyond doubt that no tampering occurred.
Answer C: Admit the vial if the court finds the chain evidence sufficient; the missing signature affects weight absent a serious tampering showing.
Answer D: Admit the vial only if the defendant handled the vial and can identify it.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Physical evidence must be authenticated with evidence sufficient to support a finding that it is what the proponent claims. A minor chain-of-custody gap usually affects weight, not admissibility, when the item is sealed, marked, and otherwise connected to the case.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because every link need not be proved by live testimony. B: B is wrong because the foundation standard is not proof beyond doubt. D: D is wrong because defendant identification is not required.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 901 / chain of custody gaps and weight
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests chain-of-custody over-demand and the weight/admissibility distinction.
