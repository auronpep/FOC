Question: Mary sues John in federal court for civil fraud. John properly invokes the Fifth Amendment when asked whether he created false invoices. Mary asks the court to instruct the jury that it may, but need not, draw an adverse inference from John's refusal in this civil case. How should the court rule?
Answer A: The court may permit an adverse inference because this is a civil case and John is a party invoking the privilege in response to probative evidence.
Answer B: The court must dismiss Mary's case because John invoked a constitutional right.
Answer C: The court must forbid any adverse inference in every proceeding whenever the Fifth Amendment is invoked.
Answer D: The court may compel John to answer without immunity because the case is civil.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A valid Fifth Amendment invocation can prevent compelled testimony, but in a civil case the factfinder may be permitted to draw an adverse inference against a party. The decisive axis is privilege validity versus civil evidentiary consequence.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because The invocation does not automatically end the civil case. C: C is wrong because This imports the criminal no-comment rule into civil litigation. D: D is wrong because Civil posture does not eliminate the privilege where the answer is testimonial and incriminating.
lb_rule_reference: Fifth Amendment / Baxter civil adverse inference
lb_student_diagnostic: Student is assuming a valid invocation has no civil consequence or, conversely, that civil posture destroys the privilege.
