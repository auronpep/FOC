Outline: Constitutional limitations and justiciability
Question: A federal agency publishes nonbinding guidance suggesting that states consider stricter inspection standards for private clinics. Several states later enact their own inspection laws. A clinic in one of those states sues the federal agency, seeking an injunction against the guidance. The state law, not the federal guidance, imposes the clinic's compliance costs, and state officials are not parties. What is the strongest standing problem?
Answer A: The clinic lacks standing because its injury is not fairly traceable to the federal agency when independent state laws impose the costs
Answer B: The clinic has standing because the federal agency politically influenced the states
Answer C: The clinic has standing because a federal court can order the states to ignore their laws
Answer D: The clinic lacks standing only because clinic regulation is a political question
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The clinic's compliance burden flows from state statutes enacted by independent state officials. Nonbinding federal guidance is too attenuated to establish traceability on these facts.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Article III traceability requires more than political influence when independent third parties make the legally operative decisions. C: An injunction against the federal agency would not bind nonparty state officials or repeal state statutes. D: The standing defect is traceability and redressability, not judicially unmanageable subject matter.
lb_rule_reference: Traceability requires the plaintiff's injury to be fairly traceable to the defendant, not to independent choices of third parties not before the court.
