MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Negligence
Outline: Affirmative duties to act
Question: A jogger at a public park saw a stranger fall from a paddleboard into a shallow lake and struggle. The jogger had a working phone and could have called for help but instead kept running. The jogger did not cause the fall, did not begin a rescue, and had no relationship with the stranger. The stranger sues the jogger for negligence. Is the stranger likely to prevail in a negligence claim against the jogger for failing to help?
Answer A: Yes, because a reasonable person would have called for help
Answer B: No, because the jogger was a mere bystander with no recognized duty-triggering exception
Answer C: Yes, because failing to call after seeing danger is always negligence
Answer D: No, because negligence never applies to omissions
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The jogger did not create the peril, undertake a rescue, induce reliance, violate a statute, or have a special relationship with the stranger. The general no-duty-to-rescue rule controls.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Negligence first requires a legal duty. A mere bystander generally has no affirmative duty to rescue a stranger without an exception. C: Common-law negligence does not impose a universal duty to rescue or report merely because the defendant sees danger. D: Omissions can support negligence when an affirmative duty exists. The jogger wins because no exception created that duty here.
lb_rule_reference: A mere bystander generally has no duty to rescue unless a recognized exception, such as risk creation, undertaking, special relationship, statute, or reliance, applies.
