Question: Defendant's first filing in federal court is a motion that says: "Dismiss under Rule 12(b)(2) for lack of personal jurisdiction. Alternatively, dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6) because the complaint fails to state a claim." Plaintiff argues that by making an alternative merits argument, Defendant submitted to the court's personal jurisdiction. How should the court treat the personal-jurisdiction objection?
Answer A: Treat it as waived because Defendant argued the merits in the same motion
Answer B: Treat it as preserved because Defendant raised lack of personal jurisdiction in the first Rule 12 motion
Answer C: Treat it as waived unless Defendant also challenged subject-matter jurisdiction
Answer D: Treat it as preserved only if Defendant files a separate special-appearance motion before any Rule 12 motion
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: A defendant may combine Rule 12 defenses and alternative arguments without waiving a properly included Rule 12(b)(2) objection.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Rule 12 allows defenses to be joined in one motion. Including PJ in the first Rule 12 motion preserves it. C: Preserving PJ does not require a subject-matter jurisdiction objection. D: In federal practice, Rule 12 permits defenses to be consolidated in one motion.
lb_rule_reference: A defendant preserves a personal-jurisdiction objection by including it in the first Rule 12 motion; adding alternative merits or pleading arguments in that same motion does not waive the PJ objection.
