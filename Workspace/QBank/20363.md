Question: Peter hired a musician to perform at a birthday dinner for Peter's mother, Hannah. The written contract stated that the performance was “for Hannah” and required the musician to take requests from Hannah during the dinner. The musician canceled without excuse. Hannah sued the musician for breach of the contract. Is Hannah likely to have enforcement rights?
Answer A: Yes, because the contract identified Hannah as the direct recipient of the promised performance.
Answer B: No, because only Peter paid the musician.
Answer C: No, because Hannah was not a party to the hiring contract.
Answer D: Yes, but only if Peter cannot sue.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Hannah is an intended beneficiary. The promised performance was expressly for her, and the contract gave her a direct role as the performance recipient. Her lack of consideration or party status does not bar enforcement.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because consideration can move from Peter even if Hannah enforces. C: C is wrong because an intended third-party beneficiary is not a party. D: D is wrong because Hannah's rights do not depend on Peter's inability to sue.
lb_rule_reference: Common law third-party beneficiary — performance to named recipient
