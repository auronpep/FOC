Question: Darius was charged with possession of cocaine with intent to distribute. The state's key evidence was a certificate of analysis signed by a forensic chemist stating that the substance tested was cocaine. The prosecution introduced the certificate through a police officer who had submitted the substance to the lab. The chemist did not testify. Defense counsel objected on Confrontation Clause grounds. The trial judge admitted the certificate, reasoning that laboratory reports are business records and therefore not testimonial. Was the admission of the certificate a Confrontation Clause violation?
Answer A: No, because laboratory reports are business records, which are a firmly rooted hearsay exception
Answer B: No, because the defendant had the opportunity to subpoena the chemist and chose not to do so
Answer C: Yes, because the certificate of analysis is a testimonial statement and the defendant had no opportunity to cross-examine the chemist
Answer D: Yes, but only because the report contained the chemist's opinion, which is inherently more prejudicial than factual testimony
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, certificates of analysis prepared for use in a criminal prosecution are testimonial statements under *Crawford*. The chemist, as a "witness" against the defendant, must appear and testify unless the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine. No such opportunity existed here. Admitting the certificate without the analyst's testimony violated the Sixth Amendment Confrontation Clause.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts* expressly rejected this argument. A lab report prepared specifically to be used at a criminal trial is testimonial because it was made for the primary purpose of establishing a fact at prosecution. The fact that it also resembles a business record does not change its testimonial character. B: The Confrontation Clause gives the defendant the right to cross-examine the witness during the prosecution's case-in-chief. The prosecution bears the burden of producing the analyst; it is not the defendant's obligation to bring an adverse witness. Defendant's failure to subpoena the chemist does not satisfy the Confrontation Clause requirement. D: The Confrontation Clause analysis under *Melendez-Diaz* does not turn on whether the analyst's findings are opinions or facts. The violation is the admission of a testimonial document without the opportunity to cross-examine the declarant. The opinion-versus-fact distinction is irrelevant to the constitutional question.
lb_rule_reference: Under Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts, a forensic analyst's laboratory report or certificate of analysis is a testimonial statement; it may not be admitted without the analyst testifying unless the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine
