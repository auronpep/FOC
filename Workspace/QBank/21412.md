Subtopic: Inchoate Crimes
Outline: Attempt
Question: Troy planned to burglarize a house he had been watching for weeks. He broke a rear window and crawled inside, at which point he heard the homeowner's car pull into the driveway. Fearing he would be caught, Troy quickly climbed back out the window and fled without taking anything. He was apprehended two blocks away. Troy testifies that he "had a change of heart" once he heard the car. Troy is charged with attempted burglary. He raises voluntary abandonment as a defense. Under the majority common-law view, is the defense available?
Answer A: Yes, because Troy left before taking any property
Answer B: Yes, because Troy testified he had a genuine change of heart
Answer C: No, because Troy's withdrawal was prompted by fear of detection, not genuine renunciation
Answer D: No, because abandonment is never a defense to attempt at common law
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Voluntary abandonment requires that the defendant freely renounce the criminal purpose for reasons not arising from external obstacles, increased difficulty, or fear of apprehension. Troy withdrew because he heard the homeowner return — a classic external obstacle. The majority common-law rule and the MPC both deny the defense when retreat is motivated by fear of detection or changed circumstances rather than a true change of heart. Troy's "change of heart" testimony is contradicted by the objective timing.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The completed crime (burglary) is the breaking and entering with intent, not the taking. The attempt was completed once Troy broke in with intent to steal. Leaving before taking property does not undo the attempt. And the withdrawal itself was caused by external circumstances, not genuine renunciation. B: Courts look to objective circumstances, not self-serving testimony. Troy heard the car pull up immediately before leaving. The timing makes clear the withdrawal was triggered by fear of detection, not a true change of purpose. Self-serving characterization of motivation does not transform an involuntary withdrawal into a voluntary one. D: Many common-law jurisdictions do recognize voluntary abandonment as a defense. The rule is not that abandonment is categorically unavailable; it is that only voluntary, complete abandonment qualifies. This answer is an overbroad statement of the common-law position.
lb_rule_reference: Abandonment is a defense to attempt only if voluntary and complete — meaning the defendant renounced the criminal purpose for reasons unrelated to external obstacles or fear of detection, not merely because success became more difficult or risky
