


BARMATRIX Q#: 16180
Outline_code: 75090400
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Right to Counsel
Question: A defendant was convicted of fraud after a jury trial in state court. The conviction was affirmed on direct appeal. The defendant timely moved for postconviction relief under the Sixth Amendment on the ground that his attorney had provided ineffective assistance of counsel. The trial judge, after a hearing, found that the attorney had performed deficiently by failing to raise a proper objection that would have resulted in exclusion of important prosecution evidence. What more, if anything, must the trial court find in order to sustain the defendant's Sixth Amendment claim?
Answer A: A: Nothing more, because the unjustifiable failure to object to important prosecution evidence is structural error.
Answer B: B: That the attorney was court-appointed and not privately retained.
Answer C: C: That there is a reasonable probability that the trial's outcome would have been different if the attorney had objected.
Answer D: D: That there is clear and convincing evidence that the trial's outcome would have been different if the attorney had objected.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: (C)is the best response, because this “reasonable probability” formulation correctly captures the “prejudice” element of the Strickland standard. The key case setting the standard for deciding Sixth Amendment ineffective-assistance-of-counsel (“IAC”) claims is Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984). There, the court held that to be entitled to a new trial on IAC grounds, the defendant must establish two things: (1)that counsel's performance was so deficient that it failed to constitute “reasonably effective assistance,” as measured by “prevailing professional norms” considering “all the circumstances”; and (2)that the deficient performance “prejudiced the defense,” something that requires the defendant to show that there is a “reasonable probability that, but for counsel's unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different.” Since the question tells you that the trial judge has determined that the defendant's counsel “performed deficiently by failing to raise a proper objection that would have resulted in exclusion of important prosecution evidence,” the question is in effect telling you to assume that requirement (1) above has been satisfied, and asking you to supply requirement (2). Choice (C), since it refers to “a reasonable probability that the trial's outcome would have been different” had defendant's counsel not failed to object, perfectly articulates requirement (2) of Strickland, as applied to the failure to object. Neither (A) nor (D) is the best response, because each is logically inconsistent with the two requirements of Strickland v. Washington, as discussed in Choice (C). (B)is not the best response, because the standards for ineffective assistance of counsel are the same regardless of whether counsel is appointed or retained. As we discuss in Choice (C), the key case setting the standard for resolving Sixth Amendment IAC claims is Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984). Although Strickland itself involved appointed counsel, the court's description there of the right to effective assistance was phrased so as to apply equally to retained and appointed counsel: “An accused is entitled to be assisted by an attorney, whether retained or appointed, who plays the role necessary to ensure that the trial is fair.” Since Choice (B) suggests that the defendant's IAC claim must automatically fail unless the trial attorney was court-appointed, the choice is based on a legally-incorrect premise.
