Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: Randall was serving a sentence at a state correctional facility. Corrections officers escorted Randall from his cell to a conference room within the prison and informed him he was free to end the interview and return to his cell at any time. Two sheriff's deputies then questioned Randall for three hours about a robbery he allegedly committed before his incarceration. No Miranda warnings were given. Randall confessed to the robbery. At trial, Randall moves to suppress the confession on the ground that he was in custody during the interrogation. Is Randall's suppression motion likely to succeed?
Answer A: Yes, because any person who is incarcerated is necessarily in custody for Miranda purposes at all times
Answer B: Yes, because Randall was removed from general population and placed in a separate room, which objectively increased his sense of restriction
Answer C: No, because Randall was told he was free to end the interview and return to his cell, and imprisonment alone does not create Miranda custody under *Howes v. Fields*
Answer D: No, because the questioning occurred inside the prison where Randall was already confined, eliminating any additional deprivation of liberty
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: In *Howes v. Fields* (2012), the Supreme Court held that a prisoner is not automatically in Miranda custody simply because of incarceration. The objective reasonable-person test applies. Key facts supporting non-custody: Randall was told he could return to his cell at any time, he was in a familiar institutional setting, and the questioning was about conduct outside the prison. A reasonable person in Randall's position would understand that compliance was not mandated beyond his existing sentence. The motion to suppress should be denied.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Supreme Court squarely rejected this argument in *Howes v. Fields* (2012). Imprisonment does not automatically equal Miranda custody for questioning about matters unrelated to the sentence. The standard remains the objective reasonable-person test, and the prison context itself is one factor, not a per se rule. B: Howes v. Fields* analyzed a nearly identical fact pattern and held the prisoner was not in Miranda custody. The Court emphasized that the prisoner was told he was free to return to his cell, was escorted (not forcibly transported), and the restriction of movement was not materially greater than his ordinary prison experience. Removal from general population alone does not create custody. D: The correct reasoning does not rest merely on location inside the prison. The dispositive factors under *Howes* are the totality: being advised of freedom to terminate, the nature of the interview setting, and the relationship between questioning and existing confinement. The answer as stated would incorrectly suggest that location alone is the test.
lb_rule_reference: Under *Howes v. Fields* (2012), a prisoner questioned about events outside the prison is not automatically in Miranda custody; the standard remains the objective reasonable-person test accounting for the prison context.
