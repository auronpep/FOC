Question: Garrett was running late for work when he entered a school zone at 60 miles per hour — triple the posted speed limit. Garrett was talking on his phone and did not notice the speed limit signs. He struck and killed a child who had stepped off the curb. Witnesses confirmed Garrett showed no apparent awareness of the children crossing the street. A toxicology test showed no alcohol or drugs. What is the most serious homicide offense for which Garrett can properly be convicted?
Answer A: Depraved-heart murder, because Garrett drove 60 mph in a school zone, demonstrating indifference to human life
Answer B: First-degree murder, because Garrett's speeding was a deliberate choice
Answer C: Involuntary manslaughter, because Garrett's grossly negligent driving caused the child's death without conscious disregard of an extreme risk
Answer D: No homicide charge, because Garrett did not intend to harm anyone
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Involuntary manslaughter at common law covers killings caused by criminal negligence — a gross deviation from the standard of care that a reasonable person would exercise. Garrett drove triple the speed limit in a school zone while distracted, a gross deviation from reasonable care. However, the facts indicate he was oblivious — he did not notice the signs or children, and there is no evidence of subjective awareness of the extreme risk he was creating. Without that conscious awareness, depraved-heart murder is not established, and involuntary manslaughter is the correct charge.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Depraved-heart murder requires subjective conscious awareness of an extreme risk. Garrett was distracted on his phone and did not notice the signs or the children — he was not consciously disregarding a known risk. Objective recklessness without subjective awareness of that extreme risk does not satisfy depraved-heart murder; it satisfies criminal negligence, which supports involuntary manslaughter. B: Deliberately choosing to speed is not the same as deliberately choosing to kill. First-degree murder requires premeditation and deliberation toward a killing. Garrett's deliberate speeding evidences recklessness, not an intent to kill or cause death. There is no basis for first-degree murder on these facts. D: Criminal homicide does not require intent to kill. Involuntary manslaughter is established by criminal negligence alone — no intent required. Garrett's grossly negligent conduct causing death satisfies the elements of involuntary manslaughter even without any intent to harm.
lb_rule_reference: Depraved-heart murder requires an extreme degree of risk and subjective awareness; involuntary manslaughter requires only criminal negligence (gross deviation from reasonable care); the line turns on the magnitude of risk and whether the defendant was consciously aware of it
