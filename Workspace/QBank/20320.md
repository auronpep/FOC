Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Two defendants — a driver and a passenger — were stopped in a car owned by the driver. Officers conducted an unlawful search of the vehicle's locked glove compartment, where the driver kept her personal documents. Inside the glove compartment, they found cocaine that belonged to the passenger. Both defendants move to suppress the cocaine. Which defendant has standing to challenge the search of the glove compartment?
Answer A: Both defendants, because both were present in the car when the illegal search occurred
Answer B: The passenger only, because the cocaine belonged to him
Answer C: The driver only, because she owned the vehicle and the locked glove compartment was her personal space; the passenger had no REE in the driver's locked glove compartment
Answer D: Neither defendant, because the glove compartment is a semi-public space in a vehicle used by multiple people
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under Rakas v. Illinois, standing to challenge a search requires a personal reasonable expectation of privacy in the area searched. The driver owned the car and kept her personal documents in the locked glove compartment — she had a clear REE there. The passenger, who had no ownership of the vehicle and no access to or expectation of privacy in the driver's locked glove compartment, has no standing to challenge that search, even though the cocaine found there belonged to him. Only the driver has standing. If the driver successfully suppresses the search, the cocaine is suppressed as to her — but the passenger cannot benefit from that suppression as a co-defendant standing challenge.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Rakas v. Illinois held that Fourth Amendment standing is personal — each defendant must demonstrate that his own constitutional rights were violated. Mere presence in the vehicle at the time of an illegal search does not confer standing on a passenger who has no proprietary or privacy interest in the searched area. B: Ownership of the contraband found does not determine standing. Standing depends on whether the defendant had a personal expectation of privacy in the place searched — the locked glove compartment — not in the item found there. The passenger had no ownership of, or privacy interest in, the driver's locked glove compartment. D: The glove compartment was locked and belonged to the driver for her personal documents — not a communally accessed space. Even within a car, a specific locked compartment reserved for personal papers retains a reasonable expectation of privacy for its owner. The driver's standing is clearly established.
lb_rule_reference: Fourth Amendment standing requires the defendant to assert violation of his own Fourth Amendment rights; a defendant cannot challenge a search of another person's property unless he had a personal reasonable expectation of privacy in the area searched or item seized (Rakas v. Illinois)
