Question: A coach angrily tossed a foam ball at an assistant, intending to hit the assistant lightly in the shoulder. The assistant stepped aside. A parent nearby saw the throw, was startled, and later claimed severe distress from watching the confrontation. The ball did not touch or nearly hit the parent. The parent sues the coach for intentional infliction of emotional distress based only on transferred intent. Is the parent likely to prevail in an intentional infliction of emotional distress claim against the coach based only on transferred intent?
Answer A: Yes, because intent transfers to any person emotionally affected by an intentional tort
Answer B: No, because transferred intent does not automatically extend to IIED
Answer C: No, because the coach did not intend to harm anyone physically
Answer D: Yes, because the parent suffered severe distress
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The parent sues for IIED based only on transferred intent. IIED is not one of the torts to which transferred intent automatically applies.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Transferred intent is limited to the traditional five torts and does not automatically create IIED liability. C: Intent to harm is not the boundary. The decisive point is that IIED is outside the automatic transferred-intent circuit. D: Severe distress alone does not supply the required intent or outrageous conduct. The parent's only theory here is transferred intent, which does not automatically apply to IIED.
lb_rule_reference: Transferred intent generally applies only among battery, assault, false imprisonment, trespass to land, and trespass to chattels, not automatically to IIED.
