Subtopic: Separation of Powers
Outline: Impeachment and other Congressional limits on executive
Question: The House impeaches a federal judge. The Senate adopts a rule assigning a committee to receive evidence and report to the full Senate before the final vote. After conviction and removal, the judge sues in federal court, arguing that the Senate did not conduct a "trial" because every senator did not personally hear each witness live. How should the court most likely respond?
Answer A: The court should decide whether the Senate's evidence procedures were the best trial method
Answer B: The challenge is likely nonjusticiable because impeachment trial procedures are committed to the Senate
Answer C: The conviction is invalid because committees may never assist the Senate with evidence gathering
Answer D: The court should review the procedure only because the impeached official was a judge
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The Constitution gives the Senate the power to try impeachments. A challenge to how the Senate structured witness review generally presents a political question inappropriate for judicial resolution.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Impeachment trials are constitutionally committed to the Senate, and courts generally do not supervise the Senate's trial procedures. C: The question is not whether the committee method is ideal; it is whether courts may review the Senate's chosen impeachment procedures. D: The political-question reasoning follows from the Senate's impeachment-trial power, not from the identity of the impeached officer.
lb_rule_reference: Challenges to the Senate's exercise of its impeachment trial procedures generally present a nonjusticiable political question because the Constitution commits the trial of impeachments to the Senate.
