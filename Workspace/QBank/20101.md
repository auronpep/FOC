Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Definition
Question: Abigail sues Peter for breach of a contract requiring Peter to deliver grain. Peter admits he failed to deliver but disputes the amount of damages. Abigail offers evidence that Peter bought expensive jewelry three months after the breach. Abigail argues that the purchase shows Peter had money. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Admit the evidence because any financial information about Peter is automatically relevant in a contract action.
Answer B: Exclude the evidence only if Peter proves the jewelry was bought with borrowed money.
Answer C: Exclude the evidence because Peter's ability to spend money does not make the amount of Abigail's contract damages more or less probable.
Answer D: Admit the evidence because wealthy parties are less likely to breach contracts accidentally.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Peter's later jewelry purchase does not logically affect the amount of damages from nondelivery. It is not relevant merely because it reflects wealth or spending.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because financial information must connect to a consequential fact. B: B is wrong because the problem is lack of consequential connection, not the source of the funds. D: D is wrong because it supplies an unsupported character-like inference rather than a relevance route.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401 / FRE 402
lb_student_diagnostic: The trap is treating wealth or spending as automatically relevant.
