MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Officers watched a suspect carry a paper bag out of a known drug house and place it in the trunk of his car. Based on prior surveillance, they had probable cause to believe the bag contained marijuana. Officers stopped the car, opened the trunk, and opened the paper bag — finding marijuana. The driver challenges the search of the bag, arguing officers needed a warrant to open a closed container. Was the warrantless search of the paper bag in the trunk constitutional?
Answer A: No, because a closed container inside a vehicle retains full Fourth Amendment protection and always requires a warrant to open
Answer B: No, because the automobile exception authorizes search of the trunk but not of closed containers inside it
Answer C: Yes, because officers had probable cause to believe the specific paper bag contained contraband, and the automobile exception authorizes the search of containers when probable cause extends to them
Answer D: Yes, because the suspect placed the bag in the car voluntarily, constituting consent to a search of the vehicle's contents
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: California v. Acevedo directly answers this question. Officers had probable cause focused specifically on the paper bag — they watched the suspect place a bag they believed contained marijuana in the trunk. Under Acevedo, police need not obtain a warrant to search a container within a vehicle if there is probable cause to believe the container holds contraband. The automobile exception applies. The search was constitutional and suppression is denied.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: California v. Acevedo held that where police have probable cause to believe a container in a vehicle holds contraband, they may search that container without a warrant under the automobile exception. The container's presence inside the vehicle does not restore a warrant requirement that the automobile exception has already dispensed with. B: Under Acevedo, the automobile exception permits search of the entire vehicle and any container within it where the contraband could be found. The probable cause here was specific to the bag — which is sufficient to open it under Acevedo. Had it been general vehicle probable cause, the same result follows under Ross. D: Placing an item in one's own car does not constitute consent to a government search. Consent requires a knowing, voluntary agreement to allow a search. Placing a bag in a trunk is not a manifestation of intent to allow police to search. The correct basis is the automobile exception/Acevedo, not consent.
lb_rule_reference: When probable cause is focused on a specific container in a vehicle, police may search that container without a warrant; when probable cause extends to the vehicle generally, police may search any area of the vehicle — including all containers — where the contraband might be found (California v. Acevedo; United States v. Ross)
