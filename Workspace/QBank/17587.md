Subtopic: Presentation of Evidence
Outline: Materiality and probative value
Question: Ruth sues Boaz after a bus collision, claiming the collision caused her back injury. Ruth offers testimony from her sister Naomi that Ruth regularly lifted heavy baskets before the collision without back pain, but complained of back pain immediately after the collision. Boaz objects that Naomi is not a doctor and that Ruth could have hurt her back another way. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the testimony because only a medical expert may testify about before-and-after symptoms.
Answer B: Exclude the testimony because alternative causes are possible.
Answer C: Admit the testimony because it has some tendency to support causation; medical limitations and alternative causes affect weight.
Answer D: Admit the testimony only if Ruth first proves the collision caused the injury by a preponderance.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Lay testimony about Ruth's condition before and after the collision is probative of causation. It does not need to conclusively prove medical causation; the possibility of other causes is for cross-examination and jury weight.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because a lay witness may testify to observed condition and timing. B: B is wrong because alternative causes usually weaken probative force but do not eliminate relevance. D: D is wrong because the admissibility of a relevant fact does not require prior proof of the ultimate causation fact.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401; FRE 402
lb_student_diagnostic: Student confuses imperfect causation proof with irrelevance.
