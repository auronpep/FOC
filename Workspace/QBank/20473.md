Question: Rachel contracted with a contractor to repair Rachel's barn. After signing, Rachel mailed a copy of the contract to her neighbor Samuel and wrote, “This should also help keep water off your field.” The contract did not mention Samuel and required repairs only on Rachel's barn. When the contractor performed poorly, Samuel sued the contractor as a third-party beneficiary. Is Samuel likely to recover?
Answer A: Yes, because Rachel sent Samuel a copy of the contract.
Answer B: No, because later notice and practical benefit do not show that the original parties intended to give Samuel enforcement rights.
Answer C: Yes, because Samuel learned of the contract before the breach.
Answer D: No, because Rachel's letter automatically revoked the contract.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Samuel is not an intended beneficiary merely because he later received a copy and would benefit from performance. Third-party beneficiary status depends on intent manifested in the contract and circumstances at formation, not later notice alone.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because receiving a copy is not an assignment or intended-beneficiary designation. C: C is wrong because knowledge before breach does not create status. D: D is wrong because Rachel's letter did not revoke the contractor's duty to Rachel.
