Question: Esther owed support to her elderly father, Isaac. Esther transferred a field to Caleb. In the sale agreement, Caleb promised Esther that he would deliver grain to Isaac every month for three years. Caleb later stopped delivering grain and argued that Isaac could not enforce because Caleb's main purpose was to buy the field. May Isaac enforce Caleb's promise?
Answer A: No, because Caleb's main purpose was to acquire the field.
Answer B: Yes, because the contract required direct recurring performance to Isaac and was intended to benefit him.
Answer C: No, because Isaac gave no consideration to Caleb.
Answer D: Yes, but only if Esther first proves that Caleb subjectively liked Isaac.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Isaac may enforce as an intended beneficiary. A contract can have a commercial purpose between the parties while also intending direct performance to a third party. The grain deliveries to Isaac were the promised performance.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because a commercial purpose does not erase intended-beneficiary status when the contract directs performance to the third party. C: C is wrong because the beneficiary need not supply consideration. D: D is wrong because subjective affection is not required; the contract's objective intent controls.
lb_rule_reference: Common law third-party beneficiary — intent to confer direct right
