Subtopic: Homicide
Outline: Manslaughter
Question: At a gym, Victor challenged Marcus to a fight after a dispute over a weight machine. Marcus agreed. The two exchanged blows for about thirty seconds when Victor knocked Marcus down. Marcus, furious at being knocked down, retrieved a nearby metal barbell collar from the floor while still on the ground and struck Victor in the head with it, killing him. Marcus claims he acted in the heat of combat. What is the most serious homicide offense for which Marcus can properly be convicted?
Answer A: No homicide charge, because both men consented to the fight and Victor assumed the risk of injury
Answer B: Voluntary manslaughter, because the mutual combat constituted adequate provocation and Marcus acted in the heat of combat
Answer C: Second-degree murder, because Marcus intentionally struck Victor with a deadly weapon during combat he initiated, and cannot claim heat-of-passion mitigation as the aggressor
Answer D: First-degree murder, because Marcus picked up the barbell collar while on the ground, demonstrating prior thought
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Marcus intentionally struck Victor with a metal barbell collar — a deadly weapon — with apparent intent to cause death or serious bodily harm. This is intentional killing, satisfying malice aforethought for murder. The heat-of-passion defense is unavailable because Marcus was an aggressor who initiated (or mutually agreed to initiate) the combat; he cannot use Victor's blows in the fight as adequate provocation. The killing is second-degree murder (not first, because there was no premeditation of a killing before the fight began).
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Consent is not a defense to homicide. Two people cannot agree to fight and thereby insulate the killer from criminal responsibility for death. The law does not recognize consent to death or serious bodily harm as a valid defense to murder or manslaughter. B: A defendant who initiates the fighting cannot claim heat-of-passion provocation from the victim's response to his own aggression. Voluntary manslaughter provocation doctrine bars mitigation when the defendant was the aggressor who provoked the victim's conduct. Here, Marcus agreed to fight but Victor's blow was a response to the mutual combat Marcus initiated. Marcus cannot use Victor's counter-blows as adequate provocation. D: Premeditation requires cool reflection — a decision to kill formed before the heat of the encounter, not a spontaneous grab during a frenzied fight. Picking up a nearby object during a physical confrontation does not establish premeditation in the sense required for first-degree murder. The killing occurred in the midst of combat; the interval of "reflection" was seconds during an ongoing fight, not the kind of prior deliberation first-degree murder requires.
lb_rule_reference: Mutual combat can constitute adequate provocation for voluntary manslaughter; however, a defendant who initiates or escalates the combat cannot claim heat-of-passion mitigation based on the victim's response to his own aggression
