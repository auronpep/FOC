Question: A builder contracted with Rachel to repair Rachel's house. The written contract required the builder to make repairs that would benefit Rachel's father, Isaac, who lived in the house. The contract also stated, “The builder's total liability for any repair-related claim is capped at $10,000.” The builder performed defectively, causing Isaac $18,000 in loss. Isaac sues the builder as an intended beneficiary and seeks $18,000. What is the builder's best response?
Answer A: Isaac cannot recover anything because beneficiaries can never enforce repair contracts.
Answer B: Isaac may enforce only within the contract's $10,000 liability limit.
Answer C: Isaac may recover the full $18,000 because he was not a party to the liability limit.
Answer D: Isaac may recover only if Rachel first assigns him the repair contract.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: An intended beneficiary's rights are measured by the contract that created them. Isaac may not enforce the beneficiary promise while avoiding a liability cap in that same contract. If he has rights, they are subject to the $10,000 limit.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because intended beneficiaries may enforce repair contracts when the contract manifests direct benefit. C: C is wrong because a beneficiary is not better off than the promisee under the contract terms. D: D is wrong because TPB status, not assignment, is the relevant route.
