Question: A defendant is on trial for evading $100,000 in taxes. The prosecution offers in evidence an anonymous letter to the IRS, identified as being in the defendant's handwriting, saying, "I promised my mother on her deathbed I would try to pay my back taxes. Here is $10,000. I'll make other payments if you promise not to prosecute. Answer yes by personal ad saying, 'OK on tax deal.'" The letter is
Answer A: A: admissible, as a statement of present intention or plan.
Answer B: B: admissible, as an admission of a party-opponent.
Answer C: C: inadmissible, because it is an effort to settle a claim.
Answer D: D: inadmissible, because the probative value is substantially outweighed by the risk of unfair prejudice.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: B is correct. The anonymous letter is being offered against the defendant and is an admission by him that he knows he owes back taxes. The letter is admissible as an admission by a party-opponent.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A is incorrect. The letter is not a statement of the defendant's then-existing mental condition; it is being offered to prove that the defendant owed back taxes. It is an admission, not a statement of the defendant's intent. C is incorrect. In the letter, the defendant was not disputing liability or the amount owed, therefore, it would not be excluded as an offer of settlement. D is incorrect. The letter is highly probative and any prejudice against the defendant is not unfair. The admission of the letter is, of course, detrimental to the defendant's defense, but that is not due to any unfair prejudice.
additional_info: The Federal Rules of Evidence (FRE) define hearsay as a statement, other than one made by the declarant while testifying at the current trial or hearing, offered into evidence to prove the truth of the matter asserted. Fed. R. Evid. 801(c). However, FRE 801(d) removes certain statements that would be hearsay under the common law definition. Those statements are admissible as substantive evidence if the statements are relevant. Under the FRE 801(d)(2), a statement by an opposing party (traditionally known as an "admission by a party- opponent") is not hearsay. Under this FRE, when the opposing party's statement is offered against that same opposing party and was made in either an individual or representative capacity, it is admissible. Evidence of offers to compromise or settle is typically inadmissible. There must be a claim at issue for the rule to apply. There need not be a filed lawsuit, but there must be at least some indication, expressed or implied of a claim. Additionally, the claim must be disputed as to liability or amount for the exclusionary rule to apply. Thus, if a party admits liability and the amount of liability, but offers to settle rather than litigate, the statements will be admissible. B is correct. The applicable rule is that a statement made by an opposing party, offered against that party, will be admissible as non-hearsay. The anonymous letter is being offered by the prosecution against the defendant, which makes it admissible as a non-hearsay admission by a party-opponent. A is incorrect. This answer reaches the correct answer with the wrong reasoning. The letter is not being offered to establish the defendant's then-existing mental condition, but rather, it is being offered substantively to prove that the defendant owed back taxes. It is admissible as an admission, not as a statement of the defendant's intention or plan. C is incorrect. The anonymous letter was not disputing liability or the amount, but rather, it was an admission of liability and an offer to settle as opposed to litigating. Therefore, the letter would not be excluded as an offer of settlement, but admissible as an admission of a party-opponent. D is incorrect. It is true that the letter is detrimental to the defendant's case. However, any detriment to the defendant's case is not because it is unfairly prejudicial. Rather, the anonymous letter is highly probative and should be admitted as an admission.
