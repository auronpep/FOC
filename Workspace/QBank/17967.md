MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Intentional Torts
Outline: Battery
Question: A patient arrived unconscious after a bike crash. While treating a deep leg wound, the surgeon discovered internal bleeding that required an immediate additional incision to prevent serious harm. No family member could be reached, and waiting would have created a substantial risk. The patient later sues the surgeon for battery based on the additional incision. Is the patient likely to prevail in a battery claim against the surgeon for the additional incision?
Answer A: Yes, because the patient never gave express consent to that incision
Answer B: No, because emergency circumstances implied consent to the medically necessary incision
Answer C: No, because physicians are never liable for battery during treatment
Answer D: Yes, because any procedure different from the first wound treatment exceeds consent
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The patient was unconscious, no surrogate was available, delay threatened serious harm, and the additional incision was immediately necessary. Implied consent defeats battery.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: In an emergency, consent may be implied when the patient cannot consent and immediate treatment is necessary to prevent serious harm. C: Physicians can be liable for unauthorized intentional contacts. The defense here is implied emergency consent, not categorical immunity. D: Emergency implied consent can extend to necessary related treatment discovered during care when consent cannot be obtained in time.
lb_rule_reference: Emergency circumstances can imply consent to medically necessary contact when the patient cannot consent and delay would threaten serious harm.
