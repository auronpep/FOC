Outline: Freedom of religion
Question: A religious school employs "faith formation instructors" who teach math and history, lead daily prayers, prepare students for sacraments, and are evaluated on how well they transmit the school's doctrine. One instructor sues after termination under a generally applicable employment statute. The instructor argues the ministerial exception cannot apply because she is not ordained and her title does not include "minister." What is the school's strongest constitutional defense?
Answer A: The exception cannot apply because only ordained clergy with the title minister are covered
Answer B: The ministerial exception may apply because the instructor performs important religious functions for the school
Answer C: The exception applies to every employee of a religious school, including purely secular janitors
Answer D: The exception is unavailable whenever the employment statute is neutral and generally applicable
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Leading prayer, teaching doctrine, and forming students in the faith are important religious functions, even if the employee is not ordained.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Courts look to function, including religious teaching and leadership, not title alone. C: The exception turns on the employee's religious functions, not employer identity alone. D: The ministerial exception is a church-autonomy rule that can bar application of otherwise neutral employment laws to ministerial relationships.
lb_rule_reference: The ministerial exception can apply to employees who perform important religious functions, even if their title is not minister.
