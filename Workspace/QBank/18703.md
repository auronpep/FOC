MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: General Principles
Outline: Responsibility
Question: Diane is diagnosed with a severe personality disorder that impairs her emotional understanding of moral consequences. She knows, in an abstract intellectual sense, that arson is illegal. However, due to her disorder, she lacks any genuine emotional appreciation of the wrongfulness of burning someone's property — she understands the legal rule but cannot internalize it as morally binding on her. She sets fire to a neighbor's home in a planned act. The jurisdiction uses the MPC/ALI test. Diane is charged with arson. Under the MPC/ALI test, does Diane have a viable insanity claim?
Answer A: No, because Diane knew arson was illegal
Answer B: No, because Diane's act was planned, showing she had cognitive capacity
Answer C: Yes, because Diane may lack substantial capacity to appreciate the criminality of her conduct under the MPC/ALI test, even if she had abstract intellectual knowledge of the law
Answer D: Yes, because the MPC/ALI test excuses any defendant diagnosed with a personality disorder
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The MPC/ALI test uses two key innovations over M'Naghten. First, it substitutes "appreciate" for "know" — appreciation requires genuine emotional and moral comprehension, not just recitation of the legal rule. Second, it requires only a lack of "substantial" capacity, not total incapacity. Diane intellectually knows arson is illegal but cannot genuinely appreciate its wrongfulness — her disorder prevents internalization of the moral reality. Under the MPC/ALI framework, this may satisfy the appreciation prong of the test. Whether it does is a jury question, but the claim is viable in a way it would not be under M'Naghten.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The MPC/ALI test does not use M'Naghten's narrow "know" standard. It requires "appreciate" — a deeper understanding that encompasses emotional and moral comprehension, not just intellectual knowledge. The MPC standard is explicitly broader. Diane's abstract intellectual knowledge of illegality is not the same as appreciating the criminality of her conduct. B: Premeditation is not inconsistent with the MPC/ALI insanity defense. The question is not whether the defendant had enough cognitive function to plan, but whether she lacked substantial capacity to appreciate the criminality of the conduct or to conform. Many defendants with severe mental disorders can plan acts while still lacking the relevant capacity. D: Even under the MPC/ALI test, the mental disease must causally produce a lack of substantial capacity to appreciate criminality or conform conduct. A diagnosis alone is not sufficient. Many people with personality disorders retain the relevant capacities. Diane's claim is viable on the facts because the disorder specifically impairs her appreciation — not because personality disorders categorically qualify.
lb_rule_reference: Under the MPC/ALI test, a defendant is not responsible if, as a result of mental disease or defect, she lacks substantial capacity either to appreciate the criminality (wrongfulness) of her conduct or to conform her conduct to the requirements of law; "appreciate" is broader than "know," and "substantial" capacity is less than full capacity
