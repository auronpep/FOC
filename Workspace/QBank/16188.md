


BARMATRIX Q#: 16188
Outline_code: 75090400
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Right to Counsel
Question: A man was charged with first-degree murder. Two lawyers were appointed to represent him because the prosecution planned to seek the death penalty. On the first day of trial, the air-conditioning malfunctioned in the courtroom, so the judge directed that the selection of the jurors take place in his chambers. Because of the large number of potential jurors, the judge directed that only one lawyer for the prosecution and one for the defense participate in the jury selection process. The defendant remained in the courtroom during the questioning of the jurors. Once the jury was selected, the trial was postponed until the next day, when the air-conditioning was again working. Did the court's jury selection process violate the defendant's federal constitutional rights?
Answer A: A: No, because a defendant's confrontation rights are limited to witnesses rather than jurors.
Answer B: B: No, because there was good cause to conduct jury selection outside the defendant's presence.
Answer C: C: Yes, because excluding the defendant undercut the presumption of innocence.
Answer D: D: Yes, because jury selection is a critical stage at which a defendant is entitled to be present.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: (D)is the best response, because a criminal defendant's due process and confrontation rights guarantee him the right to be present at every stage of his trial. The Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause — as well as the due process components of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments — include the right of a criminal defendant to be present in the courtroom at every critical stage of the proceeding against him. In particular, once the trial begins, every part of the trial is deemed to be critical. See, e.g., Illinois v. Allen, 397 U.S. 337 (1970): “One of the most basic of the rights guaranteed by the Confrontation Clause is the accused's right to be present in the courtroom at every stage of his trial.” The “trial” is deemed to begin with the selection of the jury — the defendant has a constitutional right to assist in selecting the jury, as part of his more general right to assist in conducting the defense. Id. See also Lewis v. U.S., 146 U.S. 370 (1892), expressly finding a constitutional right to be present during voir dire. (A)is not the best response, because a defendant's confrontation rights include the right to be present at all stages of the trial. As we discuss more fully in connection with Choice (D), both the Confrontation Clause and the Due Process Clauses guarantee an accused the right to be present at all stages once the trial begins, not just the right to be present when witnesses are testifying. (B)is not the best response, because “good cause” does not suffice to allow jury selection to occur outside the defendant's presence, unless the defendant expressly or impliedly waives that right. As we discuss in connection with Choice (D), an accused's right to be present for voir dire is guaranteed by the Confrontation and Due Process Clauses. The right can certainly be waived by the defendant — for instance, a defendant who misbehaves during jury selection can be deemed to have waived his right and can therefore be removed from the court. But in the absence of waiver, there is no “good cause” exception to the defendant's right to be present for jury selection (or for any other part of the trial itself); therefore, the court was required to postpone jury selection until the air-conditioning could be fixed so that voir dire could take place in a room big enough to include the defendant. (C)is not the best response, because the defendant's right to be present for voir dire did not derive from the presumption of innocence. As we discuss in connection with Choice (D), it's the Confrontation and Due Process Clauses that guarantee an accused the right to be present in the courtroom for every part of his trial. Furthermore, the reason the defendant is given the right to be present is so that the defendant can assist effectively in the presentation of his defense. This right to give effective assistance has essentially nothing to do with the presumption of innocence. Thus while this choice reaches the correct outcome, it does so based on incorrect legal reasoning.
