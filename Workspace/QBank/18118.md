Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Supplemental Jurisdiction
Question: A plaintiff, a citizen of Michigan, files a diversity action for $100,000 against a contractor, a citizen of Ohio. During litigation, the contractor impleads a subcontractor under Rule 14, asserting that the subcontractor must indemnify the contractor for any damages the plaintiff recovers. The subcontractor is a citizen of Michigan — the same state as the plaintiff. The plaintiff then seeks to assert a direct $40,000 negligence claim against the subcontractor, arguing that § 1367(a) supplemental jurisdiction covers the claim because it arises from the same project and accident as the main case. The subcontractor moves to dismiss the plaintiff's claim against it. The governing forum is federal court under the MBE baseline. Should the court dismiss the plaintiff's claim against the subcontractor?
Answer A: No, because the plaintiff's claim against the subcontractor arises from the same project and shares a common nucleus of operative fact with the main claim, satisfying § 1367(a)
Answer B: Yes, the court should dismiss the plaintiff's claim against the subcontractor because § 1367(b) bars plaintiff claims against Rule 14 third-party defendants in diversity cases when the claim would destroy complete diversity
Answer C: No, because the plaintiff is the injured party and has an independent right to assert all claims arising from her injury against all parties in the federal action
Answer D: Yes, but only because the plaintiff's $40,000 claim does not independently satisfy the $75,000 AIC threshold for diversity jurisdiction
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Section 1367(b) provides that in a case whose anchor claim rests solely on diversity jurisdiction, supplemental jurisdiction does not extend to claims by plaintiffs against persons made parties under Rule 14 when exercising jurisdiction would be inconsistent with § 1332's requirements. Here, the plaintiff (Michigan) seeks to sue the subcontractor (Michigan), a Rule 14 third-party defendant. Exercising supplemental jurisdiction would allow a Michigan plaintiff to sue a Michigan co-citizen in federal court — a result inconsistent with § 1332's complete diversity requirement. The court must dismiss.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Section 1367(b) specifically bars supplemental jurisdiction in diversity cases over claims by plaintiffs against persons made parties under Rule 14 when exercising jurisdiction would be inconsistent with § 1332. The plaintiff is Michigan; the subcontractor is Michigan — they are non-diverse. Exercising jurisdiction over the plaintiff's claim against the subcontractor would circumvent the complete-diversity requirement. Section 1367(b) bars this claim. C: Section 1367(b) was specifically enacted to prevent plaintiffs from using impleader as a vehicle to bring non-diverse defendants into federal diversity cases. The plaintiff's right to sue the subcontractor exists — in state court. Federal courts have limited jurisdiction, and Congress deliberately chose to bar this specific type of plaintiff maneuver to preserve the integrity of the § 1332 diversity requirement. D: The correct basis for dismissal is § 1367(b), not the AIC threshold. Supplemental jurisdiction under § 1367 does not require independent AIC satisfaction for supplemental claims — so the AIC issue is a red herring. If the plaintiff were diverse from the subcontractor, § 1367(a) would permit the claim regardless of the $40,000 amount. The AIC is not why the claim fails; § 1367(b)'s citizenship-protection rule is.
lb_rule_reference: 28 U.S.C. § 1367(b) — in diversity cases, no supplemental jurisdiction over claims by plaintiffs against persons made parties under Rule 14 (impleader) if exercising jurisdiction would be inconsistent with § 1332.
