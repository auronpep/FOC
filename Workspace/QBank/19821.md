Question: Leah orally promised a bank that she would pay Jacob's loan if Jacob defaulted. The next day, Leah signed a letter to Jacob stating, “I promised the bank I would guarantee your $20,000 loan due on June 1 if you default.” Jacob kept the letter. After Jacob defaulted, the bank sued Leah and produced the letter. Is the letter potentially sufficient to satisfy the Statute of Frauds?
Answer A: Yes, because a signed memorandum by the party to be charged may suffice even if it was addressed to Jacob rather than the bank.
Answer B: No, because the writing must be addressed directly to the creditor seeking enforcement.
Answer C: No, because a suretyship promise can be proved only by a formal guaranty contract.
Answer D: Yes, but only if Jacob also signed the letter.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A signed memorandum can satisfy the Statute of Frauds if it reasonably identifies the agreement and is signed by the party to be charged. The writing need not necessarily be addressed to the party seeking enforcement, so Leah's signed letter can potentially satisfy the writing requirement.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because audience is not automatically fatal if the writing supplies the necessary memorandum. C: C is wrong because a formal guaranty contract is not always required if a signed memorandum suffices. D: D is wrong because Leah, the party to be charged, is the relevant signer.
