Question: Ruth hired Jacob to convert a house into a day-care center. The contract stated that Ruth's final payment would be due “only if the city issues an occupancy permit for day-care use.” Jacob finished most of the work but failed to install the required second exit, so the city refused the permit. Jacob demanded final payment, arguing that the building was mostly complete. Who is most likely to prevail on the final-payment claim?
Answer A: Jacob, because substantial performance satisfies every condition in a construction contract.
Answer B: Ruth, because the express permit condition did not occur and Jacob's own omission caused the failure.
Answer C: Jacob, because the city's permit decision is a third-party act and therefore cannot affect contractual duties.
Answer D: Ruth, but only if she proves that Jacob acted in bad faith when omitting the exit.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The contract made issuance of the permit an express condition to final payment. The condition did not occur, and the nonoccurrence was tied to Jacob's failure to install a required feature. Jacob cannot use substantial performance to satisfy a strict express condition that he prevented by incomplete performance.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because substantial performance may satisfy constructive conditions, but it does not automatically satisfy a clear express condition. C: C is wrong because parties may condition duty on a third-party permit. D: D is wrong because Ruth need not prove subjective bad faith when the condition failed because Jacob omitted required work.
lb_rule_reference: Express condition precedent / no duty until condition occurs
