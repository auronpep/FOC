Subtopic: General Principles
Outline: Self-defense
Question: Jared becomes extremely intoxicated after voluntarily drinking heavily at a bar. He staggers to his neighbor's car, believing in his drunken confusion that it is his own car, and drives away. He is charged with (Count 1) larceny of an automobile, which requires intent to permanently deprive, and (Count 2) driving under the influence (DUI), a general intent crime. How does Jared's voluntary intoxication affect his criminal liability on each count?
Answer A: Jared is guilty of both counts; intoxication is never a defense
Answer B: Jared is not guilty of either count because intoxication negates all criminal intent
Answer C: Jared may have a defense to larceny if his intoxication negated the specific intent to permanently deprive, but intoxication is no defense to DUI, a general intent crime
Answer D: Jared may have a defense to both counts if his intoxication was severe enough to cause temporary insanity
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Voluntary intoxication operates as a partial defense only for specific intent crimes by potentially negating the required specific mental state. Larceny requires the specific intent to permanently deprive — a purposeful mental state. If Jared was so intoxicated that he genuinely believed the car was his, he may have lacked that specific intent (though this is a fact question for the jury). DUI is a general intent crime: the act of driving while intoxicated satisfies the mental state requirement because recklessness or negligence is sufficient. Intoxication cannot negate general intent; it typically demonstrates it. Jared remains fully liable for DUI regardless of how intoxicated he was.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Voluntary intoxication is a partial defense to specific intent crimes. If Jared was so intoxicated that he genuinely did not form the intent to permanently deprive (believing the car was his), he lacks the mens rea for larceny. The absolute bar applies only to general intent crimes, not specific intent crimes. B: Voluntary intoxication is a defense only to specific intent crimes where the required mental state is negated. DUI is a general intent crime — the required mental state (recklessness or negligence in operating a vehicle) is not negated by intoxication. In fact, intoxication is the very condition that proves the recklessness or negligence element of DUI. Intoxication never provides a defense to general intent crimes. D: Voluntary intoxication does not become insanity merely by being severe. The insanity defense requires a mental disease or defect — chronic alcoholism causing permanent neurological damage might qualify, but acute voluntary intoxication, no matter how severe, is treated under the intoxication doctrine, not the insanity doctrine. This answer conflates two distinct defenses.
lb_rule_reference: Voluntary intoxication may negate specific intent (e.g., premeditation, purpose, knowledge) and thus reduce the charge or provide a partial defense; it is never a defense to general intent crimes, where recklessness or negligence suffices for liability
