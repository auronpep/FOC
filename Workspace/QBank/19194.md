Question: A federal statute provides that for purposes of all federal programs and benefits, the term "spouse" means only a person of the opposite sex who is a husband or wife, and "marriage" means only a legal union between one man and one woman. A same-sex couple legally married under state law is denied federal estate tax exemptions available to opposite-sex married couples. The couple challenges the statute under the Equal Protection component of the Fifth Amendment Due Process Clause. Under Equal Protection analysis, how should a court evaluate the federal statute's limitation of marriage recognition to opposite-sex couples for purposes of federal benefits?
Answer A: The statute survives under rational basis review because Congress has a legitimate interest in maintaining a uniform definition of marriage across all federal programs and in preserving the traditional definition of marriage
Answer B: The statute is subject to strict scrutiny because it discriminates against gay and lesbian persons on the basis of sexual orientation, which is a suspect classification
Answer C: The statute fails Equal Protection review because the only purpose served by denying federal recognition to lawfully married same-sex couples is to demean their status — a bare desire to harm an identifiable group — which is not a legitimate government interest under any level of scrutiny
Answer D: The statute survives because Congress has plenary power over federal benefit programs, and the Court has never recognized a constitutional right to federal benefits for same-sex couples
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: United States v. Windsor held that the federal statute denying recognition to same-sex marriages for federal benefits purposes had the purpose and effect of imposing a disadvantage and stigma on same-sex couples who had lawfully married under their state's laws. The only identifiable purpose — disapproval of same-sex marriage and the desire to demean its participants — is not a legitimate government interest. Whether analyzed under strict rational basis or a more searching review, bare animus toward a group fails every scrutiny level.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Supreme Court struck down DOMA's Section 3 in United States v. Windsor, holding that the sole effect of the federal marriage definition was to impose a disadvantage and stigma on a class of persons who are lawfully married under state law. The Court found the only discernible purpose was to demean and injure a politically unpopular group, which is not a legitimate government interest under any scrutiny level. B: The Supreme Court did not hold in Windsor that sexual orientation is a suspect classification triggering strict scrutiny. The Court's majority opinion reached the result through a more searching rational basis analysis (or what some call rational basis with bite) combined with federalism concerns. The decision did not formally declare sexual orientation to be a suspect classification. D: While Congress has broad power over federal benefit programs, that power cannot be exercised in ways that violate the Equal Protection component of the Fifth Amendment. Windsor specifically held that denying federal benefits to lawfully married same-sex couples is unconstitutional — not because there is a right to federal benefits, but because the discriminatory purpose behind the denial violates equal protection.
lb_rule_reference: Federal laws that deny federal benefits to lawfully married same-sex couples, when the sole purpose is to express disapproval of same-sex marriage, fail the Equal Protection component of the Fifth Amendment Due Process Clause; such laws are not justified by any legitimate government interest beyond bare animus.
