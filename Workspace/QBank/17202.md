Outline: Defamation
Question: A city building inspector criticized a developer at a public hearing. The developer then sent a newsletter falsely stating that the inspector had taken bribes. Before sending it, the developer saw payroll records showing the alleged payments were reimbursements, and an employee warned that the bribery story was false. The developer sent it anyway because he disliked the inspector. Is the inspector likely to satisfy the actual malice element in a defamation claim against the developer?
Answer A: Yes, because the developer disliked the inspector
Answer B: Yes, because the developer published despite strong reasons to know the bribery charge was false
Answer C: No, because a public official can never recover for criticism about official conduct
Answer D: No, because failure to investigate is always insufficient as a matter of law
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The inspector is a public official, and the developer had records and a warning undermining the charge yet published anyway, supporting reckless disregard or knowledge of falsity.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Actual malice is not ill will; it is knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for truth. C: Public officials may recover if they prove falsity and actual malice. D: The facts show more than ordinary failure to investigate: the developer had specific contrary information before publication.
lb_rule_reference: A public official must prove actual malice, meaning knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for truth, not merely ill will.
