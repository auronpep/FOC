MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Intentional Torts
Outline: Battery
Question: A clinic patient consented to a blood-pressure check only and expressly refused any mouth examination. While the patient looked away, a physician, hoping to save the patient a second visit, intentionally inserted a gloved finger into the patient's mouth for a quick inspection. The inspection caused no injury. The patient sues the physician for battery. Is the patient likely to prevail in a battery claim against the physician?
Answer A: No, because the physician acted to help the patient and caused no injury
Answer B: Yes, because the physician intentionally made an unauthorized offensive contact with the patient's body
Answer C: No, because the physician did not intend the patient to feel offended
Answer D: Yes, but only because the physician committed medical negligence
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The patient expressly refused mouth contact. The physician intentionally touched the patient's body outside that authorization, which supports battery even without physical injury.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A benign motive does not defeat battery when the defendant intentionally makes an unauthorized offensive contact. C: The intent requirement is satisfied by intent to cause the contact. The contact was unauthorized and offensive under ordinary dignity standards. D: The claim named is battery, and the decisive fact is intentional unauthorized contact. Negligence standards are not the controlling theory.
lb_rule_reference: A defendant who intentionally causes an unauthorized offensive touching may be liable for battery even when acting from a benign motive.
