Question: Jacob owed John $7,000 on an old loan. Jacob then sold grain to Paul. In the sale contract, Paul promised Jacob that Paul would pay John $7,000 directly as part of the price. John learned of the promise and, relying on it, canceled a collection hearing against Jacob. Before Paul paid, Paul and Jacob agreed to delete the payment-to-John clause. May John enforce Paul's promise?
Answer A: No, because John's only claim is on the old loan against Jacob.
Answer B: No, because John gave no consideration to Paul.
Answer C: Yes, but only if Jacob assigned the grain-sale contract to John.
Answer D: Yes, because John was an intended creditor beneficiary and his rights vested when he canceled the collection hearing in reliance on the direct-payment promise.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: Paul's promise was made to satisfy Jacob's debt to John, making John an intended creditor beneficiary. John's cancellation of the collection hearing was reliance on Paul's promise and vested his rights before Paul and Jacob tried to delete the clause.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because the new contract created a beneficiary route independent of John's old claim against Jacob. B: B is wrong because John did not need to provide consideration to Paul. C: C is wrong because John's route is creditor-beneficiary status, not assignment.
