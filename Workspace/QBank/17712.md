Outline: Arson
Question: After receiving Miranda warnings and waiving her rights, Patrice was questioned by detectives about an arson. About twenty minutes into questioning, Patrice said, "Maybe I should just stop talking." The detectives continued questioning without acknowledging her remark, and Patrice answered several more questions, ultimately making admissions. Patrice moves to suppress all statements made after her remark about stopping. Should the statements made after Patrice's remark be suppressed?
Answer A: Yes, because any statement by a suspect expressing reluctance to continue speaking must be treated as an invocation of the right to silence
Answer B: Yes, because the detectives were required to clarify whether Patrice was invoking her rights before continuing to question her
Answer C: No, because Patrice's statement was ambiguous and equivocal, and did not constitute a clear invocation of the right to silence
Answer D: No, because Patrice had already waived her rights at the beginning of the interview, and that waiver remained in effect throughout the interview
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Davis v. United States* (1994) and *Berghuis v. Thompkins* (2010), a suspect who has waived Miranda rights must subsequently invoke them unambiguously to cut off questioning. "Maybe I should just stop talking" is conditional and equivocal — it does not clearly invoke the right to silence. Because the invocation was ambiguous, the detectives were not required to stop questioning, and Patrice's subsequent admissions are admissible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Supreme Court in *Berghuis v. Thompkins* (2010) and *Davis v. United States* (1994) held that invocation must be unambiguous. "Maybe I should just stop talking" is conditional and equivocal — it expresses uncertainty, not a clear invocation. Police are not required to stop questioning based on ambiguous statements. A suspect must make a clear, unambiguous statement invoking the right. B: Davis v. United States* held that police are not required to seek clarification of an ambiguous invocation. While clarification is permissible, it is not mandatory. If the suspect's statement is ambiguous, questioning may continue. The duty to seek clarification is not part of the Miranda doctrine as established by the Supreme Court. D: While an initial waiver does cover subsequent questioning, that is not the precise reason why the statements are admissible after the remark. A suspect may re-invoke rights during an interview even after waiving. The correct analysis focuses on whether the re-invocation was unambiguous — not on whether the initial waiver remained in effect. The answer is misleading because it suggests no re-invocation is possible after an initial waiver.
lb_rule_reference: Invocation of the right to silence must be unambiguous; an ambiguous or equivocal reference to silence does not require police to stop questioning, and subsequent statements remain admissible.
