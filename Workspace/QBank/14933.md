Question: A niece inherited vacant land from her uncle. She lived in a distant state and decided to sell the land to a colleague who was interested in purchasing the land as an investment. They orally agreed upon a price, and, at the colleague's insistence, the niece agreed to provide him with a warranty deed without any exceptions. The price was paid, the warranty deed was delivered, and the deed was promptly recorded. Neither the niece nor the colleague had, at that point, ever seen the land. After recording the deed, the colleague visited the land for the first time and discovered that it had no access to any public right-of-way and that none of the surrounding lands had ever been held in common ownership with any previous owner of the land. The colleague sued the niece for damages. For whom will the court find?
Answer A: A: The colleague, because lack of access makes title unmarketable.
Answer B: B: The colleague, because the covenants of warranty and quiet enjoyment in the deed were breached.
Answer C: C: The niece, because no title covenants were breached.
Answer D: D: The niece, because the agreement to sell was oral.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: C is correct. When the colleague discovered the lack of access problem, the deed had already been accepted, which means the window for challenging the marketability of title had closed. At that point, the only covenants in place were the future covenants against interference with possession and use of the land and claims of superior title, none of which provide a cause of action based on lack of access.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A is incorrect. The colleague accepted the deed, which completed the contract of sale, discharged most contractual obligations, and closed the window for challenging marketability of title. B is incorrect. Although the covenants of warranty and quiet enjoyment were, in fact, in place after the colleague accepted the deed, the land access problem does not violate either of these covenants, which protect against third party interference with use and/or possession of the land or claims of superior title. D is incorrect. The parties fully performed under the oral agreement, which is an exception to the Statute of Frauds requirement that land sale contracts be in writing.
