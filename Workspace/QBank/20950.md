Outline: Constitutional limitations and justiciability
Question: A business subject to a new rule has already spent money to comply and faces daily fines when the rule takes effect next week. The government argues that the owner has not yet been prosecuted and therefore has no standing at all. What is the best way to frame the justiciability analysis?
Answer A: The only possible issue is standing, and standing always fails before prosecution
Answer B: The court should distinguish concrete injury from timing and assess ripeness through fitness and hardship
Answer C: The case is moot because fines have not yet been imposed
Answer D: Political-question doctrine controls because business regulation is policy
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The business alleges concrete costs, so the remaining timing question is whether the dispute is fit and hardship supports review now.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Compliance costs can be injury; the timing question is ripeness, evaluated through credible threat and hardship. C: Mootness asks whether a once-live dispute ended, not whether an early dispute is sufficiently developed. D: Courts can review regulatory challenges; the question here is injury and timing.
lb_rule_reference: Standing asks whether this plaintiff has injury, traceability, and redressability; ripeness asks whether the dispute is sufficiently developed and hardship-producing for review now.
