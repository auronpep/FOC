Subtopic: Homicide
Outline: Murder
Question: Officers arrested Leo for murder and placed him in the back of a patrol car. Two officers in the front of the car began discussing the case between themselves, saying things like, "It's a shame there are so many handicapped kids at that school — they'll be terrified if they find that gun" and "I hope nobody picks it up before we find it." Leo had received Miranda warnings and invoked his right to silence. After about ten minutes of this conversation, Leo told the officers where the weapon was hidden. He moves to suppress his statement. Should Leo's statement be suppressed?
Answer A: No, because the officers were talking to each other, not to Leo, so there was no interrogation
Answer B: No, because Leo's decision to reveal the gun's location was voluntary and not the product of any police coercion
Answer C: Yes, because the officers' conversation was the functional equivalent of interrogation directed at a suspect who had already invoked his right to silence
Answer D: Yes, because Leo's invocation of silence created an absolute bar to any further communication from police under any circumstances
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Rhode Island v. Innis* (1980), interrogation includes the functional equivalent of questioning: any words or actions by police that they should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. The officers' staged conversation about the welfare of handicapped children and the dangers of an unfound weapon was conduct they should have known would appeal to Leo's sense of guilt and cause him to respond. Because Leo had already invoked his right to silence and the officers did not scrupulously honor that invocation, the statement must be suppressed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The functional equivalent of interrogation is defined by what officers should know is reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response — not by whether a question was directed at the suspect. In *Rhode Island v. Innis* (1980), the Supreme Court held that a nearly identical "conversation" between officers constituted interrogation. The officers should have known their remarks about handicapped children and a hidden gun would appeal to a suspect's conscience and likely produce an incriminating response. B: Miranda does not require physical coercion. The functional equivalent of interrogation requires only that police use words or conduct reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. Psychological inducement — such as appealing to a suspect's concern for children — can constitute interrogation even without overt coercion. The voluntariness inquiry is separate and does not resolve the Miranda issue. D: Invocation of the right to silence requires police to "scrupulously honor" the invocation, but it does not impose an absolute permanent bar on all communication. Police may later re-initiate questioning if they wait a significant time, re-administer warnings, and limit questioning to a different crime. The correct basis for suppression here is that the conduct in this case violated the invocation — not that invocation creates an absolute permanent bar.
lb_rule_reference: Interrogation under Miranda includes not only express questioning but also any words or actions by police that officers should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response from the suspect.
