Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Identifications
Question: Police suspected Roderick of robbing a convenience store. They assembled a five-person lineup in which Roderick was the only person wearing a red hooded sweatshirt — the same type of clothing the robber had been described as wearing. The victim viewed the lineup and identified Roderick. Before trial, the victim testified that she had watched the robber for about three minutes at close range under bright fluorescent lights and was "absolutely certain" Roderick was the man. She had given police a detailed description matching Roderick immediately after the robbery. Should the court suppress the lineup identification on due process grounds?
Answer A: Yes, because the lineup was impermissibly suggestive in that Roderick was the only participant wearing the described clothing
Answer B: No, because the victim's identification was reliable under the totality of circumstances even if the procedure was suggestive
Answer C: No, because the police did not intend to make the lineup suggestive
Answer D: Yes, because permitting the victim to see Roderick in distinctive clothing violated his Fourth Amendment right against unreasonable seizure
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The due process test for identification procedures is two-part (*Neil v. Biggers*; *Manson v. Brathwaite*). Even assuming the lineup was impermissibly suggestive (step one satisfied), suppression is required only if the procedure created a substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification (step two). The reliability factors here all point toward reliability: the victim had a three-minute close-range opportunity to view the perpetrator under good lighting, gave a detailed and accurate description, expressed high certainty, and the identification occurred shortly after the crime. These factors override the suggestiveness. Admission goes to weight, not admissibility.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Suggestiveness alone does not require suppression. The due process test has two parts: (1) was the procedure impermissibly suggestive, and (2) did it create a substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification under the totality of the circumstances? Here, even assuming step one is satisfied, the reliability factors under *Manson v. Brathwaite* strongly favor the identification: close-range three-minute observation, bright lighting, high certainty, accurate prior description, and presumably a short gap between crime and lineup. Because the identification is reliable despite the suggestiveness, due process does not require suppression. C: Police intent is irrelevant to the due process suggestiveness analysis. What matters is whether the procedure was objectively suggestive and whether the identification was unreliable — not whether the officers meant to taint it. D: Identification procedure challenges are due process challenges, not Fourth Amendment claims. The Fourth Amendment governs seizures of persons and property; it does not independently govern the manner in which a lineup is conducted. The correct constitutional hook is the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause, analyzed under the *Biggers/Brathwaite* framework.
lb_rule_reference: A pretrial identification violates due process only if (1) the procedure was impermissibly suggestive AND (2) the suggestive procedure, under the totality of the circumstances, created a substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification.
