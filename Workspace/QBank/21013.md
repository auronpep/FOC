Question: A federal statute limits eligibility for a certain federal public benefits program to U.S. citizens and lawfully admitted aliens who have resided in the United States for at least five years. A lawfully admitted alien who has resided in the country for three years and meets all other eligibility criteria is denied benefits because she does not yet satisfy the five-year residency requirement. She argues the residency-plus-citizen limitation violates the Equal Protection component of the Fifth Amendment Due Process Clause. Which standard of review applies to the federal statute's alienage-based benefit limitation, and what must the government show?
Answer A: Strict scrutiny, because alienage is a suspect classification and the Fifth Amendment applies the same Equal Protection principles to the federal government as the Fourteenth Amendment applies to states
Answer B: Intermediate scrutiny, because alienage is a quasi-suspect classification when employed by the federal government in public benefit programs affecting lawfully admitted residents
Answer C: Rational basis review, because Congress has plenary power over immigration and naturalization; the five-year residency requirement is rationally related to the legitimate federal interest in phasing in public benefit access for newly arrived residents
Answer D: Rational basis review, but the statute fails because the five-year waiting period has no genuine relationship to citizenship status or fiscal sustainability
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Federal alienage classifications are reviewed under rational basis because Congress has plenary power over immigration. The five-year residency requirement is rationally related to the legitimate government interest in gradually extending benefit access to new permanent residents and ensuring fiscal sustainability of federal programs. Under rational basis, the challenger must negate every conceivable justification, which is very difficult to do.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Supreme Court has recognized a critical asymmetry: federal alienage classifications receive rational basis review because Congress has plenary power over immigration and naturalization. Even though state alienage laws receive strict scrutiny, the federal government's power over who enters and stays in the country justifies significantly more deference. Federal alienage laws need only be rationally related to a legitimate federal interest. B: The Supreme Court has not established an intermediate tier for federal alienage classifications. Federal alienage laws receive rational basis. There is no intermediate-scrutiny lane for federal alien benefit restrictions. The asymmetry is binary: state alienage = strict; federal alienage = rational basis. D: Under rational basis review, the challenger bears the burden to negate every conceivable justification. Courts will hypothesize reasons; the government is not required to prove actual efficacy. A five-year residency requirement as a proxy for fiscal responsibility or integration is at minimum conceivable, which is all rational basis requires. The statute survives.
lb_rule_reference: Federal laws that classify based on alienage receive rational basis review because Congress has plenary power over immigration; this is a recognized exception to the general rule that alienage classifications receive strict scrutiny.
