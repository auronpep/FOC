Question: Paul is tried in federal court for wire fraud and chooses not to testify. During closing argument, the prosecutor asks the jury to treat Paul's silence as evidence that he could not deny the government's proof. Paul objects. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Overrule the objection, because adverse inferences are always permitted from silence.
Answer B: Overrule the objection if the evidence against Paul is strong.
Answer C: Sustain the objection only if Paul invoked the privilege outside the jury's presence.
Answer D: Sustain the objection, because the prosecution may not comment on a criminal defendant's failure to testify as evidence of guilt.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: In a criminal trial, the Fifth Amendment bars prosecutorial comment or court instruction treating the defendant's failure to testify as evidence of guilt. The decisive axis is criminal defendant silence, not ordinary civil adverse inference.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because This imports the civil adverse-inference rule into a criminal trial. B: B is wrong because Strength of the prosecution's case does not permit comment on the defendant's silence. C: C is wrong because The no-comment rule does not turn on where Paul asserted the privilege.
lb_rule_reference: Fifth Amendment / Griffin no adverse comment
lb_student_diagnostic: Student is failing to toggle between civil consequence and criminal no-comment rules.
