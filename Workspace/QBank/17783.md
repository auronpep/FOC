MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Intentional Torts
Outline: Battery
Question: At a picnic, Dan threw a soft rubber ball near Paula's face intending only to make Paula flinch. The ball took an unexpected bounce, struck Paula's cheek, and knocked off her glasses. Dan did not intend the ball to touch Paula. Paula sues Dan for battery. Can Dan's assault intent support battery liability?
Answer A: No, because Dan intended only apprehension, not contact
Answer B: No, because transferred intent applies only when the plaintiff is a different person
Answer C: Yes, because Dan intended a covered assault and a covered battery contact actually resulted
Answer D: Yes, but only if Paula proves Dan wanted to break her glasses
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Dan intended to create apprehension of contact, a covered intentional tort. The resulting offensive contact was battery, also in the transferred-intent circuit.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Assault and battery are both in the traditional transferred-intent circuit. Assault intent can support battery when contact results. B: Transferred intent can also move among covered torts, not just between people. D: Battery liability does not require intent to damage property or cause the exact harm.
lb_rule_reference: Intent to commit assault can transfer to battery when the defendant intends apprehension and harmful or offensive contact actually results within the transferred-intent circuit.
