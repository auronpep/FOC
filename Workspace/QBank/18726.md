Outline: Presumptions and inferences
Question: In a federal criminal trial, Paul is charged with possessing forged coins. The prosecutor requests this instruction: “If you find that Paul hid the coins in his boot and lied about his name, you may infer that he knew the coins were forged, but you are not required to draw that inference.” Paul's lawyer objects. Which objection is strongest?
Answer A: That the instruction violates a privilege against answering questions about the coins.
Answer B: That the instruction incorrectly tells the jury it must find knowledge.
Answer C: That the instruction is improper because only civil juries may draw evidentiary inferences.
Answer D: No objection is strong if the evidence rationally supports the permissive inference and the burden remains on the government.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: The proposed instruction is permissive. If the evidence rationally supports the inference, the jury may be told it may infer knowledge while remaining free to reject the inference. The prosecution still bears the burden.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because no privilege is implicated by the jury instruction. B: B is wrong because the instruction says may, not must. C: C is wrong because criminal juries may draw permissive inferences when the instruction does not shift the burden.
lb_rule_reference: Permissive inference / best objection
