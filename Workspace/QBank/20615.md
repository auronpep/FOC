MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Civil Procedure
Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Federal-Question Jurisdiction
Question: An employee sues her former employer in federal court under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), 42 U.S.C. § 12101 et seq., alleging that the employer unlawfully terminated her because of her disability. The complaint pleads only the ADA claim and seeks $120,000 in damages including back pay, compensatory damages, and attorneys' fees. The defendant files a 12(b)(1) motion to dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction, arguing that because the plaintiff did not exhaust her state administrative remedies before filing, the federal court lacks jurisdiction. The governing forum is federal court under the MBE baseline. Should the court grant the 12(b)(1) motion?
Answer A: Yes, because a plaintiff who fails to exhaust required administrative remedies deprives the federal court of subject matter jurisdiction over the claim
Answer B: No, because the ADA is a federal statute that creates the plaintiff's cause of action, satisfying § 1331's federal-question requirement, and exhaustion failure is not a jurisdictional defect
Answer C: Yes, because the plaintiff seeks $120,000, and claims in excess of $75,000 filed under federal statutes must satisfy both § 1331 and § 1332 to proceed
Answer D: No, but only because the plaintiff also satisfies § 1332 diversity jurisdiction since the damages exceed $75,000, giving the court an independent basis for jurisdiction
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Federal-question jurisdiction under § 1331 is established when a federal statute creates the plaintiff's cause of action and the plaintiff sues under that statute. The ADA expressly creates a cause of action for disability discrimination in employment. The plaintiff's complaint pleads that claim. The § 1331 inquiry is satisfied. Failure to exhaust administrative remedies may warrant dismissal on other grounds — but it is not a defect in subject matter jurisdiction. The 12(b)(1) motion should be denied.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Subject matter jurisdiction under § 1331 is satisfied when the plaintiff sues under a federal statute that creates the cause of action. The ADA is such a statute; the plaintiff has invoked it. Failure to exhaust administrative remedies may be a ground for dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) (for failure to state a claim) or under a specific statutory prerequisite defense — but it is not a subject matter jurisdiction defect under § 1331. The federal court has jurisdiction over ADA claims; exhaustion is a claim-processing rule, not a jurisdictional bar. C: A federal-question case under § 1331 requires no amount in controversy and no diversity of citizenship. The $75,000 threshold is a § 1332 diversity requirement only. A plaintiff who files under a federal statute that creates the cause of action satisfies § 1331 regardless of the amount claimed and regardless of the parties' citizenships. D: The answer reaches the right result (deny the motion) but incorrectly frames the basis. § 1332 diversity is not established — the parties' citizenships are not specified, so there is no basis to assume diversity. More importantly, § 1331 federal-question jurisdiction is independently and clearly established by the ADA claim. The court does not need § 1332, and relying on an unestablished jurisdictional basis is error.
lb_rule_reference: Under § 1331, the clearest basis for federal-question jurisdiction is when federal law creates the plaintiff's cause of action — when a federal statute expressly grants the right to sue and defines the elements of the claim.
