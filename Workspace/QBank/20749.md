Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Police unlawfully opened the locked trunk of a car owned and driven by Dana. Passenger Paul had no key, no permission to use the trunk, and no luggage in it. Inside the trunk, officers found a backpack of stolen phones that Paul admits belonged to him. Paul moves to suppress the phones. Should Paul's suppression motion be granted?
Answer A: Yes, because Paul owned the seized phones
Answer B: Yes, because any illegal search of the car protects all occupants
Answer C: No, because Paul had no personal reasonable expectation of privacy or property interest in Dana's locked trunk
Answer D: No, because evidence found in a vehicle trunk is never subject to suppression
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Paul's Fourth Amendment rights were not violated by the search of Dana's trunk. He owned the phones, but he had no key, permission, luggage, or access interest in the trunk. Without personal standing, his suppression motion fails.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Standing turns on Paul's privacy or property interest in the searched place or item searched, not ownership of contraband discovered in someone else's trunk. B: A passenger does not automatically gain standing to challenge a trunk search when he lacks a recognized interest in the trunk. D: A trunk search can be unlawful and suppressible by a defendant with standing, such as the owner or someone with a privacy interest in the trunk.
lb_rule_reference: A defendant may suppress evidence only when the challenged search or seizure violated that defendant's own Fourth Amendment interest.
