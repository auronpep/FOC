Subtopic: Intentional Torts
Outline: Battery
Question: A homeowner angrily threw a cup at a trespasser, intending to commit battery. The cup missed everyone and shattered against a fence. A neighbor who saw the incident from across the street was startled and later claimed emotional distress, but the homeowner did not know the neighbor was watching. The neighbor sues for intentional infliction of emotional distress based only on transferred intent. Is transferred intent enough for the neighbor's IIED claim?
Answer A: No, because transferred intent does not automatically move battery intent into IIED for an unknown bystander
Answer B: Yes, because intent transfers to every tort that produces any injury
Answer C: Yes, because the homeowner intended an intentional tort against someone
Answer D: No, but only because the cup hit the fence rather than a person
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: IIED is outside the ordinary transferred-intent set; the neighbor needs the IIED elements, not just transferred battery intent.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Transferred intent is limited and does not cover every tort or injury. C: A general intentional-tort label does not supply IIED intent, recklessness, extreme conduct, and severe distress. D: Contact failure matters less than the doctrinal boundary: IIED does not receive automatic transferred intent.
lb_rule_reference: Transferred intent generally applies among the traditional intentional torts, but it does not automatically transfer into IIED in the same way.
