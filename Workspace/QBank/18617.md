Subtopic: Strict Liability and Products Liability
Outline: Strict Products Liability
Question: A homeowner sold an old space heater at a neighborhood garage sale. The homeowner did not regularly sell appliances and had never operated a retail business. Unknown to both parties, the heater had a defect in its internal wiring when sold. The buyer used it normally, and it sparked a fire that damaged the buyer's furniture. The buyer sues the homeowner under strict products liability. Is the buyer likely to prevail on a strict products liability claim against the homeowner?
Answer A: Yes, because the heater was defective when it left the homeowner's possession and caused property damage
Answer B: No, because the homeowner was a casual seller rather than a commercial supplier of heaters
Answer C: No, because strict products liability covers personal injury but never property damage
Answer D: Yes, because any seller of a defective product is strictly liable once the buyer uses the product normally
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The homeowner did not regularly sell heaters or operate in the distribution chain. Strict products liability is clipped at commercial-seller status, even though ordinary negligence or warranty theories may have separate rules.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Strict products liability also requires a commercial supplier. A homeowner's isolated garage-sale transaction is not the regular commercial sale of heaters. C: Strict products liability can cover physical harm to person or property. The problem is the defendant's casual-seller status, not the presence of property damage. D: The rule does not cover every seller. It targets commercial suppliers in the product distribution chain.
lb_rule_reference: Strict products liability generally applies to commercial suppliers, not casual one-time sellers outside the regular business of selling that product.
