Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Definition
Question: Rebecca sues a restaurant, claiming that its soup caused a severe allergic reaction. The restaurant offers evidence that Rebecca ate a dessert containing peanuts twenty minutes before the reaction. Rebecca objects that the dessert evidence does not prove the soup was safe. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the evidence, because it does not conclusively establish that the dessert caused the reaction.
Answer B: Exclude the evidence unless the restaurant proves Rebecca is allergic to peanuts before the jury hears it.
Answer C: Admit the evidence, because the dessert has some tendency to support an alternative-cause theory.
Answer D: Admit the evidence only if no ingredient in the soup could cause an allergic reaction.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Evidence that Rebecca ate another possible allergen shortly before the reaction has some tendency to make an alternative cause more probable. It need not prove the soup was safe or conclusively identify the cause.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because conclusive causation is not required for relevance. B: B is wrong because the dessert evidence may become relevant through the surrounding circumstances and later proof; the question assumes no separate foundation bar. D: D is wrong because alternative-cause evidence can be relevant even if the plaintiff's theory remains possible.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401 / FRE 402
lb_student_diagnostic: The trap is excluding alternative-cause evidence unless it defeats the plaintiff's theory entirely.
