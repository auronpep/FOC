Outline: Prior inconsistent statement
Question: In a federal negligence trial, Miriam testifies that she saw the blue truck stop before entering the intersection. On cross-examination, counsel asks Miriam about a signed statement she gave to an investigator the day after the accident, in which she wrote, “The blue truck rolled through the stop sign.” Miriam admits she signed the statement but says she was mistaken then and is correct now. The opponent offers the statement. For what purpose, if any, is the statement admissible?
Answer A: It is inadmissible for any purpose because Miriam admitted making it.
Answer B: It is admissible only to impeach Miriam's credibility, not as substantive proof that the truck rolled through the stop sign.
Answer C: It is admissible as substantive proof because any prior inconsistent statement by a testifying witness is nonhearsay.
Answer D: It is admissible as substantive proof only if the investigator also testifies.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: A prior inconsistent statement may be used to impeach a testifying witness. But under the federal substantive prior-statement route, an inconsistent statement is nonhearsay only if it was given under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition. Miriam's signed investigator statement was not given in one of those settings, so it is admissible only for impeachment.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because admitting the prior statement does not bar its use to impeach; the inconsistency remains relevant to Miriam's credibility. C: C is wrong because federal law does not make every prior inconsistent statement substantive evidence. D: D is wrong because the investigator's testimony does not convert the statement into a sworn proceeding or deposition statement.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 613 and FRE 801(d)(1)(A) / unsworn prior inconsistent statement
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests the core federal distinction between impeachment use and substantive use of a prior inconsistent statement.
