Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Diversity Jurisdiction
Question: A plaintiff, a citizen of Delaware, sues a logistics corporation for property damage. The corporation was incorporated in Delaware. The corporation's largest warehouse and most of its employees are in Ohio. However, the corporation's CEO, CFO, and board of directors all operate out of the corporation's headquarters in Illinois, where all major operational and strategic decisions are made. The plaintiff asserts diversity jurisdiction and the case is filed in federal court. The corporation moves to dismiss. The governing forum is federal court under the MBE baseline. Should the court dismiss for lack of diversity jurisdiction?
Answer A: No, because the corporation's principal place of business is in Ohio, where most of its employees and physical assets are located, making the parties diverse
Answer B: Yes, because a corporation's citizenship for diversity purposes is determined solely by its state of incorporation
Answer C: Yes, because the corporation is a citizen of Delaware (incorporation) and Illinois (nerve center), and the plaintiff is a citizen of Delaware, destroying complete diversity
Answer D: No, because the corporation is incorporated in Delaware and the plaintiff is from Delaware, but the corporation's nerve center in Illinois provides an independent basis for diversity
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: A corporation is a citizen of its state of incorporation and the state where its principal place of business is located. Under *Hertz*, the principal place of business is the nerve center — where the corporation's top officers direct, control, and coordinate its activities. Here, the CEO, CFO, and board operate from Illinois, making that the nerve center. The corporation is therefore a citizen of Delaware and Illinois. The plaintiff is a citizen of Delaware. Both share Delaware citizenship; complete diversity is absent and the court must dismiss.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Supreme Court in *Hertz Corp. v. Friend* adopted the nerve center test: the principal place of business is where the corporation's officers direct, control, and coordinate the corporation's activities — typically the headquarters. Here, the CEO, CFO, and board operate from Illinois. That is the nerve center regardless of where most employees work. B: Under § 1332(c)(1), a corporation has two citizenships: state of incorporation and state of principal place of business. Both must be considered. Here the principal place of business (nerve center) is Illinois. The corporation is a citizen of Delaware and Illinois. The plaintiff is a citizen of Delaware. Delaware citizenship is shared, so diversity is destroyed — and this answer also applies the wrong one-citizenship rule. D: Complete diversity requires that no plaintiff share citizenship with any defendant. The corporation's Delaware citizenship (state of incorporation) coincides with the plaintiff's Delaware citizenship. The corporation's second citizenship in Illinois does not cure the Delaware overlap. You cannot mix-and-match citizenships to create diversity; each party's full citizenship constellation must be non-overlapping with every adverse party's citizenship.
lb_rule_reference: 28 U.S.C. § 1332(c)(1) — a corporation is a citizen of the state of incorporation and the state where its principal place of business is located (the nerve center: headquarters where officers direct, control, and coordinate the corporation's activities).
