Question: Raymond has a documented brain injury that impairs his ability to form complex premeditated plans. He kills his coworker during a sudden rage after a verbal altercation. The prosecution charges Raymond with first-degree murder, which requires premeditation and deliberation. Raymond's neuropsychologist testifies that the brain injury made Raymond incapable of forming the premeditated intent required for first-degree murder. Raymond does not meet the M'Naghten insanity standard — he knew what he was doing and knew it was wrong. The jurisdiction recognizes the diminished capacity doctrine. What is the most accurate statement about Raymond's defense options?
Answer A: Raymond should be acquitted by reason of insanity because his brain injury is a recognized mental defect
Answer B: Raymond can use diminished capacity evidence to negate premeditation, potentially reducing the charge to second-degree murder or manslaughter
Answer C: Raymond has no defense because he knew what he was doing and knew it was wrong
Answer D: Raymond should raise the Durham "product of mental disease" test as an alternative insanity theory
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Diminished capacity is a partial defense that operates within the mens rea framework rather than providing a separate excuse. Where recognized, it allows a defendant to introduce evidence of mental impairment to show that he lacked the specific intent required for a charged offense. First-degree murder requires premeditation and deliberation — specific intent elements. If Raymond's brain injury made him incapable of premeditation, the prosecution cannot establish first-degree murder. The result is not acquittal but a reduction to a lesser included offense (second-degree murder, which requires intent to kill but not premeditation, or voluntary manslaughter if provocation is present). Raymond still committed a killing; diminished capacity only removes the aggravating premeditation element.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Raymond does not meet M'Naghten — he knew what he was doing and knew it was wrong. A brain injury that fails to satisfy the applicable insanity standard does not produce an acquittal by reason of insanity. The existence of a recognized condition is a threshold but not a sufficient condition for an insanity verdict. C: Failing an insanity test does not eliminate all mental-state defenses. Diminished capacity is a distinct doctrine that operates at the mens rea level, not the excuse level. Even without insanity, Raymond may be able to use his brain injury to negate the premeditation element of first-degree murder. D: Durham ("the act was a product of mental disease or defect") is a minority test adopted only briefly in a few jurisdictions and largely abandoned. It does not apply in most states. More importantly, it is still an insanity defense that results in acquittal — not a partial defense to mens rea like diminished capacity. The correct partial remedy on these facts is diminished capacity, not an alternative insanity test.
lb_rule_reference: Diminished capacity is not an insanity defense; it is a partial defense that uses evidence of mental impairment to negate the specific mens rea element of the charged crime, potentially reducing the degree of the offense; it does not result in acquittal and is not available in all jurisdictions
