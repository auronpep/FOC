MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Identifications
Question: An undercover officer purchased narcotics directly from a dealer in a well-lit apartment. The transaction lasted approximately four minutes, during which the officer observed the dealer at close range. The officer gave a detailed description of the dealer to his supervisor immediately afterward. Two days later, a detective showed the officer a single photograph of Jimmy, described to the officer as "the guy who sold to you." The officer immediately identified Jimmy from the photo. Jimmy moved to suppress, arguing the single-photo showup was impermissibly suggestive. Should the court suppress the identification?
Answer A: Yes, because showing a single photograph and describing it as the perpetrator is per se impermissibly suggestive and requires automatic suppression
Answer B: No, because the officer was a trained law enforcement professional whose identification should receive a presumption of reliability
Answer C: No, because even though the procedure was suggestive, the identification was sufficiently reliable under the totality of the circumstances
Answer D: Yes, because the identification must be suppressed whenever police fail to use a proper multi-photo array
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The single-photo display with a suggestive description is impermissibly suggestive (step one satisfied). But under *Manson v. Brathwaite*, suppression is required only if the totality of circumstances shows a substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification. Weighing the reliability factors: the officer had a four-minute, close-range opportunity to view the dealer under good lighting (strong opportunity to view); was actively engaged in a buy transaction (high degree of attention); gave a detailed description immediately after (accurate prior description); and identified the photo immediately (high certainty). The two-day interval is short. These factors outweigh the corrupting effect of the suggestive procedure. The identification is admissible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: There is no per se rule of exclusion for suggestive identification procedures. *Manson v. Brathwaite* rejected a per se rule in favor of a totality-of-circumstances reliability analysis. Even a plainly suggestive procedure does not require automatic suppression if the identification is independently reliable. Courts weigh the corrupting effect of the suggestive procedure against the *Brathwaite* reliability factors. B: There is no constitutional presumption of reliability for law enforcement witness identifications. The correct analysis applies the *Brathwaite* factors to the specific circumstances. The officer's training is relevant to the "degree of attention" factor, but framing the outcome as a presumption misstates the doctrine and misapplies the framework. D: Failure to use a multi-photo array is relevant to the suggestiveness analysis, but it does not automatically require suppression. The due process test asks whether the suggestive procedure created a substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification — not whether the police used the ideal procedure. If the identification is reliable despite the shortcut, it is admissible.
lb_rule_reference: Under *Manson v. Brathwaite*, courts assess reliability by weighing: (1) the witness's opportunity to view, (2) the witness's degree of attention, (3) the accuracy of the prior description, (4) the level of certainty at the confrontation, and (5) the time between the crime and the confrontation — against the corrupting effect of the suggestive procedure.
