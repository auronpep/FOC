Question: A magazine published a public-concern story about violent political protests and used a photo of a peaceful volunteer beside the caption, "Organizers of the attacks." Editors had received the photographer's note saying the volunteer was not involved in violence, but used the photo anyway. The volunteer sues for false light. What mental-state showing most directly supports the claim?
Answer A: Mere embarrassment from appearing in the article
Answer B: Actual malice, shown by using the photo despite knowing the violent implication was false or seriously doubting it
Answer C: Strict liability, because the caption was misleading
Answer D: Special damages, because false light is a trade-libel tort
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The editors had a note contradicting the implication and used the photo anyway. That supports knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Public-concern false light requires attention to constitutional fault, not embarrassment alone. C: Public-concern false light has constitutional limits; strict liability is not enough. D: False light is a privacy tort and turns here on misleading publicity plus constitutional fault.
lb_rule_reference: When false light involves a matter of public concern, constitutional fault limits can require proof of actual malice.
