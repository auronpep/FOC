Question: Mary's son, Samuel, owed a bank $30,000. The bank threatened to sue Samuel. Mary orally told the bank, “If Samuel does not pay, I will pay the debt.” Mary received no business benefit from the promise, but she wanted to protect Samuel from embarrassment. Samuel defaulted, and the bank sued Mary. Which answer is best?
Answer A: The bank can enforce the promise because love and family loyalty are the main-purpose exception.
Answer B: The bank can enforce the promise because the son's original debt was supported by consideration.
Answer C: The bank generally cannot enforce Mary's oral collateral promise because it is a suretyship promise within the Statute of Frauds and no economic main-purpose exception applies.
Answer D: The bank can enforce the promise because a suretyship promise is outside the Statute of Frauds when made after the original debt exists.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Mary promised to pay Samuel's debt if Samuel did not pay. That is a collateral suretyship promise. Emotional or family motivation is not the main-purpose exception; the main object must be the promisor's own economic advantage. Without a sufficient writing, the promise is generally unenforceable.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because family affection is not enough for the main-purpose exception. B: B is wrong because consideration for Samuel's original debt does not satisfy the writing requirement for Mary's collateral promise. D: D is wrong because timing after the original debt does not remove the suretyship category.
lb_rule_reference: Suretyship SOF / family benefit not enough
