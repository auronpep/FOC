Question: A male defendant is charged with sexual assault. During voir dire, defense counsel uses five of six peremptory strikes to remove women from the jury panel. The prosecutor raises an objection under Batson. Defense counsel responds that (1) Batson only prohibits race-based discrimination and does not apply to gender; and (2) even if Batson applies to gender, it only restricts the prosecution, not the defense. The trial court sustains the objection and seats the five women. The defendant is convicted. On appeal, the defendant argues that the trial court erred in sustaining the objection. Is the defendant's argument correct?
Answer A: Yes, because Batson prohibits only race-based peremptory strikes, and the Equal Protection Clause does not extend to gender-based classifications in jury selection
Answer B: Yes, because even if Batson applies to gender, it binds only the prosecution, not defense counsel, because the Equal Protection Clause restricts only state actors
Answer C: No, because J.E.B. extended Batson to gender-based strikes, and Georgia v. McCollum extended Batson to defense peremptory strikes, so the trial court correctly sustained the objection
Answer D: No, but only because Batson applies to gender, and the trial court should have required gender-neutral explanations rather than automatically seating the stricken jurors
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Two extensions of Batson resolve the defendant's arguments. First, J.E.B. v. Alabama (1994) applied intermediate scrutiny to gender-based peremptory strikes and held they violate equal protection, just as race-based strikes do. Second, Georgia v. McCollum (1992) held that defendants are also subject to Batson because the exercise of peremptory strikes in a court proceeding constitutes state action. Accordingly, the trial court correctly required defense counsel to provide gender-neutral explanations for the pattern of strikes against women. The defendant cannot insulate discriminatory strikes behind either the "race only" or "defendant only" arguments.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B. (1994) held that the Equal Protection Clause also prohibits gender-based peremptory strikes. The Court applied intermediate scrutiny and found no exceedingly persuasive justification for sex-based generalizations in jury selection. Gender-based strikes are treated the same as race-based strikes under Batson doctrine. B: Georgia v. McCollum (1992) held that Batson applies to defense peremptory strikes as well. Although defense counsel is nominally a private actor, the Court held that jury selection is a state-sponsored proceeding and that a defendant exercising peremptory strikes acts with the assistance of the court, making the discrimination attributable to the state. Therefore, the Equal Protection Clause bars defense counsel from using race-based (and, by extension under J.E.B., gender-based) peremptory strikes. D: The answer choice is partially correct that defense counsel should first offer a gender-neutral explanation (Batson step two), but "No" is still the right answer to the defendant's core argument that the trial court erred. The choice's framing ("No, but only because") creates a distinction that is not outcome-determinative—the trial court's ruling was correct regardless of whether the record reflects that defense counsel was given a full opportunity to provide neutral explanations. This answer choice does not correctly resolve the defendant's appeal.
lb_rule_reference: J.E.B. v. Alabama extended Batson to gender-based peremptory strikes; Georgia v. McCollum extended Batson to defense peremptory strikes; both race and gender discrimination in peremptory strikes violate equal protection
