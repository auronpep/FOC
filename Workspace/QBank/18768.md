Subtopic: Inchoate Crimes
Outline: Conspiracy
Question: After Clifton was indicted for conspiracy to distribute cocaine, he was released on bail and awaiting trial. The government arranged for Clifton's longtime friend, who had agreed to cooperate with the government, to meet with Clifton socially. At the government's direction, the friend steered their conversation toward the drug conspiracy and Clifton made several detailed incriminating statements. Clifton had no idea his friend was a government agent. No Miranda warnings were given. At trial, the government sought to introduce the statements. Should the court admit Clifton's statements?
Answer A: Yes, because Clifton was not in custody and Miranda warnings were not required
Answer B: Yes, because the use of a private citizen (rather than a sworn officer) to gather information does not implicate constitutional protections
Answer C: No, because the government deliberately elicited statements from Clifton after indictment in violation of his Sixth Amendment right to counsel
Answer D: No, because any statement obtained by government deception after indictment is automatically inadmissible
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Massiah v. United States*, once formal adversarial proceedings have commenced (here, the indictment), the government may not deliberately elicit incriminating statements from the defendant in the absence of counsel without a valid waiver. The government directed the informant to steer the conversation toward the conspiracy, constituting deliberate elicitation. Clifton had no knowledge that his friend was a government agent and therefore could not have knowingly waived his right. The statements must be suppressed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The relevant protection is the Sixth Amendment right to counsel under *Massiah v. United States*, which does not require custody. Once formal adversarial proceedings begin, the government may not deliberately elicit statements from the defendant in the absence of counsel, whether in custody or not. B: Massiah* expressly held that the government cannot circumvent the Sixth Amendment by using a government agent (including an informant acting under government direction) rather than a uniformed officer. The use of an informant directed by the government to elicit statements is the paradigmatic *Massiah* violation. D: The correct basis is the *Massiah* rule — deliberate elicitation after formal proceedings without counsel — not a general rule against government deception. Not all deception-obtained statements are inadmissible; pre-indictment undercover operations, for example, do not violate the Sixth Amendment.
lb_rule_reference: Under Massiah, the government may not use an informant to deliberately elicit statements from a defendant after formal adversarial proceedings have commenced, regardless of whether the defendant is in custody
