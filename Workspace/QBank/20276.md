Outline: Laying a Foundation
Question: In a federal accounting dispute, Joseph offers a chart summarizing 3,000 invoices. Joseph's bookkeeper testifies that the chart accurately summarizes the invoices, and Joseph made the invoices available to the defendant for inspection before trial. The defendant objects that the chart is not one of the original invoices. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the chart because summaries of documents are never admissible.
Answer B: Admit the chart if the underlying invoices are voluminous, admissible, and available for examination.
Answer C: Exclude the chart unless all 3,000 invoices are read to the jury first.
Answer D: Admit the chart as a substitute even if the underlying invoices are inadmissible and unavailable.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: A summary may be used to prove the content of voluminous writings that cannot be conveniently examined in court, if the underlying materials are admissible and made available for examination or copying. The summary is not excluded merely because it is not an original invoice.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because summaries can be admissible. C: C is wrong because the point of a summary is to avoid inconvenient examination of voluminous records. D: D is wrong because the underlying materials must be admissible and available.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 1006 / summary foundation / underlying records available
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests summary-chart foundation and original-writing false positives.
