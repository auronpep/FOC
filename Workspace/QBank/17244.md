Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Identifications
Question: A federal grand jury indicted Trevor for aggravated assault. One week after the indictment, an FBI agent visited the sole eyewitness at her home and showed her a folder containing eight photographs, including one of Trevor. The agent did not notify Trevor's appointed counsel before the session. The witness identified Trevor's photograph. At trial, the prosecution called the agent to testify about the photo array identification and also asked the witness to make an in-court identification. Was Trevor's Sixth Amendment right to counsel violated when the FBI agent conducted the photo array without notifying defense counsel?
Answer A: Yes, because Trevor had already been indicted and the Sixth Amendment right to counsel had attached, requiring notice to defense counsel before any identification procedure
Answer B: Yes, because the agent showed the photos without a neutral administrator present
Answer C: No, because the Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not apply to photo arrays, even after indictment
Answer D: No, because the right to counsel attaches only at trial, not at pretrial identification procedures
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: United States v. Ash* squarely held that the Sixth Amendment does not require the presence or notification of counsel at a post-indictment photo array. The Court reasoned that because the accused is not physically present during a photo display, there is no "confrontation" at which counsel's presence is needed to observe and reconstruct events. The photo array identification is therefore admissible on Sixth Amendment grounds. (Due process suggestiveness claims remain available but are not raised on these facts.)
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Supreme Court held in *United States v. Ash* (1973) that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not extend to photo arrays, even after indictment. Because the accused is not present at the photo session, there is no "critical confrontation" between the accused and the state requiring the guiding hand of counsel. The *Wade* right is limited to in-person lineups and showups that occur post-indictment. B: There is no federal constitutional requirement that photo arrays be administered by a neutral or blind administrator. Best practices and some state statutes recommend blind administration, but the Sixth Amendment does not mandate it. The question is about constitutional rights, not best practices. D: This answer reaches the right result for the wrong reason. The Sixth Amendment right to counsel does attach at certain pretrial stages — post-indictment in-person lineups are a canonical example under *Wade*. The correct reason no violation occurred here is the specific *Ash* rule limiting counsel rights to in-person procedures, not a general rule that pretrial proceedings are exempt.
lb_rule_reference: Under *United States v. Ash*, there is no Sixth Amendment right to counsel at a photo array, even after indictment, because a photo array is not a critical stage requiring the guiding hand of counsel.
