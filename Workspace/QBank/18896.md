MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Constitutional Law
Subtopic: Individual Rights
Outline: Intermediate scrutiny (quasi-suspect) classifications
Question: A state statute requires that in divorce proceedings where the parties have a child under age six, the child's primary custody must be presumptively awarded to the mother, rebuttable only by clear and convincing evidence that the mother is unfit. The legislative record shows the presumption was based on the view that "mothers are naturally more nurturing for young children." A father challenges the maternal presumption under the Equal Protection Clause. Which analysis most accurately describes the Equal Protection problem with the maternal custody presumption?
Answer A: The statute survives intermediate scrutiny because there is empirical support for the proposition that mothers of young children have historically been primary caregivers
Answer B: The statute is subject to rational basis because family law is a traditional state function in which courts defer to legislative judgments about child welfare
Answer C: The statute likely fails intermediate scrutiny because a presumption that mothers are more nurturing rests on an overbroad gender generalization rather than an exceedingly persuasive justification tied to actual individual characteristics
Answer D: The statute fails strict scrutiny because the maternal presumption is a sex-based classification that creates a de facto suspect class
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Sex-based classifications require intermediate scrutiny with an exceedingly persuasive justification. The maternal presumption rests on a classic gender stereotype — mothers are inherently more nurturing. This is precisely the type of overbroad generalization the Equal Protection Clause prohibits: using sex as a proxy for individual parenting capacity when individualized custody hearings could address actual parenting ability without resorting to sex-based presumptions.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Intermediate scrutiny prohibits the government from relying on overbroad gender stereotypes, even stereotypes that have some statistical grounding. The government may not use sex as a proxy for nurturing capacity when individual determinations are available. Generalizations about mothers — even historically accurate ones — do not constitute the exceedingly persuasive justification required. B: The Equal Protection Clause applies to state family law statutes. Where the classification is sex-based, intermediate scrutiny applies regardless of the subject-matter domain. Traditional state authority over family law does not lower the applicable scrutiny tier. D: Sex-based classifications receive intermediate scrutiny, not strict scrutiny. Fathers are not a suspect class. The appropriate standard is the intermediate scrutiny tier established for sex. The statute fails under that standard because of the stereotype rationale — but the failure is at intermediate scrutiny, not strict scrutiny.
lb_rule_reference: A sex-based classification fails intermediate scrutiny when the government's justification rests on overbroad generalizations about the relative abilities or preferences of the sexes rather than on actual differences.
