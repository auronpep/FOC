Subtopic: Intentional Torts
Outline: Battery
Question: A commuter using a cane waited for an elevator. Another commuter wanted to board first, so he intentionally kicked the cane out from under the first commuter's hand. The cane struck the floor, and the first commuter stumbled but did not fall or feel fear before the kick occurred. The first commuter sues the other commuter for battery. Is the first commuter likely to prevail in a battery claim against the other commuter?
Answer A: Yes, because the cane was closely connected to the commuter's person and the kick was offensive contact
Answer B: No, because the defendant never touched the commuter's skin
Answer C: No, because the facts show assault rather than battery
Answer D: No, unless the commuter can prove medical expenses from the stumble
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Battery can be based on contact with something closely connected to the plaintiff's body. The defendant intentionally kicked the cane out of the plaintiff's hand, creating offensive contact.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Objects closely connected to the body, such as a cane being held for support, are treated as part of the protected personal sphere for battery. C: Assault requires apprehension of imminent contact. The plaintiff did not apprehend the kick before it happened; the completed offensive contact supports battery. D: Battery is actionable for offensive contact without proof of medical expenses. Nominal or dignitary damages may be available.
lb_rule_reference: Contact with an item closely connected to the plaintiff's body can satisfy battery when the contact is harmful or offensive.
