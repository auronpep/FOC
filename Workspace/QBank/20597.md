Outline: Mistake
Question: Scenario A: Olivia finds an expensive-looking watch on a park bench. She believes it was abandoned and takes it home, intending to keep it. The watch actually belongs to a man who set it down briefly. Olivia is charged with larceny (a specific intent crime — requires intent to permanently deprive). Scenario B: Olivia's friend Derek takes the same watch under the mistaken belief that keeping found property is not illegal in this state. Derek is charged with larceny. Derek's belief about the law was wrong. Which of the following correctly analyzes both scenarios?
Answer A: Olivia is guilty; Derek is not guilty — her mistake was unreasonable, his was about the law and is a valid defense
Answer B: Olivia is not guilty because her honest mistake of fact negates the specific intent for larceny; Derek is guilty because mistake of law is generally no defense
Answer C: Both Olivia and Derek are not guilty because both had genuine beliefs that excused their conduct
Answer D: Both Olivia and Derek are guilty because only reasonable mistakes provide a defense
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Larceny is a specific intent crime requiring the intent to permanently deprive. For specific intent crimes, any honest (genuine) mistake of fact — even an objectively unreasonable one — is a defense if it negates the specific intent. Olivia's genuine belief that the watch was abandoned means she intended to keep property she thought was ownerless, not to steal someone else's property. Her specific intent to permanently deprive another's property is absent. She is not guilty of larceny. Derek's mistake, by contrast, is a mistake of law — he knew all the factual circumstances (he was taking someone's watch) but misunderstood the legal rule about found property. Ignorance of the law is generally no defense at common law. Derek is guilty of larceny.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Both conclusions are reversed. For larceny (specific intent), an honest mistake of fact — even an unreasonable one — negates the required intent to permanently deprive. If Olivia genuinely believed the watch was abandoned, she lacked the requisite specific intent. Derek's mistake is about the law (whether keeping found property is legal), not about a fact. Mistake of law is generally no defense. C: The type of mistake matters critically. Mistake of fact (Olivia) is a defense to specific intent crimes when honest. Mistake of law (Derek) is not a defense regardless of sincerity. The law demands that citizens know the law; it does not excuse criminal conduct based on legal ignorance alone. D: For specific intent crimes, reasonableness is not required — any genuine mistake of fact that negates specific intent is a defense. The reasonableness requirement applies to mistake of fact in general intent crimes (where the defendant's mistake must be both honest and reasonable). Applying the general-intent reasonableness requirement to a specific-intent crime like larceny is a common error.
lb_rule_reference: Mistake of fact is a defense if it negates the required mental state: for specific intent crimes, any honest (even unreasonable) mistake is a defense; for general intent crimes, the mistake must be both honest and reasonable; mistake of law (ignorance of the law) is generally no defense, with narrow exceptions
