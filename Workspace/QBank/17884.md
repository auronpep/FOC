Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Rights during Sentencing
Question: Defendant is convicted by a jury of second-degree assault, which carries a maximum sentence of five years. At sentencing, the judge finds by a preponderance of the evidence that the assault was motivated by racial bias—a fact not submitted to the jury. Under the state's hate-crime sentencing enhancement statute, the judge sentences the defendant to eight years, which exceeds the five-year maximum for second-degree assault but falls within the ten-year maximum for the hate-crime enhanced offense. The defendant argues the sentence violates the Sixth Amendment. Is the defendant's Sixth Amendment challenge correct?
Answer A: No, because sentencing facts are traditionally found by judges, and the Sixth Amendment right to jury trial does not extend to the sentencing phase
Answer B: No, because the judge found the bias enhancement by a preponderance of the evidence, which is the permissible standard for sentencing findings that do not exceed the statutory maximum
Answer C: Yes, because the racial bias finding increased the sentence beyond the five-year maximum authorized by the jury's verdict, and that finding was required to be submitted to the jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt
Answer D: Yes, but only if the defendant can show that the judge's finding was inaccurate; if the bias finding was factually correct, no Sixth Amendment violation occurs
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Apprendi v. New Jersey holds: "Other than the fact of a prior conviction, any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury, and proved beyond a reasonable doubt." The statutory maximum for Apprendi purposes is the maximum sentence a judge may impose based solely on the jury's verdict. Here, the jury convicted only of second-degree assault (maximum 5 years). The judge's finding of racial bias was used to impose 8 years—three years above the jury-authorized maximum. This violates Apprendi. The bias finding had to be submitted to the jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt. Ring v. Arizona and Blakely v. Washington confirmed and extended this rule.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Apprendi v. New Jersey (2000) held that the Sixth Amendment right to jury trial extends to any fact (other than a prior conviction) that increases the penalty beyond the statutory maximum. Although judges may make factual findings within the maximum authorized by the verdict alone, they cannot find facts that push the sentence above that maximum. The line is the maximum authorized by the jury's verdict. B: The statement is accurate as far as it goes: preponderance is permissible for facts used to set a sentence within the jury-authorized maximum. But here the judge used a preponderance finding to exceed the five-year maximum authorized by the jury's second-degree assault verdict. That is the precise violation Apprendi prohibits. The correct standard for a sentence-increasing fact is beyond a reasonable doubt before the jury. D: The Apprendi violation is procedural, not factual. The constitutional defect is who found the fact and by what standard—not whether the fact is accurate. Even a perfectly accurate judicial finding of a sentence-increasing fact violates Apprendi. The Sixth Amendment guarantees that the jury, not the judge, determines facts that increase the maximum penalty, regardless of the accuracy of the judge's determination.
