Question: Caleb met privately with lawyer Rachel to obtain legal advice about a business dispute. Caleb later died. In a separate criminal investigation not involving Caleb's estate, the prosecutor subpoenas Rachel to testify about what Caleb told her during that legal consultation. Caleb's personal representative objects and asserts attorney-client privilege. No testamentary dispute or other exception is stated. Does Caleb's death alone terminate the attorney-client privilege?
Answer A: Yes. Once the client dies, there is no living holder who can claim attorney-client privilege.
Answer B: Yes. Caleb's death makes him unavailable, so the lawyer may testify under a hearsay exception.
Answer C: Yes. Rachel may waive the privilege because she is the only living participant in the privileged conversation.
Answer D: No. Attorney-client privilege may survive the client's death, and an authorized representative may assert it absent an applicable exception.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: The attorney-client privilege is not merely a trial convenience that ends when the client dies. Under the federal common-law rule, confidential attorney-client communications may remain protected after death, subject to recognized exceptions such as testamentary disputes. No such exception is stated. The decisive axis is privilege survival, not hearsay unavailability or the lawyer's possession of the information.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because death alone does not terminate attorney-client privilege. B: B is wrong because hearsay unavailability does not defeat privilege. Hearsay exceptions and privilege are independent gates. C: C is wrong because the lawyer is not the holder and cannot waive the client's privilege merely because the client died.
