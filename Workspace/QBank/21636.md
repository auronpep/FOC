Outline: Liquidated damages
Question: Moses hired Sarah to provide a one-day courier service for $3,000. The contract stated that if Sarah started late for any reason, she would owe Moses $25,000 as liquidated damages. At the time of contracting, any late-start loss was easy to estimate because Moses could hire another courier for a posted substitute price. Sarah started late. Moses hired a substitute courier for $600 and lost no other business. Which recovery is most consistent with contract remedies principles?
Answer A: $25,000, because the parties selected that amount in the contract.
Answer B: $3,000, because the original contract price is the proper measure whenever a service provider breaches.
Answer C: $600, because the $25,000 clause is an unenforceable penalty but Moses may recover his proved substitute cost.
Answer D: Nothing, because an invalid liquidated-damages clause eliminates all damages for the breach it covers.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The remedy interest is expectation under a services contract. The breach posture is Sarah's late nonperformance, and the loss category is the direct substitute cost. The penalty gate defeats the $25,000 clause because the loss was easy to estimate at contracting and the amount was grossly disproportionate to the likely late-start harm. Once the clause is invalid, the formula reverts to proved actual expectation damages: $600 for the substitute courier. The result scope is limited actual recovery, not forfeiture of the claim.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because party labeling cannot save an unreasonable penalty, triggering WAL-REM-015. B: B is wrong because it defaults to the contract price rather than the legally authorized substitute-loss formula. D: D is wrong because rejecting a penalty clause does not convert a proven breach into no recovery; it only removes the stipulated amount.
lb_rule_reference: Common law penalty doctrine — invalid liquidated-damages clause does not bar recovery of proved actual damages
