Question: Ruth agreed in a signed goods contract to buy 200 sacks of flour each week from a mill. The writing contained a merger clause and said that modifications had to be in a signed writing. Before signing, the mill orally promised to provide free display racks. Two months later, after Ruth's storage room flooded, Ruth asked the mill to deliver 400 sacks every other week instead of 200 sacks each week. The mill made two biweekly deliveries, and Ruth accepted both without objection. In a dispute about the next biweekly delivery, Ruth offers evidence of both the later delivery arrangement and the earlier display-rack promise. Which evidence may the court consider to determine the current delivery obligation?
Answer A: Both the later biweekly-delivery evidence and the earlier display-rack promise.
Answer B: Neither item, because the writing has a merger clause and a no-oral-modification clause.
Answer C: The later biweekly-delivery evidence only.
Answer D: The earlier display-rack promise only.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that the parol evidence rule targets prior or contemporaneous terms, while later conduct may be relevant to modification, waiver, or course of performance even when the writing contains integration language. The trigger facts are the later oral change, two accepted biweekly deliveries, and the separate pre-signing rack promise. The decisive lock is timing. The delivery evidence is subsequent conduct bearing on the current delivery term; the display-rack promise is a prior side promise that does not explain the delivery obligation and is barred or irrelevant to this call.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because it admits a prior side promise unrelated to the delivery dispute. B: B is wrong because it treats the merger and no-oral-modification clauses as absolute bars to later waiver or course evidence. D: D is wrong because it chooses the prior parol item while excluding the later conduct that actually bears on the delivery term.
