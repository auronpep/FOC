MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Constitutional Law
Subtopic: Individual Rights
Outline: Freedom of speech and expression
Question: A privately owned shopping mall — located on private land, open to the public for retail shopping — adopts a policy prohibiting any person from distributing leaflets, conducting surveys, or engaging in political speech on the mall's premises. A student group wishes to circulate a petition about local zoning policy at the mall entrance. The mall security staff tells the students they must leave. The students argue their First Amendment rights have been violated. Which of the following most accurately states the federal constitutional law?
Answer A: The students' federal First Amendment rights have been violated because the mall is open to the public and functions as a public square, making it a de facto public forum
Answer B: The mall may restrict expressive activity on its property without federal First Amendment limitation because the mall is a private actor and the First Amendment does not apply to private conduct
Answer C: The mall's restriction is unconstitutional because it specifically targets political speech, which receives the highest level of First Amendment protection
Answer D: The mall's restriction is constitutional because it is a reasonable, viewpoint-neutral time, place, and manner restriction on the mall's private property
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The First Amendment restricts government action — federal, state, and local — but does not reach private actors. A privately owned shopping mall, even one open to the public for commercial purposes, is a private actor. Under Hudgens v. NLRB (overruling Amalgamated Food Employees), private shopping centers do not constitute public forums under the federal Constitution. The students have no federal First Amendment claim. Some state constitutions provide broader speech protections that extend to certain private property (e.g., California under Pruneyard Shopping Center v. Robins), but the federal First Amendment claim fails.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The First Amendment protects speech from government restriction, not private restriction. In Hudgens v. NLRB, the Supreme Court rejected the argument that privately owned shopping centers are public forums subject to First Amendment constraints. The "open to the public" character of private property does not transform it into government-owned property. Without state action, the First Amendment does not apply. C: The level of First Amendment protection — strict scrutiny, intermediate scrutiny, etc. — is irrelevant when the threshold state-action requirement is not met. Private actors may restrict even the highest-value speech without violating the federal First Amendment. The nature of the restricted speech does not supply state action. D: The reason the mall's restriction does not violate the federal First Amendment is not that it satisfies a TPM test — it is that the First Amendment does not apply at all. The TPM framework applies to government actors. Applying it to a private actor suggests the First Amendment governs private property if the restriction is "reasonable," which is incorrect. The answer reaches the right result for the wrong reason.
lb_rule_reference: The First Amendment applies only to government actors. A private property owner — including a privately owned shopping center — does not violate the First Amendment by restricting expressive activity on its property. State constitutions may provide broader protection, but the federal First Amendment does not apply to private actors.
