Question: Isaac bought 200 generators from Ruth under a signed contract. Ruth also agreed to provide ordinary installation, but nearly all of the contract price was allocated to the generators. The writing required July 1 delivery and did not contain a no-oral-modification clause. On June 25, Ruth asked Isaac to extend delivery to July 10 because a carrier was unavailable, and Isaac orally agreed. Ruth delivered on July 10. Isaac rejected the generators, arguing that his oral extension was ineffective. Which argument by Isaac is least likely to be accepted?
Answer A: The July 10 agreement failed solely because Ruth gave no new consideration for the delivery extension.
Answer B: The predominant purpose of the transaction was the sale of goods, so Article 2 supplies the modification rule.
Answer C: The parol evidence rule does not bar proof of the delivery extension because it was agreed to after the signed contract.
Answer D: Because the contract had no no-oral-modification clause, an oral delivery-date modification may be effective if otherwise made in good faith.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that Article 2 modifications do not require consideration. The trigger facts are the predominant goods transaction, the absence of a no-oral-modification clause, and the post-signing oral extension. The decisive locks are source law and timing: this is a later goods-contract modification, not a common-law consideration problem or parol-evidence problem. Therefore, the lack-of-consideration objection is the least likely to succeed.
