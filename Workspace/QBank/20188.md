Subtopic: Other Crimes
Outline: Burglary
Question: Police arrested Evan for burglary, gave Miranda warnings, and began questioning. Evan said, "I do not want to talk about the burglary." The officers immediately stopped. Four hours later, a different detective gave fresh warnings and asked Evan about an unrelated assault. Evan waived and confessed to the assault. Should the assault confession be suppressed because Evan invoked silence earlier?
Answer A: Yes, because invoking silence bars all further questioning until counsel is present
Answer B: Yes, because police may never restart questioning after a suspect says he does not want to talk
Answer C: No, because police scrupulously honored Evan's silence invocation before fresh warned questioning on a different matter
Answer D: No, because Evan's statement about not wanting to talk was ambiguous
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Officers stopped immediately, waited several hours, used a different detective, gave new warnings, and questioned about an unrelated assault. That sequence supports admissibility.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The right to silence requires police to scrupulously honor the invocation. It does not impose the same counsel-only reinitiation rule. B: Later questioning can be valid if police immediately stopped, waited, gave fresh warnings, and did not badger the suspect about the same matter. D: Evan clearly invoked silence about the burglary. The admissibility depends on police honoring that invocation, not on ambiguity.
lb_rule_reference: After a clear invocation of the right to silence, police must stop questioning, but later questioning may be allowed if the invocation was scrupulously honored and fresh warnings are given.
