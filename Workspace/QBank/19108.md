Outline: Form and scope of examination
Question: In a federal negligence trial, Esther calls Naomi, a neutral eyewitness. On direct examination, Esther's lawyer asks, “The traffic light facing David was red when he entered the intersection, wasn't it?” David objects that the question is leading. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Overrule the objection because leading questions are permitted whenever they would make testimony clearer.
Answer B: Sustain the objection because the question suggests the desired answer on direct examination of a neutral witness.
Answer C: Overrule the objection because the jury, not the judge, decides whether the witness was influenced by the question.
Answer D: Sustain the objection only if Naomi first gives a nonresponsive answer.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Leading questions generally should not be used on direct examination except as necessary to develop the witness's testimony. The question itself supplies the desired answer—red light—and Naomi is a neutral direct witness. The judge should sustain the leading-question objection.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because clarity alone does not make leading proper on ordinary direct examination. C: C is wrong because the judge controls the form of questions; this is not merely jury weight. D: D is wrong because the defect is in the question before any answer, so no nonresponsive answer is required.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 611(c) / leading question on direct examination
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests leading-question form on direct examination and rejects jury-weight and motion-to-strike timing traps.
