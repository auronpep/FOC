Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Discretionary exclusion (undue prejudice, confusion, waste of time)
Question: In a federal robbery trial, the prosecutor offers surveillance video showing Daniel entering the store ten minutes before the robbery and leaving two minutes after it. Daniel objects that the video is highly prejudicial because it makes him look guilty. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Admit the video because evidence is not unfairly prejudicial merely because it strongly damages the opponent's case.
Answer B: Exclude the video because any evidence that makes the defendant look guilty is unfairly prejudicial.
Answer C: Exclude the video unless the prosecutor proves beyond a reasonable doubt that Daniel committed the robbery before the jury sees it.
Answer D: Admit the video only if Daniel first disputes that he was near the store.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The video has direct probative value on identity and presence near the crime. Rule 403 does not exclude evidence merely because it is damaging. Unfair prejudice means a risk of an improper basis for decision, not ordinary probative force.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because damaging evidence is not the same as unfairly prejudicial evidence. C: C is wrong because admissibility does not require prior proof of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. D: D is wrong because relevant evidence need not be directed only to facts already disputed by the opponent.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401; FRE 403
lb_student_diagnostic: Student equates probative damage with unfair prejudice.
