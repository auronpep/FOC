Outline: Prior inconsistent statement
Question: In a federal assault trial, the court admits David's excited utterance that “Saul hit me with a bottle.” David is unavailable at trial. The defense offers testimony from Abigail that, one hour after making that excited utterance, David told her, “I never saw who hit me.” The defense offers Abigail's testimony only to attack David's credibility as the hearsay declarant. The prosecutor objects that David cannot be confronted with the later statement. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude Abigail's testimony because David is unavailable and cannot be asked to explain the inconsistency.
Answer B: Admit Abigail's testimony to attack David's credibility as the declarant of the admitted hearsay statement.
Answer C: Admit Abigail's testimony as substantive proof that Saul did not hit David.
Answer D: Exclude Abigail's testimony because prior inconsistent statements can impeach only live witnesses.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: When a hearsay statement has been admitted, the declarant's credibility may be attacked as if the declarant had testified. A prior inconsistent statement may be used for that credibility attack. Because the later statement is offered only to impeach David as the hearsay declarant, David's unavailability does not bar the impeachment use.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because Rule 806 allows impeachment of a hearsay declarant without requiring the declarant to be confronted in the same way as a live witness. C: C is wrong because the statement is offered only for credibility, not as substantive proof of who struck David. D: D is wrong because hearsay declarants can be impeached once their statements are admitted.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 806 / prior inconsistent statement of hearsay declarant
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests declarant-impeachment use of prior inconsistent statements and the impeachment-only limit.
