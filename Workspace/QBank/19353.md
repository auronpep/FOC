MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Negligence
Outline: Proximate (legal) cause
Question: A parking-lot operator knew that several customers had recently been robbed in a dark corner of its paid lot. The operator promised tenants that it would repair the broken lights there but negligently failed to do so for two months. A customer parked in that corner at night and was robbed by an unknown assailant. The customer sues the operator for negligence. In the customer's negligence claim against the parking-lot operator, does the robbery necessarily supersede the operator's negligence?
Answer A: Yes, because criminal acts always cut off proximate cause
Answer B: Yes, because the operator did not personally participate in the robbery
Answer C: No, because the robbery was within the foreseeable risk created by leaving the known dark corner unlit
Answer D: No, because proximate cause is satisfied by any injury occurring on the operator's land
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The operator knew of prior robberies in the same dark area, and the promised lighting repair addressed that exact risk. The criminal act therefore does not necessarily supersede the negligence.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Criminal acts are not automatically superseding. When prior similar crimes made the risk foreseeable, negligent failure to guard against that risk can remain a proximate cause. B: Negligence liability can arise from failing to take reasonable precautions against foreseeable third-party crime when a duty exists. Personal participation is not required. D: Proximate cause is not automatic for all injuries on land. The correct reason is that this robbery was a foreseeable risk of the lighting breach.
lb_rule_reference: A criminal or intentional intervening act is not automatically superseding when the defendant's negligence created or failed to guard against that foreseeable risk.
