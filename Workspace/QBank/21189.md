MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Police suspected a homeowner of running a gambling operation. Without a warrant, an officer entered the homeowner's backyard — clearly within the curtilage — by hopping the locked gate. Through the back patio doors, the officer observed gambling equipment, chips, and cash spread across the dining room table in plain sight. The officer photographed what he saw, returned to the station, and used those observations to apply for a search warrant. The warrant was issued, and a search found the same equipment. The homeowner moves to suppress. Should the court suppress the gambling equipment found pursuant to the warrant?
Answer A: No, because once the officer saw the gambling equipment through the window, the plain view doctrine authorized seizure regardless of how he got there
Answer B: No, because the warrant was obtained independently of the unlawful observation, qualifying as an independent source
Answer C: Yes, because the warrant was based on observations made from an unlawful vantage point; the plain view doctrine required a lawful prior position, and the fruits doctrine tainted the warrant
Answer D: Yes, because the gambling operation was per se illegal and thus receives no Fourth Amendment protection
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Plain view requires a lawful vantage point. The officer unlawfully entered the curtilage — a constitutionally protected area — without a warrant or exception. The observations made from that illegal vantage point are tainted. Because the search warrant was derived entirely from those tainted observations, the warrant itself is a fruit of the poisonous tree. The independent source doctrine does not apply because the probable cause showing in the warrant application was based solely on the illegal entry. The evidence seized under the warrant must be suppressed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Plain view requires not just that the item is visible but that the officer observing it is in a place he has a lawful right to be. The officer unlawfully entered the curtilage without a warrant or exception. An unlawful vantage point taints the plain view observation, which cannot then justify further action. B: The independent source doctrine requires that the warrant application be genuinely untainted by the prior illegality. Here, the officer's unlawful curtilage entry was the direct cause of the observations that formed the basis for the warrant. The warrant was not independent — it was the fruit of the illegal entry. The independent source doctrine does not apply. D: The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures regardless of what the search ultimately uncovers. Evidence of crime does not lose Fourth Amendment protection because the underlying activity was illegal.
lb_rule_reference: Plain view requires a lawful vantage point from which the observation is made; if the officer is in an unlawful position when the observation occurs, plain view does not apply even if the incriminating character is immediately apparent
