Question: A farmer contracted to sell 100,000 bushels of wheat to a buyer. When the wheat arrived at the destination, the buyer discovered that the farmer had delivered only 96,000 bushels. The buyer sued the farmer for breach of contract. At the trial of the case, the court found that the written contract was intended as a complete and exclusive statement of the terms of the agreement. The farmer offered to prove that in the wheat business, a promise to deliver a specified quantity is considered to be satisfied if the delivered quantity is within 5% of the specified quantity. The buyer objected to the offered evidence. Is the court likely to admit the evidence offered by the farmer?
Answer A: A: No, because the offered evidence is inconsistent with the express language of the agreement.
Answer B: B: No, because the written contract was totally integrated.
Answer C: C: Yes, because the offered evidence demonstrates that the farmer substantially performed the contract.
Answer D: D: Yes, because the offered evidence explains or supplements the agreement by usage of trade.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: D is correct. This transaction involves a sale of goods and is subject to Uniform Commercial Code (UCC) Article 2. Under Article 2, evidence of trade usage that can be construed as reasonably consistent with an agreement's express language is admissible to interpret or supplement an agreement. The majority rule provides that trade usage will be viewed as consistent with an agreement's express language unless the usage completely negates specific express language. The trade usage allowing for a variation of up to 5% does not completely negate but rather qualifies the express language calling for the delivery of 100,000 bushels of wheat. A
Wrong Answer Explanations: A is incorrect. Because evidence of the trade usage allowing for a variation of up to 5% does not completely negate the written agreement's express language, trade usage is admissible to qualify the written agreement. B is incorrect. The rule stated above applies regardless of whether the written agreement is a complete integration. C is incorrect. Under UCC § 2-601, the applicable standard for determining a breach of a non-installment transaction involving a sale of goods is the perfect tender rule and not the substantial performance standard. However, the offered evidence of trade usage would establish that there was no breach; according to the trade usage standards, the farmer's delivery of 96,000 bushels was a performance that conformed to the agreement.
