Question: Jacob contracted with Ruth to buy Ruth's farm. The contract required Jacob to pay Ruth's husband Samuel $1,000 per month “until Ruth and Samuel are legally divorced.” Samuel knew of the contract and accepted payments for six months. Ruth and Samuel then divorced. Samuel sued Jacob for payments after the divorce. May Samuel recover the post-divorce payments?
Answer A: Yes, because Samuel's rights vested when he accepted the first six payments.
Answer B: Yes, because divorce never affects an intended beneficiary's right once the beneficiary is named.
Answer C: No, because spouses can never be intended beneficiaries.
Answer D: No, because the contract itself limited Jacob's payment duty to the period before Ruth and Samuel were legally divorced.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: Samuel's acceptance of payments may show his rights vested as to the promise actually made, but vesting does not expand the promise. The contract required payments only until a legal divorce occurred, so Jacob's duty for post-divorce payments did not mature.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because vesting protects the right within its contract scope; it does not remove an express endpoint. B: B is wrong because a relationship change can matter when the contract makes it a limit on performance. C: C is wrong because spouses may be intended beneficiaries when the contract shows direct intent.
