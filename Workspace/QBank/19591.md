Outline: Independent contractor and nondelegable duties
Question: A nightclub hired a security guard to remove disorderly patrons. While ejecting an intoxicated patron, the guard used excessive force and broke the patron's wrist. The guard acted during his shift at the club entrance. Is the nightclub likely vicariously liable?
Answer A: Yes, because the assault was closely connected to the guard's assigned security duties
Answer B: Yes, because employers are strictly liable for all intentional torts by employees
Answer C: No, because intentional torts are always outside the scope of employment
Answer D: No, because the patron was intoxicated
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Excessive force during an authorized ejection can fall within foreseeable job friction.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: The tort must still be connected to the scope of employment. C: Some intentional torts are within scope when job duties create the friction. D: The patron's condition does not eliminate vicarious liability for excessive force.
lb_rule_reference: An employer may be vicariously liable for an employee's intentional tort when the tort is closely connected to authorized duties, such as security or bouncer work.
