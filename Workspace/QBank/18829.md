Subtopic: Inchoate Crimes
Outline: Attempt
Question: Marcus planned to rob a convenience store. On Tuesday evening he drove to the store, parked across the street, loaded a pistol, pulled a ski mask over his head, and walked to within ten feet of the entrance. He then noticed a police cruiser parked at the curb and turned and walked back to his car without entering the store. He was arrested before he drove away. The jurisdiction applies the common-law dangerous proximity test for criminal attempt. Is Marcus guilty of attempted robbery under the common-law dangerous proximity test?
Answer A: No, because Marcus never entered the store or confronted any employee
Answer B: No, because Marcus voluntarily abandoned the attempt before any harm occurred
Answer C: Yes, because Marcus was dangerously close to completing the robbery
Answer D: Yes, but only because his act of loading the pistol constituted the last proximate act
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under dangerous proximity (the Holmes/Rizzo standard), Marcus's acts — loading the weapon, donning a mask, approaching within feet of the entrance — put him in imminent position to commit the robbery. He had all the means, the location, and the intent. The only thing that stopped him was police presence, an involuntary interruption, not genuine renunciation. Most courts applying this test would find the proximity sufficient.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The dangerous proximity test does not require entry or confrontation. The question is whether the defendant was dangerously close to completing the crime. Walking to within ten feet of the entrance while masked and armed is almost certainly sufficient. B: Abandonment is not voluntary when caused by an external factor — here, seeing a police cruiser. Because the withdrawal was motivated by fear of detection rather than genuine renunciation, the common-law voluntary-abandonment defense is unavailable. D: Loading the gun is not the last proximate act before the robbery — confronting the clerk and demanding property would be. The answer is yes, but for the correct reason (dangerous proximity), not because the last-proximate-act standard is satisfied.
lb_rule_reference: Under the common-law dangerous proximity test, acts are sufficient for attempt only when the defendant is dangerously close to completing the offense; under the MPC substantial-step test, any act that is strongly corroborative of criminal purpose suffices
