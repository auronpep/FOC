Subtopic: Inchoate Crimes
Outline: Conspiracy
Question: Carol and Dean agreed to kill their employer, Victor. They ambushed Victor in a parking garage. Dean fired a gun at Victor. Victor was struck by a bullet but survived. Carol and Dean are charged with: (1) conspiracy to commit murder, (2) attempted murder, and (3) assault with a deadly weapon. Victor lived. All charges arise from the same parking garage incident. Assume both Carol and Dean are convicted of all three charges. Which convictions are legally permissible under the merger doctrine?
Answer A: Conspiracy merges into attempted murder, so only attempted murder and assault convictions stand
Answer B: Convictions on all three charges are permissible because each offense has distinct elements
Answer C: Conspiracy and attempted murder stand; assault with a deadly weapon merges into attempted murder
Answer D: Only assault with a deadly weapon stands because it is the only offense completed
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Conspiracy never merges — it is a separate offense punishing the agreement itself and stands alongside any subsequent crimes. Attempted murder is the primary offense arising from Dean's shot. Assault with a deadly weapon, being a lesser-included or same-act offense relative to attempted murder, merges into the attempted murder conviction under common-law merger principles. A defendant cannot be convicted of both the greater offense (attempted murder) and a lesser offense (assault) that arises from the identical act. The permissible convictions are conspiracy and attempted murder.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Conspiracy does not merge into attempt or into any completed or attempted crime. The non-merger of conspiracy is a foundational common-law rule. Carol and Dean can be convicted of conspiracy plus all other charges. B: Even if offenses have distinct elements, the merger doctrine can bar dual conviction when a lesser offense is subsumed into a greater one arising from the same act. If Victor had died, attempt would merge into murder. More relevantly, assault with a deadly weapon and attempted murder arise from the same act of shooting — many courts would merge the lesser into the attempted murder conviction. The conspiracy conviction stands on its own. D: Attempted murder is fully completed as an offense when the defendant, with murderous intent, takes a substantial step (or dangerous-proximity act) toward killing the victim — here, actually firing a gun and hitting Victor. The fact that the victim survived does not prevent an attempted-murder conviction. And conspiracy is complete at the agreement, not at the crime.
lb_rule_reference: At common law, attempt merges into the completed crime so that a defendant cannot be convicted of both attempted murder and murder for the same transaction; conspiracy, however, does not merge into the completed crime and supports a separate conviction
