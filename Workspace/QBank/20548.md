Outline: Larceny
Question: Greta is walking through a hotel lobby and notices a leather wallet on the floor. The wallet contains $300 in cash, three credit cards bearing the name "Robert Haines," a hotel room key card, and a business card with a phone number. Greta picks up the wallet, puts it in her purse, and immediately walks to her car intending to keep the cash and discard the rest. She makes no attempt to contact the hotel front desk or call the number on the business card. Is Greta guilty of larceny?
Answer A: No, because the wallet was lost property and a finder cannot be guilty of larceny for picking up lost property
Answer B: No, because Greta found the property in a public area where no one had an immediate possessory right
Answer C: Yes, because Greta had reasonable means to identify the owner at the moment of taking and intended to keep the property from the start
Answer D: Yes, but only if she kept the wallet for more than 24 hours without reporting it
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Taking lost property constitutes larceny when two conditions are met: (1) the finder has a reasonable means of identifying or locating the owner at the time of the taking, and (2) the finder intends to permanently deprive the owner at that moment. Greta had both: the wallet contained the owner's name, a hotel room key indicating current presence, and a business card with contact information — all reasonable leads. She also formed the intent to keep the cash immediately upon picking up the wallet. Both elements are satisfied, making this larceny.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The lost-property rule has an important caveat: it applies only where the finder has no reasonable means of identifying the owner at the time of the taking. Here, Greta had the owner's name on the credit cards, a hotel room key indicating he was likely still on premises, and a business card with a phone number. These clues gave her a reasonable means of finding the owner. Taking with intent to keep under these circumstances is larceny. B: Lost property retains an owner who has a possessory right superior to everyone except that owner. The location (hotel lobby) is legally irrelevant to whether larceny was committed. The hotel lobby setting actually reinforces that Greta had reasonable means to locate the owner — the front desk was nearby and the room key card was present. D: There is no 24-hour or other grace-period rule in common-law larceny of lost property. The crime is complete at the moment of taking with the requisite intent, provided reasonable means to identify the owner existed. Greta's intent was formed at the moment she placed the wallet in her purse; no additional time is required.
lb_rule_reference: Taking lost property is larceny if, at the time of the finding, the finder has a reasonable means of identifying or locating the true owner and takes the property with intent to permanently deprive the owner of it.
