Outline: Bias
Question: In a federal negligence trial, David testifies for Sarah that he saw the defendant run a red light. On cross-examination, the defendant asks David whether Sarah promised to pay him $5,000 if she wins the case. Sarah objects that the question is improper character evidence because it does not concern David's truthfulness. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection because bias may be proved only by a conviction or reputation evidence.
Answer B: Sustain the objection because a witness's financial interest is collateral and may not be explored on cross-examination.
Answer C: Overrule the objection because the question seeks to show David's possible bias or motive to favor Sarah.
Answer D: Overrule the objection only if David has first testified that he is a truthful person.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: A party may attack a witness's credibility by showing bias, interest, or motive to testify favorably. The promised payment gives David a personal financial stake in Sarah's success, so the question is proper bias impeachment. It is not character evidence offered to prove David is generally untruthful.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because bias is not limited to conviction or reputation routes. B: B is wrong because bias is a central credibility issue, not automatically barred as collateral. D: D is wrong because bias impeachment does not depend on the witness first claiming to be truthful.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 607 / bias impeachment / financial stake
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests whether the student separates bias impeachment from truthfulness-character impeachment.
