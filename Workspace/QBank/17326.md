Outline: Constitutional limitations and justiciability
Question: A citizen sues in federal court to challenge a federal agency policy that she believes violates the Constitution. She alleges that the policy is unlawful and important, but she does not allege that it denied her a benefit, imposed a cost on her, exposed her to enforcement, or changed her legal rights. What is the strongest reason the court should dismiss?
Answer A: The plaintiff lacks standing because she alleges only a generalized grievance
Answer B: The court should reach the merits because constitutional violations are always reviewable
Answer C: The plaintiff has standing because every citizen is injured when the Constitution is violated
Answer D: The case is moot because the plaintiff did not suffer damages
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A plaintiff must show a concrete personal injury, not only that the government acted unlawfully or that the issue matters to the public.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Federal courts reach merits only after Article III standing is satisfied. C: A shared civic interest is not a concrete and particularized injury. D: The problem is standing at filing, not a once-live dispute that later ended.
lb_rule_reference: Article III standing requires a concrete and particularized injury that is fairly traceable to the defendant and likely redressable by the court; public disagreement with government action is not enough.
