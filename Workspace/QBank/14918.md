Question: A seller and a purchaser signed a contract for the sale of a 60-year-old house. The contract required a warranty deed to be given at closing. The contract was silent regarding the condition of the house, and the purchaser did not ask. The purchaser received a warranty deed with all covenants of title at the closing and promptly recorded the deed. Approximately one month after the closing, the furnace in the house stopped working, the basement flooded, and the roof leaked so badly that the second floor could not be occupied. The seller, when told of the house's condition, was genuinely surprised. There is no applicable statute. The purchaser has sued the seller for damages. Will the purchaser likely be successful?
Answer A: A: Yes, because with a conveyance of residential real property, a warranty of fitness is implied.
Answer B: B: Yes, based on the covenants of title contained in the deed the purchaser received.
Answer C: C: No, because the seller gave no warranty regarding the condition of the house.
Answer D: D: No, because of the doctrine of merger.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: C is correct. The seller made no representations about the condition of the house, and there is no duty to disclose unknown facts.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A is incorrect. The implied warranty of fitness is not applicable to this fact pattern. B is incorrect. The covenants in a warranty deed relate to the title of the property, not the physical condition of the property. D is incorrect. This answer reaches the correct answer with the wrong reasoning. The doctrine of merger is only relevant to title issues.
