Outline: Freedom of speech and expression
Question: A protestor stands on a public sidewalk outside a government office holding a large sign reading "All cops are murderous cowards." Several police officers present at the scene are offended but remain professional. An officer approaches the protestor and instructs him to leave because his sign is "fighting words directed at law enforcement." The protestor refuses and is arrested for disorderly conduct under a statute making it illegal to use "offensive and abusive language in a public place." Is the arrest for the sign's language consistent with the First Amendment?
Answer A: Yes, because the message on the sign is deeply offensive to police officers and the government may suppress offensive speech to maintain public order
Answer B: Yes, because the sign criticizes law enforcement as a group, and speech targeting a category of public officials may be regulated to protect their ability to perform their duties
Answer C: No, because the fighting-words doctrine requires face-to-face, individually-directed speech likely to provoke an immediate violent response, and a sign conveying a political message does not satisfy those requirements
Answer D: No, because the protestor is in a traditional public forum and the government may only apply content-neutral TPM restrictions there, not content-based arrests based on the message
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under Chaplinsky, fighting words are those personally directed at a specific individual, face-to-face, in a way likely to provoke the average person to immediate violence. A public sign on a sidewalk conveying political criticism of a group — however provocative — is not fighting words. It is expressive political speech. The police officers' offended reaction does not transform the speech into unprotected fighting words. The arrest is unconstitutional.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Offensive speech — even extremely offensive speech — does not lose First Amendment protection simply because it causes offense. The government may not suppress speech on the basis that it is offensive to a particular audience. The fighting-words doctrine requires much more than general offensiveness: the words must be directed face-to-face at a specific individual and likely to provoke an immediate violent response in the average person. B: No recognized First Amendment doctrine allows the government to suppress criticism of a class of public officials — including police — to protect their ability to perform their duties. That would invert the First Amendment's core protection of speech about government officials and institutions. The fighting-words doctrine requires individual-to-individual face-to-face confrontation, not group-directed messaging. D: The specific question is whether the fighting-words doctrine applies, not the general forum-analysis framework. D's reasoning is partially correct but dodges the core analysis the call requires. The better answer directly addresses whether the fighting-words doctrine applies to this speech and explains why it does not. Answering with forum analysis without engaging the fighting-words doctrine misses the direct issue.
lb_rule_reference: Under Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, "fighting words" are those that by their very utterance inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace, directed face-to-face at a specific individual, and likely to provoke the average person to an immediate violent response. The doctrine is cabined tightly and rarely applied.
