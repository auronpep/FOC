MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Civil Procedure
Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Personal Jurisdiction
Question: Thornfield Pharmaceuticals Inc. is incorporated in New Jersey with its principal place of business in New Jersey. It manufactures and sells prescription medications through licensed distributors in 12 states, but not in the State of Prentiss. A Prentiss resident purchased a Thornfield medication while visiting New Jersey and took it at home in Prentiss, where she suffered a severe adverse reaction. She sued Thornfield in a Prentiss federal district court, asserting diversity jurisdiction. Prentiss's long-arm statute reads: "A court may exercise personal jurisdiction over a defendant who (a) transacts business within this state, (b) commits a tortious act within this state, or (c) has systematic and substantial contacts with this state." Thornfield does not sell to distributors in Prentiss, does not advertise in Prentiss, and has no employees or facilities there. The plaintiff argues that exercising jurisdiction is consistent with due process because Thornfield knew its medications could be taken by traveling patients anywhere. Should the Prentiss court exercise personal jurisdiction over Thornfield?
Answer A: Yes, because Thornfield had sufficient minimum contacts with Prentiss — its product was used there and it reasonably could anticipate being sued by Prentiss residents
Answer B: Yes, because the adverse reaction occurred in Prentiss, satisfying the "tortious act within this state" provision of the long-arm statute
Answer C: No, because Prentiss's long-arm statute does not reach a defendant whose only connection to Prentiss is that a plaintiff consumed its product there after purchasing it elsewhere, so the statutory step fails before any constitutional analysis
Answer D: No, because the constitutional minimum-contacts test fails — using a traveling patient as the contact point between Thornfield and Prentiss would not satisfy purposeful availment of the Prentiss forum
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The long-arm statute's three prongs require (a) transacting business in Prentiss, (b) committing a tortious act in Prentiss, or (c) systematic/substantial contacts with Prentiss. Thornfield does none of these — it has no distributors, advertising, employees, or facilities in Prentiss, and did not sell the product there. A product purchased out-of-state and used by a traveling Prentiss resident does not satisfy the tortious-act-in-state prong under the majority rule. Because the statute does not reach Thornfield, the court lacks authority to exercise jurisdiction regardless of any due-process analysis. The motion to dismiss should be granted.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The court must first determine whether Prentiss's long-arm statute reaches Thornfield. The statute covers entities that (a) transact business in Prentiss, (b) commit a tortious act in Prentiss, or (c) have systematic and substantial contacts with Prentiss. Thornfield does none of these — it does not distribute in Prentiss, advertise there, or maintain contacts there. A product purchased out of state and consumed in Prentiss does not amount to a tortious act committed within Prentiss under the statute. The statute does not reach Thornfield; the constitutional analysis never begins. B: The majority rule interprets "tortious act within the state" to mean that the defendant committed a tort in the forum — not merely that injury was felt there. Under most formulations, a product manufactured and sold entirely outside Prentiss that is consumed there by a traveling resident does not satisfy a "tortious act committed within" Prentiss provision. The defendant's relevant conduct (manufacture, distribution, sale) all occurred outside Prentiss; the injury location alone does not trigger this prong. (Note: some states take a broader view; this answer is correct under the majority statutory interpretation.) D: In a two-step PJ analysis, the long-arm statute is the threshold question. If the statute doesn't reach the defendant, the court stops there and need not perform the constitutional analysis. This answer reverses the order and treats the constitutional question as the operative one. While the minimum-contacts conclusion may also be correct, proper analysis reaches the constitutional step only after confirming the statutory step. Procedural-posture precision matters on the bar exam.
lb_rule_reference: Before performing a constitutional minimum-contacts analysis, the court must determine whether the state's long-arm statute reaches the defendant. If the statute does not reach the defendant's conduct, jurisdiction is unavailable regardless of whether due-process would otherwise be satisfied. The two steps are independent and sequential.
