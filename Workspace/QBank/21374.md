Question: Nina is invited to a friend's house for dinner and enters through the front door with permission. While waiting in the living room, Nina notices a watch on a shelf, decides for the first time to steal it, pockets it, and leaves. Is Nina guilty of common-law burglary?
Answer A: Yes, because she committed theft inside a dwelling
Answer B: Yes, because remaining after forming intent is a new entry
Answer C: No, because she lacked intent to commit a felony when she entered
Answer D: No, because burglary requires breaking a lock
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Nina entered by invitation and formed theft intent only later. That can support larceny of the watch, but not common-law burglary.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Common-law burglary requires felonious intent at the moment of entry. Nina formed the intent only after lawful entry. B: Under the common-law formulation tested here, the required entry already occurred lawfully and without felony intent. D: The dispositive missing element is intent at entry. Breaking does not always require lock damage.
lb_rule_reference: A person who lawfully enters a dwelling without intent to commit a felony and only later forms theft intent inside has not committed common-law burglary.
