Question: Peter sues Deborah after a wagon accident, claiming Deborah failed to maintain the wagon's brakes. Peter offers testimony from a stable worker that Deborah's wagon brakes squealed loudly three days before the accident. Deborah objects that squealing brakes do not prove the brakes failed during the accident. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Admit the testimony because it has some tendency to show a possible brake problem; the precise cause of the accident remains for the jury.
Answer B: Exclude the testimony because it does not prove brake failure at the time of the collision.
Answer C: Exclude the testimony unless Peter first proves the brakes were never repaired.
Answer D: Admit the testimony only to impeach Deborah, not on maintenance or causation.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The prior squealing is circumstantial evidence of a possible brake problem. It does not conclusively establish failure at the accident, but it has probative value on maintenance and causation.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because relevance does not require complete proof of brake failure. C: C is wrong because repair or no repair affects weight and may invite further proof, but does not automatically bar the evidence. D: D is wrong because the evidence is substantively relevant to condition and maintenance, not merely impeachment.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401; FRE 402
lb_student_diagnostic: Student excludes condition evidence because it is not complete causation proof.
