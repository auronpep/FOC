Question: A supplier contracted with Moses, a retailer, to deliver wool to Moses each week. The supplier knew that Moses planned to resell the wool to Sarah, who had placed a large order with Moses. The contract between the supplier and Moses did not mention Sarah and required delivery only to Moses. When the supplier failed to deliver, Sarah sued the supplier for breach of the supplier-Moses contract. Is Sarah likely to recover as a third-party beneficiary?
Answer A: Yes, because the supplier knew Moses planned to resell the wool to Sarah.
Answer B: No, because Sarah was a downstream buyer who benefited incidentally from the supplier's performance to Moses.
Answer C: Yes, because Sarah would have benefited if the supplier performed.
Answer D: No, unless Sarah proves Moses assigned his rights to the supplier.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Sarah is likely an incidental beneficiary. The supplier's knowledge that Moses had a resale customer does not show that the supplier and Moses intended to confer an enforceable right on Sarah. The performance was owed to Moses.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because knowledge of downstream resale is not enough. C: C is wrong because benefit alone is not intended-beneficiary status. D: D is wrong because the issue is third-party beneficiary status; assignment would be a different route and is not shown.
lb_rule_reference: Common law third-party beneficiary — supply-chain benefit
