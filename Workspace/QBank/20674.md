Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Right to Jury Trial
Question: Terrence was tried in federal district court for a serious misdemeanor. The court empaneled a six-person jury, as permitted by the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure for misdemeanor offenses. The judge instructed the jury that a verdict of five-to-one was sufficient to convict. The jury returned a five-to-one guilty verdict. Terrence appeals, arguing that a non-unanimous verdict by a six-person jury violates the Sixth Amendment. Is Terrence's conviction constitutionally valid?
Answer A: Yes, because the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure permit six-person juries for misdemeanors, satisfying the Sixth Amendment
Answer B: Yes, because Apodaca v. Oregon permits non-unanimous verdicts for serious offenses in state court, which should apply equally to federal misdemeanors
Answer C: No, because a six-person jury must return a unanimous verdict, and a five-to-one verdict is constitutionally defective
Answer D: No, because any federal criminal trial — regardless of offense severity — requires a twelve-person jury that must be unanimous
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Burch v. Louisiana*, when a six-person jury is used, the verdict must be unanimous. The Court reasoned that reducing both jury size and the unanimity requirement simultaneously would too greatly threaten the Sixth Amendment jury right. A five-to-one verdict by a six-person jury is unconstitutional. Terrence's conviction must be reversed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Compliance with the Federal Rules regarding jury size does not resolve the constitutional unanimity question. *Burch v. Louisiana* held that a six-person jury must return a unanimous verdict. A procedural rule permitting a smaller jury does not authorize a non-unanimous verdict by that smaller jury. B: Apodaca* (and its limited non-unanimity holding) applied to twelve-person juries in state court. *Burch* specifically addressed six-person juries and held unanimity constitutionally required regardless of jurisdiction. Moreover, *Ramos v. Louisiana* (2020) held that even twelve-person juries must be unanimous — overruling *Apodaca*'s non-unanimity holding. D: The Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure do permit six-person juries for misdemeanors, and the Constitution does not itself mandate twelve-person juries for all offenses. The constitutional violation here is the non-unanimity of the six-person jury, not the use of a six-person jury in the first place. The answer misstates the rule by requiring twelve-person juries in all federal criminal cases.
