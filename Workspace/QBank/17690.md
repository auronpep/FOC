Outline: Prior inconsistent statement
Question: In a federal civil trial, Rachel testifies that the machine alarm never sounded. On cross-examination, Rachel is asked whether she told Noah immediately after the accident, “The alarm sounded twice.” Rachel denies making that statement. The defendant then calls Noah to testify that Rachel did say it. Rachel objects that the alleged prior statement was oral and never written down. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude Noah's testimony because only written prior inconsistent statements may be used for impeachment.
Answer B: Exclude Noah's testimony because Rachel denied the statement.
Answer C: Admit Noah's testimony for impeachment because oral prior inconsistent statements may be proved extrinsically after the Rule 613(b) opportunity is provided.
Answer D: Admit Noah's testimony as substantive proof that the alarm sounded because Rachel denied the statement.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Rule 613 covers prior statements generally; it is not limited to writings. Rachel was asked about the statement and denied it, giving her the opportunity to explain or deny. Noah's testimony may be admitted for impeachment, subject to other ordinary limits. The oral nature of the statement does not bar impeachment use.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because prior inconsistent statements can be oral. B: B is wrong because denial is what makes extrinsic impeachment useful when the procedural requirements are satisfied. D: D is wrong because denial does not make an ordinary oral prior inconsistent statement substantive evidence.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 613(b) / oral prior inconsistent statement
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests that prior inconsistent statements can be oral and still serve as impeachment material.
