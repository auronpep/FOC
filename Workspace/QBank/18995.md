Question: A state university's holistic admissions program uses a scoring rubric that evaluates applicants on academics, leadership, community service, and "diversity contributions." The diversity contributions category explicitly allows admissions officers to award additional points to applicants who check a racial or ethnic minority box on their application. A non-minority applicant denied admission sues, arguing that the race-based bonus points violate the Equal Protection Clause. The university defends the program by asserting that educational diversity is a compelling state interest and that the race-conscious element is narrowly tailored because it is one factor among many. Under current Supreme Court doctrine, which of the following most accurately describes the constitutional status of the university's race-conscious admissions bonus?
Answer A: The program is constitutional because the use of race is one factor among many in a holistic review, which historically satisfied narrow tailoring under Grutter v. Bollinger
Answer B: The program is constitutional under intermediate scrutiny because the educational benefits of diversity are an important government interest that substantially justifies race-conscious admissions
Answer C: The program is unconstitutional because current Supreme Court doctrine holds that race-conscious university admissions programs violate the Equal Protection Clause, even when race is used as one factor in a holistic review
Answer D: The program is unconstitutional, but only because it uses a mechanical point system rather than truly holistic review; a purely holistic race-conscious program would still survive strict scrutiny after Students for Fair Admissions
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard (2023) held that race-conscious admissions programs at universities violate the Equal Protection Clause. The Court found that the programs lacked measurable objectives consistent with individualized holistic review and that the diversity rationale, however important, could not justify the individualized race-based assessments at issue. The race-based bonus points in the university's scoring rubric are squarely within what SFFA held unconstitutional.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard (2023) overruled the Grutter/Gratz framework. The Supreme Court held that race-conscious admissions programs at universities violate the Equal Protection Clause. The "one factor among many" framework no longer saves race-conscious admissions. Applying the pre-2023 Grutter rule is applying outdated doctrine. B: Race-based classifications always receive strict scrutiny, never intermediate scrutiny. Moreover, even under strict scrutiny the diversity interest that was previously recognized as compelling no longer saves race-conscious admissions programs under the current Supreme Court rule. Intermediate scrutiny is the wrong standard. D: Students for Fair Admissions broadly rejected race-conscious admissions, not just mechanical systems. The Court held that universities may not use race as a determinative factor in admissions decisions, period. The holistic/mechanical distinction does not save race-conscious admissions post-SFFA.
lb_rule_reference: Race-conscious university admissions programs are subject to strict scrutiny. As of Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard (2023), race-conscious admissions programs at universities are unconstitutional; institutions may not use race as a factor in holistic admissions decisions.
