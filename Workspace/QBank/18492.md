Question: A buyer orally orders 300 banners from a seller. Each banner must display the buyer's unusual family crest and a date for the buyer's private event, making the banners unsuitable for sale to others in the seller's ordinary business. Before receiving any repudiation, the seller buys the special fabric and prints the first 80 banners. The buyer then refuses to pay, asserting the Statute of Frauds. What is the best result?
Answer A: The seller may enforce the contract despite the lack of writing under the specially manufactured goods exception.
Answer B: The seller may enforce the contract only if the buyer signed a confirmation.
Answer C: The buyer prevails because all goods contracts over $500 require a signed writing with no exceptions.
Answer D: The buyer prevails because the seller completed fewer than half the banners.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A goods contract lacking a sufficient writing can be enforceable if the goods are specially manufactured for the buyer, unsuitable for sale to others in the seller's ordinary course, and the seller made a substantial beginning or procurement commitment before repudiation. These facts satisfy that route.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because a buyer-signed confirmation is not the only way around UCC §2-201. C: C is wrong because the UCC includes exceptions. D: D is wrong because the rule requires a substantial beginning or commitments, not completion of a majority of goods.
lb_rule_reference: UCC §2-201(3)(a) — specially manufactured goods
