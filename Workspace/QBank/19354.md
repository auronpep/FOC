Outline: Rational basis review classifications
Question: A city denies a zoning permit for a group home for adults with intellectual disabilities, requiring the group home operator to obtain a special use permit that the city does not require of similar residential facilities housing other adult populations (such as fraternity houses, dormitories, or nursing homes for non-disabled adults). The city council's stated reasons include concerns about "the nature of the facility's residents," neighborhood stability, and proximity to a school. The group home operator challenges the permit requirement under the Equal Protection Clause. Which standard of review applies to the city's disparate permit requirement for homes housing adults with intellectual disabilities?
Answer A: Strict scrutiny, because discrimination against persons with intellectual disabilities is analogous to race-based discrimination and targets an immutable, visible characteristic
Answer B: Intermediate scrutiny, because persons with intellectual disabilities are politically powerless, historically stigmatized, and defined by an immutable characteristic
Answer C: Rational basis review applies; the city's permit requirement likely fails because the stated reasons — vague concerns about "the nature of the residents" and neighborhood fears — reflect irrational prejudice against persons with intellectual disabilities rather than a legitimate distinction between this facility and similar facilities
Answer D: Rational basis review applies, and the permit requirement survives because the city identified multiple conceivable rationales — neighborhood stability and proximity to schools — each of which independently satisfies rational basis
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Cleburne established that intellectual disability receives rational basis review — but then applied it to strike down the discriminatory permit requirement. The city's special permit requirement singles out homes for intellectually disabled adults while exempting functionally similar residential facilities. The stated reasons — concerns about "the nature of the residents" and vague neighborhood fears — reflect exactly the kind of irrational prejudice that fails even rational basis review when the classification's only basis is bare negative attitudes toward an identifiable group.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Supreme Court held in City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center that intellectual disability is not a quasi-suspect or suspect classification and does not trigger intermediate or strict scrutiny. Rational basis review applies. B: Cleburne expressly rejected intermediate scrutiny for intellectual disability. The Court acknowledged the historical discrimination and stigma but declined to create a new quasi-suspect classification, noting that doing so would require heightened scrutiny for a broad range of legislation affecting disabled persons. The standard remains rational basis. D: When a rational-basis analysis reveals that the only actual rationale is animus or irrational fear of the affected group, courts will look beyond surface justifications. Cleburne demonstrates this: the city's proximity-to-school and neighborhood-stability rationales failed scrutiny because the city did not require the same permit of other comparable facilities, revealing the true basis as irrational fear of this specific group.
lb_rule_reference: Disability is not a suspect or quasi-suspect classification under Equal Protection. Disability-based laws receive rational basis review. However, when the only government rationale is vague concerns, fears, or negative attitudes toward disabled persons, the law may fail even rational basis review.
