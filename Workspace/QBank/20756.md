Outline: Freedom of speech and expression
Question: At a political rally attended by approximately 300 people, an activist delivers a twenty-minute speech criticizing what she calls the government's failure to protect workers. Near the end of the speech, she says: "History shows us that when the powerful ignore the people, the people must act. Someday — when enough of us have had enough — we will need to take back what was taken from us, even if that means breaking the laws they wrote to protect themselves." No violence occurs at the rally. Three weeks later, a separate, unrelated protest turns violent. State prosecutors seek to charge the activist with criminal incitement based on her speech. Can the activist be constitutionally prosecuted for incitement based on this speech?
Answer A: Yes, because her speech advocated illegal action — breaking laws — and such speech is categorically unprotected
Answer B: Yes, because the speech created a general threat of future violence that could foreseeably inspire unlawful acts by listeners
Answer C: No, because the speech was abstract advocacy of future unlawful action in hypothetical terms and was not directed at producing imminent lawless action likely to occur immediately
Answer D: No, because the First Amendment categorically protects all political speech regardless of its content or effect
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Brandenburg v. Ohio protects abstract advocacy of illegal action from criminal punishment unless the speech is directed at producing imminent lawless action AND is likely to produce such action. The activist's speech used conditional, future-oriented language ("someday... when enough of us have had enough"). No violence occurred at the rally. The later violence was unrelated and three weeks removed. The speech does not satisfy either prong of Brandenburg. The prosecution is unconstitutional.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Under Brandenburg v. Ohio, advocacy of illegal action in the abstract does not constitute unprotected incitement. The speech must be directed to producing imminent lawless action and likely to produce such action. Hypothetical, future-oriented advocacy ("someday... we will need to") is abstract advocacy, not a directive aimed at immediate action. Brandenburg specifically overruled the broader "bad tendency" test that had been used to punish abstract advocacy. B: Brandenburg requires imminence, not foreseeability. The three-week gap between the speech and the unrelated violent protest demonstrates that the violence was not imminent when the speech was given. Additionally, the later violence was at a separate event and not the direct product of this speech. Foreseeability of future harm is the rejected "bad tendency" test, not the Brandenburg standard. D: Political speech receives strong but not absolute First Amendment protection. True threats, genuine incitement, and certain other categories of speech are unprotected even when expressed in a political context. The reason the activist's speech is protected is that it does not satisfy the Brandenburg test, not that political speech is categorically immune from incitement prosecution.
lb_rule_reference: Under Brandenburg v. Ohio, speech may be punished as incitement only if it (1) is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action, and (2) is likely to produce such action. Both prongs must be satisfied; mere advocacy of illegal action in the abstract remains protected.
