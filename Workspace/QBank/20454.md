internal_id: TR-064
Outline_code: 61020401
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Negligence
Outline: Cause in fact (actual cause)
Question: A delivery driver negligently drove with a burned-out brake light. While stopped at a red light, the driver's van was struck from behind by a cyclist who had fainted because of a sudden medical episode. The cyclist never saw the van or its brake light before fainting, and the collision would have occurred even if all brake lights had been working. The cyclist sues the driver for negligence. Is the cyclist likely to prevail in a negligence claim against the delivery driver based on the burned-out brake light?
Answer A: Yes, because a burned-out brake light creates a foreseeable risk of rear-end collisions
Answer B: Yes, because the driver violated a traffic safety rule
Answer C: No, because the missing brake light was not an actual cause of the collision
Answer D: No, because fainting is always a superseding cause
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The cyclist fainted before observing the van, so a functioning brake light would not have changed his conduct or avoided the collision. The breach created a risk but did not cause this harm.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The cyclist did not fail to react to the missing brake light; he fainted before seeing the van. The breach did not cause the collision. B: Even if the violation establishes breach, causation and damages remain separate elements. The violation did not cause this cyclist's impact. D: The problem is actual cause, not a categorical superseding-cause rule. Some medical events may affect proximate cause, but here the decisive fact is that the brake light made no difference.
lb_rule_reference: A plaintiff must prove that the defendant's negligent breach caused the injury; proof that the breach created a general risk is insufficient when the injury came from an independent cause.
