Question: Rhea has a brain injury that makes her impulsive. During an argument, she intentionally pushes a clerk, causing minor injury. The jurisdiction recognizes diminished capacity for specific-intent crimes, but battery requires only intent to make offensive contact and no further purpose. Rhea knew she was pushing the clerk. Does diminished capacity defeat the battery charge?
Answer A: Yes, because the brain injury made Rhea impulsive
Answer B: Yes, because diminished capacity is the same as insanity
Answer C: No, because the offense is general intent and Rhea intended the contact
Answer D: No, because mental-impairment evidence is never admissible in criminal cases
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The jurisdiction limits diminished capacity to specific-intent crimes. Battery requires only intentional offensive contact. Rhea intentionally pushed the clerk, so diminished capacity does not negate the required mental state.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Diminished capacity negates specific or heightened mental elements. Battery here requires only general intent to make contact, which Rhea had. B: Diminished capacity is mens rea evidence, not a complete excuse. Rhea also does not satisfy an insanity test on these facts. D: Mental-impairment evidence may be admissible for specific-intent elements or insanity. It is simply not helpful for this general-intent battery.
lb_rule_reference: Diminished capacity, where recognized, is generally limited to negating specific-intent or heightened mental elements; it does not excuse general intent merely because judgment was impaired
