Question: A lawn service company agreed in writing to purchase from a supplier all of its requirements for lawn care products during the next calendar year. In the writing, the supplier agreed to fulfill those requirements and to give the company a 10% discount off its published prices, but it reserved the right to increase the published prices during the year. After the parties had performed under the agreement for three months, the supplier notified the company that it would no longer give the company the 10% discount off the published prices. Does the company have a viable claim against the supplier for breach of contract?
Answer A: A: Yes, because part performance of the agreement by both parties made it enforceable for the full year.
Answer B: B: Yes, because the company's agreement to buy all of its lawn care products from the supplier made the agreement enforceable.
Answer C: C: No, because the supplier could, and did, revoke its offer with respect to future deliveries.
Answer D: D: No, because the absence of a minimum quantity term rendered the company's promise illusory.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: B is correct. The supplier breached this valid requirements contract by refusing to adhere to the term ensuring the company would receive a 10% discount off the published prices. Although the supplier did reserve the right to increase the published prices, it did not reserve the right to take away the company's promised 10% discount off those prices. A
Wrong Answer Explanations: A is incorrect. The parties' part performance would only be dispositive if the contract lacked consideration, which it did not. The company explicitly agreed, in writing, to purchase all necessary supplies from the supplier for one calendar year. This was a valid requirements contract, and part performance was not required to enforce it upon the supplier's breach. C is incorrect. Revocation is relevant to contract formation, often involving non-conforming goods in the context of the UCC. But given that mutual assent occurred here and a valid requirements contract was formed, the supplier no longer had the right to revoke and was bound to the terms of the contract. D is incorrect. Requirements contracts do not need minimum quantity terms under UCC § 2-306(1) because a good faith obligation is imposed on the buyer to order reasonable quantities based on those originally estimated or comparable to the contract in question.
