Outline: Damages
Question: At a neighborhood association meeting, Speaker falsely told twenty residents, "Paula stole money from the association account." Paula is a private person, the matter is private, and no constitutional public-concern issue is present. Paula suffered embarrassment but cannot prove lost income. Paula sues Speaker for defamation. Can Paula's claim proceed without proof of special damages?
Answer A: No, because every spoken defamation claim requires special damages
Answer B: No, because Paula is not a public figure
Answer C: Yes, because a false spoken accusation of a serious crime is slander per se
Answer D: Yes, but only because twenty people heard it
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The statement falsely accused Paula of theft and was communicated to third parties. In a private-concern common-law setting, that crime accusation is slander per se.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Accusing someone of a serious crime is slander per se under common-law categories. B: Private status does not defeat the common-law slander-per-se route in this private-concern setting. D: Publication satisfies a separate element. The damages route comes from the slander-per-se category.
lb_rule_reference: A spoken accusation that the plaintiff committed a serious crime is slander per se and may not require proof of special damages under common-law rules.
