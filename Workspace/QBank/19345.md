Question: Delia owned a restaurant and held a valid food-service license. A state regulation prohibited serving shellfish that had not been inspected and certified by a state health officer. Delia purchased shellfish from a supplier she had used for five years, who had always provided certified product. Unknown to Delia, the supplier's last shipment was not certified because the supplier's certification lapsed. Delia served the shellfish, and a customer became ill. Delia is charged under a strict-liability food-safety statute that makes it a misdemeanor to "serve uninspected shellfish." Which of the following best describes the effect of Delia's reasonable, good-faith reliance on her supplier?
Answer A: Delia is not guilty because she acted in good faith and exercised reasonable care
Answer B: Delia is not guilty because her supplier's failure, not her own conduct, caused the regulatory violation
Answer C: Delia is guilty because the statute imposes strict liability and her good-faith reliance on her supplier is not a defense
Answer D: Delia is guilty only if she knew or should have known that the supplier's certification had lapsed
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Statutes governing public health, food safety, and similar regulatory areas are paradigmatic strict liability offenses. Courts identify strict liability based on: (1) the statute's silence on mens rea, (2) the subject matter (public welfare), and (3) the modest penalty (misdemeanor). All three indicators are present here. Delia served uninspected shellfish — the prohibited act — and that is sufficient for liability. Her good-faith reliance on her supplier, however reasonable, does not negate strict liability because no mens rea is required.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Public welfare regulatory offenses impose strict liability. The legislature has determined that public health hazards justify placing the burden of compliance entirely on regulated businesses, without requiring proof of fault. Good-faith care and reasonable diligence do not constitute a defense to strict liability crimes. The rationale is that strict liability incentivizes more careful monitoring than a negligence or intent standard would. B: Strict liability attaches to the regulated actor (Delia) based on her conduct (serving uninspected shellfish), not on whose fault created the underlying condition. The supplier's liability and Delia's liability are independent. A business owner who violates a strict-liability food-safety regulation is liable even if the immediate cause was a supplier's failure. Strict liability is designed precisely to prevent the regulated party from deflecting responsibility to upstream actors. D: Strict liability requires no mental state at all, including negligence. "Should have known" is a negligence standard; applying it would transform the strict-liability offense into a negligence-based offense. The legislature's choice to omit any mens rea language forecloses adding a negligence floor by judicial construction. Strict liability means the act alone triggers liability.
lb_rule_reference: Public welfare offenses are strict liability crimes; the defendant need not know his conduct violates the law or regulation, and good-faith compliance efforts are not a defense
