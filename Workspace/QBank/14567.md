Question: Police, who had probable cause to arrest a man for a series of armed robberies, obtained a warrant to arrest him. At 6 a.m. they surreptitiously entered the man's house and, with guns drawn, went to the man's bedroom, where they awakened him. Startled, the man asked, “What's going on?” and an officer replied, “We've got you now.” Another officer immediately asked the man if he had committed a particular robbery, and the man said that he had. The police then informed him that he was under arrest and ordered him to get dressed. Charged with robbery, the man has moved to suppress the use of his statement as evidence. What is the man's best argument for granting his motion?
Answer A: A: The police did not give him the required Miranda warnings.
Answer B: B: The statement was not voluntary.
Answer C: C: He was not informed that he was under arrest until after he made the statement.
Answer D: D: The police did not have a search warrant authorizing entry into the house.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A is correct. The police's failure to issue Miranda warnings is the strongest basis to get the statement suppressed. The man was very likely in custody, given the timeframe and manner of the police entry, even though he was in his home. Accordingly, the police could not properly interrogate the man without first providing him with Miranda warnings.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B is incorrect. A challenge to the voluntariness of the statement would be much more difficult than a Miranda challenge, given that the man's statement was very likely the product of unwarned custodial interrogation. C is incorrect. There is no requirement that a defendant must be informed of an arrest or a warrant; what is required is that a person in custody not be subjected to interrogation without Miranda warnings. D is incorrect. The arrest warrant sufficed to allow entry into the man's home.
