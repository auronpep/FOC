Subtopic: Performance, Breach, and Discharge
Outline: Covenants
Question: Isaac agreed to sell a parcel of land to Rachel. The contract set a closing date but did not require either party to perform first. On the closing date, Rachel arrived and said she would pay the purchase price only after Isaac delivered the signed deed. Isaac said he would deliver the signed deed only after Rachel paid the price. Neither tendered first, and Isaac sued Rachel for breach. Who is most likely to prevail?
Answer A: Isaac, because a buyer must always pay before the seller tenders a deed.
Answer B: Isaac, because Rachel's statement that she would not pay first was an anticipatory repudiation.
Answer C: Rachel, because a seller must always deliver a deed before the buyer has any obligation to appear.
Answer D: Neither on this record, because land closing duties are concurrent and each party generally must be ready and able to tender performance.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: When a land-sale contract sets a closing but does not specify who performs first, delivery of the deed and payment of the price are concurrent conditions. Each party must be ready and able to tender. Neither party may demand the other's performance while refusing to tender its own concurrent performance.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because the buyer does not have an unconditional duty to pay first. B: B is wrong because Rachel's statement reflects a concurrent-tender position, not necessarily an unequivocal refusal to perform. C: C is wrong because the seller also does not have an unconditional duty to perform first.
lb_rule_reference: Common-law concurrent conditions
