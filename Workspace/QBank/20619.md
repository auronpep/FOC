Subtopic: Other Torts
Outline: Invasion of privacy
Question: A clinic receptionist told one coworker, in a private break room, that a patient had tested positive for a non-newsworthy medical condition. The coworker did not repeat the information. The patient later learned of the conversation and sued for public disclosure of private facts. Is the patient likely to prevail on that claim?
Answer A: Yes, because the medical fact was private and embarrassing
Answer B: Yes, because any disclosure of medical information is public disclosure
Answer C: No, because disclosure to one coworker was not publicity
Answer D: No, because true statements can never create privacy liability
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The facts involve a private one-person disclosure that was not repeated. That is not the broad publicity required for public disclosure of private facts.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Public disclosure requires publicity, not a single private disclosure that goes no further. B: This tort has a publicity element. Other laws may regulate medical information, but the tort requires broad publicity. D: True private facts can support this tort when widely publicized. The missing element here is publicity.
lb_rule_reference: Public disclosure of private facts requires publicity to the public or to so many people that the matter is substantially certain to become public; disclosure to one person usually is insufficient.
