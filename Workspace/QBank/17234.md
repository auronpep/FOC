Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Specific and General Jurisdiction
Question: An Oregon architect contracted in Oregon with an Oregon homeowner to design a remodel for an Oregon house. All design meetings occurred in Oregon, and all work concerned the Oregon property. One year later, the homeowner moved to Nevada and then sued the architect in Nevada federal court for negligent design. Nevada's long-arm statute extends to the constitutional limit. Does Nevada have specific personal jurisdiction over the architect?
Answer A: Yes, because the homeowner now lives in Nevada and feels the financial harm there
Answer B: Yes, because Nevada's long-arm statute reaches the constitutional limit
Answer C: No, because the architect's relevant contacts were with Oregon, not Nevada
Answer D: No, because negligence claims can never support specific jurisdiction
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The architect did not reach into Nevada, target Nevada, or perform Nevada-connected work. The plaintiff's later move cannot create specific jurisdiction.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The plaintiff's relocation and felt harm are not defendant-created contacts with Nevada. B: Statutory reach does not itself satisfy due process; the defendant still needs forum contacts. D: Tort claims can support specific jurisdiction when the defendant's suit-related forum conduct meets due process.
lb_rule_reference: A defendant does not purposefully avail itself of a forum merely because the plaintiff moved there after the relevant transaction.
