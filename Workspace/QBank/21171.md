MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Other Crimes
Outline: Larceny
Question: Miles borrowed his coworker Paula's laptop without asking, telling himself he would return it the next morning. After using it overnight, Miles discovered that the laptop performed far better than his own and decided to keep it permanently, never returning it. There was no agreement or permission from Paula. At what point, if any, did Miles commit larceny?
Answer A: Miles committed no larceny because he lacked intent to steal when he first took the laptop
Answer B: Miles committed larceny when he first took the laptop, because any unauthorized taking is larceny
Answer C: Miles committed larceny when he decided to keep the laptop, because the continuing-trespass doctrine supplies the required concurrence at that moment
Answer D: Miles committed embezzlement, not larceny, because he had lawful possession when he formed the intent to keep the laptop
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Larceny requires a trespassory taking with intent to permanently deprive, and the actus reus and mens rea must concur. The continuing-trespass doctrine resolves the timing problem: because Miles's original taking was trespassory (no permission), the trespass continued through his possession. When he formed the intent to keep the laptop permanently, the ongoing trespassory taking and the newly formed intent to permanently deprive concurred, completing all elements of larceny at that moment.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The continuing-trespass doctrine provides that where the initial taking was itself trespassory (no permission), the trespass is treated as continuing through time. When Miles later formed the intent to keep the laptop, the trespass was still "occurring," supplying the required concurrence. The original unauthorized taking satisfies the trespassory-taking element retroactively once intent to steal arises. B: Larceny requires the specific intent to permanently deprive at the time of the taking (or, via continuing trespass, at the time intent later forms). At the moment of initial taking, Miles intended to return the laptop; he lacked larcenous intent. The crime was not yet complete at that point. D: Embezzlement requires that the initial possession be lawful — typically through an entrustment by the owner. Miles took Paula's laptop without any permission, making his possession trespassory from the start. Trespassory initial possession is the province of larceny (via continuing trespass), not embezzlement.
lb_rule_reference: Under the continuing-trespass doctrine, if a defendant takes property without permission and without intent to steal, and later forms the intent to keep it permanently, the original trespassory taking is treated as continuing at the moment the intent is formed, satisfying the concurrence requirement for larceny.
