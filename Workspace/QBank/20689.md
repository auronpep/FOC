Outline: Arson
Question: Max, angry at his neighbor's dog that has been barking all night, decides to "scare" the neighbor by lighting a small pile of dry leaves on the neighbor's wooden porch. The leaves burn vigorously, producing significant smoke and heat, and scorch the surface of the porch boards black. However, the wood fibers of the porch boards are not actually eaten into or charred — only the surface is discolored. The neighbor calls the fire department, which extinguishes the small fire. No wood fiber is consumed. Has Max committed common-law arson?
Answer A: Yes, because Max maliciously set fire to another's property
Answer B: Yes, because significant smoke and heat were produced, creating a substantial risk of burning
Answer C: No, because the burning element of common-law arson requires actual charring of the wood fibers of the structure, which did not occur here
Answer D: No, because the porch is not part of the "dwelling house" for purposes of arson
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Common-law arson requires a malicious burning of the dwelling house of another, and "burning" requires actual charring — the fire must consume or char the wood fibers of the structure itself. The porch boards were scorched on the surface, but their fibers were not charred or consumed. Surface discoloration from heat and smoke, without actual fiber destruction, does not satisfy the burning element. Max may be guilty of attempted arson and certainly of reckless endangerment or a fire-setting offense, but not completed common-law arson.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Common-law arson's burning element requires actual charring — the consumption of the wood fibers of the structure itself. The porch boards were scorched on the surface but their fibers were not charred or consumed. Surface discoloration or scorching without fiber destruction is not a "burning" under common-law arson. The burning element is not satisfied. B: Common-law arson requires actual burning of the structure's fibers, not just a risk of burning. Creating a dangerous condition that could have caused charring but did not is not arson — it is attempted arson at most. The risk of fire or the production of smoke are not substitutes for the actual charring of structural fibers. D: Common-law arson extends to all parts of the dwelling structure, including attached porches. The curtilage and attached structural components of a dwelling are generally within the scope of arson protection. This is not the reason Max escapes arson liability. The dispositive reason is the absence of charring of the structure's wood fibers.
lb_rule_reference: Common-law arson requires a malicious burning of the dwelling house of another. "Burning" means the wood fibers of the structure must be charred — the fire must actually consume part of the structure. Scorching the surface or producing smoke without charring is not sufficient.
