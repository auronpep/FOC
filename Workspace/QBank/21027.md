Subtopic: Homicide
Outline: Manslaughter
Question: A licensed electrician, Petra, was hired to rewire a homeowner's kitchen. Petra failed to check whether the circuit breaker had been switched off before beginning work, as she was running behind schedule. The circuit was live, and when the homeowner's twelve-year-old child entered the kitchen and touched an exposed wire Petra had left bare during a break, the child was fatally electrocuted. A reasonable electrician would have always verified the circuit was de-energized before leaving wires exposed and before taking any break. Is Petra guilty of involuntary manslaughter?
Answer A: No, because Petra was merely negligent in failing to verify the circuit, not criminally negligent
Answer B: Yes, for depraved-heart murder, because leaving live wires exposed shows indifference to human life
Answer C: Yes, for involuntary manslaughter, because failing to de-energize a circuit before leaving exposed live wires unattended constitutes criminal negligence
Answer D: No, because Petra did not know the child was in the home at the time she left the exposed wire
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Involuntary manslaughter at common law covers killings caused by criminal negligence — a gross deviation from the standard of care that a reasonable person (here, a reasonable licensed electrician) would exercise. Petra's failure to verify the circuit was de-energized before leaving live wires exposed in an occupied home with children present is a substantial departure from the professional standard. The harm was foreseeable. A child died as a direct result. This satisfies the criminal negligence standard for involuntary manslaughter.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Criminal negligence for involuntary manslaughter is a gross deviation from reasonable care — more egregious than civil negligence, but it does not require intent. A licensed electrician who leaves live, exposed wires unguarded and then takes a break, when any reasonable electrician would first verify the power was off, represents a significant departure from professional standards. Given that children were present in the home, the risk was foreseeable and the departure was substantial, satisfying criminal negligence. B: Depraved-heart murder requires conscious, subjective awareness of an extreme risk. Petra was negligent — she forgot to check. There is no evidence she knew the circuit was live and disregarded that knowledge. Inadvertent failure to follow a safety protocol, even a critical one, is criminal negligence, not conscious disregard of a known extreme risk. D: Criminal negligence does not require knowledge of the specific victim. It requires that the defendant's conduct created a foreseeable risk of death or serious bodily harm to others. Petra was working in an occupied home — the presence of residents (including children) was a foreseeable circumstance, and the risk of a child touching an exposed live wire was a foreseeable consequence of leaving the circuit live.
lb_rule_reference: Involuntary manslaughter based on criminal negligence requires a gross deviation from the care a reasonable person would exercise — more than ordinary negligence; civil negligence is insufficient
