Question: A state legislature is concerned about the mental health effects of violent imagery on children. It enacts the Healthy Minds Act, which makes it a misdemeanor to display, sell, or distribute any publication that "depicts graphic violence, including depictions of bodily injury, death, or torture," to any person under the age of 18. The Act does not apply to educational materials, news content, or historical documentaries. A comic-book publisher whose graphic-novel series contains detailed combat scenes challenges the law as an unconstitutional restriction on speech. Which standard of review does a court apply to the publisher's First Amendment challenge, and what is the most accurate statement of why?
Answer A: Rational basis review, because protecting minors is a legitimate government purpose and the legislature has broad discretion over child-welfare regulations
Answer B: Intermediate scrutiny, because the restriction targets minors and the government has an important interest in child development
Answer C: Strict scrutiny, because the Act restricts speech based on its content — the subject matter of graphic violence — and must be narrowly tailored to a compelling interest
Answer D: Strict scrutiny, but the Act will automatically fail because graphic violence involving minors is an unprotected speech category
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The Healthy Minds Act is content-based on its face: it targets speech that depicts graphic violence as such. Under Reed v. Town of Gilbert and the general First Amendment framework, a law that restricts speech by its communicative content triggers strict scrutiny. The government must show a compelling interest and that the law is narrowly tailored — not merely a reasonable fit — to survive. The exemptions for news and educational material do not make the law content-neutral; they underscore that the government is selecting speech based on content.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Rational basis applies to economic and social regulations with no fundamental right at stake. The First Amendment is a fundamental right, and this statute restricts protected speech based on its content (depictions of graphic violence). Content-based speech restrictions trigger strict scrutiny, not rational basis, regardless of how sympathetic the government's goal. B: Intermediate scrutiny does not apply simply because the regulated audience is minors. The Healthy Minds Act restricts publications based on their content — "graphic violence" is a subject-matter category. Content-based restrictions on protected speech require strict scrutiny. The only exception is for categories historically treated as unprotected (obscenity, true threats, etc.), and depictions of violence to minors do not fall into an established unprotected category under current doctrine. D: The correct standard is strict scrutiny, but strict scrutiny is not automatic invalidation. A law could theoretically survive if it is narrowly tailored to a compelling interest. More importantly, the Supreme Court has not categorically excluded graphic-violence-to-minors as an unprotected category. The law must be evaluated under strict scrutiny with the possibility — however difficult — of surviving. Calling it automatically invalid misstates the doctrine.
lb_rule_reference: A content-based speech restriction must survive strict scrutiny: it must be narrowly tailored to serve a compelling government interest. The threshold question is whether the law distinguishes speech by its communicative content.
