Question: A narcotics trafficker who has been convicted of felony drug distribution appeals her sentence on First Amendment grounds. She argues that a state statute making it a separate offense to "advertise or promote the sale of any controlled substance" is unconstitutional because it restricts commercial speech about a real market transaction. She contends that the advertising ban must survive Central Hudson's intermediate scrutiny and that the state has not shown the restriction directly advances a substantial government interest in ways a less extensive ban would not. How should a court rule on the First Amendment challenge to the advertising-ban statute?
Answer A: The statute is unconstitutional because the state did not demonstrate that banning drug sale advertisements directly and materially advances the interest in reducing drug use
Answer B: The statute is unconstitutional because speech remains protected even if the underlying activity is illegal, and the government must apply strict scrutiny to restrict any communication
Answer C: The challenge fails at the first prong of Central Hudson because commercial speech promoting the sale of an illegal controlled substance is not protected by the First Amendment
Answer D: The challenge fails because criminal conduct statutes are presumptively constitutional and courts defer to the legislature on drug policy
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Central Hudson's first prong requires that commercial speech concern a lawful activity and not be inherently misleading to receive any First Amendment protection. Advertising the sale of controlled substances promotes an illegal transaction. The speech fails the first prong, and the court need not analyze whether the government's interest is substantial, whether the restriction directly advances it, or whether the restriction is no more extensive than necessary. The statute is constitutional.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The court never reaches prongs two through four. The first prong of Central Hudson provides that commercial speech does not receive First Amendment protection if it concerns unlawful activity. Drug distribution is a felony. Advertising the sale of controlled substances is speech promoting an illegal transaction. The analysis stops at prong one. B: Commercial speech promoting illegal activity is not protected under the First Amendment. The Central Hudson framework specifically excludes such speech at the first prong. This is not a content-based restriction on fully protected speech; it is the well-established rule that speech advertising unlawful transactions falls outside First Amendment protection. D: This answer does not engage with the First Amendment doctrine at all. The correct reason the statute survives is the first prong of Central Hudson — not legislative deference or criminal-law presumptions. When the Constitution is the basis for a challenge, the court must apply constitutional doctrine, not simply defer to legislative policy judgments.
lb_rule_reference: Under the first prong of Central Hudson, commercial speech does not receive First Amendment protection if it concerns unlawful activity or is inherently misleading. If the speech fails the first prong, the court need not proceed to the remaining three prongs.
