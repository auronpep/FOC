Question: A delivery driver negligently drove onto a sidewalk, stopping several feet from a pedestrian. The pedestrian was frightened but was not touched, was not in immediate danger when the truck stopped, and suffered no physical symptoms. The pedestrian sues for negligent infliction of emotional distress. Is the pedestrian likely to recover under a zone-of-danger theory?
Answer A: Yes, because the driver's negligence caused genuine fear
Answer B: Yes, because no physical harm is ever required for negligence damages
Answer C: No, because the facts do not satisfy the zone-of-danger NIED gate
Answer D: No, because emotional distress is recoverable only for intentional torts
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The pedestrian was not struck, no physical symptoms occurred, and the facts do not show the recognized zone-of-danger limits needed for standalone NIED.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Standalone NIED is limited. The facts do not show immediate physical danger plus physical symptoms or a recognized equivalent. B: Standalone emotional-distress claims often require limiting facts such as zone of danger and physical manifestation. D: NIED can allow recovery in recognized categories. This plaintiff loses because the gate is not satisfied.
lb_rule_reference: Under the zone-of-danger approach, standalone NIED generally requires that the plaintiff was within the zone of physical danger and suffered physical symptoms or a recognized impact/manifestation.
