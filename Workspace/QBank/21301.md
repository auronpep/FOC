Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Definition
Question: In a federal negligence case, Deborah sues Malachi for ordinary compensatory damages after a minor car accident. Punitive damages are not sought, and Malachi's ability to pay is not otherwise at issue. Deborah offers evidence that Malachi owns three houses. Malachi objects. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Admit the evidence because a defendant's wealth is always relevant to damages.
Answer B: Admit the evidence because jurors may consider wealth when deciding who is more believable.
Answer C: Exclude the evidence only if Malachi has less insurance than Deborah believes.
Answer D: Exclude the evidence because Malachi's wealth does not make negligence, causation, or compensatory damages more or less probable.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: When punitive damages and ability to pay are not at issue, the defendant's wealth does not bear on negligence, causation, or ordinary compensatory damages. It is not relevant for the stated purpose.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because wealth is not automatically relevant to compensatory damages. B: B is wrong because wealth is not a general credibility measure. C: C is wrong because insurance is not the issue; the problem is lack of consequence.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401 / wealth evidence not consequential
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests wealth evidence and fact-of-consequence discipline.
