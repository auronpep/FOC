Outline: Robbery
Question: Samuel was indicted for aggravated robbery. Police conducted a post-indictment in-person lineup without notifying Samuel's defense counsel. The store clerk who witnessed the robbery identified Samuel at the lineup. The trial court correctly suppressed the lineup identification as a violation of Samuel's Sixth Amendment right to counsel under *United States v. Wade*. At trial, the prosecution called the clerk to the stand and asked her to identify the person who robbed the store. The clerk testified that during the robbery she stood two feet from the robber for approximately eight minutes under bright overhead lights, looked directly at his face, and would never forget it. She pointed to Samuel and said "That is the man." Should the trial court permit the clerk's in-court identification?
Answer A: No, because once a lineup identification is suppressed, all subsequent identifications by the same witness are automatically barred as fruit of the poisonous tree
Answer B: No, because the clerk's in-court identification rests on an independent source — her direct observation of the robbery — that is untainted by the suppressed lineup
Answer C: Yes, because the in-court identification is derivative of the tainted lineup and must be suppressed along with it
Answer D: No, because having the witness identify the defendant in open court is itself a suggestive confrontation that violates due process
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *United States v. Wade* and *Gilbert v. California*, when a pretrial lineup is conducted in violation of the Sixth Amendment, the prosecution must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the in-court identification has a source independent of the tainted lineup. The relevant inquiry is whether, despite the suggestive lineup, the witness would still be able to independently recall and identify the perpetrator from the crime itself. The clerk's prolonged, two-foot, face-to-face observation in good lighting for eight minutes is a textbook independent source. The in-court identification is admissible because it derives from the clerk's personal perception of the crime, not from the unconstitutional lineup.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: United States v. Wade* recognized that suppression of the out-of-court identification does not automatically require suppression of an in-court identification. The prosecution may still obtain an in-court ID if it demonstrates by clear and convincing evidence that the in-court identification rests on an independent source — a basis untainted by the illegal lineup. Here, the clerk's direct eight-minute observation of the robber at two feet provides a robust independent source. The in-court ID is admissible. C: Wade explicitly allows in-court identification when the government proves an independent source. The critical question is whether the witness's in-court identification can stand on its own, independent of any taint from the unlawful lineup. Given the clerk's extensive, direct observation of the robber during the crime, the prosecution can make that showing. The poisonous tree doctrine does not operate categorically to bar all future identifications by a witness who participated in a tainted lineup. D: In-court identifications are not analyzed under the *Biggers/Brathwaite* due process framework for pretrial procedures. While courts are sensitive to in-court suggestiveness, the constitutional analysis for an in-court ID following a tainted lineup focuses on whether an independent source exists — not on the inherent suggestiveness of courtroom seating arrangements.
lb_rule_reference: When a pretrial identification is suppressed as constitutionally tainted, the prosecution may still offer an in-court identification if the witness has an independent source of knowledge — a basis to identify the defendant untainted by the unlawful procedure — such as a face-to-face observation of the crime itself.
