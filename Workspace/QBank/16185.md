


BARMATRIX Q#: 16185
Outline_code: 72060102
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: General Principles
Outline: Infancy
Question: A defendant, a 14-year-old male, was tried as an adult for an armed robbery and was convicted. No one had been physically harmed during the robbery. In light of the defendant's lengthy juvenile criminal history, which included violent offenses, and the fact that armed robbery is a crime of violence, he was sentenced to life in prison without the possibility of parole. Did the life-without-parole sentence violate the Eighth Amendment ban on cruel and unusual punishment?
Answer A: A: No, because a juvenile may be sentenced to life without parole if release would pose a danger to the community.
Answer B: B: No, because the Eighth Amendment does not apply outside the context of capital punishment.
Answer C: C: Yes, because a life-without-parole sentence is not permissible for a juvenile defendant convicted of a nonho-micide offense.
Answer D: D: Yes, because a life-without-parole sentence is not permissible for an adult or juvenile defendant convicted of a nonviolent offense.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: (C)is the best response, because juvenile offenders have less culpability than adult offenders, so juveniles who do not kill are not deserving of the second-worst possible punishment. The facts here are comparable to the ones in Graham v. Florida, 560 U.S. 48 (2010) (although the defendant in Graham was 16 at the time of the armed robbery in question). The Court reasoned that “because juveniles have lessened culpability they are less deserving of the most severe punishments[.]” It then formulated a bright-line rule: “This Court now holds that for a juvenile offender who did not commit homicide the Eighth Amendment forbids the sentence of life without parole.” Choice (C) exactly restates this holding in Graham, making it the correct choice. (A)is not the best response, because, while the state need never release an offender if release would pose a danger to the community, the state may not make the determination at the time of sentencing that future release will always involve unacceptable danger to the community. As we discuss in Choice (C), Graham v. Florida, supra, says that it is unconstitutional for the state to make, at the moment of sentencing, the advance determination that releasing this non-homicidal former juvenile offender will inevitably pose a danger to the community. But even under Graham, the state is always free to decide, at the time of possible parole, that releasing the former juvenile who did not kill would now pose a danger to the community. This choice is incorrect because it effectively states, inconsistently with Graham, that the state may make the decision that “release of this defendant will never be safe” at the time of sentencing rather than waiting for the moment of possible parole. (B)is not the best response, because the Eighth Amendment does apply to some contexts outside that of capital punishment. As we discuss in Choice (C), Graham v. Florida, supra, makes it a violation of the Eighth Amendment's ban on cruel and unusual punishment for the state to sentence a juvenile who has not been convicted of a non-homicide crime to life without the possibility of parole. Graham is thus squarely inconsistent with the assertion in this choice that the Eighth Amendment never applies outside the capital punishment context. (D)is not the best response, because (1) the Supreme Court has never held that a life-without-parole sentence is not permissible for an adult convicted of a nonviolent offense; and (2) the robbery here was not a nonviolent offense. This choice is incorrect to the extent that it asserts that even for adult defendants, a life-without-parole sentence is not permissible for a nonviolent offense. First, the Supreme Court has never explicitly decided whether life-without-parole does or doesn't violate the Eighth Amendment when applied to an adult who has been convicted of a nonviolent offense; while it's quite plausible that the Court may someday issue such a holding, it hasn't done so yet. But there is an even bigger objection to this choice: the conviction here was for “armed robbery,” and armed robbery — since it involves a threatened or actual use of force — is certainly not “nonviolent.”
