MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Civil Procedure
Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Specific and General Jurisdiction
Question: Linden Supply Corp., a Missouri company, agreed by telephone to supply industrial cleaning solvents to Hartwell Factories Inc., a company headquartered in the State of Clarion. Linden's president negotiated the deal from Linden's Missouri office, and the written contract — prepared and signed in Missouri — contains a Missouri choice-of-law clause. Linden is to manufacture the solvents in Missouri, ship them FOB Linden's Missouri warehouse, and invoice Hartwell in Clarion. Hartwell places orders by email, and Linden sends invoices by mail to Clarion. Over 18 months, Hartwell placed 40 orders. After a dispute over the quality of a shipment, Hartwell filed suit against Linden in Clarion federal district court. Linden moves to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction, arguing that all its activities took place in Missouri. Under *Burger King* principles, how should the court rule?
Answer A: Deny the motion, because Linden contracted with a Clarion company and knew Hartwell would receive the goods and pay from Clarion
Answer B: Deny the motion, because Linden engaged in a 40-order course of dealing with Clarion, creating continuous and systematic contacts with the forum
Answer C: Grant the motion, because Linden's performance and all its significant contract activities occurred in Missouri, and its only contacts with Clarion are receiving orders and sending invoices — insufficient to establish purposeful availment of the Clarion forum
Answer D: Grant the motion, because a Missouri choice-of-law clause prevents any court outside Missouri from exercising jurisdiction over Linden
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Burger King*, purposeful availment requires examining negotiation location (Missouri), performance location (FOB Missouri — delivery and risk transfer in Missouri), choice-of-law (Missouri), and course of dealing (Linden manufactured, packaged, and shipped from Missouri; it never entered Clarion). Receiving orders by email and sending invoices are contacts initiated by Hartwell, not by Linden purposefully availing itself of Clarion. These thin contacts do not support specific jurisdiction. The motion should be granted.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Burger King* explicitly held that contracting with a forum-state party, alone, is insufficient to establish purposeful availment. The Court requires examination of the totality of contacts: where negotiations occurred (Missouri), where performance was to happen (FOB Missouri warehouse — ownership and risk transferred before Clarion), the choice-of-law clause (Missouri), and the course of dealing (all Linden-side activities were in Missouri). On these facts, Linden's contacts with Clarion are limited to receiving orders by email and sending invoices by mail — not substantial enough to establish purposeful availment. B: First, 40 email orders received in Missouri are not Linden's activities in Clarion — they are Hartwell's activities in Clarion. Second, even if this were treated as a contacts-based general-jurisdiction argument, the "continuous and systematic" contacts must be Linden's contacts with Clarion, not Hartwell's. Linden's performance occurred entirely in Missouri. Third, after *Daimler*, continuous/systematic contacts don't establish general jurisdiction over a corporation unless the forum is the state of incorporation or PPB. D: A choice-of-law clause specifies which state's substantive law governs the contract; it does not designate a forum and does not deprive any court of personal jurisdiction. *Burger King* itself held that a choice-of-law clause is relevant to the purposeful-availment analysis but is not determinative and certainly does not operate as a jurisdictional limit. The reason to grant the motion is inadequate contacts, not the choice-of-law clause.
lb_rule_reference: A contract with a forum-state resident does not, by itself, establish purposeful availment. Courts examine the totality of the contacts: where the contract was negotiated, where performance was to occur, choice-of-law provisions, and the prior course of dealing. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz* (1985).
