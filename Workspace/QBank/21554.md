Question: A store hired a sign company as an independent contractor to replace a large exterior sign. The sign company controlled the work methods and brought its own equipment. Before hiring the company, the store manager received written notice that the company had lost its city license after repeated sign-collapse violations. The manager hired it anyway because it was cheaper. The sign company negligently anchored the sign, and it fell on a customer. The customer sues the store for vicarious liability and direct negligence. Is the customer likely to prevail against the store on a direct negligence claim for hiring the independent contractor?
Answer A: No, because an independent contractor's negligence can never create liability for the principal
Answer B: Yes, because the store itself may have acted negligently by hiring a contractor it knew had repeated safety violations
Answer C: Yes, because principals are vicariously liable for every tort committed by contractors working on their premises
Answer D: No, because the customer must prove the store personally installed the sign
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The claim is direct negligence. The store had specific notice of the contractor's unsafe history and license loss, so the independent-contractor label does not defeat liability for the store's own negligent selection.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Independent-contractor status generally blocks vicarious liability, but it does not immunize the principal from its own negligent hiring. The store had notice of the contractor's unsafe licensing history and hired it anyway. C: The principal is not automatically vicariously liable for all independent-contractor torts on its premises. This fact pattern supports direct negligent hiring, not a blanket vicarious rule. D: Direct negligent hiring does not require the principal to perform the dangerous work. It asks whether the principal unreasonably selected an unsafe contractor and that risk caused the injury.
lb_rule_reference: A principal is usually not vicariously liable for an independent contractor, but the principal may be directly liable for negligent selection, supervision, or instructions.
