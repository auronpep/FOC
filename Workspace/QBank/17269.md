Question: John is subpoenaed before a federal grand jury and properly invokes the Fifth Amendment because his answers could incriminate him. The government then obtains an order granting John use and derivative-use immunity for his compelled testimony. John still refuses to answer, demanding complete transactional immunity. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Permit John to refuse, because only transactional immunity can displace the Fifth Amendment.
Answer B: Compel John to answer, because use and derivative-use immunity is sufficient to remove the real risk protected by the privilege.
Answer C: Permit John to refuse unless the government also grants immunity to every possible codefendant.
Answer D: Compel John to answer only if the questions are irrelevant to any crime.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Once the government grants immunity coextensive with the privilege—use and derivative-use immunity—the witness may be compelled to testify. Transactional immunity is broader than constitutionally required. The decisive axis is whether real incrimination risk remains after immunity.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because This overstates the immunity required to supplant the privilege. C: C is wrong because The witness's privilege protects against his own compelled self-incrimination, not every codefendant's exposure. D: D is wrong because If the questions were irrelevant to crime, immunity would not be needed; the point is that immunity removes the protected risk.
lb_rule_reference: Fifth Amendment / use-and-derivative-use immunity
lb_student_diagnostic: Student is treating any theoretical criminal context as enough despite immunity that removes protected use of compelled testimony.
