Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Elaine has a documented history of epilepsy and had been seizure-free for three years. Her neurologist had cleared her to drive. While driving on a highway, Elaine suffered a sudden, unpredicted grand mal seizure. During the seizure, her body convulsed, her foot pressed down on the accelerator, and her car veered into the oncoming lane, striking a motorcyclist and severely injuring him. There was no prior warning of the seizure on that day. Is Elaine's act of accelerating into the oncoming lane sufficient to establish the actus reus element for a criminal charge?
Answer A: Yes, because Elaine voluntarily chose to drive even though she knew she had epilepsy
Answer B: Yes, because Elaine was physically operating the vehicle at the time of the collision
Answer C: No, because the movement that caused the harm was an involuntary convulsion that Elaine did not control
Answer D: No, because Elaine did not intend to harm the motorcyclist
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Actus reus requires a voluntary act — a bodily movement the defendant willed and controlled. A grand mal seizure produces involuntary convulsions that are not within the individual's control. Because the movement that caused the collision was purely reflexive and involuntary, it does not satisfy the voluntary act requirement. The facts negate the "prior voluntary act" loophole: Elaine had medical clearance and no warning that a seizure was impending. Without a voluntary act, no criminal liability attaches regardless of the harm caused.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The prior-voluntary-act theory would require knowledge that a seizure was likely before the drive began. Here, Elaine had medical clearance and no recent seizures. The act that caused harm — pressing the accelerator — was an involuntary convulsion, not a voluntary act. Without evidence that the decision to drive was itself unreasonable given what she knew, this theory fails. B: Physical causation alone does not establish actus reus. The law requires a voluntary act — one the defendant willed or controlled. A seizure is an involuntary neurological event. The fact that Elaine's body was physically present and physically caused the result does not make the causing movement a voluntary act. A statute punishing involuntary bodily movements as criminal acts would be constitutionally suspect. D: While true that Elaine lacked intent to harm, the correct reason to deny liability here is the failure of actus reus (voluntary act), not the absence of mens rea. The question specifically asks about the actus reus element. Moreover, some charges (e.g., criminal negligence or recklessness) do not require intent to harm; they require only a voluntary act coupled with the requisite mental state. Grounding the defense in actus reus is the more precise and stronger argument.
lb_rule_reference: Actus reus requires a voluntary act; an involuntary bodily movement — including a reflex, convulsion, or unconscious act — does not satisfy the voluntary act requirement
