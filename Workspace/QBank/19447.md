Subtopic: Formation of Contracts
Outline: Common law
Question: Ruth contracted with a builder to repair the roof on Ruth's store. The contract did not mention Leah, who owned the store next door. The repair reduced water runoff onto Leah's property and increased the value of Leah's building. When the builder performed poorly, Leah sued the builder for breach of the Ruth-builder contract. Is Leah likely to recover as a third-party beneficiary?
Answer A: Yes, because Leah received a foreseeable economic benefit from the repairs.
Answer B: No, because Leah was only incidentally benefited by Ruth's contract with the builder.
Answer C: Yes, because the builder's breach caused Leah financial harm.
Answer D: No, unless Ruth first rescinds her contract with the builder.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Leah is only an incidental beneficiary. A practical or foreseeable benefit from another person's contract does not create enforcement rights unless the contracting parties intended to confer a direct enforceable benefit on the third party.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because foreseeability and practical benefit do not equal intended-beneficiary status. C: C is wrong because harm from nonperformance does not supply standing to enforce someone else's contract. D: D is wrong because Ruth's rescission is not the route; Leah lacks standing as an incidental beneficiary.
lb_rule_reference: Common law third-party beneficiary — incidental beneficiary
