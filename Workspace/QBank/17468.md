Question: A decades-old statute allows an environmental agency to require factories to use the "best system of emission reduction" at each facility. The agency adopts a rule requiring electric utilities nationwide to shift a large percentage of generation from coal plants to renewable sources. The statute does not mention generation shifting, national energy mix, or power-grid restructuring. Utilities challenge the rule. Which statement best captures the separation-of-powers concern?
Answer A: The rule is valid because any statutory ambiguity requires courts to defer to the agency's interpretation
Answer B: The rule is suspect because the agency is using ambiguous language to claim authority over a major national policy question without clear congressional authorization
Answer C: The rule is invalid because Congress may never delegate policy choices to agencies
Answer D: The rule is valid because environmental protection is always within agency expertise
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The rule restructures the national energy mix, a matter of major economic and political significance. The agency needs clear congressional authorization, not merely a broad or ambiguous phrase.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Major questions require clear congressional authorization for agency claims of vast economic and political significance. Ambiguity alone is not enough. C: Congress may delegate authority with an intelligible principle. The stronger problem here is the lack of clear authorization for a major question. D: Expertise does not substitute for clear congressional authorization when the agency claims transformative regulatory power.
lb_rule_reference: An agency needs clear congressional authorization to exercise power of vast economic and political significance; ordinary ambiguity does not automatically delegate transformative authority.
