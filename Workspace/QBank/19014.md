Question: Congress passed a statute providing that parties could no longer seek review in the U.S. Supreme Court of final judgments in criminal matters made by the highest court in each state. What is the best argument supporting the constitutionality of the statute? What is the best argument supporting the constitutionality of the statute?
Answer A: Congress has the power to make exceptions to the appellate jurisdiction of the Supreme Court.
Answer B: Criminal matters are traditionally governed by state law.
Answer C: The proper means of federal judicial review of state criminal matters is by habeas corpus.
Answer D: The review of state court judgments is not within the original jurisdiction of the Supreme Court.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A is correct. Article III gives Congress broad power to restrict the U.S. Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction. Some limits on this power have been suggested, but Congress nevertheless has the authority to strip the Court of its appellate jurisdiction to hear particular types of cases.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is incorrect. While criminal matters are typically governed by state law, this is not the strongest argument for the statute's constitutionality. State supreme court decisions in criminal cases that contain federal legal issues or otherwise trigger Supreme Court review are regularly heard by the Court on appeal. C: C is incorrect. To the contrary, a habeas corpus petition is not an exercise of direct federal judicial review of a criminal conviction, it is a collateral civil action demanding the release of a prisoner who has been unlawfully imprisoned. D: D is incorrect. It is not the Court's original jurisdiction at issue here, but its appellate jurisdiction and the ability of Congress to limit its scope, which it may properly do, as stated above.
additional_info: Article III, Section 2 of the U.S. Constitution provides that in all other cases before mentioned, including cases arising under the Constitution, Acts of Congress, or treaties, the Supreme Court shall have appellate jurisdiction, both as to law and fact, with such exceptions and under such regulations as Congress shall make. Congress has the general power to decide what types of cases the Supreme Court may hear, as long as it does not expand the Court's jurisdiction beyond the federal judicial power established by Article III, Section 2. Under Ex parte McCardle, 74 U.S. 506 (1868), Congress has full power to regulate and limit the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction. However, Congress does not have unlimited power to tamper with the Court's appellate jurisdiction. Suggested limits include that Congress may eliminate specific avenues for Supreme Court review as long as it does not eliminate all areas; Congress may eliminate Supreme Court review of certain cases within federal judicial power, but it must permit jurisdiction to remain in some lower federal court; and Congress may not deny all Supreme Court review of an alleged violation of constitutional rights or deny a hearing before any federal judge on such a claim without creating a due process problem. A habeas corpus petition is a collateral civil challenge to a criminal conviction, codified in 28 U.S.C. Section 2241. The statute in this case creates an exception to the Court's appellate jurisdiction by limiting review of final criminal judgments by state supreme courts. That limit falls within Congress's power to remove specific avenues of relief without eliminating all areas. Criminal matters are often governed by state law, but state criminal judgments can still be reviewed federally when federal questions are present. Habeas corpus is collateral rather than direct appellate review. Original jurisdiction is not the relevant jurisdictional category here; the statute concerns appellate jurisdiction.
