Question: A farmer contracted with a builder to construct a storage shed. The shed would block wind that often blew dust onto Leah's nearby shop. The contract did not mention Leah or direct any performance to her. Leah learned of the contract and sent both parties a letter saying, “I accept the benefit of your shed contract.” The farmer and builder later canceled the project. Can Leah enforce the shed contract?
Answer A: No, because Leah was only incidentally benefited and assent cannot vest rights she never had.
Answer B: Yes, because she manifested assent before cancellation.
Answer C: Yes, because the shed would have directly improved conditions at her shop.
Answer D: No, but only because the contract involved construction rather than goods.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Vesting matters only after the claimant is an intended beneficiary. Leah was not an intended beneficiary because the contract did not direct performance to her or show intent to give her an enforceable right. Her letter could not vest incidental benefits.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because assent cannot create intended-beneficiary status where the contract shows only incidental benefit. C: C is wrong because a direct practical benefit is still incidental if enforceable intent is missing. D: D is wrong because the goods/services source-law label is not the decisive point.
