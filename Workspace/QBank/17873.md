Question: Kevin broke into an unoccupied warehouse at night to steal copper wire. While carrying a heavy spool of wire, Kevin accidentally dropped it onto a night-security guard who had entered the warehouse without Kevin's knowledge. The guard died from the impact. Kevin was charged with felony murder predicated on burglary. Is Kevin guilty of felony murder?
Answer A: No, because Kevin did not know the guard was present and therefore lacked malice
Answer B: No, because the death was accidental and Kevin did not cause it intentionally
Answer C: Yes, because burglary is an inherently dangerous felony and death occurred in furtherance of it
Answer D: No, because burglary is a property crime and cannot serve as a predicate for felony murder
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Felony murder at common law imposes murder liability when a death occurs during the commission of (or in furtherance of) an inherently dangerous felony. Burglary — breaking and entering a structure at night with intent to commit a felony inside — is traditionally classified as an inherently dangerous felony because nighttime intrusions create substantial risks of violent confrontations. Kevin was committing burglary, the guard died in furtherance of that felony, and the death was a foreseeable risk of the criminal enterprise. Felony murder is established.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Felony murder does not require malice toward the specific victim or even awareness of the victim's presence. The felony itself supplies the malice. If the underlying felony qualifies and death results "in furtherance of" it, the defendant is liable regardless of intent toward the victim. However, the real issue here is whether burglary qualifies as an inherently dangerous predicate — not Kevin's awareness of the guard. B: Felony murder does not require an intentional killing. The doctrine holds the defendant liable for any death that occurs as a foreseeable consequence of committing an inherently dangerous felony, even if the actual cause is accidental. Accidental deaths during a qualifying felony are precisely what the doctrine covers. D: Burglary is one of the traditional common-law felonies recognized as inherently dangerous for felony murder purposes. Unlike crimes that can be committed safely, nighttime breaking and entering creates confrontation risks. The common-law list of recognized predicate felonies typically includes burglary, arson, robbery, rape, and kidnapping — it is not limited to "violent" crimes in the colloquial sense.
lb_rule_reference: Felony murder requires that the predicate felony be inherently dangerous to human life; felonies that can be committed in a non-dangerous manner do not qualify
