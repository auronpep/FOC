Question: Leah hired Joshua to build a stone wall for $30,000. The contract stated, “Payment is due only after the project architect certifies that the wall complies with the plans.” Joshua completed a wall, but the architect honestly refused certification because the wall leaned several inches outside the plan line. Joshua sued for the contract price, arguing that the wall was attractive and usable. What is the best result?
Answer A: Joshua wins because an attractive and usable wall is substantial performance as a matter of law.
Answer B: Leah's payment duty has not matured because the contract made honest architect certification a condition to payment and the condition did not occur.
Answer C: Leah must pay the reasonable value of the wall because certification clauses are never enforceable.
Answer D: Joshua wins unless Leah proves that Joshua acted in bad faith.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The parties made architect certification an express condition to Leah's payment duty. If the architect honestly refuses certification for noncompliance, the condition has not occurred. Substantial performance does not override the express certification condition on these facts.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because substantial performance does not substitute for strict occurrence of an express condition. C: C is wrong because certification clauses can be enforceable conditions. D: D is wrong because Joshua's good or bad faith does not cause Leah's payment duty to mature when the condition failed honestly.
lb_rule_reference: Condition versus covenant / certification as condition
