Question: An indigent defendant is convicted of felony robbery in state court. The state provides a one-tier appeal as of right to an intermediate appellate court. The defendant seeks appointed appellate counsel and a free copy of the trial transcript to pursue her appeal. The state refuses both requests, citing budget constraints, and informs the defendant that she may appeal pro se. The defendant argues that the refusal violates her constitutional rights. Which of the following correctly analyzes the defendant's constitutional claims?
Answer A: The defendant has no valid claim because there is no constitutional right to an appeal; therefore, the state may impose whatever conditions it chooses on its optional appellate process
Answer B: The defendant has a valid claim to both a free transcript and appointed counsel for the first appeal as of right under the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses
Answer C: The defendant has a valid claim to a free transcript but not to appointed counsel, because the right to counsel does not extend beyond trial
Answer D: The defendant has a valid claim to appointed counsel but not to a free transcript, because transcripts are a matter of state procedure, not constitutional entitlement
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The constitutional framework has two steps. First, there is no federal constitutional right to appellate review—states are not required to provide appeals. Second, when a state does provide a first appeal as of right, the Constitution attaches: (a) Griffin v. Illinois holds that the state must provide an indigent defendant a free transcript (or adequate substitute) for appeal, because to do otherwise conditions the ability to appeal on wealth; (b) Douglas v. California holds that the state must appoint counsel for the first appeal as of right, because meaningful access to the appellate process requires legal assistance for that initial tier. The defendant's claims for both transcript and counsel on a first appeal as of right are constitutionally valid.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: While it is true that no constitutional right to appeal exists, once a state provides an appeal, the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses govern the appellate process. The Supreme Court held in Griffin v. Illinois that denying an indigent defendant a free transcript for appeal violates equal protection. Douglas v. California held that refusing appointed counsel for a first appeal as of right violates equal protection and due process. The state cannot create an appellate right and then make it accessible only to those who can pay for transcripts and counsel. C: Douglas v. California (1963) held that the Equal Protection Clause requires appointed counsel for the first appeal as of right. This is a distinct constitutional basis from the Sixth Amendment trial right. For the first tier of appeal that the state provides as a matter of right, counsel must be appointed for indigent defendants. The right does not extend to discretionary appeals (Ross v. Moffitt), but it applies fully to the first appeal as of right. D: Griffin v. Illinois (1956) held that conditioning a defendant's ability to appeal on the ability to pay for a transcript violates equal protection. The free transcript is not merely a procedural convenience—it is constitutionally required because an appeal without access to the record is not a meaningful appeal. Both the transcript and counsel are required for a meaningful first appeal as of right.
lb_rule_reference: There is no constitutional right to appellate review of a criminal conviction; however, if a state provides an appeal, the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses require that the appeal be meaningful, including that an indigent defendant receive a free trial transcript and appointed counsel for the first appeal as of right (Douglas v. California)
