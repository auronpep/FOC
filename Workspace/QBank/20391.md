Question: Police responded to a report of a shooting at an apartment building. They found Marcus standing in the hallway outside an apartment where a body was discovered. Officers told Marcus, "We need to ask you some questions," and directed him to a common room in the building. Two officers questioned Marcus in the common room for forty-five minutes. Marcus was not told he was free to leave, but he was also not told he was under arrest. Marcus eventually admitted he had argued with the victim earlier that day. He was not handcuffed or physically restrained. Was Marcus in Miranda custody during the questioning in the common room?
Answer A: Yes, because Marcus was not told he was free to leave and police outnumbered him in an enclosed space
Answer B: Yes, because the questioning lasted forty-five minutes, making the detention more than temporary
Answer C: No, because Marcus was not formally arrested, was not handcuffed, and the encounter occurred in the building where he was already present
Answer D: No, because Marcus voluntarily agreed to answer questions, which negates any finding of custody
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Miranda custody requires that a reasonable person in the suspect's position would not feel free to terminate the encounter and leave. Here, Marcus was not handcuffed, not told he was under arrest, not placed in a police vehicle, and was questioned in a common area of a building where he was already located. These circumstances fall short of formal arrest or its functional equivalent. Most courts analyzing similar scene-of-crime questioning hold the suspect was not in custody. Miranda warnings were therefore not required.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Miranda custody test is objective: whether a reasonable person in the circumstances would feel free to terminate the encounter. Failure to inform someone they are free to leave is relevant but not dispositive. Courts regularly hold that scene-of-crime questioning of witnesses is not custody absent formal arrest or equivalent restraint. B: Duration is one factor but not determinative. Courts focus on the degree of restraint on movement, the location, and the presence of formal indicia of arrest. Forty-five minutes of voluntary cooperation at a scene, without handcuffs, formal arrest, or locked room, does not automatically create custody. D: Voluntariness of cooperation is not the test for Miranda custody. The objective test asks whether a reasonable person would feel free to leave, which is an external standard not determined by subjective willingness to talk. The correct analysis focuses on the objective indicia of restraint, not on whether Marcus chose to cooperate.
lb_rule_reference: Presence at the scene of a crime and police-dominated surroundings do not automatically create Miranda custody; the objective test asks whether a reasonable person would feel free to terminate the encounter and leave.
