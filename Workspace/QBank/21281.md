MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Intentional Torts
Outline: Battery
Question: A nightclub hired an employee as an in-house security guard to remove disruptive patrons. During a crowded event, the guard ordered a patron to leave after a loud argument. When the patron hesitated, the guard shoved him hard down the stairs, causing injury. The shove was excessive, but it occurred while the guard was ejecting the patron from the club. Is the nightclub likely vicariously liable for the guard's battery of the patron?
Answer A: No, because intentional torts are always outside the scope of employment
Answer B: Yes, because the guard's excessive force occurred while performing the assigned task of removing a patron
Answer C: Yes, but only because the nightclub was negligent in hiring the guard
Answer D: No, because the guard violated the club's policy against shoving patrons
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The guard was doing the kind of work he was hired to do, at the club, during the event. Excessive force in that job-related confrontation can fall within scope.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Intentional torts can be within scope when closely connected to job duties, especially security work involving foreseeable physical confrontation. C: The facts do not state negligent hiring. The strongest theory is vicarious liability based on job-related scope. D: An employee can act within scope despite violating instructions if the act is closely connected to assigned duties.
lb_rule_reference: An employer may be vicariously liable for an employee's intentional tort when the act is closely connected to assigned duties or foreseeable job-related friction.
