Question: Nathaniel and his neighbor Felix have feuded for years. One afternoon, Felix waves his hand while telling Nathaniel to get off his property. Nathaniel, who has anxiety disorder and who genuinely interprets every gesture from Felix as a physical threat, believes Felix is about to strike him. Nathaniel punches Felix repeatedly, and Felix dies of a head injury. A psychiatrist testifies that Nathaniel's fear was completely genuine, though any objectively reasonable person would have recognized Felix was merely gesturing. Nathaniel is charged with murder. Which verdict is most appropriate under the common law?
Answer A: Not guilty, because Nathaniel genuinely believed he was being attacked
Answer B: Guilty of murder, because Nathaniel's belief was objectively unreasonable
Answer C: Guilty of voluntary manslaughter, because Nathaniel had an honest but unreasonable belief in the need for force
Answer D: Not guilty by reason of insanity, because Nathaniel has an anxiety disorder affecting his perceptions
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Imperfect self-defense applies when a defendant sincerely (honestly) believes deadly or serious force is necessary, but that belief is not objectively reasonable. The honest component negates the malice required for murder — a defendant acting out of genuine (if mistaken) fear lacks the cold-blooded or reckless indifference that defines malice aforethought. But the objective unreasonableness of the belief prevents complete acquittal. The result is voluntary manslaughter. This doctrine is explicitly recognized in most common-law jurisdictions as the gap between a perfect defense (acquittal) and no defense at all (murder).
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Common-law self-defense requires both an honest and a reasonable belief. An honest but unreasonable belief does not support full acquittal under the majority common-law rule. The reasonable person standard is an objective element that Nathaniel fails. B: Imperfect self-defense doctrine applies precisely here. When a defendant has an honest (sincere) belief in the need for force — even if that belief is objectively unreasonable — the honesty of the belief negates the malice element required for murder, reducing the charge to voluntary manslaughter. Murder requires malice; an honest (if unreasonable) belief in self-defense negates the malice component. D: Insanity is a separate defense with a specific test (M'Naghten, MPC/ALI, etc.) requiring that the defendant did not know the nature of the act or did not know it was wrong. An anxiety disorder that distorts threat perception does not meet the insanity threshold — Nathaniel knew he was punching Felix and believed it was justified self-defense, not a symptom that negated knowledge of the act's nature or wrongfulness. The correct doctrine is imperfect self-defense, not insanity.
lb_rule_reference: Imperfect self-defense: when a defendant honestly but unreasonably believes deadly force was necessary, the charge is reduced from murder to voluntary manslaughter; the defendant is not acquitted and not convicted of murder
