Question: A state prisoner was convicted of armed robbery following a trial at which the court gave a reasonable-doubt instruction that was arguably ambiguous. The defendant raised the instruction issue on direct appeal; the state supreme court affirmed, holding the instruction was proper. The defendant did not petition for certiorari to the United States Supreme Court. He now files a federal habeas petition under § 2254 claiming the instruction violated due process. The federal district court disagrees with the state supreme court's analysis and concludes the state court's interpretation of the instruction was incorrect, but also finds that the state court's reasoning was not so flawed as to be unreasonable. The district court denies relief. Was the district court correct to deny habeas relief?
Answer A: No, because AEDPA requires federal courts to conduct a de novo review of constitutional claims, and the district court's independent conclusion that the state court erred requires it to grant the writ
Answer B: No, because the defendant failed to petition for certiorari to the U.S. Supreme Court after the state supreme court affirmed, and exhaustion requires certiorari review before federal habeas is available
Answer C: Yes, because AEDPA requires the federal court to defer to the state court's adjudication unless it resulted in a decision that is contrary to, or involves an unreasonable application of, clearly established Supreme Court precedent; mere disagreement with the state court's reasoning does not justify relief
Answer D: Yes, but only because the defendant did not exhaust state remedies; if he had pursued state post-conviction relief, the merits of the instruction claim would be reviewed de novo
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under § 2254(d) as interpreted in Williams v. Taylor, a federal court may grant habeas relief only if the state court adjudication: (1) resulted in a decision contrary to clearly established Supreme Court precedent (i.e., the state court applied a rule that directly contradicts Supreme Court precedent, or reached an opposite result on materially indistinguishable facts), or (2) involved an unreasonable application of clearly established Supreme Court precedent (i.e., the state court identified the right principle but applied it to the facts in an objectively unreasonable manner). The district court here found the state court was merely incorrect but not unreasonable. That is insufficient under AEDPA. The exhaustion requirement is satisfied because the state supreme court ruled on the merits. The district court correctly denied relief.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: AEDPA eliminated de novo review of claims adjudicated on the merits in state court. The standard is highly deferential: a federal court may grant relief only if the state court decision was (1) contrary to clearly established Supreme Court precedent or (2) an unreasonable application of clearly established Supreme Court precedent. A federal court's disagreement with the state court's reasoning does not justify relief. The state court must have reached an objectively unreasonable conclusion. B: Certiorari to the United States Supreme Court is not part of the exhaustion requirement under § 2254. Exhaustion requires that the defendant fairly present his federal claims to the highest state court that has jurisdiction over the matter. Once the state supreme court has ruled, the exhaustion requirement is satisfied. The defendant is not required to file a certiorari petition before seeking federal habeas relief. D: The exhaustion requirement is satisfied here—the defendant raised the instruction claim on direct appeal through the state supreme court. There is no unexhausted claim. Moreover, even if the defendant had exhausted additional state post-conviction remedies, the standard of review would still be AEDPA deference, not de novo. De novo review applies only to claims not adjudicated on the merits by the state court.
lb_rule_reference: Under 28 U.S.C. § 2254 (AEDPA), a federal court may not grant habeas relief unless the state court adjudication (1) resulted in a decision contrary to clearly established federal law as determined by the Supreme Court, or (2) involved an unreasonable application of clearly established federal law; all state remedies must be exhausted before federal habeas is available
