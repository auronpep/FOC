Subtopic: Homicide
Outline: Murder
Question: Noah had an intense, irrational fear of dogs after a childhood trauma. When his neighbor, Paula, brought her small, non-aggressive terrier into Noah's backyard without permission, Noah panicked and experienced what he described as a total loss of self-control. He picked up a brick and struck Paula with it, killing her. Psychologists testified that Noah's phobia was genuine and severe, and that a person with his trauma history might have a more extreme response to the dog than an average person. Is Noah guilty of murder or voluntary manslaughter?
Answer A: Voluntary manslaughter, because Noah genuinely experienced extreme passion and loss of self-control
Answer B: Voluntary manslaughter, because the psychological testimony establishes that Noah lacked the capacity for full self-control in the moment
Answer C: Murder, because a small, non-aggressive dog entering a backyard does not constitute legally adequate provocation under the objective reasonable-person standard
Answer D: Murder, but the prosecution must disprove provocation beyond a reasonable doubt before a conviction is proper
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Voluntary manslaughter requires adequate provocation measured objectively: would a reasonable person have been provoked to the point of losing self-control? A small, non-aggressive dog briefly entering a yard is not objectively adequate provocation to kill. Noah's genuine subjective terror — however clinically real — does not substitute for the objective standard. The provocation element fails, and the killing is murder. Noah's psychological condition may be relevant at sentencing or to other defenses, but it does not reduce the charge to voluntary manslaughter.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The adequate provocation standard is objective, not subjective. The question is whether a reasonable person (not a person with Noah's particular psychological vulnerabilities) would have been provoked to the point of losing self-control by a small, non-aggressive dog entering one's backyard. The answer is no. Noah's individual psychology, however genuine, does not satisfy the objective standard. B: Diminished capacity is a separate doctrine from voluntary manslaughter's heat-of-passion defense, and most common-law jurisdictions do not recognize diminished capacity as a full mitigation to manslaughter. More importantly, even if diminished capacity were available, the voluntary manslaughter analysis still requires objectively adequate provocation, which is absent here. The two doctrines are not interchangeable. D: The burden of proof question is distinct from the substantive question asked. The question asks what offense Noah is guilty of — not who bears the burden. D does not answer the substantive question asked, making it legally incomplete as a choice. The answer to the question is murder because the provocation was legally insufficient.
lb_rule_reference: Provocation that is genuine and subjectively experienced by the defendant but does not meet the objective reasonable-person standard for adequate provocation leaves the charge at murder; genuine subjective passion does not reduce murder to manslaughter absent legally adequate provocation
