Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Personal Jurisdiction
Question: Calloway Textiles Corp., a Georgia corporation with its principal place of business in Atlanta, filed a breach-of-contract action against Winterbourne Design LLC, a company incorporated and headquartered in the State of Fenwick. The amount in controversy is $190,000. Calloway filed in a Fenwick federal district court, asserting diversity jurisdiction. Winterbourne is incorporated in Fenwick and headquartered in Fenwick. Winterbourne's sole contact with Georgia is that it entered a contract with Calloway by email — the contract was negotiated remotely, performance was to occur in Fenwick, the contract contains no choice-of-law clause, and all of Winterbourne's operations are in Fenwick. Winterbourne argues that (1) diversity jurisdiction is absent because Calloway is a Georgia corporation suing a Fenwick corporation in Fenwick, which means there is no real dispute between citizens of different states, and (2) the court lacks personal jurisdiction over Winterbourne in Fenwick because Winterbourne has insufficient contacts with Georgia. Which of Winterbourne's two arguments is correct?
Answer A: Both arguments are correct: there is no diversity jurisdiction because the parties are from different states but the suit was filed in one party's home state, and personal jurisdiction is absent because Winterbourne has minimal Georgia contacts
Answer B: Only the personal jurisdiction argument is correct: Winterbourne's email contracting with a Georgia company is insufficient to establish specific jurisdiction over Winterbourne in Georgia for a Fenwick-performance contract
Answer C: Neither argument is correct: diversity jurisdiction exists because Calloway (Georgia) and Winterbourne (Fenwick) are citizens of different states, and the Fenwick court has general personal jurisdiction over Winterbourne as its home state
Answer D: Only the diversity jurisdiction argument is correct: there is no diversity because both parties were dealing with a contract to be performed in Fenwick, making them both "citizens" of Fenwick for purposes of that contract
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Diversity jurisdiction: Calloway is a citizen of Georgia (state of incorporation + PPB); Winterbourne is a citizen of Fenwick (state of incorporation + PPB). Complete diversity exists. The forum where the suit is filed is irrelevant to diversity citizenship. AIC is $190,000, exceeding the $75,000 threshold. SMJ is proper. Personal jurisdiction in Fenwick: Winterbourne is incorporated and headquartered in Fenwick — Fenwick is its home state under *Daimler*. Fenwick has general personal jurisdiction over Winterbourne for any claim. Both arguments fail; the court has jurisdiction.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The diversity-jurisdiction argument is completely wrong. Diversity jurisdiction requires complete diversity between the parties — Calloway (Georgia) vs. Winterbourne (Fenwick) — complete diversity exists regardless of where the suit is filed. The fact that the suit is in Fenwick (one party's home state) is irrelevant to SMJ diversity analysis. Diversity jurisdiction concerns the citizenship of the parties, not the forum. The PJ argument is also incorrect (see choice C). Both of Winterbourne's arguments fail. B: The suit is filed in Fenwick — Winterbourne's home state. Fenwick has general personal jurisdiction over Winterbourne as its home state (incorporation and PPB are both in Fenwick). Winterbourne's PJ argument (that it has insufficient contacts with Georgia) is directed at the wrong forum: the plaintiff filed in Fenwick, not Georgia. In Fenwick, Winterbourne is at home — Fenwick has general jurisdiction. The PJ argument fails entirely. And the SMJ argument also fails (see choice A above). D: Diversity citizenship for corporations is determined by state of incorporation and principal place of business — not by where a particular contract is to be performed. Calloway is a Georgia citizen; Winterbourne is a Fenwick citizen. Contract performance location has nothing to do with citizenship for diversity purposes. There is complete diversity.
lb_rule_reference: Subject-matter jurisdiction concerns the court's authority to hear a category of case (federal question or diversity). Personal jurisdiction concerns the court's authority over the particular parties before it. A federal court sitting in diversity must have both: (1) SMJ — complete diversity + AIC over $75,000 — and (2) PJ over the defendants, typically established through state long-arm reach plus due process. Neither establishes the other.
