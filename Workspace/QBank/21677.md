Outline: Defamation
Question: An architect led a public campaign against a proposed bridge, gave interviews, ran a website, and appeared at hearings to influence the project. A newspaper then falsely reported that she had fabricated bridge-safety data. The report concerned the bridge controversy. The architect sues for defamation. What fault standard will most likely apply?
Answer A: Negligence, because the architect does not hold public office
Answer B: Strict liability, because the newspaper statement was false
Answer C: Actual malice, because she is likely a limited-purpose public figure for bridge-related statements
Answer D: Actual malice only if she is famous in every aspect of her life
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: She voluntarily became prominent in the bridge controversy to influence the outcome. The false statement related to that controversy, so actual malice applies.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A person can become a limited-purpose public figure for a specific public controversy without holding office. B: Constitutional rules require fault, and limited-purpose public figure status raises the standard. D: Limited-purpose public figure status is narrower and controversy-specific.
lb_rule_reference: A person who voluntarily injects herself into a public controversy to influence its outcome can be a limited-purpose public figure for statements related to that controversy.
