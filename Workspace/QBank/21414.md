Outline: Expectation damages
Question: Sarah hired Daniel to install software for Sarah's first inventory-service outlet. At contracting, Daniel knew that the outlet would earn revenue from signed customer subscriptions. Daniel repudiated before installing the software. At trial, Sarah offered signed subscription agreements and pilot data showing $35,000 in net lost profits with reasonable certainty. The trial judge instructed the jury that a new business may never recover lost profits. How should an appellate court rule on that instruction?
Answer A: Affirm, because lost profits of a new business are categorically unrecoverable.
Answer B: Affirm, because foreseeability is measured only when Daniel repudiated.
Answer C: Reverse, because newness alone does not bar lost profits that are proven with reasonable certainty and otherwise recoverable.
Answer D: Reverse only if Sarah also proves the reasonable value of Daniel's services.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The remedy interest is expectation under common law, and the claimed loss is lost profit. The limiting gate is certainty, with foreseeability separately supplied by Daniel's knowledge at contracting. A categorical new-business exclusion is the wrong rule; reasonably certain net profits may be recovered if the other gates are satisfied. The result scope is limited to provable expectation profits, and the answer does not decide reliance or restitution.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because it states WAL-REM-001, an outdated categorical rule overread. B: B is wrong because it uses WAL-REM-004, a timing substitution; foreseeability is measured at contracting, not repudiation. D: D is wrong because reasonable value is a restitution-style measure, not the required expectation-profit route.
lb_rule_reference: Common law lost-profit expectation damages — reasonable certainty, not categorical new-business bar
