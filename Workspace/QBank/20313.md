Question: Ruth told her lawyer, Peter, “I am considering transferring equipment to my brother before a creditor sues me. Would that be legal? If it is fraudulent, I will not do it.” Peter researched the law and advised Ruth not to make the transfer. Are Ruth's communications protected by attorney-client privilege?
Answer A: Yes, because she sought confidential legal advice to determine and avoid unlawful conduct, not to further a fraud.
Answer B: No, because any discussion of a possible fraudulent transfer triggers the crime-fraud exception.
Answer C: No, because Peter ultimately advised against the transfer.
Answer D: Yes, but only if the creditor later fails to prove substantial need for the communications.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The crime-fraud exception does not defeat privilege merely because the client asks about a transaction that could be unlawful. A client may seek advice about what the law permits and how to comply. Ruth's stated purpose was to avoid fraud, not to execute it. The decisive axis is compliance advice versus abuse of legal advice.
