Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Right to Counsel
Question: Silva, a lawful permanent resident who has lived in the United States for twenty years, was charged with a drug offense. His defense attorney negotiated a plea to a misdemeanor drug charge. The attorney told Silva only that the plea "might" affect his immigration status but did not advise him that the offense made deportation "virtually certain" under federal immigration law. Silva entered the plea. He was subsequently placed in removal proceedings. Silva filed a post-conviction petition for relief arguing ineffective assistance of counsel. Did Silva's defense attorney provide constitutionally deficient performance under *Padilla v. Kentucky*?
Answer A: No, because deportation is a collateral consequence of conviction, and counsel has no Sixth Amendment duty to advise on collateral consequences
Answer B: No, because Silva entered the plea knowingly, having been told immigration status might be affected
Answer C: Yes, because *Padilla v. Kentucky* requires counsel to advise a non-citizen client clearly about deportation consequences when the immigration law makes deportation virtually certain, and telling Silva that deportation "might" occur was misleading and deficient
Answer D: Yes, but only because Silva was a lawful permanent resident; the duty would not apply to undocumented immigrants
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Padilla v. Kentucky* (2010) held that the Sixth Amendment requires defense counsel to advise non-citizen clients about the deportation consequences of a guilty plea. Where the terms of the immigration law are "truly clear," counsel must give clear advice. A drug conviction that triggers mandatory deportation makes the consequence virtually certain; telling the client only that there "might" be an effect falls short of this standard and constitutes deficient performance under *Strickland's* first prong. Silva must still prove prejudice — that he would have rejected the plea had he been properly advised.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Padilla v. Kentucky* (2010) abrogated this rule specifically for deportation. The Court held that deportation is so intimately connected to the criminal process that counsel must advise non-citizen clients about it. When the immigration consequence of a plea is "truly clear," as here (near-certain deportation), counsel must advise the client clearly; telling Silva it "might" affect status is constitutionally deficient. B: Padilla* requires accurate, specific advice proportionate to the clarity of the immigration law. When deportation is virtually certain (as with certain controlled substance offenses), telling the client only that there "might" be an effect is affirmatively misleading and constitutionally deficient. A vague warning is worse than no warning in some respects because it understates the actual risk. D: Padilla* does not limit the duty to lawful permanent residents. The Sixth Amendment duty to advise on deportation consequences applies to all non-citizens, regardless of their immigration status, because all non-citizens face potential removal upon conviction.
