Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: Officers arrested Greg without giving Miranda warnings and questioned him. Greg voluntarily told officers that he had hidden a stolen firearm in his garage. Officers searched the garage and found the gun. Greg's statement was never used against him at trial. The prosecution seeks to introduce the gun into evidence. Greg argues that because the gun was discovered as a result of his unwarned statement, it is fruit of the poisonous tree and must be suppressed. Is the gun admissible?
Answer A: No, because the gun is the fruit of the poisonous tree — obtained directly as a result of Greg's Miranda-violating statement
Answer B: No, because Greg's statement was voluntary and a voluntary statement negates any Miranda violation
Answer C: Yes, the gun is admissible because *United States v. Patane* holds that physical evidence obtained through an unwarned but voluntary statement need not be suppressed
Answer D: Yes, the gun is admissible because the inevitable discovery doctrine would have led police to the garage regardless of Greg's statement
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: In *United States v. Patane* (2004), the Supreme Court held (plurality) that the exclusionary rule does not apply to physical evidence discovered as a result of a Miranda violation. Miranda's protections are directed at testimonial evidence — compelled self-incrimination — and the Self-Incrimination Clause is not implicated by the introduction of physical objects. Because Greg's statement, though unwarned, was voluntary (no coercion), and because the prosecution is not using the statement itself, the gun is admissible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: United States v. Patane* (2004) held that Miranda's prophylactic rule does not require exclusion of physical evidence obtained through unwarned statements. Miranda protects against the use of compelled testimonial evidence; it does not extend to physical objects derived from voluntary (though unwarned) statements. The fruit-of-the-poisonous-tree doctrine as applied to Miranda violations does not reach physical evidence. B: Miranda requires warnings independent of voluntariness. A statement can be voluntary (not coerced under Due Process) yet still violate Miranda (no warnings before custodial interrogation). The gun is admissible, but the reason is *Patane* — not the voluntariness of the statement. The correct rule is specific to physical fruits of Miranda violations, not voluntariness generally. D: Inevitable discovery may apply but is unnecessary here. The primary and correct basis for admissibility is *Patane* — physical fruits of Miranda violations are not subject to suppression at all. Invoking inevitable discovery implies there is a violation that needs to be excused by an exception, which is doctrinally inaccurate under *Patane*.
lb_rule_reference: Under *United States v. Patane* (2004), a Miranda violation does not require suppression of physical evidence obtained as a result of an unwarned but voluntary statement; only the unwarned statement itself is excluded.
