


BARMATRIX Q#: 22863
Outline_code: 94010000
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Civil Procedure
Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Federal Subject-Matter Jurisdiction
Question: An actress, a citizen of State A, married a much older man who owned property in several different states. When the man died, the actress filed a probate claim in state court challenging the last will and testament of her deceased husband. The man's will left his entire estate to his adult son, although the actress claimed that the man had promised to leave her money to provide for her financial security. At the same time as the probate claim, the actress filed a bankruptcy claim and a tortious interference claim against the son, a citizen of State B, in federal court. She claimed $5 million in damages, arguing that the son had tortiously interfered with her expected financial gift by purposely keeping the man away from her during the last year of the man's life. After a trial on the merits, the federal court ruled in favor of the actress on the tortious interference claim. Thereafter, the state court found the will to be valid. The son appealed the federal court's decision, arguing that the federal court lacked subject matter jurisdiction over the actress's claim because the claim involved probate matters. Does the federal court have subject matter jurisdiction over the tortious interference claim?
Answer A: A: Yes, because there is complete diversity of the parties and the amount in controversy is over $75,000.
Answer B: B: Yes, because the man's estate contained property from several different states.
Answer C: C: No, because the probate exception reserves to state probate courts the right to adjudicate the probate of a will and the administration of a decedent's estate.
Answer D: D: No, because the state probate court found the will to be valid.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Even if the diversity requirements are met, federal courts will not exercise subject matter jurisdiction over probate proceedings. Markham v. Allen, 326 U.S. 490 (1946). However, federal courts are not prohibited from adjudicating matters simply touching a probate matter. For the probate exception to apply, the claim must specifically involve the probate of a will. Marshall v. Marshall, 547 U.S. 293 (2006). Here, the actress is claiming $5 million in damages for tortious interference, which does not involve probate of the will. Consequently, the federal court has subject matter jurisdiction over the claim since it meets diversity requirements. B is incorrect because the location of the man's property would not give the federal court jurisdiction in a probate matter. C is incorrect because, although this is a correct statement of the law, the actress's claim does not involve the probate of a will or the administration of a decedent's estate. D is incorrect because the validity of the will is irrelevant in this situation.
