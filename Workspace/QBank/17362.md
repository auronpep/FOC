Question: A farmer owned a 200-acre property. The farmhouse sat near the road. About a quarter mile behind the house, across a creek and beyond a locked wooden gate marked "No Trespassing," the farmer grew marijuana plants. State troopers walked around the locked gate, crossed the creek, and discovered the marijuana without a warrant. The farmer moves to suppress the evidence. Was the troopers' observation of the marijuana plants a Fourth Amendment search?
Answer A: Yes, because the farmer posted "No Trespassing" signs, demonstrating a subjective expectation of privacy
Answer B: Yes, because the troopers physically trespassed on posted private property to observe the contraband
Answer C: No, because the field was beyond the curtilage of the farmhouse and open fields receive no Fourth Amendment protection
Answer D: No, because the officers had probable cause to believe illegal activity was occurring on the property
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under Oliver v. United States, the open fields doctrine holds that open lands beyond the curtilage are not "persons, houses, papers, [or] effects" and thus fall outside the Fourth Amendment's text. No amount of signage, fencing, or subjective desire for privacy converts an open field into a constitutionally protected space. The marijuana was found a quarter mile from the house, well beyond any curtilage boundary, so the officers' observation was not a Fourth Amendment search and suppression is denied.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Under Oliver v. United States, the open fields doctrine prevents fields beyond the curtilage from receiving Fourth Amendment protection regardless of how strongly the owner manifests a subjective expectation. The second Katz prong fails: society does not recognize a reasonable expectation of privacy in open fields beyond the curtilage, even if the owner erects fences or posts signs. B: Jones/Jardines trespass analysis applies to the home and its curtilage. Open fields, no matter how clearly marked, are categorically excluded from Fourth Amendment protection. Civil trespass law is separate from constitutional privacy law. D: The correct reason suppression fails here is the open fields doctrine — the observation simply was not a "search" under the Fourth Amendment. Probable cause is not the relevant concept; there was no constitutional event to justify.
lb_rule_reference: The open fields doctrine: land beyond the curtilage of a home receives no Fourth Amendment protection, regardless of fencing or no-trespassing signs (Oliver v. United States)
