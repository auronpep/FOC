internal_id: TR-071
Outline_code: 61020402
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Negligence
Outline: Proximate (legal) cause
Question: A delivery driver negligently parked a locked truck so that it blocked part of a bike lane. The risk created by the parking was that cyclists might swerve into traffic. Before any cyclist passed, a stranger broke into the locked truck, hot-wired it, drove three miles away, and intentionally crashed it through the front window of a closed bakery. The bakery owner sues the delivery company for negligence based on the bike-lane parking. Is the bakery owner likely to prevail in a negligence claim against the delivery company for the crash damage?
Answer A: Yes, because but for the negligent parking, the truck would not have been available to steal
Answer B: Yes, because intentional acts never affect proximate cause
Answer C: No, because the remote intentional crash was outside the foreseeable bike-lane risk created by the parking
Answer D: No, because property damage is never recoverable in negligence
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The negligent parking created a risk of bike-lane traffic harm, not a risk that a stranger would hot-wire the locked truck and intentionally drive it into a bakery miles away. The later act is extraordinary and superseding.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: But-for causation alone is not enough. The intentional remote crash is outside the bike-lane safety risk that made the parking negligent. B: Intentional acts are not always superseding, but they can be when extraordinary and outside the risk created by the defendant's negligence. D: Property damage is a classic negligence loss when caused within the scope of liability. This claim fails on proximate cause, not because property damage is categorically unavailable.
lb_rule_reference: An extraordinary intervening act that produces a different kind of harm from the risk created by the defendant's negligence may supersede the defendant's liability.
