Outline: Mayhem
Question: During a fistfight that Axel started by throwing the first punch, Breck knocked Axel to the ground. When Axel tried to get up, Breck stomped on Axel's right hand with his boot, intending to stop Axel from swinging again. The stomp crushed several bones and severed a tendon; Axel's index finger had to be surgically removed and he permanently lost significant grip strength. Breck later stated he was just trying to keep Axel down, not to cut off fingers. Under common law, which element analysis is most critical to determining whether Breck committed mayhem?
Answer A: Whether Breck acted with reckless disregard for the risk of permanent injury
Answer B: Whether Axel provoked the fight, because provocation is a complete defense to mayhem
Answer C: Whether Breck had the intent to maim or permanently disfigure Axel, as opposed to merely intending to stop the fight
Answer D: Whether the injury was permanent, because any permanent disfigurement satisfies all elements of mayhem without any mental state inquiry
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Common-law mayhem requires that the defendant act with malice — generally understood as specific intent to maim or disfigure, or at minimum, willfulness in committing the act knowing it would cause such a result. If Breck stomped solely to stop Axel from resuming the fight, with no intent to permanently injure, this would fall short of the mental state required. If, on the other hand, Breck intended to permanently injure or disable Axel's hand (a reasonable inference from the severity and deliberateness of the stomp), the mayhem mental state is satisfied. The critical issue is Breck's intent, not merely the result he caused.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Common-law mayhem is not a recklessness crime. It requires unlawful and malicious conduct — either specific intent to maim or disfigure, or (in some formulations) general intent to commit the act with awareness of the likely consequences. Recklessness is too low a mental state for mayhem and would more naturally describe criminal battery or a reckless endangerment offense. B: Provocation is not a complete defense to mayhem. While provocation may be relevant to self-defense (which requires proportional force — stomping a downed attacker's hand likely exceeds what is reasonably necessary to defend oneself), it does not eliminate criminal liability for mayhem if the other elements are met. It is not a categorical defense to this offense. D: Permanence is a required element but not a sufficient one. Mayhem also requires the requisite mental state — malicious or specific intent to maim. A surgeon who permanently removes a cancerous body part, or a person who accidentally causes permanent disfigurement, does not commit mayhem because the mental state is absent. The permanence of the injury does not eliminate the mental state inquiry.
lb_rule_reference: Common-law mayhem requires (1) unlawful and malicious (2) permanent (3) removal or disablement of a body part that renders the victim less able to fight, OR (4) disfigurement of a body part; the defendant must intend the act, not merely foresee the result; many courts require the specific intent to maim or disfigure
