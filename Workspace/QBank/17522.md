Question: Evelyn was shot during a robbery. As paramedics worked to stabilize her, she told a detective who had arrived at the scene that the shooter was "the man who works at the corner store, everyone calls him Nino." Evelyn stated, "I know I'm dying." She died four hours later. At Nino's murder trial, the prosecution sought to admit Evelyn's statement as a dying declaration. Defense counsel argued the statement was testimonial under *Crawford* and must be excluded because Nino had no prior opportunity to cross-examine Evelyn. Is Evelyn's statement admissible?
Answer A: No, because a statement made to a police officer is always testimonial under Crawford, with no exceptions
Answer B: No, because Evelyn's statement is testimonial and Nino lacked any prior opportunity to cross-examine her
Answer C: Yes, because dying declarations are a historical exception that survives the Confrontation Clause even when testimonial
Answer D: Yes, because Evelyn's statement is non-testimonial — the primary purpose was medical emergency, not prosecution
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Crawford* acknowledged that dying declarations may be a pre-constitutional historical exception to the confrontation right. The Framers likely understood the Sixth Amendment against the backdrop of existing common-law exceptions, including dying declarations. Most jurisdictions following *Crawford* hold that dying declarations — even when made to police and therefore testimonial — are admissible as a historical exception. Evelyn's statement identifying Nino, made under belief of imminent death, qualifies as a dying declaration and is admissible at his murder trial.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Crawford itself noted that dying declarations may represent a historical exception. *Crawford* stated: "Although we do not decide the question, we note that *Giles* and *Davis* suggest that dying declarations, if testimonial, might nonetheless be admissible as historically recognized exceptions." The statement is testimonial, but the exception survives for historical reasons. B: The dying declaration exception predates the Constitution and was likely recognized as a historical exception at the time of the Sixth Amendment's ratification. The Supreme Court in *Crawford* expressly declined to eliminate dying declarations, and most courts hold they survive as a historical exception even when testimonial. D: Evelyn's statement identifying the shooter was made to a detective, not a medical provider, and its primary purpose was to identify the perpetrator for prosecution — that is testimonial. The correct basis for admissibility is the dying declaration historical exception, not the Davis emergency exception.
lb_rule_reference: Crawford left open whether dying declarations survive the Confrontation Clause; the majority of courts hold that dying declarations are a historical exception that predates and therefore survives Crawford
