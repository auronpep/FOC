Outline: Common objections to questions
Question: Mary sues Solomon for breach of contract. In her case-in-chief, Mary calls Solomon as an adverse party. Mary's lawyer asks, “You received Mary's final shipment and refused to pay for it, didn't you?” Solomon objects that the question is leading because it is being asked on Mary's direct examination. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection because leading questions are always barred on direct examination.
Answer B: Sustain the objection unless Solomon first testifies inconsistently.
Answer C: Overrule the objection because a party may use leading questions when calling an adverse party.
Answer D: Overrule the objection because all questions to parties are treated as cross-examination.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Although leading questions are generally disfavored on direct, they ordinarily may be used when a party calls an adverse party or a witness identified with an adverse party. Solomon is the adverse party.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because the rule has exceptions. B: B is wrong because an inconsistent answer is not required before leading an adverse party. D: D is wrong because the examination is still Mary's case-in-chief; the correct reason is the adverse-party leading rule.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 611(c) / adverse party leading
