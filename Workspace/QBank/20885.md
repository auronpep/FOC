Subtopic: Negligence
Outline: Negligence per se
Question: A homeowner hired an unlicensed handyman to paint a porch. A licensing ordinance requires painters to obtain permits so the city can collect trade fees and track contractors. While painting, the handyman left a wet drop cloth on the sidewalk, and a delivery driver slipped on it. The delivery driver sues the handyman for negligence and argues negligence per se based only on the missing license. Is the delivery driver likely to establish negligence per se from the licensing violation?
Answer A: Yes, because any statutory violation by a defendant is negligence per se
Answer B: No, because this licensing ordinance does not match the delivery driver's slip-and-fall risk
Answer C: Yes, because unlicensed workers are always held to strict liability for jobsite injuries
Answer D: No, because statutes can never set the standard of care in negligence
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The ordinance is aimed at permits, fees, and contractor tracking, not protecting delivery drivers from wet drop cloths. The violation does not establish negligence per se on these facts.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The plaintiff must show that the statute protects this plaintiff class against this type of harm. A revenue and tracking license does not necessarily target sidewalk slip injuries. C: Licensing status alone does not create strict liability. The plaintiff still needs a matching safety statute or ordinary negligence proof. D: Statutes can set the standard when they fit the plaintiff class and harm type. This ordinance simply does not fit.
lb_rule_reference: A licensing violation does not automatically establish negligence per se unless the statute protects this class of plaintiff against this type of harm.
