


BARMATRIX Q#: 16181
Outline_code: 75080400
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Double Jeopardy
Question: A man kidnapped a victim in State A and transported the victim across the state border into State B. These actions violated the kidnapping laws of both states, which are identical. A jury convicted the man in a State A court, but the trial judge gave what prosecutors in State B deemed an unduly lenient sentence. The state prosecutor in State B then commenced a kidnapping case against the man for violating State B's kidnapping statute. The man's lawyer has filed a motion in State B to dismiss the charge based on the double jeopardy protection against a second prosecution for the man's single act of kidnapping. Should the court grant the motion to dismiss?
Answer A: A: No, because the double jeopardy protection only prohibits a second prosecution following an acquittal for the same offense.
Answer B: B: No, because the man's conduct violated the laws of two different states, and each has the right to enforce its laws.
Answer C: C: Yes, because the double jeopardy protection prohibits a second prosecution following a conviction for the same offense.
Answer D: D: Yes, because the government is collaterally estopped under the double jeopardy protection from prosecuting a defendant a second time for the same conduct that led to an earlier conviction.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: (B)is the best response, because the “dual sovereignty” exception to the Double Jeopardy Clause means that if a single course of conduct by D violates the laws of two states, the second may prosecute D for violating its laws regardless of the outcome in the first state's prosecution of D for that same conduct. In any double jeopardy problem where there are two separate prosecutions, by two separate governments, the “dual sovereignty” doctrine generally prevents the Double Jeopardy Clause from barring the second prosecution. The doctrine is based on the principle that the second-to-prosecute of the two governments has the right to prosecute violations of its own laws, without being hindered by the outcome of the prosecutorial decisions made by the first-to-prosecute government, even if both prosecutions involved the same action(s). Usually, the dual sovereignty doctrine applies to allow both the state where the defendant's actions occurred, and the federal government, to prosecute based on the same actions, whether the first prosecution ended in an acquittal or a conviction. See, e.g., U.S. v. Lanza, 260 U.S. 377 (1922): “[A]n act denounced as a crime by both national and state sovereignties is an offense against the peace and dignity of both and may be punished by each.” But the dual sovereignty doctrine also applies in the (relatively rare) case in which a single action or series of actions happens to violate the laws of two states, which is what we have here. The Supreme Court so held a case whose facts are strikingly like the ones here, Heath v. Alabama, 474 U.S. 82 (1985). In Heath, two men acting at D's direction kidnapped D's wife from Alabama and brought her to Georgia, where she was found dead. D pleaded guilty to murder in Georgia and was sentenced to life there; he was then tried and convicted of kidnapping-and-murder in Alabama, which sentenced him to death. The Supreme Court held that the dual sovereignty doctrine meant that Alabama was not barred from imposing the death sentence on account of double jeopardy: “A State's interest in vindicating its sovereign authority through enforcement of its laws by definition can never be satisfied by another State's enforcement of its own laws.” (A)is not the best response, because it is not true that the double jeopardy protection only prohibits a second prosecution following an acquittal for the same offense. The Double Jeopardy Clause not only may, but in many circumstances does, apply even though the first prosecution ends in something other than an acquittal (either a mistrial under certain circumstances, or a conviction). For instance, if State A, which has the death penalty, tries D for murder and the jury votes for life imprisonment instead of death, the state is not permitted to re-try D for the same murder in the hopes that the next jury will vote for death. This choice therefore states the correct outcome, but does so based on incorrect legal reasoning. As we discuss in Choice (B), what prevents double jeopardy from being a bar here is the fact that two different states are involved, triggering the “dual sovereignty” doctrine. (C)is not the best response, because it ignores the significance of the fact that two different sovereign governments are involved. If both prosecutions here had been brought by the same government (e.g., both by State A), this choice would be correct: once D has been convicted by the State A courts of a crime based on a given act, State A may not retry him for the same offense in the hopes of, say, getting a more stringent penalty. But here, the fact that the two prosecutions are by different governments triggers the “dual sovereignty” principle. As discussed in Choice (B), that principle prevents the Double Jeopardy Clause from barring the second government's prosecution. (D)is not the best response, because (1) the doctrine of “collateral estoppel” does not apply in criminal cases; and (2) the “dual sovereignty” principle prevents double jeopardy from applying to the second (State B) prosecution. The doctrine of “collateral estoppel” is a civil procedure concept; it does not apply to bar the government from proving any fact in a criminal case. Furthermore, even if the doctrine did apply in criminal cases, it would not be relevant to whether the defendant is protected by double jeopardy, which is a constitutional protection. This choice not only makes a mistake of law by asserting the applicability of collateral estoppel, but it also gets the outcome wrong, since as we discuss in Choice (B) the doctrine of “dual sovereignty” prevents the Double Jeopardy Clause from operating here.
