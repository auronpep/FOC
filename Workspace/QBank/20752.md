Subtopic: Presentation of Evidence
Outline: Opinion or reputation
Question: In a federal fraud trial, Andrew testifies that Rebekah signed the disputed invoice. The defendant calls Sarah, who testifies that Andrew has a reputation for untruthfulness. The defendant then asks the judge to instruct the jury that Sarah's testimony proves Rebekah did not sign the invoice. The plaintiff objects. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Give the instruction because untruthfulness reputation proves the opposite of whatever Andrew said.
Answer B: Refuse the instruction and, on request, limit Sarah's testimony to evaluating Andrew's credibility.
Answer C: Give the instruction only if Sarah personally saw Rebekah refuse to sign the invoice.
Answer D: Refuse the instruction because reputation evidence is inadmissible for any purpose.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Opinion or reputation evidence under Rule 608(a) is credibility evidence. Sarah's testimony may help the jury assess whether to believe Andrew, but it is not substantive proof that Rebekah did or did not sign the invoice. A limiting instruction is proper on request when evidence is admissible for one purpose but not another.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because attacking Andrew's credibility does not substantively prove the opposite of his testimony. C: C is wrong because if Sarah personally observed the signing issue, that would be separate fact testimony, not the use of reputation evidence. D: D is wrong because reputation for untruthfulness is admissible to attack credibility.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 105 and FRE 608(a) / credibility-only use of opinion or reputation
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests limited-purpose use of opinion/reputation impeachment evidence and prevents substantive/impeachment collapse.
