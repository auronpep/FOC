Outline: Causation
Question: Two hunters, Archer and Brennan, acting entirely independently and without knowledge of each other, each fired a single shot at a deer at exactly the same moment. Both bullets struck the victim, Victor, a hiker standing behind the deer who was hidden from the hunters' view. Either bullet alone would have been immediately fatal. Both bullets hit simultaneously and each independently caused death. Victor died instantly. The prosecution charges both Archer and Brennan with criminal homicide. With respect to actual causation, which of the following correctly states how the but-for test applies to Archer's liability?
Answer A: Archer is not an actual cause because Victor would have died from Brennan's shot alone
Answer B: Archer is an actual cause because his shot was a substantial factor in Victor's death, even though Brennan's shot was also independently sufficient
Answer C: Archer is not an actual cause because the simultaneous nature of the shots means neither shot individually caused the death
Answer D: Archer is an actual cause only if his bullet struck Victor before Brennan's bullet
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The traditional but-for test encounters difficulty when two independent sufficient causes operate simultaneously because strict application exonerates both defendants. In this scenario, courts substitute the substantial-factor test: a defendant is an actual cause if his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the result. Archer's bullet independently struck and killed Victor; it was unquestionably a substantial factor. Both Archer and Brennan are actual causes under this standard, preserving liability for both despite the logical quirk of simultaneous sufficient causation.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: When two independent sufficient causes simultaneously produce a result, strict but-for analysis leads to an absurd outcome: neither party is a "but-for" cause and neither would be liable. Courts avoid this result by applying the substantial-factor test: each actor whose conduct was a substantial factor in bringing about the result is an actual cause. Archer's shot independently and simultaneously caused Victor's death and was therefore a substantial factor. C: Identifying the logical problem with but-for analysis is the beginning of the analysis, not the end. Courts recognize that strict but-for reasoning produces an unjust outcome in simultaneous-sufficient-cause scenarios and apply the substantial-factor test as the solution. The right conclusion is that both actors are actual causes, not that neither is. D: The facts state both shots were simultaneous. More importantly, the substantial-factor test does not require temporal priority. Either party whose conduct substantially contributed to the result is an actual cause, regardless of which shot landed a millisecond before the other. This choice creates a fictional factual distinction (priority) and applies an incorrect legal standard.
lb_rule_reference: Actual cause is established by the but-for test: but for the defendant's act, the result would not have occurred; where two independently sufficient causes each contribute, both may be actual causes under the substantial-factor test
