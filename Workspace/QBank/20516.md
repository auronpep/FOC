Outline: Duress
Question: Quinn's criminal boss, Dante, calls Quinn on the phone and says: "Steal $50,000 from the bank where you work by Friday or I will have your family hurt sometime next week." Quinn is frightened and believes the threat is serious, but Dante is currently across the country. Quinn steals the money. When arrested, Quinn claims duress. Is Quinn's duress defense likely to succeed?
Answer A: Yes, because Quinn had a genuine and reasonable fear of harm to his family
Answer B: Yes, because the threat was directed at Quinn's family, which is sufficient
Answer C: No, because the threat was not imminent — harm was promised for a future time and the threatener was absent
Answer D: No, because duress is only available as a defense to violent crimes
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Common-law duress requires that the threatened harm be imminent — meaning the defendant faces an immediate choice between compliance and harm, with no time to seek protection. Here, Dante threatened harm "sometime next week" by phone from across the country. Quinn had days before the deadline and the opportunity to contact law enforcement, move his family, or otherwise seek protection. The imminence element is not met. Without imminence, duress fails as a matter of law, regardless of how genuine or credible the underlying fear was. Quinn is guilty of theft.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Duress requires an imminent threat. Dante's threat was conditional ("sometime next week") and he was physically absent and across the country. Imminence means the threatened harm must be immediate — not speculative or scheduled for a future date. A telephone threat from a distant person about harm "next week" does not satisfy imminence. B: Threats to close family members are one element of duress — but not the only one. All elements must be satisfied: the threat must also be imminent, from a human source, and leave no reasonable opportunity to escape. Selecting an answer that states one true element while ignoring the imminence failure is a classic half-right-answer trap. D: Duress is available as a defense to most non-homicidal crimes, including property crimes such as theft. The categorical bar is limited to intentional homicide at common law, not to non-violent crimes generally. Quinn's claim fails because of imminence, not because theft is outside the defense's scope.
lb_rule_reference: Common-law duress requires (1) an imminent threat of death or serious bodily harm (2) to the defendant or a close family member (3) from another person (not natural forces), (4) with no reasonable opportunity to escape or seek help; a non-imminent or conditional future threat does not satisfy the defense
