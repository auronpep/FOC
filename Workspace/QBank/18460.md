Subtopic: Judicial Review
Outline: Constitutional limitations and justiciability
Question: After being impeached by the House, a federal officer is tried in the Senate. The Senate adopts procedures allowing a committee to receive evidence and report findings to the full Senate before the final vote. After conviction and removal, the officer sues in federal court, arguing that the Senate's procedures were unfair and asking the court to reinstate him. What is the strongest justiciability defense?
Answer A: The claim is likely a political question because the Constitution gives the Senate the sole power to try impeachments
Answer B: The claim must be heard because every removed officer has a right to appellate review in Article III courts
Answer C: The claim is justiciable because unfairness allegations always create due process jurisdiction
Answer D: The claim is nonjusticiable only if the President asks the court not to hear it
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The impeachment trial function is textually committed to the Senate, and courts lack manageable standards to supervise the Senate's trial procedures.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Impeachment trials are constitutionally assigned to the Senate, not ordinary Article III appellate review. C: The political-question doctrine can bar review before the court reaches due process merits. D: Political-question analysis comes from constitutional commitment and judicial standards, not presidential permission.
lb_rule_reference: Challenges to how the Senate tries an impeachment can present a political question because the Constitution textually commits impeachment trials to the Senate.
