


BARMATRIX Q#: 16175
Outline_code: 75080100
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: A police officer stopped a driver who had run a red light. Upon approaching the car, the officer noticed a strong odor of alcohol and immediately asked whether the driver had been drinking. The driver admitted having had several alcoholic drinks that evening. The driver, charged with driving while intoxicated, moved to suppress the officer's testimony regarding the driver's statement about his drinking. The driver argued that the officer had elicited the statement without providing the requisite Miranda warnings. The prosecutor has responded that the statement should be allowed in the prosecution's case-in-chief or, at a minimum, should be allowed as impeachment in the event the driver testifies and denies drinking. How should the court rule regarding the driver's statement admitting his drinking?
Answer A: A: The statement should be allowed, because although the driver was in custody, the officer's spontaneous utterance upon smelling alcohol did not rise to the level of interrogation.
Answer B: B: The statement should be allowed, because the driver was not in custody for Miranda purposes when the admission was made.
Answer C: C: The statement should be suppressed both in the prosecution's case-in-chief and as impeachment evidence, even if the driver testifies.
Answer D: D: The statement should be suppressed in the prosecution's case-in-chief, but it may be used as impeachment evidence if the driver testifies.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: (B)is the best response, because (1) Miranda warnings are required only when the suspect is in custody; and (2) a driver who is subjected to a roadside stop is not, viewed from the perspective of a reasonable observer, in custody of the sort associated with a formal arrest. According to Miranda itself, Miranda warnings are only required before “custodial interrogation,” and custodial interrogation means “questioning initiated by law enforcement officers after a person has been taken into custody[.]” In Berkemer v. McCarty, 468 U.S. 420 (1984)—whose facts are quite similar to the ones here — an officer stopped D for a traffic violation, concluded from D's behavior that D was drunk, and asked him whether he had been drinking; D answered affirmatively, and then tried to suppress this admission on the grounds that he had been entitled to Miranda warnings. The Court disagreed, concluding that persons subjected to a roadside stop like the one here are not “in custody,” given the “comparatively nonthreatening” and “presumptively temporary and brief” character of the stop. The stop here would similarly seem to a reasonable person in the driver's situation to be temporary and comparatively nonthreatening, and therefore not equivalent to the “custody” found in a full-scale arrest. Since the absence of custody prevented the officer from having a duty to give the driver the warnings, nothing prevents the driver's admission to having had several drinks from being admitted substantively (as an admission of a party-opponent). That being so, the statement can also come in for impeachment purposes. (A)is not the best response, because (1) the driver was not in custody and (2) the officer's question about drinking probably constituted interrogation. As we discuss further in Choice (B), the comparatively non-threatening and presumably brief nature of this roadside stop prevented the driver from being in custody, a fact that is by itself enough to prevent this choice from being correct. Furthermore, the officer's question about whether the driver had been drinking would likely be found to constitute interrogation (of the “express” rather than “implied” variety). There is no Supreme Court case law supporting the proposition that an officer's question to a suspect will avoid constituting interrogation merely because the question was a “spontaneous utterance.” (If it was the suspect who had made a spontaneous utterance, i.e., a statement that was not in response to a question — like “In case you were wondering, I had a few drinks a while ago”—the spontaneous, “volunteered” nature of the statement would prevent it from being covered by the Miranda rule, even if it occurred in custody.) (C)is not the best response, because the absence of any custodial interrogation made the statement admissible for both substantive and impeachment purposes. As we discuss in Choice (B), the fact that the driver was not in custody was enough to prevent the officer from being required to give the Miranda warnings. Therefore, neither the Miranda doctrine nor any other principle of criminal procedure or evidence prevented the statement from being fully admissible, i.e., for both case-in-chief (substantive) and impeachment purposes. (D)is not the best response, because the absence of any custodial interrogation made the statement admissible as part of the prosecution's case-in-chief. As we discuss further in Choice (B), the fact that the driver was not in custody was enough to prevent the officer from being required give the Miranda warnings. Therefore, neither the Miranda doctrine nor any other principle of criminal procedure or evidence prevented the statement from being admissible for all purposes, including as part of the government's case-in-chief (i.e., for substantive purposes). This choice is, however, correct insofar as it says that the statement may be used to impeach the driver if he testifies.
