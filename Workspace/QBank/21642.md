Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Consent
Question: Dermott, a resident of the State of Elbreck, drove his personal vehicle through the State of Colven on a road trip. Colven has a nonresident motorist statute providing: "Any nonresident who operates a motor vehicle on the public roads of Colven thereby appoints the Colven Secretary of State as his agent for service of process, and consents to the jurisdiction of Colven courts, for any civil claim arising from the use or operation of that vehicle on the Colven public roads." While driving through Colven, Dermott ran a red light and struck a vehicle driven by a Colven resident, who was injured. The resident filed a personal-injury suit against Dermott in Colven state court and served process on the Colven Secretary of State, who mailed the summons and complaint to Dermott's Elbreck address via certified mail (as authorized by the statute). Dermott moves to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction and improper service. Should the court grant Dermott's motion?
Answer A: Yes, because Dermott never expressly agreed to be sued in Colven and does not reside or own property there, so no consent or contact-based jurisdiction exists
Answer B: Yes, because Dermott's brief transit through Colven does not create minimum contacts with Colven, and minimum contacts are required for specific personal jurisdiction
Answer C: No, because Dermott used Colven's public roads with statutory notice that doing so constitutes consent to Colven's jurisdiction for claims arising from that use, and service on the Secretary of State as authorized agent satisfies due process
Answer D: No, because the accident occurred in Colven, giving Colven courts subject-matter jurisdiction over the personal injury claim and therefore personal jurisdiction over Dermott
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Hess v. Pawloski*, a nonresident motorist statute is constitutionally valid. By driving on Colven roads, Dermott impliedly consented to Colven's jurisdiction for claims arising from that road use — exactly the claim here (personal injury from a Colven accident). The statute provides notice of the consent arrangement. Service on the Secretary of State as designated agent, with certified mail forwarding to Dermott's known address, satisfies the due-process notice requirement. The motion to dismiss should be denied.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Implied consent to jurisdiction is a recognized constitutional basis for PJ dating from *Hess v. Pawloski* (1927). The Supreme Court upheld statutes that treat use of a state's roads as implied consent to jurisdiction for claims arising from that road use. The constitutional validity of nonresident motorist statutes rests on the state's legitimate interest in regulating road use within its borders and providing a remedy for its residents injured there. Express agreement is not required — the statute provides notice of the consent rule, and using the roads is the operative act. B: The minimum-contacts framework applies to contact-based jurisdiction. Consent-based jurisdiction is an independent theory; it does not require minimum contacts. When a defendant consents to jurisdiction under a valid implied-consent statute — by using state roads with statutory notice — the minimum-contacts analysis is not the operative test. *Hess* predates *International Shoe* and its consent basis remains valid. D: Subject-matter jurisdiction and personal jurisdiction are wholly distinct. An accident occurring within Colven gives Colven courts subject-matter jurisdiction over the type of claim (personal injury under state law) — not personal jurisdiction over the parties. Personal jurisdiction requires an independent analysis of whether the court can compel the defendant to appear. Here, the basis is implied consent under the motorist statute, not the fact that the accident occurred in Colven.
lb_rule_reference: Implied consent statutes — such as nonresident motorist statutes — provide that a nonresident who uses the state's roads appoints the Secretary of State or other official as agent for service of process and thereby consents to jurisdiction for claims arising from that use. These statutes are constitutional as long as they give the defendant notice of the arrangement and provide a reasonable means of service. *Hess v. Pawloski* (1927).
