Outline: Right to Speedy Trial
Question: Morris was arrested and charged with grand theft. He was held in pretrial detention for twenty-two months before trial. The delay was caused primarily by the prosecution's repeated requests for continuances to locate additional witnesses. Morris's attorney requested a trial date four times but the court always granted the prosecution's continuances. Morris suffered no identifiable prejudice to his defense — his key witnesses were available and his memory was intact — but he spent twenty-two months in jail. Morris moves to dismiss on speedy trial grounds. Which of the following best describes the likely outcome?
Answer A: The motion will be denied because Morris cannot show actual prejudice to the defense, which is the controlling factor in the Barker analysis
Answer B: The motion will be granted and the case dismissed, but Morris may be retried because dismissal for speedy trial violation is without prejudice
Answer C: The motion will likely be granted and the case dismissed with prejudice, because the delay, reason, and repeated assertion of the right weigh heavily in Morris's favor
Answer D: The motion will be denied because the prosecution had legitimate reasons (locating witnesses) for each continuance
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Barker v. Wingo*, courts apply the four-factor test: (1) length of delay — twenty-two months is presumptively prejudicial and triggers the full analysis; (2) reason for delay — prosecution-caused continuances weigh heavily against the government; (3) assertion of the right — Morris's counsel asserted the right four times, a strong factor in his favor; (4) prejudice — pretrial detention for twenty-two months is itself a form of prejudice (the speedy trial right protects against oppressive pretrial incarceration, not only impairment of the defense). On balance, these factors favor Morris, and the court should dismiss with prejudice, barring retrial.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Barker v. Wingo* established a four-factor balancing test, and no single factor is determinative. The court weighs all four factors together. A twenty-two-month delay is presumptively prejudicial (the triggering factor for the full analysis), the prosecution caused the delay, and Morris repeatedly asserted the right. Even if actual prejudice to the defense is minimal, the other factors may outweigh it. B: The Supreme Court has held that the only remedy for a Sixth Amendment speedy trial violation is dismissal with prejudice. Retrial is barred. This is a categorical rule — unlike some constitutional violations that permit other remedies, there is no lesser remedy for a speedy trial violation. D: Under *Barker*, the reason for delay is one of four factors — it does not automatically excuse a lengthy delay when the other factors favor the defendant. Negligent or deliberate prosecution delay weighs more heavily against the government than legitimate reasons, but even legitimate delay does not neutralize the other three factors when they strongly favor the defendant.
lb_rule_reference: The Sixth Amendment speedy trial right is analyzed under the four-factor balancing test from Barker v. Wingo: length of delay, reason for delay, defendant's assertion of the right, and prejudice to the defendant; the only remedy for a violation is dismissal with prejudice
