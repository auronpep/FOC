


BARMATRIX Q#: 16179
Outline_code: 75080102
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Waiver
Question: A defendant was charged with armed robbery. The defendant had only an eighth-grade education and no legal knowledge. At a pretrial hearing, the defendant told the judge that he was unhappy with the public defender who had been appointed to represent him and that he wanted the judge to appoint another lawyer. The judge refused to appoint another lawyer, telling the defendant, with no further explanation, that his only other choice was to represent himself. The defendant said that he would represent himself at trial then, as “anything [was] better than having this lawyer.” The defendant did not raise the subject again, and he represented himself at trial. After hearing overwhelming evidence of the defendant's guilt, the jury convicted him of armed robbery. On appeal, the defendant has requested a new trial on the ground that he was deprived of his right to counsel. Should the appellate court grant the defendant's request?
Answer A: A: No, because the defendant voluntarily waived his right to counsel.
Answer B: B: No, because the trial court's error was harmless in light of the overwhelming evidence.
Answer C: C: Yes, because the defendant was not capable of effectively representing himself.
Answer D: D: Yes, because the record does not establish a valid waiver of the right to counsel.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: (D)is the best response, because the trial judge did not determine whether the defendant's waiver of the right to counsel was “knowing and intelligent.” When a criminal defendant wishes to waive his Sixth Amendment right to assistance of counsel, and to instead represent himself at trial, he has a Sixth Amendment right to elect to do so. However, because the right to assistance of counsel — especially at trial — is so important, the Supreme Court requires that the defendant's waiver of that right be not only (1) voluntary; but also (2) made “knowingly and intelligently.” Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806 (1975). Requirement (2) (“knowingly and intelligently”) means that the trial judge must ensure that the defendant is “made aware of the dangers and disadvantages of self-representation, so that the record will establish that ‘he knows what he is doing and his choice is made with eyes open.' ” The trial judge did not comply with requirement (2) here: the judge rejected the defendant's request for a different appointed counsel, but the judge accepted the defendant's decision to represent himself without explaining “the dangers and disadvantages of self-representation.” Therefore, the defendant did not make an effective waiver of his right to counsel. (A)is not the best response, because the defendant's waiver, although it may have been voluntary, was not made “knowingly and intelligently.” As we discuss in Choice (D), a criminal defendant's waiver of the Sixth Amendment right to effective assistance of counsel must be made not only (1) “voluntarily” but also (2) “knowingly and intelligently.” The latter requirement is satisfied only where the record shows that the trial judge made the defendant “aware of the dangers and disadvantages of self-representation.” Since the trial judge here did not do so, the defendant's apparent waiver of his right to counsel was ineffective even though it may have been voluntary. By the way, the judge's refusal to grant the defendant's request to replace his public defender with a different one was not improper — and therefore did not render the defendant's waiver of the right to counsel involuntary — because a defendant is not entitled to a change of appointed counsel except in special situations like a conflict of interest on the part of the current counsel, which didn't exist here. L,I,K&K Hornbook (6th Ed.), § 11.4(b), p. 733. (B)is not the best response, because harmless-error analysis is not used in cases where the defendant's waiver of the right to counsel was ineffective. As we discuss in Choice (D), the trial judge's failure to ensure that the defendant was aware of the dangers of self-representation meant that the defendant's purported waiver of his right to counsel was ineffective. The Supreme Court has held that certain types of constitutional errors constitute “structural defects,” which “affect the framework within which the trial proceeds.” United States v. Gonzalez-Lopez, 548 U.S. 140 (2006). Unlike non-structural defects (e.g., the erroneous admission or exclusion of evidence in a jury trial), structural defects are not subjected to harmless-error analysis. Id. The Supreme Court has generally held that errors involving the defendant's right to have counsel of his choice fall into this structural-defects area, and thus require automatic reversal rather than harmless-error analysis. For instance, a defendant's right to represent himself falls into this category, so that if the defendant is deprived of the right the appellate court will automatically reverse. McKaskle v. Wiggins, 465 U.S. 168 (1984). While the Supreme Court has never squarely held that a trial judge's erroneous conclusion that the defendant waived his right to appointed counsel falls into this no-harmless-error-analysis category, a lower court would almost certainly hold that that is the case. Therefore, the appellate court will not use harmless-error analysis, and will grant a new trial despite what the question calls “overwhelming evidence of the defendant's guilt.” (C)is not the best response, because the issue is whether the defendant effectively waived his right to appointed counsel, not whether the defendant was capable of effectively representing himself. A criminal defendant has an absolute right to choose to represent himself rather than employing retained counsel, as long as the defendant's decision to do so meets the fairly strict requirements for waiver discussed in Choice (D). Once the defendant makes an effective waiver of the right to assistance of counsel, neither the trial court nor the appellate court will further inquire into whether the defendant was “capable of effectively representing himself,” as this choice suggests the appellate court should do. See Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993): “[A] criminal defendant's ability to represent himself has no bearing upon his competence to choose self-representation.” Therefore, it's not the fact that the defendant may have been incapable of effectively representing himself that will cause the appellate court to grant a new trial. (Rather, the appellate court will grant a new trial because the defendant's waiver was ineffective, as discussed in Choice (D).)
