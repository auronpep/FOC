Question: A cyclist was injured when the front fork on an old custom racing bicycle failed. The frame had been repainted several times, and the maker's label was gone. The cyclist sues five boutique bicycle manufacturers whose frames were sold in the region twenty years earlier. The companies used different metals, fork shapes, testing methods, and warnings. The cyclist cannot show which company made the frame. In the cyclist's negligence claim against the manufacturers, is market-share liability likely to supply causation?
Answer A: Yes, because all defendants sold bicycles in the same region
Answer B: Yes, because plaintiff identification is unnecessary in all product cases
Answer C: No, because the frames were not fungible products with interchangeable risk profiles
Answer D: No, because several manufacturers can never be liable for one product injury
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The products differed in design, material, testing, and warnings. Those differences make market-share liability inappropriate because each defendant's product was not interchangeable for risk and proof purposes.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Market presence is not enough. Market-share liability is narrow and usually requires fungible products, not custom goods with materially different designs and warnings. B: Product cases still generally require proof tying the defendant to the product unless a recognized special doctrine applies. D: Multiple defendants can be liable in proper product or causal-uncertainty settings. This claim fails because the market-share doctrine does not fit nonfungible custom products.
lb_rule_reference: Market-share liability generally does not apply to nonfungible, customized products whose designs, warnings, and risk profiles differ by manufacturer.
