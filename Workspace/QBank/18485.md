Question: In a federal arson prosecution, Nathan denies being near the burned barn. The prosecutor offers testimony that Nathan's coat had fresh scorch marks when officers found him two hours after the fire. Nathan objects that the scorch marks could have come from an unrelated campfire. How should the court rule on relevance?
Answer A: Exclude the testimony because the prosecutor must first prove the scorch marks came from the barn fire.
Answer B: Exclude the testimony because physical trace evidence is relevant only when scientifically matched to the charged event.
Answer C: Admit the testimony because fresh scorch marks shortly after the fire have some tendency to connect Nathan to a fire, even though he may offer another explanation.
Answer D: Admit the testimony only if Nathan admits owning the coat.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Fresh scorch marks on Nathan's coat shortly after the arson tend to make his presence near a fire more probable. The possibility of a campfire is a competing inference for the jury and does not defeat relevance.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A and B demand too much at the relevance stage. D is wrong because ownership can be proved by other evidence; the relevance objection does not require an admission.
