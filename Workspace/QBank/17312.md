Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Officers stopped a car for a traffic violation. While speaking with the driver, an officer noticed a syringe in the driver's shirt pocket. The driver admitted the syringe was for illegal drug use. Officers then searched the entire car for drugs. In the back seat, they found a passenger's purse. They opened the purse and found methamphetamine. The passenger, who had not been arrested or suspected of anything, moves to suppress the methamphetamine found in her purse. Was the search of the passenger's purse constitutional?
Answer A: No, because the passenger had a stronger Fourth Amendment interest in her purse than the driver had in his car, and individual ownership determines searchability
Answer B: No, because the passenger was not arrested and therefore cannot be searched
Answer C: Yes, because once police had probable cause to search the vehicle for drugs, the authority extended to all containers — including the passenger's purse — where drugs might be found
Answer D: Yes, but only because the passenger consented to riding in a car owned by a known drug user, thereby assuming the risk of a search
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Wyoming v. Houghton holds that when police have probable cause to search a lawfully stopped vehicle for contraband, they may inspect all passengers' belongings in the vehicle capable of concealing the contraband. The driver's admission of drug use and the syringe provided probable cause to believe drugs were in the car. The purse was in the car, large enough to hold drugs, and within the scope of the probable cause. The search was constitutional and the methamphetamine is admissible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Wyoming v. Houghton held that ownership of a container by a passenger does not insulate it from a valid automobile search. When police have probable cause to search a vehicle for contraband, the authority extends to all containers in the vehicle where contraband might be found — including passengers' belongings. B: The automobile exception is independent of arrest. It flows from probable cause to believe the vehicle contains contraband. Once that predicate exists, all containers in the vehicle — regardless of who owns them — may be searched if they are places the contraband might be hidden. D: The correct justification is the automobile exception under Houghton — not an assumption-of-risk theory. Houghton's rule is doctrinal, not based on the passenger's decision to ride with a drug user. An assumption-of-risk theory has no Fourth Amendment footing here.
lb_rule_reference: Probable cause to search a vehicle for contraband extends to containers within the vehicle that could hold the contraband, including a passenger's belongings — but only if the probable cause could reasonably extend to that container (Wyoming v. Houghton)
