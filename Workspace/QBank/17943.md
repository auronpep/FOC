Question: A supervisor angrily sent a private text to an employee stating, "You stole tools from the shop." The accusation was false. The supervisor sent the text only to the employee, and no one else saw or heard it. Is the employee likely to prevail in a defamation claim against the supervisor?
Answer A: Yes, because accusing someone of theft is defamatory per se
Answer B: No, because the accusation was not communicated to any third party
Answer C: Yes, because written accusations are libel and libel never requires publication
Answer D: No, because a private workplace accusation is absolutely privileged
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Defamation requires publication, meaning communication to someone other than the plaintiff. The private text reached only the plaintiff.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Even a defamatory per se accusation must be published to a third party; a statement only to the plaintiff is not enough. C: Libel still requires publication to a third party; writing alone does not satisfy that element. D: The better reason is lack of third-party publication; the facts do not establish an absolute privilege category.
lb_rule_reference: A defamatory statement must be communicated to someone other than the plaintiff.
