Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Definition
Question: Moses is charged with embezzling $40,000 from his employer. The prosecutor offers bank records showing that, during the two months after the missing funds disappeared, Moses deposited $31,000 in cash despite earning a regular salary of $3,000 per month. Moses objects that the deposits could have come from legitimate sources. Assuming the bank records are otherwise admissible, how should the court rule?
Answer A: Admit the evidence, because the unusual cash deposits have some tendency to make embezzlement more probable; Moses's alternative-source argument goes to weight.
Answer B: Exclude the evidence, because the prosecutor has not proved that the cash was the exact money taken from the employer.
Answer C: Exclude the evidence, because circumstantial evidence cannot satisfy the relevance requirement.
Answer D: Admit the evidence only if the prosecutor first proves by a preponderance that no legitimate source existed.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The deposits are relevant because they make a consequential fact—whether Moses took the missing funds—more probable than it would be without the evidence. The prosecution need not trace the exact bills or eliminate every innocent explanation at the relevance stage.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because relevance does not require proof that the evidence conclusively establishes the ultimate fact. C: C is wrong because circumstantial evidence can be relevant. D: D is wrong because the court does not require the proponent to eliminate all alternative explanations before admitting logically probative evidence.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401 / FRE 402
lb_student_diagnostic: The trap is treating relevance as sufficiency. Repair by stating the fact of consequence and the inferential chain before asking whether the proof is conclusive.
