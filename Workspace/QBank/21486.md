Question: Esther signed a goods contract requiring a barrel supplier to provide “all barrels Esther requires for the wine season,” with an estimate of 1,000 barrels. In prior seasons, Esther's purchases from the supplier ranged from 900 to 1,100 barrels. After deciding to buy bulk wine from several other vineyards for resale, Esther demanded 5,000 barrels under the contract. The supplier refused. Assume the court has interpreted the writing as a requirements contract. What is the likely legal effect of that interpretation?
Answer A: The supplier must fill any number Esther demands, because a requirements term gives the buyer complete discretion.
Answer B: The contract is unenforceable because it lacks a fixed quantity term.
Answer C: The supplier may resist a demand that is not tied to Esther's good-faith requirements and is unreasonably disproportionate to the stated estimate and prior course.
Answer D: The court must exclude the estimate because the parol evidence rule bars all quantity evidence outside a fixed number.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that a UCC requirements contract is enforceable through the buyer's actual good-faith requirements, but demands may not be unreasonably disproportionate to a stated estimate or comparable prior quantities. The trigger facts are the requirements language, the 1,000-barrel estimate, the prior 900-to-1,100 course, and Esther's 5,000-barrel demand driven by a resale decision. The decisive lock is call type: the court has already interpreted the term as a requirements contract, so the issue is the legal effect of that content. C states the good-faith quantity limit.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because requirements language does not create unlimited buyer discretion. B: B is wrong because UCC requirements contracts can be enforceable without a fixed number. D: D is wrong because the call asks legal effect after interpretation, not admissibility of the estimate under the parol evidence rule.
