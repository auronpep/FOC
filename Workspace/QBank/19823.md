Subtopic: Other Crimes
Outline: Burglary
Question: Tessa is indicted for assault after a bar fight and counsel is appointed. Detectives later suspect Tessa of an unrelated warehouse burglary that has not been charged. At the jail, after Miranda warnings and a waiver, detectives question Tessa only about the burglary. Tessa admits participating and later moves to suppress under the Sixth Amendment. Should the burglary statement be suppressed on Sixth Amendment grounds?
Answer A: No, because the Sixth Amendment right attached only to the charged assault and not to the separate uncharged burglary
Answer B: Yes, because once Tessa had appointed counsel on any charge, police could not question her about any crime
Answer C: Yes, because both assault and burglary are felonies
Answer D: No, because Miranda warnings always waive any Sixth Amendment right on any offense
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The right is offense-specific. Formal proceedings had begun for assault, but not for the separate burglary, so the Sixth Amendment did not bar burglary questioning.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: The Sixth Amendment does not create a blanket shield against all questioning about all offenses. C: Offense specificity does not turn on whether both crimes are felonies. It turns on whether formal proceedings began for the specific offense questioned. D: The better Sixth Amendment analysis is that the right had not attached to burglary. Waiver of an attached right is a separate question.
lb_rule_reference: Attachment on one charged offense does not attach the Sixth Amendment right for a different uncharged offense, though Fifth Amendment Miranda rules may still apply.
