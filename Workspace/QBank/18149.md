Question: Leah signed a written agreement to prepare a grant proposal for Jacob's nonprofit. The writing listed the fee, deadline, and deliverables, and it contained a merger clause. At signing, both parties orally agreed that the agreement would not take effect unless the foundation confirmed by Monday that Jacob's nonprofit was eligible to apply. The foundation confirmed on Monday that the nonprofit was not eligible. In litigation, the court admits the oral eligibility evidence for the limited purpose of determining whether the written agreement became effective. Assuming the court finds the oral condition was agreed to and did not occur, may Leah recover the contract fee for Jacob's refusal to proceed?
Answer A: Yes, because the written agreement states a fee and deadline and the oral eligibility condition contradicts the writing.
Answer B: Yes, because the Statute of Frauds bars all oral conditions relating to nonprofit grant work.
Answer C: No, because the condition was a condition precedent to the writing's effectiveness and the condition failed.
Answer D: No, but only because the oral condition was collateral to the writing rather than part of the parties' agreement.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that an oral condition precedent to effectiveness can prevent contractual duties from arising if the condition fails. The trigger facts are the contemporaneous eligibility condition, the foundation's denial, and the court's limited admissibility ruling. The decisive lock is call type: the question asks legal effect after the evidence has been admitted, not whether the evidence is admissible. Because the condition failed, Jacob's duty to proceed and Leah's right to the contract fee did not mature.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because it reverts to condition-as-contradiction even after the court has admitted the condition evidence. B: B is wrong because no writing-sufficiency issue under the Statute of Frauds is presented. D: D is wrong because the operative point is not a collateral-label doctrine; the point is that the condition gated effectiveness.
