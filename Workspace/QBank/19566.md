Question: Greg shot Mia, inflicting a serious abdominal wound. An ambulance transported Mia to the hospital. On the way, the ambulance driver, while following proper emergency protocols, was forced to swerve to avoid a child who ran into the road. The swerve caused the ambulance to bounce violently; Mia's wound ruptured further and she began hemorrhaging severely. At the hospital, the surgeon performed an emergency operation. Due to the additional blood loss caused by the ambulance swerve, Mia died on the operating table. The coroner determined that Mia would have survived with a 90% probability if the wound had not ruptured during transport. Is Greg the proximate cause of Mia's death?
Answer A: No, because the ambulance swerve was an independent event that breaks the causal chain
Answer B: No, because the surgeon's actions, not Greg's shooting, were the direct cause of death
Answer C: Yes, because emergency medical complications from treating a gunshot wound are foreseeable consequences of the shooting
Answer D: Yes, because Greg is the but-for cause, and but-for causation is sufficient to establish proximate cause
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Proximate causation requires that the defendant's act be a foreseeable cause of the result. The test is whether the result, including any intervening events, fell within the range of foreseeable consequences of the original act. When Greg shot Mia, it was entirely foreseeable that she would be transported by ambulance, that emergency transport carries inherent risks, that those risks could worsen her condition, and that she might die from the cumulative injury. The ambulance swerve and subsequent complications are dependent intervening causes — they were responses to the original harm Greg created. Dependent, foreseeable intervening causes do not break the causal chain.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Not every intervening cause breaks the causal chain. A dependent intervening cause — one that is a foreseeable response to the defendant's initial act — does not relieve the defendant of liability. Calling an ambulance and the ambulance encountering road hazards during an emergency run is a foreseeable consequence of shooting someone. The ambulance swerve was a normal and foreseeable complication of emergency medical transport, not an extraordinary or unforeseeable event that would constitute a superseding cause. B: Medical treatment of gunshot wounds is entirely foreseeable. Medical malpractice can sometimes be a superseding cause, but only if it is grossly negligent or extraordinary. Ordinary medical complications or even ordinary surgical errors flowing from treatment of a wound the defendant inflicted do not break the causal chain. There is no indication of gross negligence here; the surgery was a foreseeable consequence of the shooting. D: But-for causation (actual cause) is necessary but not sufficient for proximate cause. Proximate cause is an independent, additional requirement that limits liability to foreseeable consequences. A defendant can be the but-for cause of a result without being the proximate cause (e.g., if a freakish, unforeseeable event intervenes). The correct answer affirms proximate causation on foreseeability grounds, not merely because but-for causation is present.
lb_rule_reference: A defendant is the proximate cause of a result if it was a foreseeable consequence of his act; a dependent intervening cause that is a foreseeable response to the defendant's act does not break the causal chain
