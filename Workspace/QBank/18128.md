Subtopic: Individual Rights
Outline: Intermediate scrutiny (quasi-suspect) classifications
Question: A state statute provides that a decedent's estate passes by intestate succession to the decedent's "lawful heirs," defined to include children born within marriage and children born outside marriage who were formally legitimated by court order before the decedent's death. A child born outside of marriage to a decedent father who died before seeking a legitimation order challenges the statute, arguing that the legitimation requirement unconstitutionally bars her from inheriting. The state justifies the legitimation requirement on the ground that it prevents fraudulent claims by minimizing the risk that opportunistic third parties will assert false paternity claims after a decedent can no longer testify. Under Equal Protection analysis, which standard governs the state's intestacy classification, and how should the state's fraud-prevention rationale be evaluated?
Answer A: Rational basis, because inheritance is a state law matter and the state has broad power to structure intestate succession rules
Answer B: Strict scrutiny, because the statute discriminates against the child based on circumstances entirely beyond her control, constituting a suspect classification
Answer C: Intermediate scrutiny, and the blanket legitimation requirement may fail because the state's fraud-prevention interest, while important, is not substantially served by a categorical exclusion when less restrictive means — such as DNA paternity evidence — could address fraudulent claims
Answer D: The statute is constitutional under any standard because the legitimation process was available to the decedent during his lifetime, so the bar on inheritance is self-imposed, not a state-imposed penalty on the child
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Legitimacy-based classifications receive intermediate scrutiny. The state's fraud-prevention interest in intestate succession is an important interest, but the categorical legitimation-before-death requirement may not be substantially related to that interest when less restrictive alternatives exist. Courts applying intermediate scrutiny have struck down overbroad legitimacy-based restrictions that use blunt categorical bars rather than actual fraud-protective mechanisms.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Supreme Court has established that classifications based on legitimacy are quasi-suspect, receiving intermediate scrutiny. A child's status as born within or outside of marriage is a classification the Court treats as requiring substantial justification. State authority over property law does not override the Equal Protection Clause. B: The Supreme Court has analyzed legitimacy under intermediate scrutiny, not strict scrutiny. The immutability of the characteristic, while relevant to the historical case for heightened review, does not automatically elevate the standard to strict scrutiny. Legitimacy is a quasi-suspect, not suspect, classification. D: Equal Protection analysis focuses on the impact on the child, not the availability of a workaround for the parent. A classification that penalizes the child for circumstances she did not control and cannot remedy still requires substantial justification. The availability of the legitimation remedy to the decedent does not cure the constitutional problem of penalizing the child for the decedent's failure to use it.
lb_rule_reference: Classifications penalizing non-marital children (children born outside of marriage) receive intermediate scrutiny under the Equal Protection Clause; the government must show the classification is substantially related to an important governmental interest.
