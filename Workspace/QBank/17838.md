Question: In a federal assault trial, Ezra is called to testify that he saw Benjamin strike Paul. Ezra has been treated for a mental illness that sometimes causes delusions. At a brief hearing, Ezra answers questions coherently, states that he saw the event from ten feet away, and gives an affirmation to tell the truth. Benjamin objects that Ezra's mental illness makes him incompetent. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Overrule the objection because Ezra's condition goes to credibility and weight if he can understand the truth duty and testify from perception.
Answer B: Sustain the objection because any diagnosed mental illness makes a witness incompetent under the Federal Rules.
Answer C: Sustain the objection unless Ezra's doctor first testifies that Ezra is always accurate.
Answer D: Overrule the objection only if the jury first finds that Ezra was not delusional at the time of the assault.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The Federal Rules favor competency. A mental condition does not automatically disqualify a witness who can testify coherently, understand the duty to tell the truth, and claim perception of the event. The condition is fertile ground for cross-examination and credibility assessment, but it is not an automatic competency bar.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because mental illness is not a categorical ground of incompetency. C: C is wrong because the proponent need not prove perfect accuracy before testimony. D: D is wrong because the jury may weigh the condition after admission; it does not decide the witness's threshold competency before testimony.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 601 / mental condition affects credibility not automatic competency
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests competence versus credibility and rejects automatic exclusion based on mental-condition labels.
