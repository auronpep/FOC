MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Police legally entered a suspect's apartment while investigating a reported shooting. While there, an officer noticed an expensive stereo system that seemed out of place. The officer did not see any incriminating markings. To read the serial number on the back of the stereo, the officer moved the component slightly, then ran the serial number and confirmed it was stolen. The officer seized the stereo. The suspect moves to suppress. Was the seizure of the stereo lawful under the plain view doctrine?
Answer A: Yes, because officers were lawfully on the premises and any object visible in the apartment was subject to seizure
Answer B: Yes, because the officer's minimal movement of the stereo did not constitute a meaningful intrusion
Answer C: No, because the officer moved the stereo to reveal the serial number — meaning the incriminating character was not immediately apparent and plain view does not apply
Answer D: No, because the stereo was in open view and the officers' entry was justified by exigency, which authorized seizure of anything in the apartment
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Arizona v. Hicks is directly on point. Plain view requires that the incriminating nature of the item be immediately apparent — i.e., the officer must have probable cause before touching or moving the object. Here, the officer had no probable cause to believe the stereo was stolen before moving it; the serial number was not visible without manipulation. Moving the stereo constituted a search, which required a warrant or an exception. No exception applied. The stereo must be suppressed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A lawful vantage point alone does not satisfy plain view. The incriminating character of the item must be immediately apparent — officers must have probable cause to believe the item is evidence or contraband before seizing it. Here, the stereo showed no incriminating markings visible without manipulation. B: Arizona v. Hicks held that even minimal manipulation of an object to reveal its incriminating character is a search under the Fourth Amendment. The magnitude of the intrusion is irrelevant; any manipulation to obtain information not available in plain view requires independent constitutional justification. D: Exigency justifies entry to address the emergency — it does not authorize a general search of everything in the premises. Plain view is not unlimited by the lawfulness of entry; each item seized under plain view still requires independently satisfying the three-part test.
lb_rule_reference: Plain view requires (1) lawful vantage point, (2) immediately apparent incriminating character (probable cause), and (3) lawful right of access; an officer may not manipulate or move an object to confirm incriminating character (Arizona v. Hicks)
