Question: Daniel agreed to restore an old house for Naomi for a single price of $90,000, payable “when the restoration is finished.” The contract listed plumbing, flooring, windows, and painting as tasks but did not allocate a separate price to any task. Daniel completed the plumbing and flooring, then stopped and demanded $45,000 as the contract price for half the tasks. How should the court treat Daniel's contract-price demand?
Answer A: Reject it because the contract was an entire lump-sum contract and Daniel had not substantially completed the restoration.
Answer B: Grant it because completing half the listed tasks always proves a divisible half of the contract.
Answer C: Grant it because each listed task is automatically a separate contract.
Answer D: Reject it only if Naomi proves the unfinished tasks were more expensive than the finished tasks.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A list of tasks does not make a contract divisible when the parties used one lump price payable upon completion and did not allocate prices by task. Daniel had not substantially completed the restoration, so Naomi's contract-price payment duty had not matured.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because completing half the task list does not automatically create divisibility. C: C is wrong because listed tasks are not separate contracts without allocation or separate payment triggers. D: D is wrong because the payment-duty issue turns on the entire contract and lack of substantial completion, not relative cost proof.
lb_rule_reference: Common-law entire contract / substantial performance
