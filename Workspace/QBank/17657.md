Question: Walsh was charged in state court with felony robbery. The state prosecutor filed a criminal information rather than seeking a grand jury indictment. Walsh's attorney moved to dismiss the information, arguing that the Fifth Amendment requires a grand jury indictment for any felony prosecution and that this requirement applies equally to state courts through the Fourteenth Amendment. The state court denied the motion. Walsh was convicted following trial and appealed. Was the state court correct to deny Walsh's motion to dismiss?
Answer A: No, because the Fifth Amendment grand jury requirement applies to states through the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause
Answer B: No, because the Due Process Clause independently requires grand jury review of all serious felony charges
Answer C: Yes, because the Fifth Amendment grand jury indictment requirement applies only to federal prosecutions and has not been incorporated against the states
Answer D: Yes, because Walsh waived the right to indictment by failing to object before the preliminary hearing
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The Fifth Amendment provides that "[n]o person shall be held to answer for a capital, or otherwise infamous crime, unless on a presentment or indictment of a Grand Jury." This clause applies in federal court. Unlike most other Bill of Rights provisions, the Supreme Court has not incorporated the grand jury indictment requirement against the states. States remain free to prosecute felonies by criminal information, and Walsh's prosecution by information in state court was constitutionally proper.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Supreme Court has never incorporated the Fifth Amendment grand jury indictment requirement against the states. *Hurtado v. California* (1884) held that states may initiate felony prosecutions by information rather than by indictment, and this remains good law today. B: Due process does not independently require grand jury indictment for state felonies. *Hurtado* specifically held that a state information procedure satisfies due process. Information-based prosecution is a well-established alternative with its own procedural protections. D: While waiver is a legitimate doctrine, the correct answer here turns on whether the right existed at all in state court. Since states have no constitutional obligation to use grand juries, there was nothing for Walsh to waive. The motion was properly denied on the merits, not as a procedural matter.
