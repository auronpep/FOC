Question: Police officer Delgado, in plain clothes, is lawfully arresting a suspected drug dealer named Porter. Porter struggles. Bystander Hank, who does not know Delgado is a police officer and genuinely believes he is watching a street mugging, strikes Delgado from behind, fracturing his skull. Hank's belief that Porter was being attacked was entirely reasonable given what he could observe. The jurisdiction follows the minority alter-ego rule. Hank is charged with aggravated battery. Under the minority alter-ego rule, does Hank have a valid defense-of-others claim?
Answer A: Yes, because Hank's belief that Porter was being attacked was objectively reasonable
Answer B: Yes, because Hank was acting to protect a third party from what appeared to be serious harm
Answer C: No, because Porter had no right to resist a lawful arrest, and Hank steps into Porter's shoes under the alter-ego rule
Answer D: No, because only deadly force may be used to defend a third party
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The alter-ego (minority) rule provides that an intervener acquires exactly the same defensive rights as the person she is defending — no more, no less. Porter was being lawfully arrested. A person has no right to use or authorize force to resist a lawful arrest. Because Porter's actual right to use defensive force was zero, Hank's derivative right under the alter-ego rule is also zero. Hank's reasonable mistake is legally irrelevant in this jurisdiction. His defense-of-others claim fails, and he is liable for the battery on Officer Delgado. This is the core reason most jurisdictions have abandoned the alter-ego rule in favor of the majority reasonable-belief approach.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The question specifies the alter-ego (minority) rule. Under that rule, the intervener's state of mind — even if entirely reasonable — is irrelevant. The intervener stands in the shoes of the person defended. Porter had no right to resist a lawful arrest. Because Porter could not validly use self-defense against Officer Delgado, Hank (as Porter's alter ego) inherits that limitation. Hank's defense fails regardless of how reasonable his mistake was. B: The alter-ego rule does not reward good intentions or reasonable perceptions — it ties the intervener's rights to the actual rights of the person defended. Porter, resisting a lawful arrest, had no right to use force. Hank inherits that limitation. Legal outcome, not moral sympathy, controls. D: Defense of others does not require deadly force — proportionate non-deadly force may also be used. The level of force permitted mirrors what the person defended could have used. The reason Hank's claim fails has nothing to do with the type of force used; it fails because Porter had no right to defensive force under the alter-ego rule.
lb_rule_reference: Under the minority alter-ego rule, the intervener steps into the shoes of the person defended; if the person defended had no right to use force (e.g., was actually the aggressor), the intervener has no defense even if the intervener's mistake was reasonable
