Question: A beverage company designed its energy drink to contain only filtered liquid and approved ingredients. One bottle from a production batch contained a small shard of glass sealed inside it. Other bottles from the same batch met specifications. A customer swallowed from the contaminated bottle and suffered cuts in his mouth. The customer sues the beverage company under strict products liability for a manufacturing defect. Is the customer likely to prevail on a strict products liability claim for a manufacturing defect?
Answer A: Yes, because this bottle departed from the intended design and was more dangerous than properly made bottles
Answer B: No, because the customer must prove which employee negligently allowed the glass into the bottle
Answer C: No, because other bottles from the same batch were safe
Answer D: Yes, because the drink's entire design was unreasonably dangerous
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The product line was intended to contain only the drink, but this particular bottle contained glass. That rogue-unit departure from specifications is a manufacturing defect that caused injury.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Strict liability for manufacturing defect does not require proof of negligent manufacturing or identification of the careless employee. The departure from intended design is enough if it caused harm. C: Safe sibling units actually support a manufacturing-defect theory by showing this bottle was the outlier. A manufacturing defect is often a one-off departure. D: The facts do not challenge the formula or design of all bottles. The defect is that this bottle contained glass contrary to the intended design.
lb_rule_reference: A manufacturing defect exists when the particular product departs from its intended design and is more dangerous than properly made units.
