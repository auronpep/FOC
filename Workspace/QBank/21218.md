Question: At a private industry lunch, a supplier falsely told three contractors that a licensed elevator inspector "signs off on elevators without checking the cables." The inspector has not yet identified a lost job or specific customer who refused to hire her because of the statement. Can the inspector maintain a defamation claim for slander per se without proving special damages?
Answer A: No, because all spoken defamation requires proof of specific financial loss
Answer B: Yes, because the statement attacks her competence in her profession
Answer C: No, because the statement was made at a private lunch rather than in the press
Answer D: Yes, but only because every false statement about work is libel
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: A false oral statement that directly impugns professional competence falls within slander per se, so special damages are not required under common law.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Statements that injure a person in her business or profession are slander per se and do not require special damages at common law. C: Publication means communication to third parties; the statement reached three contractors. D: The statement was spoken, so it is slander, not libel; it qualifies because it is slander per se.
lb_rule_reference: Spoken words injuring a person in business or profession are slander per se, so common law does not require special damages.
