Question: Officers on patrol in a neighborhood with a history of drug activity saw a man standing on a corner. When the patrol car turned onto the block, the man immediately fled at full speed down an alley. One officer pursued and stopped him. A pat-down revealed a loaded firearm. The suspect argues the stop was unconstitutional because the officer had no individualized suspicion before the chase. Was the Terry stop constitutional?
Answer A: No, because flight from police, standing alone, cannot establish reasonable suspicion for a Terry stop
Answer B: No, because the officer needed probable cause to pursue the suspect, and flight alone does not supply probable cause
Answer C: Yes, because unprovoked flight from police in a high-crime area, considered in totality, gave the officer reasonable articulable suspicion to make a Terry stop
Answer D: Yes, because in a high-crime area, police may stop and frisk any person they encounter for officer safety
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under Terry v. Ohio and Illinois v. Wardlow, a brief investigatory stop is constitutional when based on specific, articulable facts that criminal activity may be afoot. The totality includes (1) a high-crime neighborhood known for drug activity and (2) the suspect's immediate, unprovoked flight upon seeing police. Wardlow held these facts are sufficient to generate reasonable suspicion. The stop was lawful. If the subsequent pat-down was also lawful (officer had reasonable suspicion the suspect was armed and dangerous), the firearm is admissible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Illinois v. Wardlow held that unprovoked flight from police in a high-crime area is a relevant factor that, combined with the location, can constitute reasonable articulable suspicion sufficient for a Terry stop. The officer in Wardlow stopped the fleeing defendant under essentially identical facts, and the Supreme Court found reasonable suspicion. Flight is not conclusive, but it is not irrelevant either. B: A Terry stop requires only reasonable articulable suspicion — a lower standard than probable cause. Officers do not need probable cause to conduct a brief investigatory stop. Wardlow confirms that the combination of high-crime area and unprovoked flight can establish reasonable suspicion for a stop without reaching probable cause. D: This states an overbroad and unconstitutional rule. Being in a high-crime area alone never justifies a stop. Officers must still point to specific, articulable facts about the particular individual suggesting criminal activity. High-crime context is a factor in the totality, not a blanket authorization.
lb_rule_reference: Reasonable articulable suspicion for a Terry stop requires specific, articulable facts that criminal activity is afoot; flight from police in a high-crime area contributes to reasonable suspicion but a hunch alone is insufficient (Terry v. Ohio; Illinois v. Wardlow)
