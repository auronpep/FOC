Outline: Statute of Frauds
Question: A supplier refused to extend more credit to Peter's store unless Peter paid an old debt owed by John, Peter's key distributor. Peter orally promised the supplier, “If John does not pay you, I will pay the debt, but keep supplying my store because I need John's deliveries to stay in business.” The supplier resumed supplying Peter's store. Peter later refused to pay when John defaulted. Does the Statute of Frauds bar enforcement of Peter's promise?
Answer A: Yes, because every promise to answer for another's debt must be in a signed writing.
Answer B: Yes, because the supplier extended credit to John rather than Peter.
Answer C: No, but only because Peter used the word “if,” creating an express condition rather than a suretyship promise.
Answer D: No, because Peter's main purpose was to advance his own economic interest, bringing the promise outside the suretyship writing requirement.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: A promise to answer for another's debt is generally within the suretyship Statute of Frauds. But the main-purpose exception applies when the promisor's leading object is to serve the promisor's own economic interest. Peter's promise was made to keep supplies flowing to his own store, so the writing defense should fail.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because the main-purpose exception can remove a collateral promise from the writing requirement. B: B is wrong because the fact that John owed the debt creates the suretyship issue but does not defeat the exception. C: C is wrong because the condition language does not change the reason the promise is enforceable; Peter's economic main purpose does.
