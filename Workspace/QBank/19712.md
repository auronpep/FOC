Question: Chen was arrested for wire fraud involving an alleged $4 million scheme. At the bail hearing, the magistrate set bail at $500,000, citing Chen's substantial financial resources, his ownership of a home abroad, the large sum of money at issue, and the fact that he had previously failed to appear in an unrelated civil matter. Chen argues that $500,000 bail is constitutionally excessive for a non-violent offense and that the Eighth Amendment entitles him to a lower amount. Is the $500,000 bail constitutionally excessive under the Eighth Amendment?
Answer A: Yes, because the Eighth Amendment guarantees a right to bail in all non-capital cases
Answer B: Yes, because $500,000 is disproportionate to a non-violent white-collar crime
Answer C: Probably not, because the magistrate set bail based on individualized factors reasonably related to ensuring Chen's appearance at trial
Answer D: No, because the Eighth Amendment Excessive Bail Clause does not apply to the states
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The Eighth Amendment's Excessive Bail Clause prohibits bail set at a higher figure than reasonably necessary to ensure the defendant's presence. Courts may consider the severity of the charge, the defendant's financial resources, flight risk indicators (foreign real estate, prior failure to appear), and community ties. Here, each factor the magistrate cited is relevant to flight risk, making the bail amount likely defensible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Eighth Amendment prohibits excessive bail but does not guarantee a right to bail in every case. Congress and state legislatures may permit bail denial in certain circumstances (e.g., dangerousness under *United States v. Salerno*). Additionally, the right is not violated merely because bail is high. B: The Eighth Amendment does not calibrate bail to the nature of violence. The question is whether bail is set higher than necessary to ensure appearance. Given Chen's foreign property, substantial assets, prior failure to appear, and the large fraud amount, a high figure may be reasonably calculated to secure his presence. D: The Excessive Bail Clause has been incorporated against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. Even if a court has not explicitly resolved incorporation of this specific clause, nearly all state constitutions have parallel provisions, and federal analysis governs federal court bail. The argument that the clause is inapplicable is incorrect.
lb_rule_reference: The Eighth Amendment prohibits "excessive bail," meaning bail may not be set at a figure higher than reasonably calculated to ensure the defendant's appearance at trial; courts consider the nature of the offense, criminal history, flight risk, and community ties
