Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: An officer sought a warrant to search Sol's home. The affidavit stated that an informant had bought drugs inside the home that morning. In fact, the officer knew the informant had only walked past the home and guessed drugs were inside. Without the false statement, the affidavit lacked probable cause. Officers found drugs during the search. Does the good-faith exception save the drugs?
Answer A: Yes, because a magistrate issued the warrant
Answer B: Yes, because the officer subjectively wanted to catch criminals
Answer C: No, because the officer knowingly or recklessly included a false statement necessary to probable cause
Answer D: No, because good faith never applies to warrants for homes
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The warrant depended on a false controlled-buy claim the officer knew was false. Reliance on a warrant obtained through material falsehood is not objectively reasonable.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Good faith does not apply when the officer misled the magistrate with a knowingly or recklessly false material statement. B: Good faith is objective and does not excuse misleading the magistrate. D: Good faith can apply to home warrants in proper cases. It fails here because of the officer's material falsehood.
lb_rule_reference: The good-faith exception fails when the affiant knowingly or recklessly misleads the magistrate.
