Question: Joseph hired Miriam to create a payroll-compliance audit for his company. The signed agreement required Miriam to deliver a report “subject to Joseph's approval.” It did not state whether approval was a matter of personal taste or compliance usefulness. When Miriam submitted the report, Joseph rejected it because it lacked the state-law checklist his managers had repeatedly requested during drafting. Miriam sues for the full fee, and Joseph offers evidence of those drafting communications to show what “approval” meant in this commercial audit context. Should the court admit Joseph's evidence for that purpose?
Answer A: Yes, because the evidence may help classify and explain an ambiguous commercial approval term.
Answer B: No, because approval clauses always give the approving party absolute personal discretion.
Answer C: No, because any pre-signing communication is barred once a final services agreement exists.
Answer D: No, because evidence about the checklist goes only to damages, not to contract meaning.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that extrinsic evidence may be used to explain an ambiguous satisfaction or approval clause, including whether the clause concerns personal taste or commercial utility. The trigger facts are the undefined approval phrase and the commercial audit context. The decisive locks are purpose and term status: Joseph offers the communications to explain the approval standard, not to add an unrelated duty. The court should admit the evidence for that interpretive purpose.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because approval clauses are not always absolute; commercial utility commonly calls for objective reasonableness or good-faith limits. C: C is wrong because finality does not bar evidence offered to explain ambiguity. D: D is wrong because the checklist goes to the meaning of the approval term before any damages issue.
