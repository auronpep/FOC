Question: Jalen stabbed Theo in the abdomen during a fight. At the hospital, a doctor negligently delayed ordering surgery for several hours. Theo died from internal bleeding that surgery likely would have stopped if performed promptly. The medical delay was careless but not extraordinary or intentionally harmful. Is Jalen the proximate cause of Theo's death?
Answer A: No, because the doctor's negligence was the immediate cause of death
Answer B: No, because Theo might have survived with better treatment
Answer C: Yes, because the negligent treatment was a foreseeable response to the stabbing and did not supersede Jalen's act
Answer D: Yes, but only because medical malpractice can never affect causation
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Jalen's stabbing was an actual cause of the fatal bleeding. Ordinary negligent medical treatment is foreseeable and dependent on the original injury. It does not replace the risk Jalen created, so proximate cause remains.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Ordinary negligent medical treatment is a foreseeable response to a dangerous wound and generally does not supersede the defendant's causal responsibility. B: The defendant remains a proximate cause when his wound creates the need for treatment and ordinary negligence worsens the result. Possible survival with perfect care does not break causation. D: Extremely abnormal or independent medical misconduct can affect causation. The reason Jalen remains liable is that this negligence was ordinary and foreseeable, not that medical treatment is categorically irrelevant.
lb_rule_reference: Ordinary negligent medical treatment is a foreseeable response to injury and generally does not break causation; only grossly abnormal, independent treatment may supersede the defendant's act.
