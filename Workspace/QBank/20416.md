Question: The defendant, a Black man, is on trial for drug trafficking. During jury selection, the prosecutor uses peremptory strikes to remove three Black jurors—Jurors 4, 9, and 12. Defense counsel raises a Batson objection, noting that all three struck jurors are Black and that the prosecutor passed over similarly situated white jurors who gave comparable answers on voir dire. The prosecutor responds that Juror 4 "seemed nervous," Juror 9 "made eye contact with the defendant," and Juror 12 "had a relative convicted of a drug offense." The trial court overrules the objection without making factual findings. The defendant is convicted and appeals. Which of the following best states the correct analysis of the Batson claim on appeal?
Answer A: The Batson challenge fails because the prosecutor offered a race-neutral explanation for each strike, which automatically satisfies the prosecution's burden at step two
Answer B: The Batson challenge fails because the defendant cannot raise an equal protection claim on behalf of the excluded jurors—only the jurors themselves can assert the violation
Answer C: The Batson challenge has merit because the trial court failed to complete the mandatory step-three analysis, and the record of similarly situated white jurors not being struck supports a finding of pretext
Answer D: The Batson challenge fails because peremptory strikes are presumptively valid and the Equal Protection Clause does not restrict a party's ability to use them
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Batson v. Kentucky requires a three-step inquiry. At step one, the defendant must make a prima facie showing of discrimination—the pattern of striking all Black jurors while retaining comparable white jurors satisfies this. At step two, the prosecution must offer a facially race-neutral explanation—it did. At step three, the trial court must determine whether purposeful discrimination has been proven, weighing the explanations against evidence of pretext such as disparate treatment of similarly situated jurors. Here, the trial court made no factual findings at step three, which is reversible error. The comparator evidence (white jurors with similar characteristics retained) strongly supports pretext. Appellate courts may reverse if the record compels a finding of discrimination or remand for proper step-three findings.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Step two is not the end of the analysis. After the prosecution offers a race-neutral explanation, the court must proceed to step three and determine whether the defendant has carried the ultimate burden of proving purposeful discrimination. If the trial court fails to make that finding—particularly when similarly situated white jurors were not struck—the appellate court will scrutinize whether the explanations are pretextual. The step-two explanation need not be persuasive, but the step-three finding is mandatory. B: The Supreme Court has held that a defendant has standing to raise a Batson claim because the defendant has the right to a jury selection process free from racial discrimination. Powers v. Ohio further held that defendants may challenge discrimination against jurors of a different race. The defendant's standing to raise the challenge is well established. D: Batson v. Kentucky (1986) overruled the prior rule and held that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits racially motivated peremptory strikes. Peremptory strikes are no longer unreviewable when race-based discrimination is alleged.
