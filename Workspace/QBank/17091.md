Subtopic: Presentation of Evidence
Outline: Personal knowledge
Question: Ruth sues David after a collision, claiming that the crash caused continuing shoulder pain. Ruth offers her own testimony that she had never experienced shoulder pain before the collision. David objects that Ruth is not a medical expert and that other events after the crash could have caused the pain. Assuming Ruth has personal knowledge of her own prior condition, how should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the testimony, because only a medical expert may testify about whether an accident caused pain.
Answer B: Exclude the testimony, because later events could have caused the pain.
Answer C: Admit the testimony, because the absence of prior shoulder pain has some tendency to support causation, even if it does not prove medical causation by itself.
Answer D: Admit the testimony only if Ruth first proves by clear and convincing evidence that she had no prior shoulder pain.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Ruth's testimony is relevant because her condition before the collision makes the causation issue more or less probable. It need not independently prove medical causation. Alternate causes and lack of expertise affect weight or may require other proof, but they do not defeat threshold relevance.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because a lay witness may testify from personal knowledge about her own condition; the question is relevance, not expert qualification. B: B is wrong because possible alternative causes go to weight, not threshold relevance. D: D is wrong because relevance does not require clear-and-convincing proof of the offered fact.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401 / FRE 402
lb_student_diagnostic: The trap is importing expert-proof and sufficiency requirements into the relevance threshold.
