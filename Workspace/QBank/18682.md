Question: Officers responding to a reported armed robbery saw the suspect running down the street. They chased him on foot and watched him run into a private residence. Without obtaining a warrant, officers followed him inside and found the suspect, a loaded gun, and stolen cash. The suspect was arrested inside the home. He moves to suppress the gun and cash. Should the court grant the motion to suppress?
Answer A: Yes, because the Fourth Amendment requires a warrant for entry into any private home, with no exceptions
Answer B: Yes, because the suspect had a right to privacy in the home he entered, even if he did not own it
Answer C: No, because officers in hot pursuit of a fleeing armed robbery suspect had an exigent circumstance justifying warrantless entry
Answer D: No, because the homeowner impliedly consented to the officers' entry by allowing the suspect to enter the residence
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Warden v. Hayden established the hot pursuit doctrine: police in continuous pursuit of a fleeing felon may follow the suspect into private premises without a warrant. Armed robbery is a serious felony. Officers were in uninterrupted pursuit when the suspect entered the residence. The exigency — preventing escape, officer safety, and potential destruction of evidence — justified the warrantless entry. Once inside pursuant to a valid exigency, the gun and cash found in plain view or on the suspect's person are admissible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The warrant requirement for home entry has recognized exceptions, including exigent circumstances. Hot pursuit of a fleeing felon is a recognized exigency. Where police are in immediate, continuous pursuit of a felony suspect who enters a private residence, the Fourth Amendment does not require them to abandon the chase and obtain a warrant. B: The exigency exception does not depend on the suspect's REE in the home he fled into — it turns on the government's immediate need to pursue. Moreover, for suppression purposes, the arrested suspect must assert his own Fourth Amendment interest, not the homeowner's. The standing analysis is separate. The hot pursuit exigency permits warrantless entry on these facts. D: Implied consent from allowing a third party in does not extend to law enforcement pursuit. Consent must be voluntary and directed at the government, not inferred from letting a different person enter. The correct justification is hot pursuit exigency, not consent.
lb_rule_reference: Hot pursuit of a fleeing felon creates an exigent circumstance that justifies warrantless entry into a private home to apprehend the suspect and seize evidence (Warden v. Hayden); but Lange v. California limits hot pursuit entry for minor offenses
