Outline: Constitutional limitations and justiciability
Question: A city and a nonprofit jointly file a federal lawsuit asking the court to decide whether a proposed ordinance would be constitutional if enacted next year. No enforcement has occurred, no one is threatened with penalties, and both parties agree on the answer they want. They ask for a declaratory judgment to guide policy planning. What is the strongest Article III objection?
Answer A: The court lacks jurisdiction because the parties seek an advisory opinion without concrete adversity or a live dispute
Answer B: The court has jurisdiction because both parties consent to federal review
Answer C: The court has jurisdiction because declaratory judgments are always available before a law exists
Answer D: The court lacks jurisdiction only if the proposed ordinance concerns foreign affairs
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Federal courts decide actual cases and controversies. Friendly requests for legal advice about hypothetical future laws are advisory opinions.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Parties cannot create Article III jurisdiction by agreement when no case or controversy exists. C: Declaratory judgment still requires a concrete, ripe controversy. D: The advisory-opinion problem exists regardless of subject matter.
lb_rule_reference: Article III forbids advisory opinions; federal courts require concrete adversity and cannot approve abstract legal questions merely because parties want guidance.
