Question: Naomi commissioned Peter to paint her portrait for $12,000, with payment due “only if Naomi is personally satisfied with the portrait.” Peter delivered a technically skillful portrait. Naomi honestly disliked the way it portrayed her expression and refused to approve it. Peter sued for the price, arguing that any reasonable person would like the portrait. What is the best result?
Answer A: Peter wins because any objectively good portrait satisfies a satisfaction condition.
Answer B: Peter wins because personal satisfaction clauses are always illusory and ignored.
Answer C: Naomi's payment duty has not matured if she honestly, in good faith, was not personally satisfied.
Answer D: Naomi loses unless she proves the portrait was commercially worthless.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: For matters of personal taste or aesthetics, a satisfaction condition is usually judged by honest subjective satisfaction. If Naomi honestly and in good faith was not personally satisfied with the portrait, the condition to payment did not occur.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because personal-taste satisfaction is not controlled solely by objective reasonableness. B: B is wrong because a satisfaction condition is not automatically ignored as illusory. D: D is wrong because the portrait's commercial value is not the condition in a personal portrait commission.
lb_rule_reference: Satisfaction condition / honest personal taste
