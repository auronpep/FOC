Subtopic: Homicide
Outline: Murder
Question: During his murder trial, the defendant repeatedly interrupts witnesses, shouts at the judge, and tears up papers at the defense table despite two explicit warnings from the trial judge. After the second warning, the defendant knocks over the water pitcher and screams profanities at the prosecutor. The judge orders the defendant removed from the courtroom. The trial continues in the defendant's absence, and the remaining three prosecution witnesses testify without the defendant present. The defendant later claims the removal violated his Sixth Amendment right to confrontation with the witnesses against him. Which of the following best states the law governing the defendant's confrontation claim?
Answer A: The defendant's claim succeeds because the Sixth Amendment right to confrontation is absolute and cannot be forfeited by courtroom conduct
Answer B: The defendant's claim fails, and the court properly exercised its three options: removal, binding and gagging, or contempt. But the defendant has no right to be returned to the courtroom once removed
Answer C: The defendant's claim fails because, after two warnings, the court was permitted to remove the defendant; however, the defendant retains the right to be returned to the courtroom if he promises to behave
Answer D: The defendant's claim fails because removal for disruptive conduct is constitutional, and the court may permanently exclude the defendant for the remainder of the trial without offering a chance to return
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Illinois v. Allen (1970) held that a defendant who is warned and then persists in disruptive conduct that makes it impossible to conduct the trial forfeits the right to be present, without Sixth Amendment violation. The Court emphasized three prerequisites: (1) the defendant must have been warned, (2) the misconduct must persist despite the warning, and (3) the defendant must be given the opportunity to return upon promising to conduct himself properly. All three elements are satisfied here—two warnings were given, disruption persisted, and the Allen framework permits removal. The remaining question is whether the defendant was offered the chance to return; if not, that portion of the procedure was defective.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Illinois v. Allen (1970) expressly held that the right to confrontation is not absolute. A defendant who engages in disruptive conduct after warnings may forfeit the right to be present. The Court recognized that allowing disruptive defendants to hold a trial hostage would make orderly judicial proceedings impossible. The right survives but is conditioned on the defendant's conduct. B: Illinois v. Allen identified three options—remove, bind and gag, or cite for contempt—but it also held that a removed defendant must be permitted to return to the courtroom upon giving assurances that he will conduct himself properly. Permanent removal for the duration of the trial is not constitutionally permissible under Allen without continuing misbehavior. The defendant's right to return is an essential part of the Allen framework. D: Illinois v. Allen explicitly requires that the removed defendant be told he may return upon giving proper assurances of good behavior. A court cannot simply exile the defendant for the duration. If the defendant continues to be disruptive after being returned, further removal is permissible, but the process is iterative, not a one-time permanent exclusion.
lb_rule_reference: Under Illinois v. Allen, a defendant who is warned and persists in disruptive conduct may be removed from the courtroom without violating the Sixth Amendment right to confrontation; the defendant must be permitted to return upon promising to behave; the court may also bind and gag the defendant as an alternative
