Subtopic: Remedies
Outline: Liquidated damages
Question: Naomi, a food manufacturer, agreed to buy a custom packaging line from Peter, a merchant seller, for $200,000. The contract contained a $30,000 liquidated-damages clause if Naomi repudiated after Peter committed production capacity. At trial after Naomi's repudiation, Peter offered evidence that the amount was reasonable at contracting because production disruption, resale delay, and customer scheduling losses were difficult to estimate. Peter's actual damages after mitigation were $10,000. The court instructed the jury: 'A liquidated-damages clause is unenforceable whenever it exceeds the actual damages suffered.' The jury refused to enforce the clause. On appeal, what is the best disposition?
Answer A: Affirm, because a clause that exceeds actual damages is necessarily a penalty.
Answer B: Reverse, because the instruction used an automatic actual-loss comparison instead of the reasonableness and proof-difficulty standard.
Answer C: Reverse only to enter judgment for $10,000, because later proof of actual damages always replaces liquidated damages.
Answer D: Affirm, because production disruption and customer scheduling losses are consequential damages and cannot be liquidated in a goods contract.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The remedy interest is expectation under UCC goods law, potentially measured by a stipulated amount. Naomi is the buyer, Peter is the merchant seller, and the custom packaging line is goods. The breach posture is buyer repudiation after production capacity was committed. The limiting gate is penalty, but the legal test is reasonableness of the liquidated amount in light of anticipated or actual harm, difficulty of proof, and inconvenience or nonfeasibility of obtaining an adequate remedy. The instruction was legally wrong because it made excess over actual damages automatically fatal. The result scope is appellate reversal for the proper standard; the answer does not itself decide the ultimate dollar recovery.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because it states the actual-damages-only shortcut, triggering WAL-REM-015. C: C is wrong because it also treats later actual damages as automatically replacing a potentially valid stipulated measure. D: D is wrong because Article 2 permits reasonable liquidated damages; the fact that some anticipated losses are consequential does not categorically bar a valid clause, so it uses the wrong limiting gate under WAL-REM-002.
lb_rule_reference: UCC §2-718 liquidated damages — enforceability turns on reasonableness and proof difficulty, not an automatic comparison to actual damages alone
