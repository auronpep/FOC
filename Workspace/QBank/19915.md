Subtopic: General Principles
Outline: Self-defense
Question: Caleb suffers from a documented brain injury that impairs his ability to plan. During a sudden fight, Caleb intentionally stabs Victor once, killing him. The prosecution charges first-degree murder, which requires premeditation and deliberation. Caleb knew he was stabbing a person and knew the act was wrong. The jurisdiction recognizes diminished capacity. What is the most accurate effect of Caleb's brain-injury evidence?
Answer A: It produces an acquittal by reason of insanity because brain injury is a mental defect
Answer B: It may negate premeditation and reduce the offense from first-degree murder
Answer C: It has no possible effect because Caleb knew the stabbing was wrong
Answer D: It proves involuntary intoxication because the brain injury impaired judgment
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Diminished capacity attacks the mental element of a specific-intent or heightened-intent offense. First-degree murder requires premeditation and deliberation. If Caleb's brain injury prevented premeditation, the prosecution may fail to prove first-degree murder, though Caleb can still be guilty of a lesser homicide.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Caleb does not satisfy M'Naghten because he knew the nature of the act and knew it was wrong. Diminished capacity is separate and does not create a complete acquittal. C: Failing insanity does not foreclose all mental-state evidence. Diminished capacity may still negate the specific element of premeditation. D: Involuntary intoxication requires unknowing or coerced ingestion of an intoxicant. A brain injury is analyzed under insanity or diminished capacity, not intoxication.
lb_rule_reference: Diminished capacity, where recognized, is a partial doctrine that may negate specific intent; it is not a complete insanity defense
