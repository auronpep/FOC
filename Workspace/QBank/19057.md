Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: At booking, an officer asked Theo for his name, date of birth, and current address as part of the jail intake form. Theo gave an address that later matched the address printed on forged checks. The officer did not know the address would link Theo to the checks when the question was asked. No Miranda warnings were given. Should Theo's address answer be suppressed under Miranda?
Answer A: Yes, because Theo was in custody and the answer became incriminating
Answer B: Yes, because Miranda bars all questions asked after booking begins
Answer C: No, because the address was requested as routine biographical booking information, not as interrogation
Answer D: No, because a suspect's address is never protected by the Fifth Amendment
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The officer asked for standard intake information and did not design the question to elicit incriminating evidence. Miranda does not require suppression on these facts.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Routine booking questions are not interrogation merely because the answers later become useful evidence. B: Routine administrative questions are treated differently from investigative interrogation. D: A biographical question could become interrogation if designed to elicit incriminating information. The routine purpose is what matters here.
lb_rule_reference: Ordinary biographical questions asked for booking purposes generally fall outside Miranda interrogation unless designed to elicit incriminating information.
