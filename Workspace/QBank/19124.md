Outline: Spousal Privileges
Question: Daniel is prosecuted in federal court for bribery. Esther, Daniel's current wife, is willing to testify. The prosecutor asks Esther to testify to two facts: first, that she saw Daniel put envelopes of cash in a safe; second, that Daniel privately told her during the marriage, “Those envelopes are bribe money.” Daniel objects to both. Which ruling is most proper?
Answer A: Exclude both items, because all adverse spousal evidence is barred once Daniel objects.
Answer B: Admit both items, because Esther is willing to testify against Daniel.
Answer C: Admit the observation but exclude the private statement if Daniel properly asserts the marital communications privilege.
Answer D: Exclude the observation but admit the private statement because words are more reliable than conduct.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Esther's willingness defeats Daniel's adverse-spousal-testimonial objection to her observed facts. A separate marital communications privilege can still protect Daniel's confidential statement to Esther during the marriage. The decisive axis is splitting observation from confidential communication.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because This gives Daniel a veto over willing adverse testimony, contrary to the witness-spouse holder rule. B: B is wrong because This ignores the separate marital communications privilege, which is not defeated merely because the witness-spouse is willing. D: D is wrong because This reverses the doctrine. Observations are not confidential communications; private marital statements may be privileged.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 501 / testimonial privilege plus marital communications split
lb_student_diagnostic: Student is collapsing the two spousal doctrines and failing to route each evidence item separately.
