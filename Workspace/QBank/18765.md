Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: An officer unlawfully stopped Reed without reasonable suspicion and asked for identification. Dispatch reported a preexisting valid arrest warrant for Reed on an unrelated traffic offense. The officer arrested Reed on the warrant and, during a search incident to that lawful arrest, found methamphetamine. Reed moves to suppress the methamphetamine. What is the best argument against suppression?
Answer A: The evidence is admissible because the valid preexisting warrant was an intervening circumstance that attenuated the taint before the search incident to arrest
Answer B: The evidence is admissible because unlawful stops never require suppression of physical evidence
Answer C: The evidence must be suppressed because any evidence found after an unlawful stop is fruit forever
Answer D: The evidence must be suppressed unless Reed confessed before the search
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The stop was unlawful, but the discovery of a valid preexisting warrant can break the causal chain. The later arrest rested on the warrant, and the search was incident to that arrest.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Unlawful stops can lead to suppression. The specific reason admission is possible here is attenuation through the preexisting warrant. C: Fruit analysis asks whether an exception or attenuation breaks the chain. A valid preexisting warrant can do so. D: Attenuation can arise from other intervening circumstances, including a valid warrant.
lb_rule_reference: Attenuation can admit evidence when an intervening circumstance breaks the causal chain between police illegality and the evidence.
