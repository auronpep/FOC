Question: A bank agreed to lend a merchant $10,000 for one year at 8% interest. The loan proceeds were to be disbursed within two weeks. The merchant intended to use the loan proceeds to purchase a specific shipment of carpets for resale at an expected profit of $5,000 but said nothing about these plans to the bank. The bank failed to disburse the proceeds and refused to assure the merchant that it would do so. The merchant was able to secure a loan from another lender at 10% interest for one year. However, by the time the merchant started the application process for a substitute loan, it was too late to pursue the opportunity to buy the shipment of carpets. In an action against the bank for breach of contract, which of the following amounts is the merchant likely to recover?
Answer A: A: Nothing, because damages for lost opportunities are not recoverable.
Answer B: B: Nothing, because the parties failed to tacitly agree that the merchant would be entitled to damages in the event of a breach by the bank.
Answer C: C: The difference in cost over time between a loan at 10% and a loan at 8%.
Answer D: D: $5,000, the merchant's foreseeable loss.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: C is correct. Contract remedies typically strive to put the parties in the same position they would have been in if the contract had been fully performed. Here, other than the loss from paying extra interest, there are no damages to the merchant (other than the higher rate of interest they had to pay to cover for the bank's breach). A
Wrong Answer Explanations: A is incorrect. Under some circumstances, damages for lost opportunities – or consequential damages – that result from a breach are allowable. So, the statement is not just false under the facts, but in fact all-together false. B is incorrect. A tacit agreement about damages is never required for a non-breaching party to a contract to recover damages for breach. D is incorrect. Consequential damages, by definition, have to be foreseeable to both parties at the formation of the contract.
