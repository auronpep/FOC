Question: During a street confrontation, Leo punched Nathan repeatedly, committing felonious assault. Nathan fell, struck his head on a curb, and died. The prosecution charged Leo with felony murder, using the felonious assault as the predicate felony. Leo was not simultaneously engaged in any other crime. Should the court sustain a motion to dismiss the felony murder charge?
Answer A: No, because felonious assault is a felony and death resulted from it, satisfying all elements of felony murder
Answer B: No, because Leo had the intent to commit assault, which is a separate intent from the intent required for murder
Answer C: Yes, because the assault merges into the homicide, leaving no independent predicate felony to support the felony murder charge
Answer D: Yes, because assault is not an inherently dangerous felony and therefore cannot serve as a felony murder predicate
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The merger doctrine holds that when the predicate felony is an integral part of the homicide itself — typically an assaultive offense that is the direct cause of death — the felony merges with the killing and cannot serve as an independent predicate for felony murder. The purpose is to prevent every intentional killing from automatically becoming felony murder simply by labeling the assault a felony. Because Leo's only criminal conduct was the assault that directly caused Nathan's death, no independent predicate exists. The felony murder charge should be dismissed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The merger doctrine prevents exactly this application. If any assaultive felony could serve as a predicate, virtually every intentional killing would be converted to felony murder, eliminating the distinction between murder degrees. The doctrine requires that the predicate felony be independent of — not merge into — the homicide itself. B: The merger doctrine is not satisfied simply by identifying different mental states. It asks whether the predicate felony has an independent criminal purpose beyond the homicide. An assault that directly causes death has the same criminal act as the homicide — the two are not independent. Intent analysis does not resolve the merger question. D: Felonious assault is often considered inherently dangerous to life. The correct basis for dismissal here is the merger doctrine, not the inherently dangerous requirement. D reaches the right result but for a legally incorrect reason; on an exam, choosing the right doctrine matters.
lb_rule_reference: The merger doctrine bars the use of an assaultive felony as the predicate for felony murder when the assault is an integral part of the homicide itself; the underlying felony must have an independent felonious purpose beyond the killing
