Outline: Right to Fair Trial
Question: Ramos was charged with drug trafficking. The government's key witness was Acosta, a co-conspirator who agreed to testify for the government. Before trial, the prosecutor made a written agreement with Acosta promising that Acosta would receive a substantially reduced sentence in exchange for his cooperation. The prosecutor did not disclose this agreement to Ramos's defense attorney before or during trial. After Ramos was convicted, defense counsel discovered the agreement through a FOIA request. Ramos moved for a new trial. Was the prosecution's failure to disclose the agreement with Acosta a constitutional violation?
Answer A: No, because the agreement did not directly prove Ramos's innocence and therefore is not exculpatory under Brady
Answer B: No, because the prosecutor's obligation extends only to documents in the prosecutor's own file, and Acosta's agreement was held by a different division
Answer C: Yes, because a leniency deal with the key government witness is impeachment evidence that must be disclosed under *Giglio v. United States*
Answer D: Yes, but only if Acosta's testimony was the sole evidence of guilt
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Giglio v. United States* (1972) held that a promise of leniency to a key government witness constitutes Brady material because it bears on the witness's credibility. Defense counsel is entitled to know about such deals so they can cross-examine the witness effectively. The prosecution's failure to disclose the agreement violated Giglio. Materiality is assessed by asking whether there is a reasonable probability that disclosure would have produced a different result — with Acosta as the only key witness, there is a strong argument the agreement was material.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Giglio v. United States* (1972) extended Brady's disclosure obligation to impeachment evidence — including deals with witnesses. A leniency agreement with a key witness is powerful impeachment material because it shows the witness had a motive to testify favorably for the government. Failure to disclose it violates Brady/Giglio even if the evidence does not directly prove innocence. B: The Brady/Giglio duty applies to all evidence in the possession of any member of the prosecution team, including law enforcement. Prosecutors have a duty to learn of and disclose exculpatory and impeachment evidence held by agents working with them. The division holding the agreement is irrelevant; the prosecution team is treated as a single unit. D: Giglio's disclosure requirement is not conditioned on the witness being the only evidence. Materiality is evaluated under the reasonable-probability standard. A key witness whose testimony is central to the case will almost always make undisclosed leniency deals material, but the obligation to disclose is not triggered only when the witness is the sole source of evidence.
lb_rule_reference: Under *Giglio v. United States*, the prosecution's Brady duty extends to evidence that could be used to impeach a key government witness, including promises of leniency or deals made with the witness in exchange for testimony
