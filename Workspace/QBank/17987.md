Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Personal Jurisdiction
Question: GlobiTech Sensors GmbH is a German company that manufactures sensor arrays for industrial equipment. GlobiTech has no offices, employees, or agents in the United States. It ships products to U.S. customers through a Rotterdam-based freight forwarder, with no direct U.S. distribution presence. Its total U.S. sales over three years amount to $320,000, spread across 22 states — no single state received more than $30,000 in purchases. A U.S.-based industrial equipment manufacturer asserts a federal antitrust claim against GlobiTech in a Texas federal court, alleging that GlobiTech conspired with a competitor to fix prices in the U.S. market. Texas's long-arm statute reaches defendants who "transact business in Texas." GlobiTech has made two $8,000 sales to Texas customers. The plaintiff argues that Rule 4(k)(2) provides an alternative basis for jurisdiction. Can the Texas federal court exercise personal jurisdiction over GlobiTech based on Rule 4(k)(2)?
Answer A: No, because GlobiTech made sales in Texas and is therefore subject to Texas's long-arm statute, meaning Rule 4(k)(2) is unavailable
Answer B: No, because Rule 4(k)(2) applies only to foreign defendants who have no U.S. contacts at all — GlobiTech's $320,000 in U.S. sales disqualifies it
Answer C: Yes, because the antitrust claim arises under federal law, GlobiTech's $30,000-per-state contacts are likely insufficient for any single state's jurisdiction, and GlobiTech's aggregate $320,000 in U.S. contacts may satisfy due process when measured against the U.S. as the relevant forum
Answer D: Yes, because Rule 4(k)(2) allows any federal court to exercise jurisdiction over foreign defendants whenever the case involves federal law, regardless of contacts
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Rule 4(k)(2) applies when (1) the claim arises under federal law (federal antitrust — yes), (2) the defendant is not subject to jurisdiction in any state's courts (GlobiTech's per-state contacts of $8,000–$30,000, combined with its foreign status and no direct U.S. presence, make single-state jurisdiction unlikely for this claim), and (3) exercising jurisdiction is consistent with the Constitution. Under 4(k)(2), the constitutional analysis uses the United States as the forum — GlobiTech's aggregate $320,000 in U.S. sales and the allegedly U.S.-market-targeting price-fixing conspiracy provide sufficient national contacts. The Texas federal court may exercise jurisdiction under Rule 4(k)(2).
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Rule 4(k)(2) is unavailable only if the defendant is subject to jurisdiction in the courts of some state. Whether GlobiTech's two $8,000 Texas sales satisfy Texas's long-arm and due-process requirements for a federal antitrust claim is a separate question. GlobiTech's minimal Texas sales likely do not satisfy purposeful availment for an antitrust conspiracy claim, so it may not be subject to jurisdiction in Texas state courts. If GlobiTech cannot be reached by any state's courts, Rule 4(k)(2) becomes available. The answer assumes Texas jurisdiction without analyzing it. B: Rule 4(k)(2) does not require zero U.S. contacts. It applies when no single state can exercise jurisdiction — but the defendant may have contacts with the U.S. as a whole that, when aggregated, satisfy the due-process requirement (using the U.S. as the relevant geographic unit). $320,000 in sales spread across 22 states, with no single state having more than $30,000, is precisely the situation the rule addresses: aggregate national contacts that satisfy due process nationally, even if they are too thin for any single state's jurisdiction. D: Rule 4(k)(2) still requires that the exercise of jurisdiction be consistent with the U.S. Constitution. It does not eliminate due-process requirements — it merely shifts the geographic reference point from the forum state to the United States as a whole. A foreign defendant with truly no U.S. contacts could not be reached under 4(k)(2) even in a federal-law case. The rule aggregates contacts; it does not dispense with the minimum-contacts requirement.
lb_rule_reference: FRCP 4(k)(2) authorizes a federal court to exercise personal jurisdiction over a defendant not subject to any state's long-arm statute, for claims arising under federal law, if (1) the defendant is not subject to jurisdiction in any state's courts, and (2) exercising jurisdiction is consistent with the U.S. Constitution and laws (aggregate national contacts). This is the "federal long-arm" provision for foreign defendants.
