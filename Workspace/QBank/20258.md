Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: Detectives gave Ana Miranda warnings, and Ana said she understood. During questioning, detectives falsely told Ana that unless she confessed, they would arrest her elderly mother that night and have child protective services remove Ana's children. After six hours with no food, Ana confessed to arson. Is Ana's confession admissible in the prosecution's case-in-chief?
Answer A: Yes, because Ana received and understood Miranda warnings before confessing
Answer B: Yes, because police may use any deception during interrogation
Answer C: No, because coercive police threats and conditions rendered the confession involuntary
Answer D: No, but only because Ana did not sign a written waiver
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The police threatened severe consequences to Ana's family and combined that pressure with a lengthy interrogation and no food. Under the totality of circumstances, the confession was involuntary and inadmissible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Due process voluntariness is separate from Miranda. Threats against family plus prolonged deprivation can overbear the will despite warnings. B: The facts include coercive threats against family and deprivation, not merely ordinary deception. D: A written waiver is not always required. The defect is due process coercion, not lack of a form.
lb_rule_reference: A confession is involuntary under due process when coercive police conduct overbears the suspect's will under the totality of circumstances.
