Question: A borrower owed a lender $50,000 due on March 1. On January 10, the lender telephoned the borrower and said that he would discharge the debt if the borrower would promise to pay the lender $45,000 by January 15. The borrower responded, “I will attempt to get the money together.” On January 11, the lender again telephoned the borrower and said that he had changed his mind and would expect the borrower to make full payment on March 1. On January 15, the borrower tendered $45,000 as full payment, which the lender refused to accept. On March 1, the borrower refused the lender's demand for $50,000, and the lender sued for that amount. Which of the following statements best supports the lender's position?
Answer A: A: The borrower's January 10 statement was not a return promise, and therefore the lender effectively revoked his offer on January 11.
Answer B: B: The January 10 telephone conversation between the lender and the borrower created an executory accord and therefore did not operate as a discharge of the $50,000 debt.
Answer C: C: The lender's offer to discharge the debt was a gift promise and therefore was not binding on the lender.
Answer D: D: The lender's promise to discharge the $50,000 debt was not enforceable because it was not in writing.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A is correct. By not returning an actual promise to pay the $45,000 by January 15, the borrower's vague response to the offer on January 10, although intentional, was not sufficiently promissory to constitute acceptance of an offer and create a binding contract. B
Wrong Answer Explanations: B is incorrect. The January 10 conversation did not contain sufficient mutual assent to enforce any sort of executory accord, which requires a promise to give substitute performance. As stated above, the borrower did not make a return promise. C is incorrect. The lender's January 10 phone call was not a gift promise, but a valid offer supported by consideration that was subsequently revoked, as stated above. D is incorrect. The Statute of Frauds does not apply because this case involves a promisor, who is also the creditor, offering to discharge the debtor's obligation. The Statute of Frauds would apply to a situation where someone is offering to be responsible for the debt of another.
