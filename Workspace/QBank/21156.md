Outline: Larceny
Question: Marcus works at a used car lot. His employer hands him a $100 bill and instructs him to go to the hardware store to buy a specific can of touch-up paint. At the hardware store, Marcus spends $8 on the paint and pockets the remaining $92. Marcus's employer never authorized him to retain any portion of the change. Is Marcus most likely guilty of larceny or embezzlement as to the $92?
Answer A: Embezzlement, because Marcus's employer entrusted him with the $100 bill
Answer B: Larceny, because the $92 was never within the scope of lawful possession Marcus received from his employer
Answer C: Neither, because Marcus was given the money voluntarily and thus had valid possession
Answer D: Embezzlement, because Marcus was in a fiduciary relationship with his employer
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Embezzlement requires that the specific property converted was lawfully possessed by the defendant at the time of conversion. Marcus was entrusted with $100 only to buy paint and return the change. His lawful possession did not extend to retaining any portion of the change. The $92, once the paint was purchased, was property he was required to return to his employer. He had no lawful authority to possess it even momentarily for his own benefit. Because the $92 was never within the scope of his lawful possession, taking it constitutes larceny (trespassory taking) rather than embezzlement.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The entrustment was limited: Marcus was given $100 for the specific purpose of buying paint and returning with the change. His lawful possession extended only to the $8 cost of the paint and the act of returning the remainder. The $92 change was never lawfully possessed by Marcus — he had no authority to retain it. Taking property never lawfully possessed is larceny, not embezzlement. The scope of the entrustment determines the scope of lawful possession. C: Lawful possession in the embezzlement sense means possession within the scope of authority granted. Even though the $100 bill was physically handed to Marcus, his authority over it was strictly limited. Once the purchase was made, his authority to possess the remainder ceased. There is no "no crime" outcome — the taking of the $92 satisfies the elements of larceny. D: While employment can give rise to the trust relationship needed for embezzlement, that relationship must extend to the specific property misappropriated. Here, Marcus's trust relationship with his employer did not include authority to retain change from a purchase. Embezzlement requires that the property converted was specifically entrusted to the defendant; the $92 was never entrusted to him at all — it was employer property he was obligated to return immediately.
lb_rule_reference: Where an employer entrusts an employee with property for a specific limited purpose, any property beyond the scope of that entrustment is never lawfully possessed; taking it is larceny, not embezzlement.
