Subtopic: Separation of Powers
Outline: Treaties and foreign affairs
Question: Congress enacts a statute requiring specific licensing before American companies may export encrypted equipment to a listed country. The President later announces a diplomatic initiative and orders the Commerce Department to allow exports to that country without the statutory license because the initiative will improve relations. A competitor sues, arguing that the executive order violates the statute. What is the best separation-of-powers answer?
Answer A: The order is valid because foreign affairs always belong exclusively to the President
Answer B: The order is likely invalid because the President is acting contrary to Congress's export-control statute
Answer C: The order is valid because licensing decisions are made by an executive department
Answer D: The order is invalid only if Congress used the word "President" in the statute
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Congress has enacted a rule directly governing the exports. When the President contradicts that statutory rule, executive power is at its lowest ebb unless the President has exclusive constitutional authority that Congress cannot regulate.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Foreign-affairs framing does not let the President disregard a valid statute. Congress has regulated exports, and the President is acting against that command. C: Executive administration of a licensing system does not authorize the President to cancel statutory conditions that Congress imposed. D: A statute can limit executive action by creating binding legal conditions even if it does not name the President in every sentence.
lb_rule_reference: Foreign-affairs context may strengthen presidential authority, but the President's power is weakest when executive action conflicts with a valid congressional statute regulating the same subject.
