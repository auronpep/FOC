Question: A homeowner, John, hired a builder, Peter, to renovate a house. Peter owed Naomi, a mason, $4,000 for stonework already completed. To keep the project moving, John and Peter modified their contract to state, “John will pay Naomi $4,000 directly from the next progress payment.” John later refused to pay Naomi. May Naomi enforce John's promise?
Answer A: No, because Naomi was not a party to the John-Peter renovation contract.
Answer B: Yes, because the modified contract required direct payment to Naomi to satisfy Peter's obligation to her.
Answer C: No, because Peter did not assign Naomi his right to receive progress payments.
Answer D: Yes, but only if Naomi separately promised John to continue working.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Naomi may enforce as an intended creditor beneficiary. John's promise to Peter required direct payment to Naomi and would satisfy Peter's debt to her. The beneficiary route does not require an assignment or separate consideration from Naomi.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because nonparty status does not defeat intended-beneficiary enforcement. C: C is wrong because assignment is not required when the contract itself directs payment to Naomi. D: D is wrong because Naomi's separate promise to John is not necessary to enforce the intended-beneficiary promise.
lb_rule_reference: Common law third-party beneficiary — direct-pay promise
