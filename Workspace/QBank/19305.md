Outline: Lay opinion testimony
Question: In a federal battery trial, Ruth testifies that she spoke with Jonah at a restaurant shortly before the fight. Ruth says Jonah smelled strongly of wine, slurred his words, and stumbled into a chair. Ruth is asked whether Jonah appeared drunk. Jonah objects that the question calls for an opinion. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Overrule the objection because the opinion is based on Ruth's perception and is helpful shorthand for her observations.
Answer B: Sustain the objection because a witness may testify only to raw facts and never to conclusions.
Answer C: Sustain the objection unless Ruth is a medical expert.
Answer D: Overrule the objection only if Ruth first proves Jonah's blood-alcohol level.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A lay witness may describe an observed condition through a helpful opinion. Whether someone appeared drunk is a common shorthand conclusion based on perception—odor, speech, balance, and conduct. Expert testimony or a chemical test is not required for that limited lay observation.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because the Federal Rules do not require witnesses to avoid all conclusions. C: C is wrong because ordinary intoxication appearance does not require medical expertise. D: D is wrong because a blood-alcohol number is not a prerequisite to appearance-based lay testimony.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 701 / lay opinion on intoxication
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests ordinary lay shorthand and rejects expert-only and scientific-proof overdemands.
