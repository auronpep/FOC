Outline: Duty to mitigate
Question: Leah hired John to install a certified fire-suppression system in her warehouse for $40,000. John repudiated before beginning work. One crew offered to install a system for $42,000, but the crew was unlicensed and could not obtain the certificate Leah needed to occupy the warehouse. The only licensed substitute available in time charged $49,000. Leah hired the licensed substitute. What is Leah's best recovery from John?
Answer A: $2,000, because Leah had to choose the cheapest available substitute.
Answer B: Nothing, because Leah did not minimize the replacement cost as much as possible.
Answer C: $40,000, because John repudiated the contract.
Answer D: $9,000, the reasonable substitute-performance premium.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: The remedy interest is expectation under common law. The breach posture is repudiation of a services/construction contract, and the loss category is the direct substitute-performance differential. Mitigation requires reasonable efforts, not acceptance of an inadequate substitute. The unlicensed crew was not an equivalent substitute because it could not provide the needed certificate. The formula is $49,000 reasonable licensed substitute cost − $40,000 contract price = $9,000. The result scope is partial recovery of the reasonable premium only.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because it treats the cheapest inadequate alternative as the mitigation benchmark. B: B is wrong because reasonable mitigation does not require using a defective or legally inadequate substitute, and mitigation failure would not forfeit all recovery anyway. C: C is wrong because the contract price is not the expectation measure when substitute performance supplies the direct differential.
lb_rule_reference: Common law mitigation — injured party need only use reasonable substitute performance, not inadequate or risky alternatives
