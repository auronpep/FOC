MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Other Considerations
Question: A man was charged with first- degree murder. The charge carried a possible sentence of life imprisonment or the death penalty. In exchange for a second- degree murder conviction, the man agreed to plead guilty. When the man took the stand, he testified that he was innocent and was only pleading guilty to avoid the death penalty. The judge questioned the man and made sure that the man made his decision freely and had consulted his attorney. The man's attorney had told the man that there was a lot of evidence against him and the intelligent choice was to plead guilty. Because the man had an extensive criminal history, the judge sentenced him to the maximum 30- year sentence. The man petitioned for a writ of habeas corpus, claiming that his guilty plea was invalid and violated the United States Constitution. Was the man's guilty plea valid?
Answer A: A: No, because the man said that he was innocent.
Answer B: B: No, because the man told the court he only pled guilty to avoid the death penalty.
Answer C: C: Yes, because the man made his plea in open court.
Answer D: D: Yes, because the plea was an intelligent choice by the defendant.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: If a defendant pleads guilty to avoid a harsher sentence, courts view that choice to be an intelligent choice that is valid and constitutional. North Carolina v. Alford, 400 U.S. 25 (1970). Here, the man was facing the death penalty, and his attorney told him that the intelligent choice was to plead guilty because there was a lot of evidence against him. Consequently, even though the man said he was innocent and only pled guilty to avoid the death penalty, the court will still uphold his plea. Consequently, A and B are incorrect. C is incorrect because a guilty plea is always made in open court, and the fact that it is made in open court does not necessarily make it valid.
