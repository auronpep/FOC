Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Underlying Offense
Question: A federal grand jury is investigating a racketeering enterprise. The government grants Rosa use and derivative use immunity pursuant to federal statute and orders her to testify. Rosa refuses, arguing that use immunity is insufficient because she could still be prosecuted for the underlying conduct using evidence gathered independently of her testimony. The government seeks a court order compelling Rosa's testimony. Must Rosa testify?
Answer A: No, because the Fifth Amendment requires transactional immunity — immunity from prosecution for the underlying conduct — before a witness can be compelled to testify
Answer B: No, because the government could use independently gathered evidence to prosecute Rosa for the underlying crime, so her privilege is not fully protected
Answer C: Yes, because use and derivative use immunity is constitutionally coextensive with the Fifth Amendment privilege and is sufficient to compel testimony under *Kastigar v. United States*
Answer D: Yes, because once a court grants immunity and orders testimony, a witness loses all Fifth Amendment protection and can be prosecuted based on her testimony
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: In *Kastigar v. United States* (1972), the Supreme Court held that use and derivative use immunity is coextensive with the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination. Immunity that bars the government from using the witness's compelled testimony — and any evidence derived from it — against her in any criminal case adequately displaces the privilege. Transactional immunity is broader (it bars all prosecution for the underlying conduct), but the Constitution demands only coextensiveness with the privilege, not maximum protection. Rosa must testify. If the government later prosecutes her, it bears the burden of proving all its evidence came from independent sources.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Kastigar v. United States* (1972) expressly held that use and derivative use immunity is coextensive with the Fifth Amendment privilege and is therefore constitutionally sufficient to compel testimony. Transactional immunity is broader but is not constitutionally required. The federal immunity statute provides use immunity, and that suffices. B: This concern is exactly what *Kastigar* addressed. The Court held that use and derivative use immunity — barring use of the testimony and anything derived from it — is coextensive with the constitutional privilege. The government retains the burden at any subsequent prosecution to prove its evidence is derived from sources independent of Rosa's testimony. As long as the government can satisfy that burden, prosecution on independent evidence is permissible. This residual prosecution risk does not make the privilege inadequately protected. D: A grant of use and derivative use immunity does not strip Rosa of all protection — it substitutes immunity for the privilege. The government is absolutely barred from using Rosa's compelled testimony or any evidence derived from it against her. She must testify, but her testimony cannot be used to convict her. The government bears the burden of proving independence of any evidence used at a subsequent prosecution. This answer misstates the nature of use immunity.
lb_rule_reference: Use and derivative use immunity is constitutionally sufficient to compel testimony over a Fifth Amendment objection under *Kastigar v. United States* (1972); transactional immunity is broader (bars all prosecution for the underlying conduct) but is not constitutionally required — use immunity is enough.
