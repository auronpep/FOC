Outline: Lay opinion testimony
Question: In a federal negligence trial, Abigail testifies that she stood on the sidewalk and watched Simon's car pass the crosswalk just before the collision. Abigail is not a police officer or accident-reconstruction expert. Leah asks Abigail, “About how fast was Simon's car going?” Simon objects that Abigail is not qualified as an expert. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection because only an expert may estimate the speed of a moving car.
Answer B: Overrule the objection if Abigail's estimate is rationally based on what she personally observed and would help the jury.
Answer C: Sustain the objection because speed is an ultimate issue for the jury.
Answer D: Overrule the objection only if Abigail first states the exact number of seconds the car was in her view.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: A lay witness may give an opinion if it is rationally based on the witness's perception, helpful to the jury, and not based on specialized knowledge. A rough speed estimate from observing a passing car is ordinary perception testimony. Weakness in the estimate goes to weight, not admissibility.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because speed estimates can be ordinary lay perception. C: C is wrong because an otherwise proper lay estimate is not barred merely because it relates to a fact the jury must decide. D: D is wrong because the witness need not give stopwatch-level precision before offering an estimate.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 701 / lay speed estimate from perception
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests the expert-only trap and the difference between admissibility and weight for ordinary perception.
