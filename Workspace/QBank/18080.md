Question: In a federal vandalism prosecution, Joel denies damaging Amos's market stall. The prosecutor offers evidence that Joel had lost several customers to Amos the week before the vandalism and complained that Amos was “ruining my trade.” Joel objects that the evidence does not show he actually damaged the stall. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the evidence because motive evidence is relevant only if it includes an admission of guilt.
Answer B: Admit the evidence because Joel's rivalry and complaint tend to make his motive to damage Amos's stall more probable.
Answer C: Exclude the evidence because business disputes are civil matters and cannot be relevant in a criminal case.
Answer D: Admit the evidence only if another witness saw Joel at the stall.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Evidence of rivalry and anger toward the victim tends to make motive more probable. Motive is a consequential circumstantial issue even when it does not prove the act by itself.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A and D demand direct proof or an admission. C is wrong because a civil-style business dispute can supply motive in a criminal case.
