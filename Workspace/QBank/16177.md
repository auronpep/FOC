


BARMATRIX Q#: 16177
Outline_code: 75070200
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Searches and Seizures
Question: Police officers had probable cause to believe that a man had committed a series of armed bank robberies with an accomplice. The officers obtained a valid arrest warrant for the man and went to his house to arrest him. The officers found the front door ajar and could hear a radio playing inside. They knocked on the door, identified themselves as police officers, and stated that they had a warrant. Getting no response, the officers entered the house. After the officers called the man's name several times and again identified themselves as police officers, the man emerged from the basement and was arrested. The officers went into the basement to look for the accomplice. They opened a closet door and found cocaine in plain view. They did not find the accomplice. The man was indicted for cocaine possession. He has moved to suppress the cocaine as evidence on the ground that it was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment. Should the court grant the motion to suppress?
Answer A: A: No, because the officers discovered the cocaine during a lawful protective sweep of the house looking for the man's accomplice.
Answer B: B: No, because the search was incident to the man's arrest.
Answer C: C: Yes, because the officers did not have a search warrant.
Answer D: D: Yes, because the officers did not have probable cause to believe that cocaine would be in the closet.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: (A)is the best response, because, when the conditions for a protective search during an arrest are satisfied, the police may seize any contraband they discover in plain sight during that search. To determine whether a search (and seizure) of evidence-of-crime or contraband violated the Fourth Amendment, you typically have to start at the beginning of the police's encounter with the suspect or the site of the search, and move chronologically forward step-by-step to see whether the police did something that was constitutionally improper in a way that ultimately tainted the search. If the police did not commit any constitutionally-significant errors up to the point where they found the evidence or contraband in plain view, we know that their seizure of it was not a violation of the Fourth Amendment. So let's conduct such a chronological analysis here. To begin with, we have to decide whether the police — with their validly-issued warrant in hand — were entitled to enter the house through the open front door. The answer is yes: once police are entitled to execute an arrest warrant at a dwelling where they have reason to believe the arrestee is present, then as long as they first “knock and announce” and receive no response, they are entitled to enter. (The fact that they could hear a radio playing was enough to give them a reasonable suspicion that the man named in the warrant was home.) Then, when the man emerged from the basement, they properly arrested him pursuant to the warrant. The next key issue is whether, following the arrest, the officers (1) properly went into the basement to look for the accomplice; and if so, (2) properly opened the closet door. The answer to these two sub-questions, too, is “yes,” because of the case law governing so-called “protective sweeps.” In the case establishing the police's general right to make such sweeps, Maryland v. Buie, 494 U.S. 325 (1990), the Supreme Court defined a protective sweep as “a quick and limited search of premises, incident to an arrest and conducted to protect the safety of police officers or others.” The Court in Buie also set the limits on such a sweep: “It is narrowly confined to a cursory visual inspection of those places in which a person might be hiding.” And, finally, in Buie the Court articulated what it would take for the police to have a reasonable belief in the need for their protective sweep: the Fourth Amendment permits the sweep “if the searching officer ‘possessed a reasonable belief based on “specific and articulable facts which, taken together with the rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warranted” the officer in believing' . . . that the area swept harbored an individual posing a danger to the officer or others.” Let's see whether the sweep here met these requirements. At the moment the police began to climb down into the basement, they had “specific and articulable facts” (plus “rational inferences from those facts”) that warranted them in believing that: (1) not only the man named in the warrant (let's call him the “arrestee”) but also a second person (the accomplice) had been involved in the crime; (2) the accomplice might be in the basement with the arrestee; (3) the accomplice might have heard the officers' self-identification; and (4) the accomplice might thus be preparing to surprise and/or attack the officers. Therefore, according to Buie, supra, the police were entitled to make a “quick and limited search of [the] premises” to protect themselves and/or others, and to make a “cursory visual inspection of those places in which a person might be hiding.” More precisely, since the person (the possibly-present accomplice) might have been in the basement, the police were entitled to enter the basement. Then, since the possible accomplice might have been in the closet, they were entitled to open the closet to make a “cursory visual inspection” to see whether he (or anyone) was hiding there. Once they had the closet door open, they were then permitted to seize any contraband or evidence of crime that they found in plain view during this “cursory visual inspection. Since Choice (A) articulates why and how the officers' conduct fell within the protective-sweep doctrine — i.e., that they were looking for the accomplice in order to protect themselves — this choice gives a quite accurate and precise explanation of why the court will deny the motion to suppress. But assuming you understand the rule allowing protective searches, you ought to be able to quickly spot this fact pattern as being engineered to test you on the protective sweep doctrine. That would give you a shortcut by which you could quickly infer that the only choice that mentions the doctrine is likely the correct answer. (B)is not the best response, because the search was beyond the area conceivably controlled by the man, and was thus not incident to his arrest. The rationale for allowing searches incident to arrest is to prevent the arrestee from concealing or destroying evidence and to prevent resistance or escape. Chimel v. Calif., 395 U.S. 752 (1969). The rationale allows the police to search not only the arrestee's person, but also “the area into which [the] arrestee might reach in order to grab a weapon or evidentiary items … [such as] a gun on a table or in a drawer in front of [the arrestee].” Id. But the rationale does not extend to searching “any room other than that in which [the] arrest occurs[.]” Id. Since the arrest here took place after the man emerged from the basement, immediately following the arrest the basement was no longer within the arrestee's reach, so the search incident-to-arrest rationale did not extend to that basement. (C)is not the best response, because the absence of a search warrant did not prevent the protective-sweep rationale from applying. Although the default rule is that a search warrant is required before the police may search a place in which the subject of the search has a reasonable expectation of privacy, there are many exceptions to this default rule. One of these exceptions is the one that allows a protective sweep of the premises where an arrest has occurred, if the police have reason to fear that another person may be present who might be dangerous to the police. As we discuss in Choice (A), the exception to the search warrant for protective sweeps applies here, so that no warrant was necessary. (D)is not the best response, because the availability of the protective-sweep exception to the requirement of a search warrant also furnishes an exception to the requirement of probable cause to believe that evidence or contraband will be found in the place being searched. As we discuss in Choice (A), the fact that the police had reasonable grounds for suspecting that the accomplice might be hiding in the basement meant that the police were permitted to perform a protective search of all places in the house in which the accomplice might be found. Not only does the protective-sweep rationale render a search warrant unnecessary, it also eliminates the need for the police to have probable cause to believe that evidence of crime or contraband will be found in the place being searched. (But the police do have to have a reasonable suspicion, based on articulable facts, that the possibly-dangerous person whose presence they are trying to eliminate might be hiding in the place being searched — for instance, the police are not entitled, while doing a protective sweep, to open a compartment or container too small to house a person. However, a “closet” would normally be big enough to hide a human, so the police's mere suspicion that the accomplice might be in the closet — a suspicion that did not rise to the level of probable cause, and that had nothing to do with the possibility of finding cocaine — was enough to allow them to open the closet door to verify that no one was hiding inside it.)
