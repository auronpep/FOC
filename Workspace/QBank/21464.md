Question: Two scenarios involving purse-snatching: **Scenario 1:** Alice is walking down the street with her purse slung over her shoulder. Paul runs up from behind, grabs the purse strap, and yanks hard. Alice instinctively grabs the strap and resists; Paul pulls so forcefully that Alice is spun around and knocked to the ground. Paul runs off with the purse. **Scenario 2:** Beth is walking with her purse loosely in her hand. Derek runs by, grabs the purse cleanly out of Beth's relaxed grip in a single swift motion, and sprints away before Beth even realizes what happened. Beth felt no resistance, suffered no injury, and made no attempt to hold on. Under common law, which statement is most accurate?
Answer A: Both Paul and Derek are guilty of robbery because both forcibly took a purse from another person
Answer B: Paul is guilty of robbery; Derek may be guilty only of larceny from the person if Beth offered no resistance
Answer C: Neither is guilty of robbery because purse-snatching is always classified as larceny from the person, not robbery
Answer D: Derek is guilty of robbery because he intended to permanently deprive Beth of her property and used physical force
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Common-law robbery requires force or intimidation in the taking. For purse-snatching, courts distinguish between (1) force that overcomes a victim's resistance — where the victim holds on or resists and the defendant uses extra force to break free — and (2) a sudden snatch before the victim can react. In Scenario 1, Paul overcame Alice's resistance and caused injury — that is clearly robbery. In Scenario 2, Derek made a clean, unresisted, unexpected grab from a loose grip. The majority of courts following the traditional rule hold that this may be only larceny from the person (or pickpocketing), not robbery, because no resistance was overcome and no force beyond the taking itself was used.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The force element of robbery requires more than the bare physical act of taking. Scenario 1 clearly satisfies the force requirement — Paul overcame Alice's resistance, causing physical harm. Scenario 2 is more contested: Derek took the purse by a swift surprise snatch before Beth could react. Courts applying the traditional rule hold that taking by quick snatch before the victim can offer resistance, without injury or overcominig resistance, may be only larceny from the person, not robbery. C: Purse-snatching can constitute robbery when force is used to overcome the victim's resistance. Paul clearly committed robbery by physically overpowering Alice. The rule that purse-snatching may be only larceny applies only to quick, unresisted snatches — not to forceful overcoming of victim resistance. D: Intent to permanently deprive is an element of the underlying larceny, but it is not what elevates larceny to robbery. The elevated element — the one that makes it robbery — is force or intimidation. Derek's intent to keep the purse does not supply the force element. The force element requires overcoming resistance or creating immediate apprehension of bodily harm, neither of which occurred in Derek's unresisted quick snatch.
lb_rule_reference: Purse-snatching is robbery if the defendant uses force beyond what is minimally required to remove property from the victim's grasp — for example, overcoming the victim's resistance or causing injury. If the property is taken by a quick, unexpected snatch and the victim offers no resistance, some courts hold this is only larceny from the person, not robbery.
