Outline: Spousal Privileges
Question: David is prosecuted in federal court for mail fraud. Rachel, David's former wife, divorced him two years before trial. The prosecution calls Rachel to testify that, while they were married, she saw David keep two sets of invoices. Rachel is willing to testify and is not asked to repeat any private statement by David. How should the court rule on David's adverse-spousal-testimony objection?
Answer A: Sustain it, because Rachel and David were married when she observed the invoices.
Answer B: Overrule it, because the adverse spousal testimonial privilege requires a current marriage at the time of testimony, and the testimony is not a confidential marital communication.
Answer C: Sustain it, because all testimony about events during a marriage remains privileged after divorce.
Answer D: Overrule it only if Rachel received immunity from prosecution.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The adverse spousal testimonial privilege depends on a current marriage at the time of testimony. Rachel is a former spouse, and the proposed testimony concerns observed conduct rather than confidential marital communications. The decisive axis is current marital status plus evidence type.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because This uses the date of the observation instead of the date of testimony for the testimonial privilege. C: C is wrong because This overstates spousal protection. Divorce ends the adverse testimonial privilege, though a separate marital communication may survive. D: D is wrong because Immunity does not supply the status requirement for spousal privilege.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 501 / current-marriage requirement
lb_student_diagnostic: Student is failing to separate the current-marriage gate for testimonial privilege from the survival rule for confidential marital communications.
