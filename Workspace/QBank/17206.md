Question: Federal agents subpoena an accountant, Luke, for preexisting ledgers that Caleb gave Luke months earlier. Caleb objects under the Fifth Amendment, arguing that the ledgers contain incriminating entries. The subpoena does not require Caleb to testify or personally produce anything. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection, because incriminating contents of private papers are always protected by the Fifth Amendment.
Answer B: Overrule the objection, because the subpoena compels Luke's production of preexisting documents rather than Caleb's testimonial communication.
Answer C: Sustain the objection unless Luke is granted immunity.
Answer D: Overrule the objection only if Caleb waives attorney-client privilege.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The Fifth Amendment protects against compelled testimonial self-incrimination by the person asserting it. A subpoena to Luke for preexisting ledgers does not compel Caleb to testify or produce them. Incriminating contents alone are not enough. The decisive axis is personal compulsion.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because This treats the Fifth Amendment as a general shield for incriminating documents. C: C is wrong because Luke's immunity is not needed to resolve Caleb's personal-compulsion objection. D: D is wrong because Attorney-client privilege is not implicated by an accountant subpoena on these facts.
lb_rule_reference: Fifth Amendment / preexisting documents held by third party
lb_student_diagnostic: Student is confusing incriminating evidence with compelled testimonial self-incrimination by the privilege claimant.
