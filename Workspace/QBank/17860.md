Subtopic: Separation of Powers
Outline: Appointment and removal
Question: A federal statute creates a five-year position called "review analyst" inside an agency. The analyst conducts hearings, takes evidence, issues binding compliance orders, and can impose civil penalties subject only to limited review by the agency head. The agency treats analysts as ordinary employees hired by human resources, not appointed by the President, a court, or a department head. A regulated company challenges a penalty issued by one of the analysts. What is the best Appointments Clause argument?
Answer A: The analyst is not an officer because Congress used the title "analyst" rather than "officer."
Answer B: The analyst is likely an officer because the position is continuing and carries significant authority to bind private parties under federal law
Answer C: The analyst is validly hired because agencies may fill all positions through ordinary civil-service hiring
Answer D: The analyst is unconstitutional only if the analyst also has for-cause removal protection
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The analyst holds an ongoing statutory office and exercises significant federal authority by conducting hearings and issuing binding penalty orders. That combination triggers Appointments Clause limits on how the position must be filled.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Constitutional officer status turns on continuing office and significant authority, not on Congress's label. C: Ordinary hiring is not enough for constitutional officers. Officer appointments must follow Article II's appointment routes. D: Appointments Clause status is a separate threshold. An improperly appointed officer can violate Article II even without a removal-protection issue.
lb_rule_reference: A person who holds a continuing federal office and exercises significant authority under federal law is an officer; job title and contractor labels do not control.
