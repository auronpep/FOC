Question: The United States ratifies a treaty promising to create a national permit system for certain wildlife imports. The treaty states that each nation will implement the permit system "through future domestic legislation." Congress does not pass implementing legislation. A federal agency nevertheless denies an importer's shipment solely under the treaty. What is the importer's strongest constitutional argument?
Answer A: The agency cannot rely solely on the treaty because the treaty calls for future implementing legislation before it operates as domestic law
Answer B: Every ratified treaty automatically overrides the need for federal statutes
Answer C: The treaty is invalid because Congress did not approve it by ordinary statute before ratification
Answer D: The agency may enforce the treaty only against foreign importers
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The treaty language makes domestic implementation depend on later legislation. Without implementing legislation, the treaty does not itself supply a domestic rule that the agency can enforce against the importer.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Some treaties are self-executing, but this one expressly depends on future legislation. Ratification alone is not enough. C: Treaty ratification uses the Article II process, not ordinary bicameralism and presentment. The problem is domestic enforceability, not treaty validity. D: The self-execution question does not turn on the importer's nationality. It turns on whether the treaty itself supplies enforceable domestic law.
lb_rule_reference: A non-self-executing treaty may bind the United States internationally but does not itself create enforceable domestic law unless Congress enacts implementing legislation.
