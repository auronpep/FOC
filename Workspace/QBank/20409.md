Question: A limited partnership ("LP") sues a corporation in federal court asserting a diversity claim for $200,000. The LP has two limited partners: Partner A, a citizen of Colorado, and Partner B, an LLC whose sole member is a citizen of Texas. The LP's sole general partner is a corporation incorporated in Delaware with its nerve center (principal place of business) in Nevada. The defendant corporation is incorporated in Texas and has its nerve center in Texas. The governing forum is federal court under the MBE baseline. Do the parties satisfy the complete diversity requirement?
Answer A: Yes, because the LP is a citizen of Delaware and Nevada (its general partner corporation's citizenships), and those states are diverse from the Texas defendant
Answer B: No, because the LP is a citizen of every state in which any of its partners is a citizen, and tracing through Partner B's LLC membership reveals a Texas citizen who shares citizenship with the Texas defendant
Answer C: Yes, because the LP's general partner is a corporation, and corporations are the correct unit of analysis for determining an LP's citizenship — the limited partners' citizenships are not counted
Answer D: No, but only because Partner A is a Colorado citizen and the defendant is a Texas citizen — the limited partners' citizenships control and the general partner's citizenship is disregarded
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: A limited partnership takes the citizenship of all its partners. Here the LP has three partners: Partner A (Colorado citizen), Partner B (an LLC whose sole member is a Texas citizen, making the LLC — and thus the LP through it — a citizen of Texas), and the general partner corporation (Delaware and Nevada, applying corporate dual-citizenship). The LP is a citizen of Colorado, Texas, Delaware, and Nevada. The defendant is a citizen of Texas. The LP and defendant share Texas citizenship; complete diversity is absent, and the court lacks § 1332 jurisdiction.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A limited partnership takes the citizenship of all its partners — both general and limited — not just the general partner. The LP must also include the citizenship of its limited partners: Partner A (Colorado) and Partner B (an LLC). Partner B's citizenship is that of its sole member — a Texas citizen. The LP is therefore a citizen of Colorado, Delaware, Nevada, and Texas (through Partner B's member). The defendant is a Texas citizen. The LP and the defendant share Texas citizenship, destroying complete diversity. C: Limited partnerships, unlike corporations, must include the citizenship of both general and limited partners. There is no rule that limits the analysis to general partners. The Supreme Court's decision in *Carden v. Arkoma Associates* confirms that unincorporated entities take the citizenship of all members, not only the managing or general partners. D: Partner A (Colorado) is actually diverse from the Texas defendant. The reason complete diversity fails is the Texas citizenship embedded in Partner B's LLC membership, traced through to the LLC's sole member. This answer mistakes the source of non-diversity and misapplies the rule by disregarding the general partner corporation entirely — which is also wrong.
lb_rule_reference: A general or limited partnership is not a citizen of its state of formation but takes the citizenship of each partner; where a partner is itself an entity, courts trace through that entity to its ultimate individual members or owners.
