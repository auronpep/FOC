Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: Police arrested Eli, who has a documented history of severe paranoid schizophrenia. Officers gave Eli Miranda warnings and he signed a waiver form. Officers questioned Eli in a standard interview room for two hours using ordinary, non-coercive questioning techniques. Eli, experiencing delusions, believed that confessing was the only way to escape what he perceived as invisible persecutors. He confessed in detail to the crime. A psychiatrist testified that Eli lacked the rational capacity to understand the consequences of confessing. Eli moves to suppress the confession as involuntary. Under federal constitutional standards, is Eli's confession involuntary?
Answer A: Yes, because Eli's mental illness prevented him from making a knowing and voluntary waiver of his Miranda rights
Answer B: Yes, because a confession produced by delusional thinking cannot be considered the product of a rational, free choice
Answer C: No, because under *Colorado v. Connelly*, a confession is not constitutionally involuntary unless it is the product of coercive police conduct, regardless of the suspect's mental state
Answer D: No, because Eli signed a Miranda waiver form, conclusively establishing that the confession was voluntary
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: In *Colorado v. Connelly* (1986), the Supreme Court held that coercive police conduct is a necessary condition for a finding of constitutional involuntariness. A suspect's mental illness, standing alone, does not make a confession involuntary for federal constitutional purposes. The voluntariness doctrine is designed to deter police misconduct, not to protect against all confessions by those with impaired judgment. Officers used standard, non-coercive techniques. The confession survives federal constitutional challenge. (Note: state law may provide broader protection.)
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Mental illness bears on the Miranda waiver analysis and the general voluntariness analysis, but under *Colorado v. Connelly* (1986), the Supreme Court held that coercive police conduct is a necessary predicate to finding a confession involuntary under the Due Process Clause. The knowing-and-voluntary Miranda waiver standard likewise requires police overreaching. Eli's internal delusions, without any police misconduct, do not by themselves satisfy the constitutional test. B: This argument was squarely rejected in *Colorado v. Connelly* (1986). The Supreme Court held that the Due Process voluntariness inquiry is anchored in police overreaching, not in the suspect's rational capacity. Without some coercive government conduct, the confession does not violate the Constitution, regardless of the suspect's internal mental state. State courts may impose higher standards, but the federal constitutional floor requires police misconduct. D: A signed waiver form is evidence of voluntariness but not conclusive. Courts evaluate whether the waiver was knowing, voluntary, and intelligent under the totality of circumstances. A signed form by someone in the grip of a severe psychotic delusion may not be a knowing and intelligent waiver, and courts would consider the circumstances. The correct reason for the confession's admissibility is *Connelly*'s coercion requirement, not the mechanical effect of a signed form.
lb_rule_reference: A suspect's mental illness or diminished capacity does not by itself render a confession involuntary; the Due Process voluntariness test requires coercive police conduct — the state of mind must be combined with police overreaching to establish involuntariness.
