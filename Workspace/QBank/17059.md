Question: Congress enacts two provisions of a campaign finance reform bill. Provision A limits the total amount any political action committee may spend on independent campaign advertising supporting or opposing a federal candidate. Provision B limits the total dollar amount any individual may contribute directly to a federal candidate's campaign committee. A civil liberties organization challenges both provisions as unconstitutional restrictions on political speech. Under Buckley v. Valeo, how should a court analyze the two provisions?
Answer A: Both provisions are subject to strict scrutiny because both involve restrictions on money spent in connection with political speech, and money equals speech under Buckley
Answer B: Provision A (independent expenditure limit) fails strict scrutiny; Provision B (direct contribution limit) may be constitutional under a more deferential standard if it serves the government's interest in preventing corruption or its appearance
Answer C: Both provisions are unconstitutional under Citizens United because any limit on money in politics is an unconstitutional restriction on political speech
Answer D: Provision B (contribution limit) fails because Buckley held that any limit on money supporting political campaigns violates the First Amendment if the limit is too low to allow effective participation
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Under Buckley v. Valeo (and later Citizens United as to independent expenditures), a limit on independent expenditures is a direct restriction on the quantity of political speech and must survive strict scrutiny, which it cannot — preventing corruption is not a sufficient interest to override independent speech expenditures because there is no quid pro quo corruption risk in independent spending. Contribution limits, by contrast, receive a more deferential review because they impose only a marginal burden on expression while serving the government's anti-corruption and anti-appearance-of-corruption interest.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Buckley explicitly distinguished contributions from expenditures. Expenditure limits directly restrict the quantity of political expression — how much speech a speaker can buy. Contribution limits impose a less direct burden because the contributor is not directly speaking; the campaign uses the money to speak. Strict scrutiny applies to expenditure limits; contribution limits receive lower scrutiny weighted toward the government's anti-corruption and appearance-of-corruption interest. C: Citizens United struck down limits on independent expenditures by corporations and unions but did not invalidate contribution limits. Direct contribution limits to candidate campaigns remain permissible under Buckley's contribution-expenditure distinction and were not overruled by Citizens United. The Court has consistently upheld contribution limits to candidate committees as an acceptable means of preventing quid pro quo corruption. D: Buckley upheld contribution limits in principle and did not hold that any contribution limit that restricts participation is unconstitutional. The Court applies a form of intermediate scrutiny to contribution limits — looking at whether they are sufficiently closely drawn to the anti-corruption interest. Only contribution limits that are set so low as to be effectively confiscatory or that impose "markedly disproportionate" restrictions would be struck down. The general principle from Buckley is that contribution limits are permissible.
lb_rule_reference: Under Buckley v. Valeo, expenditure limits on political speech are subject to strict scrutiny because they directly restrict the amount of political expression. Contribution limits receive lower scrutiny because they impose only a marginal restriction on expression while serving the government's anti-corruption interest.
