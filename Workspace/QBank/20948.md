Outline: Double Jeopardy
Question: Sandra was tried for tax fraud and convicted by a jury. She appealed, arguing that the trial court incorrectly instructed the jury on the intent element. The appellate court agreed and reversed the conviction. The state seeks to retry Sandra on the same tax fraud charges. Sandra argues that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial. Does the Double Jeopardy Clause bar retrial?
Answer A: Yes, because jeopardy had attached at Sandra's first trial and the Double Jeopardy Clause bars a second trial for the same offense regardless of how the first trial ended
Answer B: Yes, because the state cannot relitigate facts the jury necessarily resolved in favor of the defendant during the first trial
Answer C: No, because when a defendant successfully appeals a conviction, the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial; the defendant's appeal is treated as consenting to a new proceeding
Answer D: No, because the error was in the jury instructions, not in the sufficiency of the evidence, and therefore the court of appeals did not make a finding equivalent to an acquittal
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *United States v. Ball* (1896) and *United States v. Scott* (1978), a defendant who successfully challenges a conviction on appeal may be retried. The rationale is that by appealing, the defendant seeks to overturn the conviction and implicitly consents to a fresh proceeding. The reversal nullifies the prior conviction; the defendant cannot use their own successful appeal both to escape the conviction and to claim permanent immunity from trial. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrials after acquittals but not after defendant-initiated successful appeals of convictions.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar all retrials after the first jeopardy attaches. It bars retrial after acquittal (absolute bar) and retrial after certain mistrials (manifest necessity analysis). When a defendant successfully appeals a conviction, the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial because the defendant's successful appeal is treated as a consent to a new proceeding. *United States v. Ball* (1896) established this principle. B: Collateral estoppel precludes relitigation of issues the jury necessarily resolved in the defendant's favor. But here the jury convicted — it did not resolve any factual issue in Sandra's favor. The reversal was based on a legal error in jury instructions, not on a factual finding favoring Sandra. Collateral estoppel does not apply, and the retrial may proceed. D: This answer reaches the correct result and raises a correct legal distinction, but it is stated as a narrow technical rule rather than the governing principle. The full rule is that any successful defense appeal of a conviction — regardless of the basis (instruction error, insufficient evidence excluded, procedural error) — generally permits retrial. The exception is when the reversal is based on insufficiency of the evidence, which is treated as an acquittal. The answer here is incomplete and potentially misleading as a general statement of the law.
lb_rule_reference: An acquittal is an absolute, unreviewable bar to retrial; a conviction reversed on appeal at the defendant's request does not bar retrial because the defendant consented to the nullification of the first verdict.
