Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Officers obtain a search warrant from a neutral magistrate based on an affidavit later found to be insufficient for probable cause. The warrant appears facially valid, describes the place and items with particularity, and there is no evidence that officers lied, recklessly omitted facts, or relied on a plainly deficient warrant. They execute the warrant and find stolen tools. Should the evidence automatically be suppressed?
Answer A: Yes, because any warrant defect requires exclusion
Answer B: No, because the good-faith exception may apply to officers' reasonable reliance on the facially valid warrant
Answer C: Yes, unless the magistrate later testifies that she still thinks probable cause existed
Answer D: No, because a warrant always validates a search even when officers lie to obtain it
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: None of the usual good-faith limits is present. Excluding the evidence would not meaningfully deter police misconduct.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The good-faith exception may admit evidence when officers reasonably relied on a facially valid warrant. C: The issue is objective reasonable reliance and good-faith limits, not a later subjective defense of the warrant. D: Good faith has limits, including dishonest or reckless police conduct and facially deficient warrants.
lb_rule_reference: Evidence seized under a defective warrant may still be admitted when officers reasonably relied on a facially valid warrant, unless a good-faith limit applies.
