


BARMATRIX Q#: 16184
Outline_code: 73031000
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Other Crimes
Outline: Burglary
Question: A man was in jail after being arrested for burglary. When the police attempted to question him, the man invoked his Miranda rights and refused to answer any questions. The man was subsequently tried, convicted, and sentenced to a prison term for the burglary. Three years later, while the man was serving his prison sentence for the burglary, a police detective from a nearby town questioned him about an unsolved homicide. The detective did not know that the man had invoked Miranda at an earlier time. The man waived his Miranda rights and made several incriminating statements to the detective. When he was later charged with the homicide, the man moved to suppress these statements, claiming that his earlier refusal to waive his Miranda rights should have been honored. Should the court suppress the statements?
Answer A: A: No, because the detective was unaware that the man had originally invoked his Miranda rights.
Answer B: B: No, because the man's prior invocation of his Miranda rights did not preclude the later interrogation.
Answer C: C: Yes, because the man had earlier invoked his Miranda rights, and the police were not permitted to resume questioning, even after a time lapse of years.
Answer D: D: Yes, because the man was incarcerated, and his earlier invocation of his Miranda rights shielded him from further questioning until he was released.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: (B)is the best response, because after the man refused to answer any questions in the burglary, the passage of a significant period of time — during which the man was not subjected to custodial interrogation — was enough to nullify the effects of the man's earlier invocation of Miranda. First, notice a slight ambiguity in the fact pattern here: the package of “Miranda rights” includes two distinct rights: (1) the right not to be interrogated in custody unless counsel is present; and (2) the right to refuse to answer questions while in custody. We know from the facts of the question that when the man “invoked his Miranda rights,” he at least invoked right #2 (his right to remain silent); but we don't know whether he also invoked right #1 (right to counsel). Under the Supreme Court's Miranda case law, the right of the police to resume custodial interrogation is more limited when the suspect has asserted his right to counsel than where he has merely asserted his right to remain silent. Therefore, to be safe in our analysis, we should assume that the man invoked both his right to remain silent and his right not to be interrogated without counsel present — that way, if we can determine that the police still acted properly given this most-adverse-to-the-police assumption, we'll know for sure that the court should not suppress the statements. As it turns out, when we make this “both types of rights asserted” assumption, the case closely matches the facts of Maryland v. Shatzer, 559 U.S. 98 (2010). There, while D was in jail for sexually abusing Child 1, Detective 1 questioned him about whether he had also sexually abused Child 2 (D's son) on a particular occasion. D refused to answer the questions without a lawyer present, and was returned to the general prison population. Two and a half years later, while D was still in prison, Detective 2 took him into an interrogation room and asked him additional questions about the same alleged incident involving Child 2; this time, D waived his Miranda rights and incriminated himself under interrogation. The Court held in Shatzer that D's prior invocation of his Miranda rights did not carry forward so as to invalidate the new waiver. In fact, the Court laid down a bright-line rule: Once 14 days passes between the first custodial interrogation of a suspect and the next one, that is enough to nullify the effect of the suspect's prior invocation of his right to counsel, because the 14 days “provides plenty of time for the suspect to get reacclimated to his normal life, to consult with friends and counsel, and to shake off any residual coercive effects of his prior custody.” (And that's true even if the suspect's “normal life” is a life in prison, so long as he's not being interrogated during the 14 days.) In our problem, since the gap between interrogations was three years (i.e., vastly longer than 14 days), any effect from the man's initial invocation of his Miranda rights was long since dissipated under the Shatzer holding. (A)is not the best response, because the detective's awareness that the man previously invoked his Miranda rights is irrelevant. As we discuss in Choice (B), what nullifies the effect of the man's prior invocation of his rights is the passage of time; anything more than 14 days between custodial interrogations suffices to nullify the prior invocation, under Maryland v. Shatzer, supra. And that's true whether or not the officer who conducted the second interrogation was aware of the suspect's prior invocation of his Miranda rights. The purpose of giving Miranda protections to suspects being subject to custodial interrogation is that such interrogation is inherently coercive. This anti-coercion rationale has the same strength regardless of whether the second interrogating officer is aware that the suspect previously invoked his Miranda rights. Therefore, although this choice correctly predicts the outcome, it does so based on a factor (the interrogator's awareness of what happened during the prior invocation) that is irrelevant. (C)is not the best response, because the lapse of years between interrogations was enough to dispel the coercive effect of the first interrogation. As we discuss in Choice (B), the passage of more than 14 days between the two interrogations is by itself enough to nullify the effect of the suspect's initial invocation of his Miranda rights. Since this choice asserts that the initial invocation had, in effect, a perpetual life, it's inconsistent with the more-than-14-day rule. Indeed, when the Court decided Maryland v. Shatzer, supra, it relied on the rationale that if a substantial time lapse between an initial invocation of Miranda and a subsequent questioning were not enough to nullify the invocation's effect, “every … prohibition of custodial interrogation of a particular suspect would be eternal[,]” a result the Court found nonsensical. (D)is not the best response, because the passage of time between the two interrogations was enough to nullify the man's original invocation of his Miranda rights, regardless of the fact that he was incarcerated during the gap. As we discuss in Choice (B), Maryland v. Shatzer, supra, says that the passage of more than 14 days between two custodial interrogations is sufficient to nullify the effect of the suspect's invocation of his Miranda rights during the first interrogation. The rationale is that if the suspect is not interrogated during this 14-day or longer period, the gap is sufficient to dispel any coercion that might remain from the first interrogation session. While it's true that here (as well as in Shatzer), the suspect was continuously in custody between the two sessions, neither suspect was being subjected to interrogation during the gap — each received “plenty of time . . . to get reacclimated to his normal life, to consult with friends and counsel, and to shake off any residual coercive effects of his prior custody.” Shatzer. This choice — since it asserts that the mere fact of continuous incarceration would shield the man from further questioning until he was released — is inconsistent with Shatzer's “time to get reacclimated to his normal life” rationale.
