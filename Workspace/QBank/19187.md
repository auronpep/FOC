MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Negligence
Outline: Cause in fact (actual cause)
Question: Two construction companies worked on opposite sides of a dry storage yard. Each company negligently allowed welding sparks to ignite separate brush fires. The fires spread at the same speed and merged before reaching the yard owner's warehouse. Fire experts agree that either fire, standing alone, would have destroyed the warehouse before firefighters could arrive. In the yard owner's negligence claim against one construction company, is that company likely to defeat liability by arguing that its fire was not a but-for cause of the warehouse loss?
Answer A: Yes, because the warehouse would have burned even if that company's fire had not occurred
Answer B: Yes, because proximate cause requires the company to be the only cause of the loss
Answer C: No, because the company's fire was a substantial factor in destroying the warehouse
Answer D: No, because negligence is presumed whenever two fires merge
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Each negligently caused fire was independently sufficient to destroy the warehouse. In that duplicate-sufficient-cause setting, substantial-factor analysis treats each fire as an actual cause.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Literal but-for analysis breaks down when each of two negligent forces is independently sufficient. Substantial-factor analysis prevents each defendant from escaping because the other sufficient cause existed. B: Torts does not require a negligent actor to be the sole cause. Multiple tortfeasors can each be actual and proximate causes of one indivisible injury. D: Negligence is not presumed merely because fires merge. The facts independently establish negligent sparks; the legal issue is actual cause, not a presumption of breach.
lb_rule_reference: When two independent negligent forces are each sufficient to cause the same indivisible harm, each may be treated as an actual cause under substantial-factor analysis even though neither is a but-for cause.
