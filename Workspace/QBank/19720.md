Outline: Battery
Question: Gus harbored a grudge against his former business partner, Elton. One evening, Gus spotted Elton walking alone on a quiet path and approached from behind. Before Elton could hear or turn around, Gus struck Elton across the back of the head with an open palm, causing Elton to stumble and suffer a mild concussion. Elton had no warning and no awareness that Gus was nearby until the moment of impact. Gus argues that because Elton never feared the blow — he never anticipated any contact — the apprehension element of assault was not met, and he cannot be convicted of battery either. Is Gus's argument that battery requires prior apprehension legally correct?
Answer A: Yes — battery and assault share the apprehension element; since Elton had no apprehension, neither crime was committed
Answer B: Yes — because Elton had no apprehension, he was not put at risk of harm before the contact, and the harm-avoidance purpose of battery law was not implicated until it was too late
Answer C: No — battery requires only an intentional harmful or offensive contact, not that the victim anticipated or was aware of it beforehand
Answer D: No — but only because the blow caused actual injury (a concussion); if there had been no injury, the apprehension element might be required to support a battery charge
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Common-law battery is the unlawful application of force to the person of another that is either harmful or offensive to a person of ordinary sensibilities. The elements are: (1) intent to make the contact (or engage in the act that causes contact), (2) actual contact, (3) harmful or offensive nature of the contact. The victim's prior awareness, apprehension, or anticipation of the contact is not an element of battery. Assault — the distinct and separate offense — requires apprehension. Gus intentionally struck Elton from behind; all battery elements are met. His argument conflates the two crimes.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Apprehension is an element of assault — not battery. Battery is the completed unlawful application of force to another person. The victim's awareness, anticipation, or fear is entirely irrelevant to battery. Striking someone from behind who had no warning is a paradigmatic battery. Gus's argument confuses the two offenses. B: The harm-avoidance rationale does not create an apprehension requirement for battery. Battery is a completed-harm offense; its elements are satisfied at the moment of harmful or offensive contact. The crime is not premised on the victim's ability to avoid the contact. A sucker punch is a battery regardless of the victim's awareness. D: Battery does not require injury; it requires only harmful OR offensive contact. Even if Elton suffered no concussion, a deliberate strike from behind would be battery (offensive contact). The injury-or-apprehension trade-off this choice implies does not exist in common-law battery doctrine.
lb_rule_reference: Battery does not require that the victim be aware of or apprehensive about the contact; a defendant who strikes a victim from behind, or strikes an unconscious person, commits battery even though the victim had no apprehension
