Question: Parker robbed a pharmacy with an accomplice. They escaped, changed clothes at a rented apartment, divided the money, and stayed there unnoticed for three hours. Later, Parker argued with the accomplice about the split and shot him. The prosecution charged Parker with felony murder based on the robbery. Is felony murder the best theory for the accomplice's death?
Answer A: Yes, because the argument would not have occurred but for the robbery
Answer B: Yes, because robbery is an inherently dangerous felony
Answer C: No, because the robbery had ended when Parker reached a place of temporary safety before the later shooting
Answer D: No, because felony murder never applies when the person killed is a co-felon
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Felony murder extends through immediate flight but ends when the felons reach a place of temporary safety. Parker and the accomplice had escaped, changed clothes, divided money, and remained hidden for hours. The later killing was a separate episode, so felony murder is not the best theory.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Felony murder requires more than but-for connection. The death must occur during the felony transaction, including immediate flight. Here, the felons had reached a place of temporary safety before the later argument. B: A qualifying predicate is necessary but not sufficient. The killing also must occur during the felony, attempted felony, or immediate flight. The transaction had ended. D: The reason felony murder fails here is timing, not a universal co-felon-victim rule. Some jurisdictions and fact patterns treat co-felon deaths differently, but this question turns on place of safety.
lb_rule_reference: Felony murder generally covers deaths during the felony, attempted felony, or immediate flight, but the felony transaction ends once the felons reach a place of temporary safety.
