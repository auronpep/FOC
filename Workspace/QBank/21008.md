Question: John contracted with a builder to add a room to John's house. John told the builder during negotiations that the new room would make life easier for John's father, Isaac, who lived with him. The written contract required the builder to build the room for John and did not mention Isaac or require any performance to Isaac. After defective work, Isaac sued the builder as a third-party beneficiary. Is Isaac likely to recover?
Answer A: Yes, because Isaac is John's father and the builder knew Isaac would benefit.
Answer B: Yes, because family members are automatically intended beneficiaries of household improvement contracts.
Answer C: No, because a construction contract can never create third-party beneficiary rights.
Answer D: No, because the facts show only that Isaac would benefit from John's contract, not that the parties intended to give Isaac an enforceable right.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: Isaac is likely only an incidental beneficiary. A family relationship and the promisor's knowledge that a family member will benefit can support intent when paired with direct performance or contract language, but they do not by themselves create enforceable beneficiary rights.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because relationship plus benefit is not enough. B: B is wrong because there is no automatic family-beneficiary rule. C: C is wrong because construction contracts can create intended beneficiaries if the contract shows that intent.
