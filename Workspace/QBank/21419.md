Question: A state legislature draws districts for its lower house based on geography rather than population. Rural districts are given one representative for every 15,000 residents; urban districts receive one representative for every 80,000 residents. The legislature justifies the plan on the ground that rural areas have distinctive interests that require greater per-capita representation, and that the Senate-style geographic weighting reflects the state's tradition. A coalition of urban residents challenges the districting plan under the Equal Protection Clause. Under Equal Protection analysis, how should the court evaluate the population-disparity districting plan?
Answer A: The plan is valid because states have inherent authority to structure their legislatures based on geographical and political considerations, analogous to the federal Senate model
Answer B: Rational basis applies because legislative apportionment is a political question not subject to judicial review under Equal Protection
Answer C: The plan violates the Equal Protection Clause because the one person, one vote principle requires that state legislative districts be drawn with substantial population equality; a 5:1 population disparity between rural and urban districts is constitutionally impermissible
Answer D: The plan survives if the state can show the geographic weighting is rationally related to the legitimate interest in preserving rural representation, even though it does not maximize population equality
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The one person, one vote principle, established in Reynolds v. Sims, requires that state legislative districts be drawn with substantial population equality so that each vote carries approximately equal weight. A 5:1 ratio between rural and urban districts (15,000 vs. 80,000 residents per representative) reflects a substantial population deviation that systematically dilutes the voting power of urban residents and violates Equal Protection. The Senate analogy was specifically rejected.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Supreme Court rejected this analogy in Reynolds v. Sims. The federal Senate structure was the product of a unique constitutional compromise among sovereign states; it does not serve as a model for state legislative apportionment. All houses of state legislatures must be apportioned on a population basis — one person, one vote applies to every legislative chamber. B: Baker v. Carr (1962) held that legislative apportionment is justiciable — not a political question. Reynolds v. Sims then established the one person, one vote standard, which is enforced by federal courts under the Equal Protection Clause. Malapportionment cases are squarely reviewable. D: The right to vote — including the right to have one's vote carry equal weight — is a fundamental right triggering strict scrutiny. Apportionment deviations cannot be justified by merely rational interests; the one person, one vote standard applies and requires substantial population equality. Rational basis is the wrong standard.
lb_rule_reference: Under the Equal Protection Clause, legislative districts for state and federal legislatures must be drawn with substantial population equality (one person, one vote). Significant population deviations among districts violate the right to an equally weighted vote.
