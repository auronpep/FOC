Subtopic: General Principles
Outline: Necessity
Question: Ben, an experienced hiker, deliberately ignores clearly posted trail closure signs and ventures into a restricted wilderness area during a severe storm warning. His trail is soon blocked by a rising river. To survive, Ben breaks into a ranger's locked supply cabin, destroys the lock, and uses food, blankets, and a radio without authorization — causing $3,000 in damage. Ben is charged with criminal trespass and destruction of property. He claims necessity. Will Ben's necessity defense succeed?
Answer A: Yes, because saving human life is always a greater good than protecting property
Answer B: Yes, because Ben had no other reasonable alternative once he was stranded
Answer C: No, because Ben created the emergency by ignoring trail closure signs and storm warnings
Answer D: No, because necessity is never available as a defense to property crimes
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Necessity as a defense to criminal conduct requires that the emergency was not of the defendant's own making. A defendant who negligently or recklessly creates the conditions requiring a criminal choice cannot later claim necessity to excuse the resulting crime. Ben deliberately entered a restricted area during a storm warning, ignoring explicit closure signs. This volitional act of negligence created the emergency. The fault-creation bar renders necessity unavailable regardless of how compelling the actual emergency became. Ben is liable for trespass and destruction of property.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The necessity defense contains a critical limitation: if the defendant created or substantially contributed to the emergency through his own fault (here, negligence in ignoring storm warnings and trail closures), the defense is barred. Ben's own negligence produced the emergency he is now trying to escape through necessity. B: Satisfying one element of necessity does not establish the defense if another element is not met. The fault-creation bar defeats Ben's claim even if no immediate alternative existed. The law looks at how the defendant got into the situation, not just what options were available after the situation arose. D: Necessity is not categorically unavailable for property crimes. If all elements were satisfied — proportional harm, no lawful alternative, defendant did not create the situation — necessity would be a valid defense to trespass and property destruction. The defense fails here for the fault-creation reason, not because property crimes are categorically excluded.
lb_rule_reference: Necessity (choice of evils) requires that (1) the harm avoided is greater than the harm caused, (2) no lawful alternative existed, and (3) the defendant did not create the emergency through his own fault; the defense is unavailable if the defendant negligently or recklessly created the situation
