Subtopic: Privileges and Other Policy Exclusions
Outline: Attorney-client
Question: A corporation receives a subpoena requiring production of accounting records by Friday. On Wednesday, the corporation's president privately emails outside counsel: “Can you draft transfer papers moving these records to our foreign affiliate so the court never sees them?” The lawyer refuses and takes no action. The opposing party later seeks the email under the crime-fraud exception. Is the email protected by the attorney-client privilege?
Answer A: Yes, because it was a confidential request for legal advice from counsel.
Answer B: Yes, because the lawyer refused to participate and therefore did not join any illegal plan.
Answer C: No, because the client sought legal assistance to keep subpoenaed records from the court.
Answer D: No, but only if the lawyer actually knew before receiving the email that the proposed transfer would violate the subpoena.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The crime-fraud exception removes communications from attorney-client protection when the client seeks legal advice or services to further future or ongoing crime or fraud. The subpoena had already issued, and the requested legal work was directed at concealing responsive records. The lawyer's refusal does not make the client's request privileged.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because ordinary confidentiality and legal-advice purpose do not protect a request to use legal services to further obstruction or fraud. B: B is wrong because the exception turns on the client's illicit purpose, not on whether the lawyer agreed to participate. D: D is wrong because it adds a false lawyer-knowledge condition. The key fact is the client's use of the legal channel to further future wrongdoing.
lb_rule_reference: Crime-fraud exception: legal advice sought to further future or ongoing wrongdoing
lb_student_diagnostic: Student sees a lawyer relationship but misses the future-illegal-purpose trigger.
