Question: A bookstore is fined under a city ordinance for selling certain books to adults. The bookstore sues, asserting the First Amendment rights of its customers. The customers are reluctant to sue because doing so would reveal sensitive reading choices, and the bookstore has lost sales from the ordinance. Is third-party standing likely available?
Answer A: No, because a plaintiff can never assert another person's constitutional rights
Answer B: Yes, because the bookstore has its own injury, a close customer relationship, and customer hindrance
Answer C: Yes, solely because the First Amendment is important
Answer D: No, because lost sales are economic and cannot support standing
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The bookstore is injured by lost sales, is closely connected to customers' expressive interests, and customers face a practical obstacle to suing.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Third-party standing can be available when the plaintiff has injury, close relationship, and hindrance. C: Importance of the right does not replace the plaintiff-injury and third-party standing elements. D: Economic injury is a concrete injury in fact when traceable and redressable.
lb_rule_reference: A litigant generally must assert her own rights, but third-party standing may be allowed when the plaintiff has her own injury, a close relationship with the third party, and a hindrance to the third party's ability to sue.
