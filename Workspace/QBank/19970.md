MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Civil Procedure
Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Federal Subject-Matter Jurisdiction
Question: A plaintiff files a state-court action asserting only diversity claims. The defendant files a notice of removal. The original complaint did not allege the plaintiff's domicile, but the defendant knew informally that the parties were citizens of different states. Forty-five days after the notice of removal is filed, the plaintiff moves to remand, arguing two grounds: (1) the notice of removal was procedurally defective because one defendant failed to join in the removal notice; and (2) the court lacks subject matter jurisdiction because, on closer inspection, the plaintiff and defendant are both domiciled in the same state. The governing forum is federal court, post-removal, under the MBE baseline. How should the court rule on the motion to remand?
Answer A: Grant remand on both grounds because both defects were discovered within 45 days of the notice of removal and the motion was filed promptly after discovery
Answer B: Deny remand on both grounds because the plaintiff waived any right to remand by waiting 45 days rather than challenging removal immediately
Answer C: Deny remand on the procedural ground (waived after 30 days) but grant remand on the jurisdictional ground (no diversity), which may be raised at any time
Answer D: Grant remand on the procedural ground only, because § 1447(c) treats procedural defects as non-waivable and the jurisdictional defect is barred because it was not raised within the same 30-day window
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Section 1447(c) provides two separate tracks. Procedural defects in removal — such as a co-defendant's failure to join in the removal notice — must be challenged by motion within 30 days of the notice of removal or they are waived. The plaintiff here waited 45 days, which is beyond the 30-day window; the procedural defect is waived. By contrast, lack of subject matter jurisdiction — here, the absence of complete diversity — may never be waived and may be raised at any time before final judgment, including by the court on its own motion. The court should deny remand on the procedural ground but grant remand on the jurisdictional ground.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Section 1447(c) draws a sharp distinction. A procedural defect in removal (here, the missing defendant's joinder) must be raised by motion within 30 days of the notice of removal — or the defect is waived. Forty-five days is too late for the procedural defect ground. The jurisdictional defect (no actual diversity) may be raised at any time and is not waivable. B: Lack of subject matter jurisdiction is a nonwaivable defect under § 1447(c) — it may be raised at any time before final judgment, even sua sponte by the court. The 30-day waiver rule applies only to procedural defects. The jurisdictional ground (no diversity) was not waived and the court must consider it regardless of when it is raised. D: The rule is the exact reverse: procedural defects are waived if not raised within 30 days; jurisdictional defects are never waived. This answer inverts the § 1447(c) framework.
lb_rule_reference: 28 U.S.C. § 1447(c) — a motion to remand for a defect other than lack of subject matter jurisdiction must be made within 30 days after filing of the notice of removal; lack of jurisdiction may be raised at any time.
