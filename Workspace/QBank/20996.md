Question: Luca voluntarily takes a large amount of an illegal hallucinogen at a party. During the hallucination, he smashes a neighbor's window, believing the glass is a waterfall. He has no underlying mental disease and no evidence of chronic intoxication-related disorder. He is charged with malicious destruction of property. Can Luca claim insanity based only on the hallucination?
Answer A: Yes, because he did not understand reality while hallucinating
Answer B: Yes, because any hallucination satisfies M'Naghten
Answer C: No, because acute voluntary intoxication alone is not legal insanity
Answer D: No, because intoxication is never relevant in criminal law
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Luca's impairment came from voluntarily taking an intoxicant. Without a separate mental disease or defect, the insanity defense is unavailable. He may try ordinary intoxication arguments if the offense contains a specific intent element, but he cannot convert acute voluntary intoxication into insanity.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Acute voluntary intoxication is not a mental disease for insanity purposes. It is governed by intoxication doctrine. B: The hallucination must be caused by a qualifying mental disease or defect, not merely voluntary drug use. D: Intoxication can matter for specific-intent crimes or when involuntary. It just does not become insanity on these facts.
lb_rule_reference: Acute voluntary intoxication is analyzed under intoxication doctrine, not insanity; insanity requires mental disease or defect
