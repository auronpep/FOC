Question: A defendant is convicted of felony theft in state court by an 11-to-1 jury verdict under a state statute permitting non-unanimous verdicts for non-capital felonies. On appeal, the defendant raises two arguments: (1) the jury venire underrepresented Hispanic residents compared to the community, violating the fair cross-section requirement; and (2) the non-unanimous verdict violated the Sixth Amendment as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The state concedes that the venire included fewer Hispanic jurors than their proportion in the community but argues that the defendant cannot show the underrepresentation was due to systematic exclusion. The conviction was entered after the Supreme Court's decision in Ramos v. Louisiana. Which of the following is the most accurate statement of the law regarding the defendant's two arguments?
Answer A: Both arguments fail: fair cross-section requires a proportionate jury, and non-unanimous state verdicts remain constitutional as a matter of historical practice
Answer B: The fair cross-section argument fails because the requirement only applies to the venire and the defendant has not proven systematic exclusion; the unanimity argument succeeds under Ramos
Answer C: The fair cross-section argument succeeds because any underrepresentation of a cognizable group in the venire is a per se violation, and the unanimity argument also succeeds under Ramos
Answer D: The unanimity argument fails because Ramos applies only to states that amended their constitutions after the decision; all pre-Ramos statutes remain grandfathered
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: On the fair cross-section claim: Duren v. Missouri sets out the three-part test—the defendant must show (1) the group is distinctive, (2) systematic underrepresentation, and (3) the underrepresentation results from systematic exclusion in the jury-selection process. Critically, the requirement applies to the venire, not the composed jury; there is no right to a jury proportionately drawn from all groups. The state concedes underrepresentation but the defendant has not shown systematic exclusion, so this claim fails. On unanimity: Ramos v. Louisiana (2020) held that the Sixth Amendment's unanimous-verdict requirement, which applies in federal courts, also applies to the states via the Fourteenth Amendment. A felony conviction by 11-to-1 after Ramos is unconstitutional.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Both premises are wrong. The fair cross-section requirement applies only to the venire, not the seated jury—defendants are not entitled to a jury that proportionately reflects the community. On the non-unanimous verdict issue, Ramos v. Louisiana (2020) overruled Apodaca v. Oregon and held that the Sixth Amendment's unanimous verdict requirement applies to states through the Fourteenth Amendment. Post-Ramos, non-unanimous verdicts for serious offenses are unconstitutional in state courts. C: Under Duren, statistical underrepresentation alone does not establish a fair cross-section violation. The defendant must also demonstrate that the underrepresentation stems from systematic exclusion in the selection process—a showing about how the venire was assembled, not just its statistical composition. The state's concession of underrepresentation is necessary but not sufficient. D: There is no grandfathering exception for pre-existing statutes. Ramos applies to all cases on direct review at the time of the decision and to cases tried after the decision. The defendant's conviction here was entered after Ramos, so there is no retroactivity problem. The non-unanimous verdict is unconstitutional regardless of when the state statute was enacted.
lb_rule_reference: The Sixth Amendment fair cross-section requirement applies to the jury pool (venire), not to the composition of the actual jury; under Ramos v. Louisiana (2020), the Sixth Amendment requires a unanimous verdict in all serious criminal cases, including in state courts
