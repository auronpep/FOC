Outline: Materiality and probative value
Question: Sarah sues Peter after their cars collide at an intersection. Sarah claims Peter entered the intersection without stopping. Sarah offers photographs of short skid marks beginning just past the stop sign in Peter's lane. Peter argues that the skid marks could have been made by another car. Should the court admit the photographs?
Answer A: Yes, because the skid marks have some tendency to make Peter's failure to stop more probable, even if another explanation is possible.
Answer B: Yes, but only if Sarah first proves by a preponderance of the evidence that Peter's car made the marks.
Answer C: No, because the skid marks do not conclusively prove that Peter failed to stop.
Answer D: No, because circumstantial evidence is inadmissible unless no other explanation is possible.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The relevance threshold is low. The skid marks near the stop sign tend to support Sarah's theory even though Peter can argue an innocent or alternate explanation. That weakness goes to weight, not admissibility.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because Sarah need not prove the ultimate inference by a preponderance before the jury may consider the photographs. C: C is wrong because evidence need not be conclusive to be relevant. D: D is wrong because circumstantial evidence is admissible if it has any tendency to prove a consequential fact.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401 / weak but probative
