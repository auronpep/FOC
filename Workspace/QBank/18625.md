Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Identifications
Question: Shortly after a purse snatching in a park, officers spotted a man matching the victim's description — male, early thirties, green jacket, tan backpack — two blocks away. They detained him and brought him back to the park within fifteen minutes of the crime. The victim, still at the scene, was asked: "Is this the man who grabbed your purse?" She looked at the detained man, said "Yes, that's him," and signed a field identification card. The detained man, Leo, was arrested. He moved to suppress the identification as the product of an impermissibly suggestive showup. Is the showup identification admissible?
Answer A: No, because a one-on-one showup in which the police present a single suspect in police custody is per se impermissibly suggestive and therefore inadmissible
Answer B: No, because the officer's leading question — "Is this the man who grabbed your purse?" — was so suggestive it violated Miranda
Answer C: Yes, because although the showup was suggestive, the identification was reliable given the victim's fresh memory, close-range observation, and the prompt nature of the confrontation
Answer D: Yes, because showups within thirty minutes of a crime are categorically exempt from due process scrutiny
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The one-on-one showup is impermissibly suggestive (step one satisfied): presenting a single detained suspect with police present and asking "Is this the man?" is maximally leading. But suppression requires more — a substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification under the totality of circumstances (*Manson v. Brathwaite*). Here the reliability factors favor admission: the victim saw the snatcher during the theft at close range (opportunity to view), was directly engaged (high attention), gave a matching description (accurate prior description), identified Leo with certainty within fifteen minutes of the crime (short interval, high certainty). The identification is reliable despite the suggestive procedure and is admissible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Courts have repeatedly held that showups, while inherently suggestive, are not per se unconstitutional. *Neil v. Biggers* and *Manson v. Brathwaite* rejected categorical exclusion rules in favor of a totality-of-circumstances reliability analysis. Prompt showups near the crime scene serve the legitimate interests of reliability (fresh memory, accurate description of the actual perpetrator) and permit rapid release of innocent suspects. B: Miranda* governs custodial interrogation of suspects, not questions posed to witnesses during identification procedures. The relevant constitutional framework is due process under the Fourteenth Amendment, analyzed under *Biggers/Brathwaite*. While the question's phrasing is relevant to suggestiveness, *Miranda* is simply the wrong doctrine. D: There is no categorical time-based exemption from due process scrutiny. The promptness of the showup is one reliability factor (short time between crime and confrontation) that weighs in favor of admission under *Manson v. Brathwaite*, but the full two-part test always applies. An unreliable identification obtained in a prompt showup would still be suppressible.
lb_rule_reference: A one-on-one showup is inherently suggestive because it presents a single suspect to the witness, but it is not per se inadmissible; admissibility depends on whether the identification is reliable under the *Manson v. Brathwaite* totality-of-circumstances test.
