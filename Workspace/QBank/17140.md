Outline: Use of impeachment material
Question: In a federal civil-rights trial, Rachel testifies that an officer struck her without warning. On cross-examination, the officer suggests that Rachel invented the accusation after she hired a lawyer. Rachel then offers a text message she sent to her sister the night of the incident, before she hired a lawyer, saying, “The officer struck me without warning.” Rachel is still subject to cross-examination. The officer objects. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the text because prior consistent statements are never admissible.
Answer B: Admit the text only to show that Rachel recently repeated the same story, not for its truth.
Answer C: Admit the text to rebut the charge of recent fabrication and as substantive evidence, because it predates the alleged motive to fabricate.
Answer D: Admit the text only if the officer first offers reputation evidence that Rachel is untruthful.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: A prior consistent statement may be admitted when the declarant testifies and is subject to cross-examination and the statement is offered to rebut an express or implied charge of recent fabrication or improper motive. Because the text predates the alleged motive, it may rehabilitate Rachel and is also treated as nonhearsay under FRE 801(d)(1)(B).
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because some prior consistent statements are admissible. B: B is wrong because the qualifying statement is not limited to non-substantive repetition. D: D is wrong because the trigger is the charge of recent fabrication or improper motive, not reputation impeachment.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 801(d)(1)(B) / prior consistent statement after fabrication charge
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests rehabilitation material and when it can be used substantively as well as for credibility.
