MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Homicide
Outline: Murder
Question: Armed gang members abduct Walt and hold a gun to his head. They tell Walt that if he does not shoot and kill a specific pedestrian (an innocent stranger), they will immediately execute Walt. Walt genuinely believes the threat and has no means of escape. Walt shoots and kills the pedestrian to save his own life. Walt is charged with murder and raises duress as a defense. Under the common law, what is the most accurate statement of Walt's defense?
Answer A: Walt is not guilty because the threat was immediate and he had no opportunity to escape
Answer B: Walt is not guilty because he chose the lesser of two evils
Answer C: Walt is guilty of murder because duress is never a defense to intentional homicide at common law
Answer D: Walt is guilty only of manslaughter because he acted under extreme emotional disturbance
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Common law imposes a categorical exception to the duress defense for intentional homicide. No matter how credible, immediate, and unavoidable the threat, a defendant who purposely kills an innocent third party cannot escape murder liability through duress. The rule reflects the policy that the law does not authorize trading the life of an innocent victim for the defendant's own survival. Walt intentionally killed the pedestrian — an innocent person who posed no threat to anyone. Duress cannot justify that killing. Walt is guilty of murder.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Even when all elements of duress are otherwise satisfied, the common law categorically bars the defense as a justification for intentional killing of an innocent person. The rationale is that the law will not sanction the trading of one innocent life for another. This categorical bar applies regardless of how credible or immediate the threat was. B: Neither duress nor necessity provides a defense to intentional homicide at common law. Necessity, which involves a lesser-evil choice between evils, also bars its application when an innocent is intentionally killed. The "lesser evil" framing resonates morally but does not match the legal rule. D: The EED doctrine (an MPC concept) or heat-of-passion doctrine (common law) applies to situations where the defendant kills in response to provocation that arouses extreme passion. Walt's situation is coercion-driven, not passion-driven. More importantly, the question tests the duress/murder rule, which results in murder, not manslaughter. EED/heat-of-passion is a separate doctrine not triggered on these facts.
lb_rule_reference: At common law, duress is never a defense to intentional homicide; a defendant who intentionally kills an innocent person under coercion is guilty of murder regardless of how compelling the threat was
