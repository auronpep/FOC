Question: After voluntarily drinking heavily, Arden climbed onto an apartment roof and began throwing bricks onto a busy sidewalk for amusement. One brick struck and killed a pedestrian. Arden argues that he was too drunk to appreciate the risk clearly. Does voluntary intoxication bar a depraved-heart murder conviction?
Answer A: Yes, because intoxication prevents any murder conviction
Answer B: Yes, because depraved-heart murder requires a specific intent to kill
Answer C: No, because voluntary intoxication generally does not negate reckless extreme-risk malice
Answer D: No, but only because intoxication is never relevant to any criminal offense
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Arden voluntarily became intoxicated and then engaged in conduct creating an obvious extreme risk of death. Voluntary intoxication may matter for some specific-intent crimes, but it generally does not bar depraved-heart murder.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Voluntary intoxication is not a blanket defense. It generally does not negate recklessness or depraved-heart malice. B: Depraved-heart murder is based on extreme recklessness showing indifference to human life, not purpose to kill. Voluntary intoxication does not normally defeat that malice route. D: Voluntary intoxication can be relevant to specific-intent crimes in limited circumstances. It fails here because the charged malice route is reckless extreme indifference.
lb_rule_reference: Voluntary intoxication may negate specific intent in limited settings, but it generally does not negate recklessness, negligence, or depraved-heart malice.
