Question: Miriam hired “Deborah Design LLC” to paint a mural in her store. The signed agreement's first page identified “Artist: Deborah Design LLC,” but the performance paragraph said, “Deborah will complete the mural by June 1.” The signature line contained only the handwritten name “Deborah,” with no title. When the mural was not completed, Miriam sought judgment against Deborah personally based only on the signature page. Is Miriam entitled to personal liability against Deborah solely from the face of the writing?
Answer A: Yes, because any person who signs without a precise title is personally liable as a matter of law.
Answer B: Yes, because the word “Deborah” in the performance paragraph conclusively identifies Deborah as the only party.
Answer C: Probably not solely from the face of the writing, because the entity heading and unclear signature line create a party-capacity ambiguity that may require interpretation evidence.
Answer D: No, because an LLC name anywhere in a contract always prevents personal liability, even if the signer expressly promised personal performance.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that ambiguous party-identity or capacity language can be resolved through interpretation rather than by automatic signature rules. The trigger facts are the LLC heading, the body's use of “Deborah,” and the signature without a title. The decisive lock is term status: the writing is ambiguous as to whether Deborah signed personally or for the LLC. The court should not impose personal liability solely by rigidly reading the signature page.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because it treats signature format as dispositive despite ambiguity. B: B is wrong because the body language is one piece of the ambiguity, not conclusive proof. D: D is wrong because entity references do not automatically defeat personal liability if the contract clearly creates it.
