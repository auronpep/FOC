Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: A grand jury subpoenas Leo to testify about a fraud scheme. Leo invokes the Fifth Amendment because truthful answers would incriminate him. The prosecutor grants use and derivative-use immunity, barring the government from using Leo's compelled testimony or evidence derived from it against him. Leo still refuses, arguing only transactional immunity would be enough. May Leo be compelled to testify after the immunity grant?
Answer A: No, because only transactional immunity satisfies the Fifth Amendment
Answer B: No, because a witness may always refuse to answer incriminating questions
Answer C: Yes, because use and derivative-use immunity is sufficient to compel testimony over the privilege claim
Answer D: Yes, because grand jury witnesses never have Fifth Amendment rights
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The immunity prevents the government from using Leo's compelled testimony or its fruits against him. That protection is coextensive with the Fifth Amendment privilege.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Constitution does not require transactional immunity. Use and derivative-use immunity is coextensive with the privilege. B: Once adequate immunity removes the incrimination risk from compelled testimony, the privilege no longer permits refusal on that ground. D: Grand jury witnesses can invoke the privilege. The reason Leo can be compelled is the adequate immunity grant.
lb_rule_reference: The government may compel incriminating testimony after granting immunity coextensive with the privilege; transactional immunity is not constitutionally required.
