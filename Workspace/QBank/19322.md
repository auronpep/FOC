Outline: Robbery
Question: Beatrice was robbed at gunpoint in a well-lit parking garage. During the robbery, she stared at the robber's face for approximately ninety seconds from a distance of three feet. She immediately called 911 and gave a detailed description. Three days later, police showed Beatrice a photo array of six individuals. The detective administering the array inadvertently placed the defendant Anthony's photograph in a slightly brighter position than the others because of a photocopying error. Beatrice identified Anthony without hesitation. Anthony moved to suppress the photo array identification, conceding that the reliability factors from *Manson v. Brathwaite* strongly favored the identification but arguing that any suggestive procedure requires exclusion. How should the court rule on Anthony's motion to suppress?
Answer A: Grant the motion, because any impermissible suggestiveness in a police-arranged identification procedure requires automatic suppression
Answer B: Grant the motion, because the correct remedy for any constitutional violation is suppression of the tainted evidence
Answer C: Deny the motion, because even if the procedure was suggestive, the strong reliability factors establish that there is no substantial likelihood of misidentification, and the suggestiveness goes to weight, not admissibility
Answer D: Deny the motion, because photo arrays are never subject to due process suppression regardless of how they are administered
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The due process test for identification procedures (*Neil v. Biggers*; *Manson v. Brathwaite*) requires both (1) an impermissibly suggestive procedure AND (2) a substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification under the totality of circumstances. The parties have conceded that the *Brathwaite* reliability factors — ninety-second close-range observation in good lighting, immediate detailed description, prompt and certain identification — strongly support the identification. Even assuming the brighter photograph constitutes suggestiveness, step two is not satisfied. No constitutional violation occurred. The admissibility is proper; any argument about the brightness disparity goes to the weight the jury should assign the identification, not to whether it comes in at all.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: There is no automatic exclusion rule for suggestive identification procedures. *Manson v. Brathwaite* rejected a per se exclusion approach in favor of a reliability-based totality-of-circumstances test. Even police-caused suggestiveness does not compel suppression unless it also creates a substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification. Where the identification is reliable, it is admissible. B: The due process clause does not treat all suggestive identification procedures as constitutional violations requiring suppression. The constitutional violation — and the suppression remedy — only arises when the suggestive procedure creates a substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification. Where reliability is established, no constitutional violation has occurred, and there is nothing to suppress. The suggestiveness is a matter for the jury. D: Ash* addressed only the Sixth Amendment right to counsel; it did not exempt photo arrays from due process analysis. Photo arrays are subject to the *Biggers/Brathwaite* due process framework when police arrange them suggestively. The correct reason to deny the motion here is not immunity from review but rather the failure to satisfy step two of the due process test.
lb_rule_reference: If a suggestive identification procedure does not create a substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification under the totality of circumstances, the identification is admissible and the suggestiveness goes to weight — not admissibility — for the jury to evaluate.
