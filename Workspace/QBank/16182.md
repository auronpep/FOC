


BARMATRIX Q#: 16182
Outline_code: 75070202
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Exceptions to warrant requirement
Question: The police suspected a woman of dealing in stolen credit cards. An undercover police officer knocked on the woman's door and told her that he was willing to pay for usable credit cards. When the woman asked if he was a police officer, the officer replied, “No,” giving her a false name and saying, “You can call Harvey — he'll vouch for me.” The woman admitted him to her house. After she left the room to call Harvey, the officer lifted some papers off a desk and underneath discovered three credit cards bearing different names. He seized the cards, and when the woman returned, he arrested her. The woman has moved to suppress the evidence seized on the ground that her Fourth Amendment rights were violated. Should the court grant the motion to suppress?
Answer A: A: No, because it was immediately apparent that the cards were stolen.
Answer B: B: No, because the woman assumed the risk that the officer would seize items in plain sight.
Answer C: C: Yes, because the woman's rights were violated when the officer lifted the papers.
Answer D: D: Yes, because the woman's rights were violated when the officer obtained entry by deceit.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: (C)is the best response, because the officer's act of lifting the papers did not fall within the plain view rule, and was therefore a search for which both probable cause and a search warrant were required. As is typically the case with a search-and-seizure problem, you have to analyze the police conduct in chronological order, to see whether the police violated the target's Fourth Amendment rights at any point. Here, the officer's initial entry into the woman's house was not a violation of her Fourth Amendment rights. (For a discussion of why this is so, see Choice (D).) Therefore, any evidence of crime or contraband that the officer saw in plain view while he was standing in the room into which he had been invited would not have been the product of a Fourth Amendment search at all. But the plain view doctrine applies only to a view that occurred: (1)while the officer was in a place the officer had a right to be, and (2)after the officer had taken only those physical actions that she had a right to take. (As an example of a correct use of plain view on approximately these facts, suppose the officer had spotted obviously-stolen credit cards in plain view face up on top of a table in the room where he was invited to stand. Now, he would have been entitled to seize them without a warrant, regardless of the fact that he had no probable cause to suspect criminality at the time he entered the house.) But just as the officer would not have been entitled to roam through the house looking for evidence (since the scope of the woman's “consent” to his entry extended only to his standing in the front room), he was not entitled to pick up objects (like the papers on the desk), to open closed containers, or to take any other action that would count as a new “search” for Fourth Amendment purposes, since the woman didn't expressly or impliedly consent to those actions. Therefore, his act of lifting the papers violated the woman's Fourth Amendment rights in two respects, first by being done without probable cause to believe that he would find contraband or evidence of crime underneath, and second by being done without a search warrant when there was no applicable exception to the requirement of such a warrant. (A)is not the best response, because an illegal Fourth Amendment search occurred even before the officer saw the cards and realized they were stolen. As we discuss in connection with Choice (C), the officer's act of lifting the papers off the desk was itself a search, which because it was done without probable cause and without a search warrant violated the woman's Fourth Amendment rights. Therefore, anything that happened after the instant when the lifting occurred — including the officer's initial view of the cards — was fruit of the already-complete Fourth Amendment violation. Consequently, for Fourth Amendment purposes it's irrelevant that, as of the moment the officer first saw the cards, it was “immediately apparent that the guards were stolen.” (B)is not the best response, because (1) the officer's opportunity to see (and then seize) the items in plain sight came about only after the officer went beyond the scope of the woman's invitation; and therefore (2) the woman did not assume the risk that the officer would ever see the cards in plain sight. This choice is partially correct: when the woman invited the officer into her home she assumed the risk (despite the officer's deception about his true purpose, as to which see Choice (D)) that the officer might see an item of criminal evidence or contraband in plain sight, at which point he would probably be entitled to seize the item. But the woman's consent to the officer's entry into her house was for the limited purpose of her (1) calling Harvey; and (2) then selling the officer credit cards if Harvey vouched for him. The consent did not include authorizing the officer to lift papers (or anything else) off a desk, any more than it authorized him to open closets, enter additional rooms, or otherwise go beyond the two limited purposes. Therefore, the woman “assumed the risk,” for Fourth Amendment purposes, only of those activities she authorized. (More precisely, after the limited consent, she surrendered her reasonable expectation of privacy as to anything the officer might see in plain view provided that he remained within the scope of the consent, but not as to anything the officer might see as the result of his exceeding the consent, such as his view of the credit cards after he lifted the papers off the desk.) (D)is not the best response, because the officer's entry, although procured by deceit, would not be considered to be a violation of the woman's Fourth Amendment rights. The extent to which a police officer's use of deceit in order to gain consent to enter and/or search the property of the consenter will be deemed to vitiate the consent is controversial, and the Supreme Court has never articulated a broad rule about when this type of deceit makes the resulting entry or search invalid. But what is clear is that, at the very least, “the Fourth Amendment affords no protection to the person who voluntarily reveals incriminating evidence to another in the mistaken belief that the latter will not disclose it.” L,I, K&K Hornbook, § 3.10(c), p. 335. What's also clear is that the just-cited principle includes situations in which the person voluntarily reveals the incriminating evidence to a police officer solely because the officer has falsely denied or concealed the fact that he is (or is working on behalf of) a police officer. Id. For instance, Lewis v. U.S., 385 U.S. 206 (1966) involved a similar situation to the one here: D invited X, who unbeknownst to D was an undercover narcotics agent, into D's home for the express purpose of selling the agent narcotics; X then saw narcotics in D's home, and purchased some. The Court held that for Fourth Amendment purposes, X's deceit did not nullify D's consent to X's entry: “[W]hen, as here, the home is converted into a commercial center to which outsiders are invited for purposes of transacting unlawful business, that business is entitled to no greater sanctity than if it were carried on in a store, a garage, a car, or on the street. A government agent, in the same manner as a private person, may accept an invitation to do business and may enter upon the premises for the very purposes contemplated by the occupant.” Here, similarly, the woman gave her consent to the officer's entry as part of a proposed illegal business transaction, so the fact that the officer obtained the consent by deceit did not make the consent invalid.
