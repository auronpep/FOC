Question: In a federal slip-and-fall trial, Esther claims that she slipped on oil in a store aisle. The store denies notice of the spill. Esther offers testimony that dust and cart tracks were visible in the oil when she fell. The store objects that the dust and tracks do not identify who spilled the oil. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Admit the testimony because dust and tracks tend to show the oil may have been present long enough for the store to discover it.
Answer B: Exclude the testimony because notice can be proved only by a witness who saw an employee look at the spill.
Answer C: Exclude the testimony because the evidence does not identify the person who spilled the oil.
Answer D: Admit the testimony only if Esther proves the tracks were made by store carts.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Dust and tracks in the oil can support an inference that the spill existed for some time. That tends to make notice more probable even though it does not identify who spilled the oil.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B incorrectly limits proof of notice to direct evidence. C is wrong because identity of the spiller is not required for notice relevance.
