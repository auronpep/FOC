Subtopic: Presentation of Evidence
Outline: Materiality and probative value
Question: In a federal embezzlement trial, the prosecutor must prove that Daniel stole $8,000 from his employer's safe. The day after the money disappeared, Daniel deposited $7,500 in cash into his bank account. Daniel objects that the deposit is irrelevant because he might have received the money from lawful freelance work. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Admit the evidence because the deposit has some tendency to make the theft more probable; Daniel's lawful-source explanation affects weight.
Answer B: Exclude the evidence because the prosecutor cannot prove the deposited cash was the stolen money.
Answer C: Exclude the evidence unless the prosecutor proves by a preponderance that Daniel had no lawful source of cash.
Answer D: Admit the evidence only if Daniel first testifies about his freelance work.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The cash deposit is weak but logically probative. A sudden deposit close in time to the missing money tends to make theft more probable than it would be without the evidence. Daniel's alternative explanation is a weight argument for the jury, not a threshold relevance bar.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because relevance does not require proof that the cash was definitely the stolen cash. C: C is wrong because the prosecutor need not disprove every lawful source before admission. D: D is wrong because relevance does not depend on Daniel testifying first.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401; FRE 402
lb_student_diagnostic: Student treats alternative lawful explanations as automatic exclusion rather than jury-weight arguments.
