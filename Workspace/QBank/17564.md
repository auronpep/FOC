Question: Nora learned that her brother had removed her from their mother's will. She sat alone in her car for ten minutes, searched online for the effects of a household poison, bought it, and then drove to her brother's home with a bottle of wine. During dinner, she poured the poison into his glass while he was in the kitchen. He drank the wine and died. Nora was charged with first-degree murder. Is Nora guilty of first-degree murder?
Answer A: No, because she formed the plan and acted on it during the same evening
Answer B: No, because motive alone cannot prove premeditation
Answer C: Yes, because she intentionally killed after reflection and planned use of poison
Answer D: Yes, but only because any poisoning is automatically first-degree murder at common law
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: First-degree murder is supported when the defendant forms an intent to kill after premeditation and deliberation. Nora had time to think, researched and acquired poison, arranged the setting, and administered the poison secretly. The short overall timeline does not defeat deliberation.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Premeditation has no minimum clock time. Nora reflected, researched the poison, obtained it, created an opportunity, and used it after a pause. Those sequence facts support a deliberate killing. B: The prosecution is not relying on motive alone. The poison search, purchase, planned dinner, and secret dosing all show reflective formation of intent. D: The safer exam reason is the combination of intent, reflection, and planning shown by the facts. If a statute makes poison a degree trigger, that would also matter, but this answer ignores the required mental-state analysis.
lb_rule_reference: First-degree murder requires intent to kill plus premeditation and deliberation; no fixed minimum time is required if the facts show reflection and planned execution.
