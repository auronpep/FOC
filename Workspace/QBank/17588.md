Question: In a federal car-accident trial, Naomi calls Daniel to testify about the speed of Leah's car. Daniel states only that he read a newspaper account of the collision and was not at the intersection. Naomi's lawyer then asks, “How fast was Leah driving?” Leah objects. What is the best ruling?
Answer A: Overrule the objection because speed is always relevant in a collision case.
Answer B: Overrule the objection because Daniel read about the collision in a newspaper.
Answer C: Sustain the objection because no evidence supports a finding that Daniel has personal knowledge of the car's speed.
Answer D: Sustain the objection only if Daniel admits the newspaper was inaccurate.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: A lay witness may testify to a matter only if there is evidence sufficient to support a finding that the witness has personal knowledge. Daniel was not present and has only read an account, so the speed question lacks a personal-knowledge foundation.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because relevance does not supply personal knowledge. B: B is wrong because reading a newspaper account does not make Daniel a percipient witness. D: D is wrong because the defect is Daniel's lack of personal knowledge, not the paper's accuracy.
