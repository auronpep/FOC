Question: Rachel, a cabinetmaker, signed a contract with a lumber mill to buy 500 boards described as “Grade M kiln-dried maple.” The contract included a merger clause but did not define “kiln-dried.” Rachel rejected the boards because their moisture content was 12%. The mill offered evidence that, in the commercial hardwood trade, “Grade M kiln-dried maple” allows moisture content up to 14% at delivery. Should the court admit the trade-usage evidence?
Answer A: No, because the merger clause makes the written quality description exclusive.
Answer B: No, because “kiln-dried” is an express term and any moisture contradicts it.
Answer C: Yes, if the usage is established and reasonably consistent with the written quality term, because it explains a technical trade phrase.
Answer D: Yes, because trade usage controls even if it directly negates the written quality description.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that UCC final writings may be explained or supplemented by trade usage, and technical commercial terms may be given their trade meaning if the usage is established and reasonably consistent with the writing. The trigger facts are a goods sale, a merger clause, and the undefined technical phrase “Grade M kiln-dried maple.” The decisive locks are source law and purpose: the evidence explains a trade term; it does not add a separate quality promise.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because a merger clause does not automatically bar UCC usage evidence. B: B is wrong because it treats the ordinary sound of “kiln-dried” as legally self-defining and overreads the express term. D: D is wrong because usage must be reasonably consistent with express terms and does not prevail over an irreconcilable writing.
lb_rule_reference: UCC 2-202 / UCC 1-303 trade usage and technical term
