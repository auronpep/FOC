Outline: Rights during Sentencing
Question: Felix was charged with aggravated battery. His appointed attorney was present throughout the trial but was absent during sentencing because of a scheduling conflict. The judge proceeded with sentencing without granting a continuance. Felix received the maximum sentence. Felix's new counsel argues that his sentencing attorney's absence constitutes per se reversible error. The state argues that Felix must show he would have received a lesser sentence if counsel had been present. Must Felix demonstrate actual prejudice to obtain relief?
Answer A: Yes, because Strickland applies to all Sixth Amendment ineffective assistance claims and always requires a showing of prejudice
Answer B: Yes, because Felix had counsel throughout the trial and the absence at sentencing alone is insufficient to presume prejudice across the entire proceeding
Answer C: No, because complete absence of counsel at a critical stage (sentencing) triggers a conclusive presumption of prejudice under Cronic
Answer D: No, because the maximum sentence is inherently prejudicial regardless of counsel's presence
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: United States v. Cronic* held that in certain circumstances, prejudice is so likely that a case-by-case showing is not required. Complete denial of counsel at a critical stage is the clearest such circumstance. Sentencing is a critical stage of criminal prosecution. The attorney's total absence — not mere ineffectiveness — at sentencing requires no individualized showing of prejudice. The conviction (or in this case, the sentence) is presumptively infirm and reversal follows without requiring Felix to prove a different outcome would have occurred.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: United States v. Cronic* established a distinct category: complete denial of counsel at a critical stage. In that circumstance, prejudice is conclusively presumed — Strickland does not govern. Sentencing is a critical stage. The complete absence of counsel during sentencing falls under *Cronic*, not *Strickland*. B: Cronic*'s presumption applies stage by stage: if counsel is completely absent at a critical stage, prejudice is presumed for that stage. The fact that counsel was present at trial does not cure the absence at sentencing. Sentencing is independently a critical stage, and the *Cronic* presumption operates independently for it. D: The legal basis for relief is the *Cronic* complete-denial rule, not the severity of the sentence. The sentence would be presumptively invalid even if it had been lenient. The correct rule is structural error from complete denial of counsel at a critical stage — the sentence's severity is legally irrelevant to that analysis.
lb_rule_reference: Under United States v. Cronic, prejudice is conclusively presumed when counsel is completely absent at a critical stage; Strickland's reasonable-probability prejudice test applies only to alleged errors during representation, not to complete denial of counsel
