Subtopic: Strict Liability and Products Liability
Outline: Strict Products Liability
Question: A restaurant bought a professional chef's knife with a sharp exposed blade. The knife was manufactured as designed, and all units had the same blade. The knife was balanced, had a textured handle, and used ordinary materials. A cook cut a finger while chopping quickly and sued the manufacturer, arguing that the knife should have had a retractable guard over the blade. The proposed guard would block ordinary chopping strokes and prevent the knife from functioning as a chef's knife. The cook sues under strict products liability for a design defect. Is the cook likely to prevail on a strict products liability claim for a design defect?
Answer A: Yes, because the exposed blade made the knife capable of causing serious injury
Answer B: No, because the proposed guard would eliminate the knife's ordinary function and the blade danger is obvious and inherent
Answer C: Yes, because the manufacturer must adopt any design that would prevent the specific injury
Answer D: No, because design-defect claims are unavailable for products used by professionals
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The proposed alternative would prevent ordinary chopping, and the risk of a sharp chef's knife is obvious and integral to its utility. These facts do not support a design defect.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The sharp exposed blade is integral to the knife's ordinary function and is an obvious danger. Design defect requires more than the fact that a useful product can injure. C: A reasonable alternative must reduce risk without destroying the product's utility or creating an unreasonable tradeoff. The law does not require any injury-preventing change regardless of function. D: Professional use can inform expectations and warnings, but it does not categorically bar design-defect claims. The claim fails because the proposed design is not reasonable and the danger is inherent.
lb_rule_reference: A product is not defectively designed merely because it has an obvious inherent danger when the danger is integral to its useful function and no reasonable safer design preserves that function.
