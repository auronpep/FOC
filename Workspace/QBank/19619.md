MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Officers stopped a suspect on the street based on a tip that he was selling drugs. They lacked probable cause to arrest him. The officers told the suspect to get in the police car. He was driven to the station, placed in an interrogation room, and questioned for three hours. He eventually confessed to drug sales. He moves to suppress the confession as the fruit of an unlawful arrest. Should the court grant the motion to suppress the confession?
Answer A: No, because the officers had reasonable suspicion based on the tip, which justified detaining the suspect for the duration of the investigation
Answer B: No, because the suspect got into the police car without physically resisting, suggesting voluntary acquiescence
Answer C: Yes, because transporting the suspect to the station for interrogation without probable cause or consent converted the Terry stop into a de facto arrest, requiring suppression of the resulting confession
Answer D: Yes, because any questioning of a suspect about criminal activity without Miranda warnings violates the Fifth Amendment and triggers suppression
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Dunaway v. New York and Florida v. Royer establish that a Terry stop becomes a de facto arrest — requiring probable cause — when the restraint on liberty exceeds the brief, on-the-spot investigation that Terry authorizes. Transporting the suspect to the station against his will, placing him in an interrogation room, and detaining him for three hours are characteristic features of an arrest, not a Terry stop. Officers lacked probable cause. The de facto arrest was unconstitutional. The confession obtained during that unlawful detention is a fruit of the poisonous tree and must be suppressed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Terry permits a brief investigatory stop on the street — not an open-ended, station-house interrogation. Florida v. Royer held that moving a person from the place of the stop to a separate location (a more secluded room) can itself transform the stop into an arrest. A three-hour interrogation at the station, initiated without consent and without probable cause, is a de facto arrest regardless of what officers call it. B: Compliance under police authority is not the same as voluntary consent. When officers tell a detainee to get in the patrol car, compliance is submission to authority — not a free choice. For consent to a station visit to defeat the de facto arrest analysis, it must be genuinely voluntary and uncoerced. D: The motion targets Fourth Amendment suppression of the fruits of an unlawful seizure, not a Fifth Amendment Miranda violation. While Miranda may also apply, the correct doctrinal basis for this suppression motion is the de facto arrest without probable cause. Fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine suppresses evidence derived from Fourth Amendment violations — that is the operative issue here.
lb_rule_reference: A Terry stop that exceeds its investigatory purpose in scope or duration becomes a de facto arrest requiring probable cause; transporting a suspect to the station without consent or probable cause exceeds Terry's limits (Dunaway v. New York; Florida v. Royer)
