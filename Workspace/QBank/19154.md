MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Civil Procedure
Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Federal Subject-Matter Jurisdiction
Question: A plaintiff files a diversity action in federal court claiming $80,000 in compensatory damages for personal injury. After trial, the jury returns a verdict of $15,000. The defendant moves to dismiss the action for lack of subject matter jurisdiction, arguing that the actual recovery of only $15,000 proves that the amount in controversy never truly exceeded $75,000. The governing forum is federal court under the MBE baseline. Should the court dismiss the action for lack of subject matter jurisdiction based on the post-trial verdict?
Answer A: Yes, because the $15,000 verdict proves that the actual amount in controversy was only $15,000 from the beginning, retroactively destroying subject matter jurisdiction
Answer B: No, because the plaintiff's good-faith claim of $80,000 at filing satisfies the AIC threshold under the legal certainty test, and the lower verdict does not retroactively destroy jurisdiction
Answer C: Yes, because under the better-rule approach, jurisdiction must be continuously satisfied throughout the litigation, and a post-trial recovery below $75,000 proves it was not
Answer D: No, but the plaintiff should be ordered to refile in state court because the verdict demonstrates the dispute belonged in state court all along
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Under the legal certainty test, the amount in controversy is determined by the plaintiff's good-faith demand at the time of filing. Courts do not dismiss for insufficient AIC merely because the plaintiff ultimately recovers less than $75,000, unless it was clear to a legal certainty at the time of filing that the plaintiff could not recover the jurisdictional amount. Here, the $80,000 claim was made in good faith; the lower verdict does not retroactively destroy jurisdiction. However, the court may impose cost-shifting under 28 U.S.C. § 1332 penalties for recoveries below the threshold.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The AIC is determined at the time of filing, not at the time of verdict. The plaintiff claimed $80,000 in good faith; the legal certainty test is satisfied if a reasonable lawyer could have believed the claim was worth the amount stated. A subsequent recovery less than the jurisdictional minimum does not retroactively destroy jurisdiction; it may, however, affect cost-shifting under § 1332. C: Federal courts do not apply a continuous-satisfaction rule for AIC. Jurisdiction is fixed at the time of filing under the legal certainty test. The Supreme Court has held that a post-complaint reduction in the amount in controversy does not divest federal jurisdiction that was properly invoked. The better-rule argument describes a rule that federal courts do not actually apply. D: There is no post-verdict procedure for ordering a litigant to refile in state court. Once a case is decided on the merits, it is final subject to appeal. The court cannot retroactively redirect a decided case to another tribunal. The only consequences for a low verdict in a diversity case brought in good faith are potential cost-shifting, not dismissal or transfer.
lb_rule_reference: Under the legal certainty test, the AIC is the amount claimed by the plaintiff in good faith; jurisdiction is not defeated by a subsequent recovery below $75,000 unless, at the time of filing, it was legally certain the plaintiff could not recover the claimed amount.
