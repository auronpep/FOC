Question: A motorist sues a city in federal court under a federal civil-rights statute, alleging that officers used excessive force during an arrest. The city subpoenas the motorist's emergency-room physician to testify about statements the motorist made during treatment. The state where the treatment occurred recognizes a broad physician-patient privilege. The motorist objects solely on that state privilege. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Overrule the objection, because federal privilege law governs the federal claim and federal common law does not generally recognize a physician-patient privilege.
Answer B: Sustain the objection, because state privilege law governs all privilege claims arising from treatment within that state.
Answer C: Sustain the objection, because federal common law recognizes a general physician-patient privilege whenever medical treatment was confidential.
Answer D: Overrule the objection only because relevant evidence always defeats a state-law privilege in federal court.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: In a federal-question case, privilege is governed by federal common law unless the Constitution, a federal statute, or a Supreme Court rule provides otherwise. A state physician-patient privilege does not control the federal civil-rights claim, and federal common law does not generally recognize a physician-patient privilege. The decisive axis is source law before relationship label.

The state where treatment occurred does not control privilege for a federal claim merely because the conversation occurred there. C is wrong because This uses a medical-setting shortcut. Federal common law recognizes some relationship privileges, but not a general physician-patient privilege on these facts. D is wrong because This reaches the right result for the wrong reason. Privilege is not defeated merely by relevance; the problem is that the asserted state privilege does not govern and the federal physician privilege is not recognized.
