Subtopic: Intentional Torts
Outline: Privileges
Question: At night in a parking garage, Walker saw Stranger rush toward him with a metal object raised. Walker reasonably believed Stranger was about to strike him. Walker pushed Stranger away, causing Stranger to fall. The object was later revealed to be a flashlight Stranger was waving to return Walker's dropped keys. Stranger sues Walker for battery. Can Walker rely on self-defense?
Answer A: No, because Stranger was not actually attacking Walker
Answer B: No, because Walker used intentional force
Answer C: Yes, because Walker reasonably believed nondeadly force was necessary to prevent imminent contact
Answer D: Yes, but only because Stranger was in a parking garage at night
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Stranger's apparent rush with a raised metal object made Walker's belief reasonable. The push was nondeadly and proportionate to the perceived threat.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Self-defense can rest on a reasonable mistake about the need for force. B: Self-defense is a privilege that can justify intentional nondeadly force when reasonable and proportionate. D: The privilege depends on the full apparent threat and proportional response, not the location alone.
lb_rule_reference: A person may use reasonable nondeadly force in self-defense when reasonably believing such force is necessary, even if the belief later turns out to be mistaken.
