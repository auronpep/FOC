Question: Howard discovered that his wife, Renata, had been having an affair with his closest friend, Felix, after reading Renata's text messages. Howard was devastated and furious. He drove home, paced for three hours, then drove across town to Felix's apartment. Howard confronted Felix at the door and, when Felix confirmed the affair, Howard drew a pistol and shot Felix twice, killing him. Howard told police he had been in a "white rage" the entire time. Is Howard guilty of murder or voluntary manslaughter for Felix's death?
Answer A: Voluntary manslaughter, because discovering spousal infidelity is adequate provocation and Howard remained subjectively enraged throughout
Answer B: Voluntary manslaughter, because Howard confronted Felix immediately upon seeing him, showing the passion had not yet dissipated
Answer C: Murder, because even if the provocation was adequate, three hours is sufficient time for a reasonable person to cool off
Answer D: Murder, because infidelity is not legally adequate provocation for voluntary manslaughter
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Voluntary manslaughter requires: (1) adequate provocation, (2) actual provocation (defendant was provoked), (3) no sufficient cooling-off period, and (4) a causal connection between provocation and killing. Discovering spousal infidelity may satisfy adequate provocation. But the cooling-off requirement is evaluated objectively — would a reasonable person have cooled in three hours? Courts consistently hold that several hours is ordinarily sufficient time for a reasonable person's passions to cool. Howard's continued subjective rage does not substitute for the objective cooling-off standard. The voluntary manslaughter reduction is lost.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Voluntary manslaughter requires not only adequate provocation but also that the defendant acted before a reasonable person would have cooled off. Even accepting that discovery of infidelity is adequate provocation, three hours of pacing and then driving across town is sufficient time for a reasonable person to cool. Howard's subjective claim of continued rage is irrelevant — the standard is objective. B: The cooling-off inquiry focuses on the interval between the initial provocation and the killing, not on the defendant's emotional state in the final seconds before pulling the trigger. The three-hour gap, not the brief confrontation, is the relevant period. During those three hours, a reasonable person would have cooled. D: At common law, discovery of spousal infidelity is one of the recognized categories of adequate provocation (along with assault, mutual combat, and illegal arrest). D's stated reason for murder is incorrect — the correct basis is the cooling-off period, not inadequate provocation.
lb_rule_reference: Even if the initial provocation was adequate, voluntary manslaughter is unavailable if a reasonable person would have cooled off in the time that elapsed between the provocation and the killing
