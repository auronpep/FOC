Question: A plaintiff files a state-court complaint against a corporate defendant. The complaint alleges only state-law negligence and does not allege the plaintiff's citizenship, the defendant's citizenship, or any damages amount. The defendant receives the complaint on March 1. On April 5, the defendant's attorney learns through informal investigation that the plaintiff is a citizen of a different state than the defendant and that the plaintiff's medical bills alone exceed $80,000. On June 10, the plaintiff files an amended complaint that for the first time explicitly states the plaintiff's citizenship and claims $90,000 in damages. The defendant files a notice of removal on June 30 — twenty days after the amended complaint. The governing forum is initially state court; the issue is whether removal is timely under the MBE baseline. Was the removal timely?
Answer A: No, because the 30-day clock ran from April 5, when the defendant's attorney actually knew the citizenship and damages facts that established removability, and removal on June 30 was too late
Answer B: No, because the 30-day clock ran from March 1 when the defendant received the original complaint, regardless of whether removability was apparent
Answer C: Yes, because the amended complaint on June 10 was the paper from which removability first became apparent, and the notice of removal filed June 30 was within 30 days of that paper
Answer D: Yes, because the one-year outside limit under § 1446(c)(1) applies, and the action is less than one year old, making the June 30 removal timely regardless of when the 30-day window opened
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under § 1446(b)(1), if the initial pleading does not show that the case is removable, the 30-day clock does not start at that time. Under § 1446(b)(3), the clock then begins when the defendant receives "an amended pleading, motion, order or other paper" from which removability first becomes apparent. The June 10 amended complaint was the first document to affirmatively allege the parties' diverse citizenship and a damages amount exceeding $75,000. The defendant filed the notice of removal June 30 — twenty days after the amended complaint — well within the 30-day window. Removal was timely.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Section 1446(b)(3) starts the clock from "receipt of a copy of an amended pleading, motion, order or other paper from which it may first be ascertained that the case is one which is or has become removable." The defendant's attorney's informal investigation and extrajudicial knowledge do not constitute a "paper" under § 1446(b). The clock runs from the paper — here, the June 10 amended complaint — not from the date of informal knowledge. B: Section 1446(b)(1) starts the 30-day clock from receipt of the initial pleading only if the pleading shows that the case is removable. Here, the original complaint alleged no citizenship, no damage amount, and stated only state-law claims — it did not show the case was removable. When the initial pleading does not establish removability, the clock runs instead from receipt of the paper that first discloses removability, which here is the June 10 amended complaint. D: The one-year outside limit is a maximum cap on late removal, not a substitute for the 30-day window. A defendant who missed the 30-day window after receiving a paper showing removability cannot rely on the one-year cap to revive removal rights. The one-year limit operates in parallel with the 30-day window — both must be satisfied, and the 30-day window is the operative limit here. Because the June 30 filing was timely under § 1446(b)(3), the one-year cap is beside the point; but if the 30-day window had closed earlier, the one-year cap would not save the removal.
lb_rule_reference: 28 U.S.C. § 1446(b) — notice of removal must be filed within 30 days after receipt of the initial pleading if removability is apparent, or within 30 days after receipt of the paper from which removability first becomes apparent if the initial pleading did not show removability.
