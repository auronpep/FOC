Question: A daycare van negligently struck a child while backing out of a driveway. The child's parent was at work five miles away and did not know about the accident until an hour later. That evening, the parent watched security-camera footage of the accident and suffered severe emotional distress with physical symptoms. The parent sues the van company for negligent infliction of emotional distress as a bystander. Is the parent likely to recover on a bystander negligent infliction of emotional distress claim?
Answer A: Yes, because the parent is a close relative and suffered physical symptoms
Answer B: Yes, because video footage is legally identical to being present at the accident
Answer C: No, because the parent did not contemporaneously perceive the injury-causing event
Answer D: No, because parents can never recover for emotional distress unless they are physically hit
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Although the parent is a close relative and suffered symptoms, the parent learned of the accident later and watched footage afterward. That falls outside the usual bystander NIED gate.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Bystander NIED generally also requires contemporaneous perception of the injury-causing event. Learning later and watching a recording usually is not enough. B: The recognized bystander category is built around contemporaneous sensory perception of the event, not later review of a recording. D: Some bystander NIED claims by close relatives are recognized without impact. This claim fails because perception was not contemporaneous.
lb_rule_reference: Bystander NIED generally requires contemporaneous perception of the injury-causing event; learning of the event later or watching a recording usually falls outside the recognized category.
