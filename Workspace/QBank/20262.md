Question: A store contracted with a training company to provide safety training “to all current delivery employees of the store.” The contract required the training company to issue certificates directly to those employees after the course. Deborah was a current delivery employee. The training company refused to train her, saying she was not named in the contract. May Deborah enforce the training promise?
Answer A: Yes, because the contract required direct performance to a defined class that included Deborah.
Answer B: No, because only the store paid consideration to the training company.
Answer C: No, because Deborah was not individually named in the contract.
Answer D: Yes, but only if the store assigns its rights to Deborah.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Deborah may enforce as an intended class beneficiary. The contract identifies a definite class, current delivery employees, and requires direct performance to class members. Individual naming is unnecessary.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because consideration from the beneficiary is not required. C: C is wrong because class identification can be enough. D: D is wrong because Deborah's enforcement route is third-party beneficiary status, not assignment.
lb_rule_reference: Common law third-party beneficiary — class beneficiary and direct performance
