Question: A federal grand jury subpoenas recordings of Oval Office conversations relevant to a criminal prosecution of former campaign officials. The President asserts executive privilege, describing a generalized need for candid advice. The prosecutor shows that the recordings contain specific evidence unavailable elsewhere and does not seek military or diplomatic secrets. How should a court most likely analyze the privilege claim?
Answer A: The privilege automatically defeats the subpoena because presidential communications are absolutely confidential
Answer B: The privilege claim likely yields to the subpoena because the prosecutor shows specific need for evidence in a criminal proceeding and the claim is generalized
Answer C: The subpoena is unenforceable because courts may never review disputes involving the President
Answer D: The privilege does not exist at all because the Constitution does not expressly mention it
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The President has a legitimate confidentiality interest, but it is not absolute. Specific, demonstrated need for evidence in a criminal case can overcome a generalized privilege claim when no military or diplomatic secret is at stake.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Executive privilege is qualified. A specific need for evidence in a criminal proceeding can outweigh a generalized confidentiality interest. C: Courts can adjudicate executive-privilege disputes and enforce criminal process subject to constitutional limits. D: Courts recognize a qualified executive privilege rooted in separation of powers and the need for candid presidential advice.
lb_rule_reference: Executive privilege for presidential communications is qualified; a demonstrated need for evidence in a criminal proceeding can overcome a generalized confidentiality claim.
