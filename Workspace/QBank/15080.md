Question: A mother executed a will devising vacant land to her son. The mother showed the will to her son. Thereafter, the son purported to convey the land to a friend by a warranty deed that contained no exceptions. The friend paid value for the land and promptly recorded the deed without having first conducted any title search. The friend never took possession of the land. The mother later died, and the will devising the land to her son was duly admitted to probate. Thereafter, the friend conducted a title search for the land and asked the son for a new deed. The son refused, because the value of the land had doubled, but he offered to refund the purchase price to the friend. The friend has sued to quiet title to the land. Is the friend likely to prevail?
Answer A: A: No, because the friend failed to conduct a title search before purchasing the land.
Answer B: B: No, because the son had no interest in the land at the time of conveyance.
Answer C: C: Yes, because of the doctrine of estoppel by deed.
Answer D: D: Yes, because the deed was recorded.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: C is correct. The son purported to convey the land via warranty deed with no exceptions, but did not yet have valid title. The friend, who paid value, with no notice or evidence that the mother's interest was recorded, was a bona fide purchaser with no obligation to do a title search. As such, estoppel by deed caused the son's prior conveyance to become effective when he received valid title.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A is incorrect. The friend had the option to do a title search but was not required to. And there is no evidence that the mother did record, meaning the friend's search may have been futile anyway. B is incorrect. Even though the son had no interest in the land at the time of conveyance, estoppel by deed allows for the conveyance to the friend to go into effect at the time the son did receive valid title. D is incorrect. Recording has no effect on the title in this case because estoppel by deed applies.
