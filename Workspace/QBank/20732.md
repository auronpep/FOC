Outline: Presumptions and inferences
Question: Jonah is charged in federal court with stealing a silver cup from a merchant's stall. The prosecutor proves that Jonah was found the next morning trying to sell the cup and that Jonah gave no explanation for how he got it. The prosecutor asks the judge to instruct the jury: “If you find that Jonah possessed the cup shortly after it was stolen and has no credible explanation, you may infer that he stole it, but you are not required to do so.” Jonah objects. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection, because any inference about guilt from possession is improper character evidence.
Answer B: Sustain the objection, because the instruction would shift the burden of persuasion to Jonah.
Answer C: Overrule the objection, because the instruction is permissive and leaves the prosecution with the burden of proof.
Answer D: Overrule the objection only if the judge first finds by a preponderance of the evidence that Jonah stole the cup.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: A permissive inference may be allowed when the jury remains free to accept or reject the inference and the prosecution keeps the burden of proof. The proposed instruction uses may-language and does not require Jonah to prove innocence.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because the inference is based on possession of the stolen item soon after the theft, not on character. B: B is wrong because a permissive inference does not shift the burden of persuasion. D: D is wrong because the judge does not decide the ultimate theft fact before the jury may consider the inference.
lb_rule_reference: Permissive inference / criminal burden
