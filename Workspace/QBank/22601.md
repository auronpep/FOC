


BARMATRIX Q#: 22601
Outline_code: 44040203
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Constitutional Law
Subtopic: Individual Rights
Outline: Procedural due process
Question: A state university hired new professors on a three- year probationary basis. During the probationary period, the professor could be fired for any cause. When the three- year period was over, the tenured faculty would vote on whether the professor would be hired on as a tenured member. If the professor was granted tenure, he or she was guaranteed lifetime employment. Historically, professors fired during the probationary period were given notice and hearing before a firing decision was made. A new dean was put in charge of the school. In an attempt to give professors more time in class and less time in meetings, the new dean said notice and hearing would no longer be given when firing probationary employees. A probationary employee was fired without any notice or hearing. The fired employee sued the university, arguing that the firing was unconstitutional. Is the employee's firing constitutional?
Answer A: A: Yes, because the employee was a probationary employee.
Answer B: B: Yes, because state employment matters are reserved to the states under the Tenth Amendment.
Answer C: C: No, because the university's policy for giving notice and hearing changed without sufficient due process.
Answer D: D: No, because the employee had a property interest in her employment.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Because a probationary employee has no expectation of continued employment, he or she has no due process rights regarding the loss of that employment. Consequently, because the employee was still in the probationary period, the university did not have to give her notice and a hearing before firing her. B is incorrect because the Tenth Amendment does not mean the university could have fired the employee without sufficient due process if that employee had a property right in her continued employment. Because she was still on probation, the employee did not have any due process rights in her continued employment, making any Tenth Amendment defense unnecessary. C and D are incorrect because the employee was a probationary employee with no property right in her employment who could be fired at any time without notice and hearing.
