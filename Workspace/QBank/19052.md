Question: Esther signed a contract with John to restore an old meeting hall. The first paragraph stated that “the Miriam Trust owns the hall.” The customer line listed “Esther, trustee.” The signature line read “Esther,” with no further title. After nonpayment, John sued Esther personally. Esther offered emails and trust documents showing that John agreed to look only to the trust for payment. What is Esther's best Content/Meaning argument?
Answer A: The Statute of Frauds bars all restoration contracts not signed by every trust beneficiary.
Answer B: The merger clause prevents the court from considering any evidence about capacity.
Answer C: The writing is ambiguous about whether Esther signed personally or as trustee, so explanatory evidence may be considered to determine who is bound.
Answer D: The word “trustee” automatically eliminates personal liability in every contract Esther signs.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that recitals, customer lines, and signature blocks can create ambiguity about party status and signing capacity. The trigger facts are the trust recital, “Esther, trustee” customer line, bare signature, and offered explanatory documents. The decisive lock is term status: the evidence is offered to explain who contracted, not to add a new payment term.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because the issue is not Statute of Frauds writing sufficiency. B: B is wrong because integration does not bar evidence explaining ambiguous party capacity. D: D is wrong because trustee status is relevant but not automatically dispositive in every signing configuration.
lb_rule_reference: Common-law signature capacity / recital ambiguity
