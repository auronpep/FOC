Outline: Authentication and Identification
Question: In a federal drug trial, the government offers a packet of white powder as the substance seized from Joshua. The packet is a fungible substance with no distinctive features. The officer who seized it says he sealed it in an evidence bag. The lab analyst testifies that when she received the bag, the seal was broken, the label was partly missing, and she cannot tell who handled it before delivery. Joshua objects to authentication. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection because the evidence does not sufficiently support a finding that the tested powder is the same powder seized from Joshua.
Answer B: Overrule the objection because all chain-of-custody problems go only to weight.
Answer C: Overrule the objection because the analyst's test results automatically authenticate the source of the powder.
Answer D: Sustain the objection only if Joshua proves someone actually substituted a different powder.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: For fungible evidence, chain of custody may be essential to source identity. A broken seal, missing label, and unknown handling can leave no sufficient basis for a reasonable finding that the tested powder is the seized powder.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because serious chain failures in fungible evidence can defeat authentication. C: C is wrong because testing the powder does not establish that it is the powder seized from Joshua. D: D is wrong because the proponent must provide sufficient authentication; Joshua need not prove actual substitution.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 901(a)
lb_student_diagnostic: Student overapplies 'chain gaps go to weight' to fungible evidence with a serious identity break.
