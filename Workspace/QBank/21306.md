Outline: Rape
Question: A defendant is convicted under a state statute of the aggravated rape of an eight-year-old child. The crime caused severe physical and psychological injury to the victim. The jury, applying the state's capital sentencing statute and considering all aggravating and mitigating circumstances, sentences the defendant to death. The aggravating circumstances found by the jury include the extreme youth of the victim, the severity of the harm, and the defendant's prior sex offense conviction. No death resulted from the crime. The defendant challenges the death sentence as cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment. Is the defendant's challenge correct?
Answer A: No, because the jury individually assessed all aggravating and mitigating circumstances, which satisfies the Eighth Amendment's individualized sentencing requirement
Answer B: No, because the severity of the harm and the victim's age justify the death penalty on proportionality grounds, and courts must defer to legislative judgments about which crimes warrant death
Answer C: Yes, because Kennedy v. Louisiana categorically bars the death penalty for non-homicide crimes against individual persons, even where the crime is the rape of a child and causes severe harm
Answer D: Yes, but only because the prior sex offense conviction is an aggravating factor rather than a separate element, which violates Apprendi and requires the sentence to be vacated
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Kennedy v. Louisiana (2008) held that the Eighth Amendment categorically prohibits imposing the death penalty for the rape of a child where the victim did not die and death was not intended. The Court found a national consensus against such sentences (only six states authorized it) and independently concluded that death is disproportionate for non-homicide offenses against individuals. The Court distinguished crimes against individuals from crimes against the state (e.g., treason, espionage) for which death may still be constitutional. The categorical bar applies regardless of the severity of the harm to the victim, the age of the victim, or the individualized quality of the sentencing proceeding.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Kennedy v. Louisiana (2008) imposed a categorical rule: the Eighth Amendment bars the death penalty for crimes against individual persons where no death occurs, regardless of how egregious the crime or how meticulously the sentencing proceeding was conducted. Individualized sentencing is necessary but not sufficient—the category of crime must itself be death-eligible. Non-homicide crimes against persons fall outside that category. B: The Supreme Court in Kennedy v. Louisiana applied its own proportionality analysis rather than deferring to legislative determinations. The Court conducted a national consensus review—examining state statutes, jury behavior, and evolving standards of decency—and concluded that a national consensus against death for non-homicide crimes against individuals exists. The Court then exercised independent judgment that imposing death for child rape is disproportionate. Legislative authorization does not insulate a punishment from Eighth Amendment review. D: The correct basis for vacating the death sentence is the Kennedy v. Louisiana categorical bar, not an Apprendi violation. The prior conviction exception under Apprendi actually removes prior convictions from the jury-finding requirement. Moreover, at sentencing in a capital case the jury is making the ultimate sentencing determination, so Apprendi concerns are differently structured. The dispositive issue is the Kennedy categorical prohibition on death for non-homicide crimes against persons.
lb_rule_reference: Under Kennedy v. Louisiana (2008), the Eighth Amendment categorically bars the death penalty for non-homicide crimes against individual persons, including child rape; the death penalty is also barred for intellectually disabled defendants (Atkins) and juvenile offenders (Roper); individualized sentencing is required in all capital cases
