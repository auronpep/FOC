Question: A manufacturer moves for summary judgment in a products case. The record contains an email from an engineer warning that a part "may fail under ordinary household pressure," plus later testing that supports the warning. The manufacturer argues the same email could also mean only rare pressure spikes. At summary judgment, how should the court treat the competing inference from the email?
Answer A: Draw reasonable inferences from the record in favor of the nonmoving plaintiff
Answer B: Draw all inferences for the manufacturer because it filed the motion
Answer C: Ignore the email because documents cannot be considered on summary judgment
Answer D: Weigh both interpretations and select the more persuasive one
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: If the warning and testing support a reasonable inference of ordinary-use failure, that inference belongs to the nonmovant at Rule 56.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: The movant does not get favorable inferences merely by filing a Rule 56 motion. C: Documents can be considered if they are part of the Rule 56 record and can be presented in admissible form. D: The judge may not resolve a reasonable competing inference against the nonmovant at summary judgment.
lb_rule_reference: On summary judgment, the court views the evidence and reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party.
