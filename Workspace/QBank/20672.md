Subtopic: Inchoate Crimes
Outline: Conspiracy
Question: Nina managed a warehouse. Her employee, Torres, told her he planned to use one of the loading docks on Saturday to receive stolen electronics that he would resell. Nina said, "Do whatever you want — I don't care what happens in that building on weekends." On Saturday, Torres received the stolen goods at the dock. Nina was not present and received no money from the transaction. The prosecution charges Nina with conspiracy to receive stolen property. Is Nina guilty of conspiracy to receive stolen property?
Answer A: Yes, because Nina knew about Torres's plan and did nothing to prevent it
Answer B: Yes, because Nina provided the means (the loading dock) for the crime to occur
Answer C: No, because Nina did not specifically intend to achieve the objective of receiving stolen property
Answer D: No, because Nina was not present when the stolen goods were received
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Conspiracy at common law requires (1) an agreement between two or more persons and (2) specific intent to achieve the unlawful objective. Nina's statement was an expression of indifference, not an agreement. She did not intend to further Torres's scheme; she simply did not care enough to stop it. Specific intent means the defendant must consciously desire that the criminal objective be accomplished. Indifference, even negligent or reckless indifference, does not satisfy the specific-intent requirement of conspiracy.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Conspiracy requires specific intent — the intent to achieve the unlawful objective, not merely knowledge of it. A person who knows about a conspiracy and does nothing has not agreed to further it. Nina's passive knowledge and failure to prevent is not an agreement with specific intent to accomplish the criminal goal. B: Conspiracy requires a meeting of the minds — an agreement — plus specific intent to achieve the criminal end. Nina did not agree to Torres's plan; she expressed indifference. Making a facility available does not establish conspiratorial intent. This might support an aiding and abetting theory, but it falls short of conspiracy because there is no agreement with the requisite intent. D: Conspiracy does not require presence at the time the underlying crime is committed. Co-conspirators can be liable without being physically present. The real deficiency is the absence of a specific-intent agreement, not the absence of physical presence.
lb_rule_reference: Conspiracy requires specific intent: (1) intent to agree, and (2) intent to achieve the unlawful objective; mere knowledge of another's criminal plan is insufficient
