


BARMATRIX Q#: 22658
Outline_code: 43020201
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Constitutional Law
Subtopic: Judicial Review
Outline: Constitutional limitations and justiciability
Question: A company operated a website that provided economic information about individuals to anyone who paid a small fee. A man sued the company in federal court, alleging that the company published false information about him on the website. Although the man had not suffered any concrete harm, the man claimed that the company violated the federal Fair Credit Reporting Act by willfully publishing the false information about him. The company removed the man's information from its website and moved to dismiss the man's claim, arguing that he did not have standing to sue. How should the court rule?
Answer A: A: Deny the motion, because the man is asserting a violation of federal law.
Answer B: B: Deny the motion, because the man could be harmed by the false information in the future.
Answer C: C: Grant the motion, because the company removed the man's information from its website.
Answer D: D: Grant the motion, because the man didn't suffer any concrete harm.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: A court will not hear an action unless the person bringing the action has standing. In order to have standing, that person must show that he or she has suffered a concrete and personal injury in fact. Importantly, a plaintiff does not gain standing simply by alleging that the defendant has violated a federal act. Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins, 136 S. Ct. 1540 (2016). Here, the man would not have standing because he didn't suffer any concrete harm. A is incorrect because a person does not gain standing simply by asserting a violation of federal law. B is incorrect because the fact that the man might be harmed in the future is not enough to give the man standing to sue now. C is incorrect because whether the information was removed from the website would not affect the issue of standing.
