Question: A recorded easement allowed Rachel to use “the existing gravel lane along the east boundary of Paul's land” to reach Rachel's parcel. Years later, Paul built a new lane along the west boundary of his land. The new lane was equally convenient and safe for Rachel, and Paul wanted to close the east-boundary lane. Rachel refused to consent. Under the traditional common-law rule, which is the best result?
Answer A: Paul may relocate the easement unilaterally because the new lane is equally convenient.
Answer B: Paul may not relocate the fixed easement unilaterally without Rachel's consent or applicable legal authority.
Answer C: Paul may relocate the easement unilaterally because the servient owner always controls the route.
Answer D: Rachel's only remedy is damages because easement location disputes cannot support injunctive relief.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Under the traditional common-law rule, once an easement location is fixed, the servient owner generally may not unilaterally relocate it without the easement holder's consent or applicable statutory or jurisdictional authority. The recorded easement fixed the route along the east boundary, and Rachel did not consent.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because equal convenience does not supply unilateral relocation power under the traditional rule. C: C is wrong because servient ownership does not include unlimited control over a fixed easement route. D: D is wrong because injunctive relief may be available to protect easement rights.
lb_rule_reference: Fixed easement location / traditional common-law relocation rule
