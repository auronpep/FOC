Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: An officer stops Erin for a broken taillight. The officer checks license and registration, writes a warning, and returns Erin's documents. Without reasonable suspicion of drugs and without consent, the officer orders Erin to wait ten more minutes for a drug dog. The dog alerts, and officers find narcotics. Should the narcotics be suppressed?
Answer A: Yes, because the officer prolonged the completed traffic stop for an unrelated dog sniff without reasonable suspicion
Answer B: No, because any lawful traffic stop permits a dog sniff for as long as police want
Answer C: No, because a dog sniff is never a search
Answer D: Yes, because police may never use drug dogs during traffic stops
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Once the traffic mission ended, further detention for a dog sniff required independent reasonable suspicion or consent. Neither existed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: The validity of the initial stop does not authorize unrelated prolongation after the mission is complete. C: The issue is the seizure's duration, not whether the sniff itself is a search. D: A dog sniff may occur if it does not prolong the stop or is supported by reasonable suspicion. This one unlawfully prolonged the stop.
lb_rule_reference: Police may conduct ordinary traffic-stop tasks, but extending the stop to conduct a dog sniff requires reasonable suspicion or consent.
