Outline: Authentication and Identification
Question: In a federal extortion trial, the prosecutor offers a voicemail recording. A coworker of Eli testifies that the voice on the voicemail is Eli's. The prosecutor also seeks to prove the exact words spoken in the voicemail by playing the recording. Eli objects that the coworker may not identify the voice because the recording itself must speak for itself. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection because a witness may never identify a voice heard through a recording.
Answer B: Sustain the objection because the original-writing rule bars all witness testimony about recordings.
Answer C: Overrule the objection; the coworker may identify the voice if he has a proper basis, and the recording can be used for its contents if otherwise admissible.
Answer D: Overrule the objection only if the coworker is a voice-analysis expert.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: A witness familiar with a person's voice may identify that voice even if it is heard through a recording. That identification is separate from using the recording to prove the words spoken. If the recording itself is properly admitted, it can prove its contents.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because voice identification can be based on a voice heard through mechanical or electronic transmission or recording. B: B is wrong because the original-writing rule does not bar a properly based voice-identification opinion. D: D is wrong because lay voice identification can be sufficient.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 901; FRE 1002
lb_student_diagnostic: Split-purpose failure: student merges voice authentication with content proof.
