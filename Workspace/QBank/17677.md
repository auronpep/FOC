Question: Abraham owned Orchard Lot, which benefited from a recorded easement over Sarah's private lane “for access to Orchard Lot.” Abraham later divided Orchard Lot into 30 cabin lots and sold them to vacation renters. Hundreds of cars now use Sarah's lane each weekend, causing congestion and dust far beyond the prior orchard access. All cabin lots are physically within the original Orchard Lot. What is Sarah's strongest argument?
Answer A: Sarah has no claim because every parcel within the original dominant estate may use the easement without limit after subdivision.
Answer B: Sarah may seek to limit or enjoin the intensified use if the subdivision unreasonably increases the burden beyond the easement's contemplated scope.
Answer C: The easement automatically terminated when Abraham subdivided Orchard Lot.
Answer D: Sarah must allow the use unless she proves that each renter personally signed the original easement deed.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: An appurtenant easement may generally be apportioned among subdivided portions of the dominant estate, but the use cannot unreasonably increase the burden on the servient estate or exceed the grant's contemplated scope. Although the cabin lots remain within the original dominant estate, the heavy weekend traffic creates a serious overburden argument.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because subdivision does not create unlimited easement use. B is correct for the reasons above. C: C is wrong because subdivision alone does not automatically terminate an appurtenant easement. D: D is wrong because successors within the dominant estate need not personally sign the original deed to benefit from an appurtenant easement.
lb_rule_reference: Apportionment after subdivision / unreasonable burden
lb_student_diagnostic: The trap is treating dominant-estate subdivision as either unlimited apportionment or automatic termination, instead of asking whether the burden materially increased.
