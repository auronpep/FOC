


BARMATRIX Q#: 16178
Outline_code: 75070200
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Searches and Seizures
Question: Without a warrant, police officers searched the garbage cans in the alley behind a man's house and discovered chemicals used to make methamphetamine, as well as cooking utensils and containers with the man's fingerprints on them. The alley was a public thoroughfare maintained by the city, and the garbage was picked up once a week by a private sanitation company. The items were found inside the garbage cans in plastic bags that had been tied closed and further secured with tape. The man was charged in federal court with the manufacture of methamphetamine. Did the search of the garbage cans violate the Fourth Amendment?
Answer A: A: No, because the man had no reasonable expectation of privacy in garbage left in the alley.
Answer B: B: No, because the probative value of the evidence outweighs the man's modest privacy claims in his garbage.
Answer C: C: Yes, because the alley was within the curtilage of the man's home and entry without a warrant was unconstitutional.
Answer D: D: Yes, because there is a reasonable expectation of privacy in one's secured garbage containers.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: (A)is the best response, because garbage left in a public thoroughfare is readily accessible to members of the public, so even if the man subjectively expected the contents of the garbage bags to be private, his expectation was not “objectively reasonable.” A search and seizure cannot implicate a defendant's Fourth Amendment rights unless the defendant had a “reasonable expectation of privacy” in the place or item being subjected to the search or seizure. Furthermore, the expectation of privacy that's required is not merely one that is subjective on the part of the defendant (i.e., one he actually and honestly holds); the defendant's expectation must be one that “society is prepared to accept … as objectively reasonable.” California v. Greenwood, 486 U.S. 35 (1988). Therefore, whenever a person is found to have “abandoned” property he once owned, he is deemed to no longer have an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy as to it. This reasoning applies to trash left for collection: when a person leaves his garbage for collection on a public street (even if it's sealed in bags, and even if it's left directly adjacent to the person's dwelling), the person is deemed no longer to have the required objectively-reasonable expectation that the garbage will remain private. The Court so held in Greenwood, supra. In fact, the setup of this question is closely modeled on the facts of Greenwood: there, the Ds left plastic garbage bags filled with their trash in front of their house, and the police intercepted the bags and searched them without a warrant (and perhaps without probable cause). The Court held that the Ds' Fourth Amendment rights were not even implicated (let alone violated) by the search and seizure: there could be no objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in such abandoned trash, because “[i]t is common knowledge that plastic garbage bags left on or at the side of a public street are readily accessible to animals, children, scavengers, snoops, and other members of the public.” Since we're told that the alley here was “a public thoroughfare maintained by the city,” Greenwood is precisely on point; therefore, the man could not have had an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the garbage he left there. (B)is not the best response, because the success of a Fourth Amendment claim does not depend on the probative value of the evidence seized. This explanation is basically gibberish. It seems to assert that the degree to which the evidence is probative is to be weighed against the strength of the defendant's privacy claims. But the Fourth Amendment isn't even triggered — and thus cannot possibly be violated, no matter how weak the evidence's probative value — unless the defendant had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the place or item being searched. Since this choice ignores the need for the defendant to have a reasonable expectation of privacy in the trash, it's not the best response. (C)is not the best response, because the alley was not within the curtilage of the man's home, and even if it had been, he probably would not have had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the trash. The Fourth Amendment protects an individual's “house” (as well as an individual's “person … papers and effects”). But a person's “house” is not deemed to include all of the person's residential real estate — merely the actual house and what's called the “curtilage.” The curtilage is “the area immediately surrounding and associated with the home.” Florida v. Jardines, 133 S.Ct. 1409 (2013). (For instance, on a farm, the curtilage will typically include the farmhouse and those immediately-surrounding structures used mainly for primarily-family purposes like a garage, but not open fields owned by the same family.) In any event, the curtilage never includes land that's not owned by the homeowner, such as public streets. Therefore, this choice makes a misstatement of law when it asserts that “the alley was within the curtilage of the man's home.” Furthermore, even if the trash bags had been left within the curtilage — such as inside the man's rear fence — if he authorized the private sanitation company to retrieve the garbage bags, that fact alone would likely have been enough to cause the court to say that he had surrendered his reasonable expectation of privacy in the bags. (D)is not the best response, because the man did not in fact have a reasonable expectation of privacy in his garbage containers, even if they were “secured.” As we discuss in Choice (A), a person's Fourth Amendment rights can be violated only if the person had an “objectively reasonable expectation of privacy” in the item or place to be searched or seized. And on the very issue in this case — whether a person has such an expectation as to bagged trash he puts on the street — the Supreme Court ruled in Greenwood that such an expectation of privacy is not “objectively reasonable,” since “plastic garbage bags left on or at the side of a public street are readily accessible to animals, children, scavengers, snoops, and other members of the public.” The fact that, as we're told, the plastic bags in the garbage cans “had been tied closed and further secured with tape” makes no difference — once the garbage cans were on the street, any member of the public standing on the street could have easily cut through the tape and untied the bags, giving that person access to the bags' contents. In Greenwood, the Supreme Court did not even discuss whether the garbage bags put in the street there were tied or sealed, indicating that this issue would not have made a difference to the Court's analysis.
