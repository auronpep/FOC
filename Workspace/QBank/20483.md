MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Inchoate Crimes
Outline: Conspiracy
Question: Omar approached Sofia and asked her to burn down a rival's warehouse for $10,000. Sofia said "I'll do it — count me in." They discussed the plan in detail and arranged to meet the following week to obtain the necessary materials. Omar is charged with both (1) solicitation to commit arson and (2) conspiracy to commit arson arising from the same conversation. Under common law, may Omar be convicted of both solicitation and conspiracy to commit arson?
Answer A: Yes, because solicitation and conspiracy are separate offenses that both arose from Omar's conduct
Answer B: Yes, because conspiracy does not merge with solicitation under common law
Answer C: No, because solicitation merges into the conspiracy once Sofia agreed
Answer D: No, because Omar withdrew from the conspiracy before any overt act was committed
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: At common law, once the solicited person agrees to commit the crime, the solicitation merges into the conspiracy that has now been formed. The agreement transforms the inchoate solicitation into an inchoate conspiracy, and the solicitation offense is subsumed. Omar may be convicted of conspiracy to commit arson. He may not additionally be convicted of solicitation for the same communication. The merger doctrine prevents double punishment for what is, in essence, the same criminal event.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Although solicitation and conspiracy have separate elements, solicitation merges into conspiracy once the solicited party agrees to commit the crime. The two cannot be stacked into separate convictions when the solicitation is the same communication that formed the conspiracy. B: The non-merger rule for conspiracy means conspiracy does not merge into the completed target offense. It says nothing about solicitation merging into conspiracy. When solicitation ripens into an agreement, the solicitation merges upward into the conspiracy. These are two different merger questions. D: There is nothing in the facts to suggest Omar withdrew. The plan was made, an arrangement to meet was set, and the question is about dual conviction, not withdrawal. This answer fabricates a defense that the facts do not support.
lb_rule_reference: When the person solicited agrees to commit the crime, the solicitation merges into the conspiracy; the defendant cannot be convicted of both solicitation and conspiracy arising from the same agreement
