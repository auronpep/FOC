Subtopic: Intentional Torts
Outline: Privileges
Question: During a line dispute, a shopper raised an open hand and moved as if to shove another shopper lightly. The threatened shopper immediately pulled a knife and stabbed the first shopper in the arm. A reasonable person would have expected, at most, a minor shove. The first shopper sues for battery. Can the defendant rely on self-defense?
Answer A: No, because the force used was disproportionate to the apparent minor nondeadly threat
Answer B: Yes, because any threatened contact permits any defensive force
Answer C: Yes, but only if the shove actually landed first
Answer D: No, because self-defense is unavailable in civil battery cases
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Self-defense requires reasonable and proportionate force. Deadly or serious force is not privileged for a minor shove.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: The force must be proportionate to the apparent threat. C: Self-defense can respond to imminent apparent contact before it lands, but only with reasonable force. D: Self-defense can privilege what would otherwise be battery when its elements are met.
lb_rule_reference: Self-defense permits reasonable and proportionate force against imminent contact; deadly force is not privileged against a minor nondeadly threat.
