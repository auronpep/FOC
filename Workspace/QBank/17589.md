Question: Nguyen was convicted of aggravated assault. After conviction, his attorney discovered that the prosecution had possessed, but never disclosed, a statement by a bystander who said she saw two people fighting near the scene but could not identify which person struck the other first. The eyewitness testimony at trial from the victim and one other bystander was consistent, and the jury deliberated for less than two hours. Nguyen argues that suppression of the undisclosed statement violated *Brady v. Maryland* and entitles him to a new trial. Under *Brady*, was the undisclosed bystander statement material such that its suppression requires reversal?
Answer A: Yes, because any undisclosed exculpatory evidence automatically entitles the defendant to a new trial
Answer B: Yes, because the statement could have been used to create reasonable doubt
Answer C: Probably not, because the undisclosed statement is likely not material given the consistent trial testimony and brief deliberation suggesting strong evidence of guilt
Answer D: No, because *Brady* applies only to physical evidence, not witness statements
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Brady v. Maryland* (1963) and its progeny, particularly *United States v. Bagley* (1985) and *Strickler v. Greene* (1999), suppressed evidence is material only if there is a reasonable probability that disclosure would have produced a different result. Here, the bystander merely said she could not identify who struck first — a statement that impeaches no specific witness and does not directly exculpate Nguyen. With consistent eyewitness testimony and a short deliberation, the probability that this statement would have changed the outcome is low. The *Brady* claim likely fails on materiality.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Brady requires more than mere non-disclosure; the withheld evidence must be material. *United States v. Bagley* (1985) clarified that materiality means a reasonable probability of a different outcome, not that any suppressed evidence automatically warrants reversal. B: The standard is not whether the evidence "could have" created doubt but whether there is a "reasonable probability" — defined as a probability sufficient to undermine confidence in the outcome — that the result would have differed. A marginally relevant statement from a non-identifying witness among consistent trial testimony likely does not meet this threshold. D: Brady* applies to all exculpatory evidence in the prosecution's possession, including witness statements, police reports, and investigative notes. The type of evidence does not determine applicability; only the exculpatory nature and materiality matter.
lb_rule_reference: Under *Brady v. Maryland*, the prosecution must disclose exculpatory evidence; a Brady violation occurs only when the suppressed evidence is "material," meaning there is a reasonable probability that, had the evidence been disclosed, the result of the proceeding would have been different
