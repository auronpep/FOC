Question: A warrant authorized police to search "all homes owned by Lee and seize all evidence of any crime." The warrant did not list an address, unit, item category, offense, or limiting description. Officers executed it at Lee's duplex and seized financial records. Lee moves to suppress. Can officers rely on the good-faith exception?
Answer A: Yes, because a judge signed the warrant
Answer B: Yes, because financial records are the type of evidence officers expected to find
Answer C: No, because the warrant was so facially deficient that objective reliance was unreasonable
Answer D: No, because financial records can never be seized with a warrant
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: A warrant authorizing searches of all homes and all evidence of any crime lacks particularity. The good-faith exception does not save reliance on that facial defect.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The warrant is facially deficient. A reasonable officer would know it lacks particularity as to place and items. B: Officer expectation cannot fix a warrant that fails to specify what may be searched and seized. D: Financial records can be seized under a valid particular warrant. The problem is this warrant's breadth.
lb_rule_reference: Officers cannot reasonably rely on a warrant that fails on its face to describe the place to be searched or items to be seized with sufficient particularity.
