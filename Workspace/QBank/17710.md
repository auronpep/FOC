Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Specific and General Jurisdiction
Question: Leonard, a food blogger based in the State of Tressin, posted a YouTube video harshly criticizing the food quality at Oasis Kitchen, a restaurant located and operating entirely in the State of Premia. Leonard made the video after visiting the Oasis Kitchen restaurant during a trip to Premia. In the video — addressed to his 400,000 subscribers nationwide — Leonard stated that Oasis Kitchen's owner, Vera, "committed health-code violations I personally witnessed" and that Vera "has a criminal record she's hiding from health inspectors." The criminal-record statement is false. Vera is domiciled in Premia, where Oasis Kitchen operates and where the reputational harm was concentrated. After the video was posted, Oasis Kitchen's reservations dropped 60% and several Premia vendors terminated their contracts. Vera sued Leonard for defamation in Premia federal court. Leonard moved to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction. Under the *Calder* effects test as clarified by *Walden v. Fiore*, does the Premia court have personal jurisdiction over Leonard?
Answer A: Yes, because Leonard's defamation of a Premia restaurant owner, knowing the primary harm would fall in Premia where Oasis Kitchen operates, satisfies the Calder effects test
Answer B: Yes, because Leonard visited Premia (to eat at the restaurant) and thus purposefully availed himself of Premia as the basis for his video review
Answer C: No, because Leonard directed the video at his nationwide audience — not specifically at Premia — and although the primary harm fell in Premia due to Vera's location there, Leonard did not expressly aim his conduct at the Premia forum under the Walden clarification of Calder
Answer D: No, because defamation claims against individual bloggers cannot support personal jurisdiction — only commercial defendants are subject to the Calder effects test
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Calder* requires express aiming at the forum, and *Walden v. Fiore* clarified that the focus is the defendant's relationship to the forum, not the plaintiff's. Leonard's video was directed at his nationwide subscriber base, not at Premia specifically. Leonard lives and operates in Tressin. The harm in Premia results from Vera being located there — it is a consequence of the plaintiff's forum connections, not of Leonard targeting Premia. Under *Walden*, this is insufficient. The Premia court lacks personal jurisdiction over Leonard.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: This answer reads *Calder* as written but ignores *Walden*'s clarification. *Walden* held that PJ cannot rest solely on the plaintiff's forum connections. The defendant's relationship to the forum is what matters. Leonard visited Premia once (when he ate at the restaurant) but is based in Tressin. The video was addressed to a nationwide audience, not to Premia specifically. Leonard did not aim the video at Premia — he aimed it at Vera, who happens to be in Premia. The fact that Vera's business suffered in Premia is a consequence of her forum location, not of Leonard targeting the forum. B: A brief tourist visit to a restaurant, used as the factual basis for a later video posted from the visitor's home state, is not the kind of deliberate commercial contact with the forum that satisfies purposeful availment. More importantly, the *Calder* effects test is the applicable framework for intentional-tort defamation claims. A single tourist visit does not transform into a forum contact for the purpose of subsequent media publication. The relevant analysis is whether the publication was aimed at the forum — not whether the author once visited. D: Calder v. Jones* itself involved individual journalists (the author of an article and the editor of a magazine). The effects test applies to individual defendants as well as entities. There is no categorical immunity for individual defendants in defamation cases. The test applies equally regardless of the defendant's commercial or non-commercial status.
lb_rule_reference: The *Calder* effects test for intentional torts requires: (1) an intentional act, (2) expressly aimed at the forum state, (3) with knowledge that the brunt of the injury would be felt primarily in the forum. *Walden v. Fiore* (2014) held that the defendant's relationship with the forum — not the plaintiff's — is the focus. A social media post that is universally accessible but targeted at the plaintiff personally does not automatically satisfy express aiming at the plaintiff's forum.
