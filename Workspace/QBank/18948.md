Question: A cafe customer moved another customer's bicycle two feet along a rack so she could unlock her own bicycle. The moved bicycle remained locked, visible, undamaged, and available for immediate use. The owner saw the move and was annoyed. The owner sues the customer for trespass to chattels. Is the owner likely to prevail in a trespass to chattels claim against the customer?
Answer A: Yes, because any intentional touching of another's chattel is actionable
Answer B: No, because the brief move caused no dispossession, impairment, or loss of use
Answer C: Yes, because the owner's annoyance is enough dignitary harm
Answer D: No, because the owner must prove the bicycle's full market value
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The bicycle stayed available, visible, locked, and undamaged. The owner's annoyance does not supply the required chattel interference.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Trespass to chattels requires dispossession, impairment, or loss of use. Harmless brief contact alone is not enough. C: Trespass to chattels protects possessory and use interests in personal property. Mere annoyance without impairment or loss of use is insufficient. D: Full market value is associated with conversion. The claim fails because there was no actionable chattel interference at all.
lb_rule_reference: Mere harmless contact with a chattel is not trespass to chattels unless it causes dispossession, impairment, or loss of use.
