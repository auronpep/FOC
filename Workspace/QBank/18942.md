Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Definition
Question: In a federal negligence trial, Isaac claims that Rebekah was driving too fast before hitting his wagon. Isaac offers testimony from a responding officer that there were no skid marks before the impact point. Rebekah objects that the absence of skid marks does not prove speed because she might have failed to brake for another reason. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Admit the testimony because the absence of skid marks has some tendency to make speed or failure to brake more probable, even if other explanations exist.
Answer B: Exclude the testimony because circumstantial evidence is relevant only when it eliminates every alternative explanation.
Answer C: Exclude the testimony because an officer may testify about skid marks only if qualified as an expert.
Answer D: Admit the testimony only if Isaac proves that Rebekah's brakes were working properly.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The absence of skid marks is circumstantial evidence that may support an inference about speed, braking, or driver conduct. Alternative explanations affect the force of the inference, not threshold relevance.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because relevance does not require eliminating all alternatives. C: C is wrong because the objection is stated as relevance; separate qualification limits do not make the fact irrelevant. D: D is wrong because full brake-function proof is not a prerequisite to relevance.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401 / circumstantial braking evidence
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests admissibility of circumstantial proof despite alternate explanations.
