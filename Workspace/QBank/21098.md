Question: A state statute denies enrollment in public schools to children who cannot establish lawful immigration status. An undocumented child who has lived in the United States since infancy is denied enrollment in the local public school solely because neither she nor her parents can establish lawful immigration status. Her parents brought her to the country when she was an infant, and she has lived here her entire life. The state argues the exclusion conserves educational resources for lawful residents and discourages illegal immigration. Under Equal Protection analysis, how should a court evaluate the state's exclusion of undocumented children from public education?
Answer A: The statute easily survives rational basis review because undocumented aliens are not a suspect class, and the state has a rational interest in reserving educational resources for lawful residents
Answer B: Strict scrutiny applies because undocumented aliens are a suspect classification and denying education to them violates the Equal Protection Clause
Answer C: The statute fails under Plyler's heightened rational basis analysis because the state's fiscal and deterrence interests are not substantial enough to justify imposing a lifelong educational disadvantage on children who had no role in their undocumented entry
Answer D: The statute is constitutional because the state may constitutionally distinguish between lawful and unlawful residents when allocating government services, and the federal government's plenary power over immigration confirms that states may reinforce federal immigration goals
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Plyler v. Doe applied a form of intermediate or heightened rational basis to strike down a state law denying public education to undocumented children. The Court emphasized: (1) undocumented children did not choose to violate immigration law; (2) education has substantial importance for future civic and economic participation; (3) the state's fiscal and deterrence rationales do not constitute a substantial state interest sufficient to justify the classification. The statute fails EP under Plyler's framework.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Plyler v. Doe rejected this reasoning. The Supreme Court applied a more demanding form of review, noting that the law targeted children who had no control over their immigration status, that education's importance to future civic participation is substantial, and that the state's fiscal savings rationale was not rationally related to any substantial state interest when weighed against the lifelong burden on innocent children. The state's deterrence and resource rationale did not survive scrutiny. B: The Supreme Court has not declared undocumented aliens a suspect classification. Plyler deliberately avoided declaring them suspect. The opinion applied a heightened rational basis analysis that focused on the particular circumstances of the children — their blamelessness, the importance of education, and the disproportionate harm — rather than on the suspect-class framework. D: Plyler specifically rejected this argument for the context of children and education. Even if the state's classification tracks federal immigration status, the Equal Protection Clause still applies to how the state distributes public benefits and services. Reinforcing federal immigration categories does not give states carte blanche to deny education to innocent children. The EP analysis under Plyler is independent of the federal/state immigration power structure.
lb_rule_reference: Under Plyler v. Doe, a state statute that denies free public education to undocumented alien children fails Equal Protection because: (1) undocumented children did not themselves choose to violate immigration law; (2) education is too important to be denied to children based on their parents' conduct; and (3) the classification is not rationally related to a substantial state interest.
