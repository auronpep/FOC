Outline: Personal knowledge
Question: In a federal slip-and-fall trial, Solomon testifies that he walked through the store ten minutes after Rachel fell and saw that the floor was wet. The plaintiff then asks Solomon, “The employees must have mopped the floor just before Rachel fell, correct?” Solomon admits that he did not see anyone mop and does not know how the floor became wet. The store objects. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Overrule the objection because Solomon personally saw the wet floor after the fall.
Answer B: Sustain the objection because Solomon lacks personal knowledge of who mopped the floor or when it was mopped.
Answer C: Overrule the objection because any inference about the wet floor is for the jury.
Answer D: Sustain the objection only if the store proves that no employee mopped that day.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Solomon may testify to what he personally observed—the floor was wet ten minutes after the fall. But the proposed answer that employees must have mopped just before the fall asks him to speculate about facts he did not perceive. The objection should be sustained as to that speculative conclusion.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because personal knowledge of the wet floor does not create personal knowledge of who mopped or when. C: C is wrong because the jury may draw reasonable inferences from admissible facts, but a witness may not present speculation as fact testimony. D: D is wrong because the opponent need not disprove the speculative theory before objecting.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 602 / speculation versus perceived fact
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests the line between perceived fact testimony and speculative causal testimony.
