Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: Vincent was indicted for fraud. His cellmate, unknown to Vincent, had agreed to report any statements Vincent made. The cellmate never asked Vincent any questions about the fraud and never steered any conversation toward the charges. One night, Vincent spontaneously told his cellmate the details of the fraud scheme and bragged about it. The cellmate reported the statements to prosecutors, who sought to use them at trial. Vincent moved to suppress. Should the court suppress Vincent's statements?
Answer A: Yes, because any use of a government informant in a cell with an indicted defendant violates the Sixth Amendment
Answer B: Yes, because Vincent was in custody and the cellmate was a government agent, so no statement could be used without Miranda warnings
Answer C: No, because the informant did not deliberately elicit the statements; Vincent volunteered them spontaneously
Answer D: No, because Vincent waived his Sixth Amendment rights by voluntarily confessing to a fellow inmate
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Kuhlmann v. Wilson*, the Sixth Amendment's *Massiah* protection requires deliberate elicitation — the government agent must take some action designed to draw out incriminating statements. A passive informant who merely "keeps his ears open" and reports volunteered statements does not deliberately elicit. Vincent's spontaneous bragging is not the product of government elicitation and is therefore admissible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Sixth Amendment is violated only when a government agent *deliberately elicits* statements. *Kuhlmann v. Wilson* held that a passive informant who merely listens to the defendant's volunteered statements without any elicitation does not violate the Sixth Amendment. B: Miranda applies to custodial *interrogation* — express questioning or its functional equivalent. A cellmate who says nothing is not interrogating. *Illinois v. Perkins* held that an undercover officer posing as an inmate does not conduct interrogation by merely listening. No Miranda violation occurred. D: The correct analysis is that no Sixth Amendment violation occurred in the first place because there was no deliberate elicitation — not that there was a violation that was subsequently waived. Framing this as waiver implies a violation occurred and then was cured, which misstates the doctrine.
lb_rule_reference: Massiah requires deliberate elicitation; a passive informant who does not deliberately elicit statements does not violate the Sixth Amendment even if the defendant's statements are overheard or reported
