Question: Daniel is tried in federal court for criminal tax evasion. The prosecutor calls Abigail, Daniel's accountant, to testify about a private conversation in which Daniel described how he classified several cash receipts on his tax returns. Daniel objects, citing a State A statute that creates an accountant-client privilege for confidential communications with licensed accountants. No federal statute creating a privilege for this criminal prosecution is invoked. Should the federal court exclude Abigail's testimony under the state accountant-client privilege statute?
Answer A: Yes. The state privilege governs because the accountant was licensed in State A and the conversation occurred there.
Answer B: No. Federal privilege law governs in the federal criminal prosecution, and the state-created accountant-client privilege does not by itself bar the testimony.
Answer C: Yes. Professional confidentiality rules for accountants create an evidentiary privilege whenever the client expected the conversation to remain private.
Answer D: No. Privileges do not apply in federal criminal cases, so no confidential relationship could ever block the testimony.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Rule 501 makes federal common law the ordinary privilege source in a federal criminal case unless a federal constitutional, statutory, or Supreme Court rule supplies a different privilege. Daniel is invoking only a state-created accountant-client privilege. The decisive axis is source law, not professional confidentiality or the state location of the conversation. Because the state privilege does not control the federal criminal prosecution by itself, Abigail's testimony is not excluded on that ground.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because it treats state location and state licensing as the privilege source. In a federal criminal prosecution, federal privilege law controls unless federal law creates or incorporates the privilege. C: C is wrong because professional confidentiality and evidentiary privilege are not the same thing. A professional duty of confidence does not automatically create a federal privilege. D: D is wrong because federal criminal cases can recognize privileges, including constitutional and common-law privileges. The problem is not that criminal cases have no privileges; the problem is that this state accountant privilege is not the governing source.

lb_student_diagnostic: Student treats privilege as profession-based rather than source-law-based.
