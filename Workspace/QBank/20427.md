Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: Tanya was indicted for wire fraud. Before the arraignment, while being held in the county jail, Tanya was brought to an interrogation room. An agent read her Miranda rights. Tanya said, "I want a lawyer before I answer any questions." The agent immediately stopped questioning. The next morning, a different detective returned, re-read the Miranda rights, and began asking Tanya about an unrelated drug trafficking ring that had not been charged. Tanya, believing she only had rights regarding the fraud charge, agreed to talk and made incriminating statements about the drug trafficking. Are Tanya's statements about the drug trafficking admissible?
Answer A: Yes, because the Sixth Amendment right to counsel is offense-specific and had not attached to the drug trafficking charge
Answer B: Yes, because Tanya implicitly waived her earlier invocation by agreeing to talk the next morning
Answer C: No, because Tanya's invocation of Miranda right to counsel barred police from reinitiating questioning on any matter
Answer D: No, because Tanya's Sixth Amendment right to counsel had attached at the indictment, covering all subsequent interrogations
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: When Tanya said "I want a lawyer," she invoked her Fifth Amendment/Miranda right to counsel. Under *Edwards v. Arizona*, once that right is invoked, police must cease interrogation, and they may not reinitiate questioning about any offense — not just the charged offense — until an attorney is present or the suspect initiates further communication. Because police (not Tanya) reinitiated the next morning, the interrogation violated *Edwards*, and the statements must be suppressed. The Sixth Amendment analysis (offense-specific) is separately true but does not resolve the case because Miranda provides broader protection here.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: While the Sixth Amendment defense fails (no formal proceedings on drug trafficking), Tanya invoked her Miranda right to counsel. Under *Edwards v. Arizona*, once a suspect invokes the Miranda right to counsel, police may not reinitiate interrogation on any matter until counsel is present or the suspect reinitiates communication. The re-approach the next morning violated *Edwards*, regardless of the Sixth Amendment analysis. B: Under *Edwards*, once counsel is invoked during custodial interrogation, only the suspect — not the police — may reinitiate communication. Police reinitiating questioning the next day was improper, and Tanya's agreement to talk after impermissible police initiation is not a valid waiver. D: The Sixth Amendment right is offense-specific and would not bar questioning about the uncharged drug trafficking offense. The correct basis for suppression is the Miranda/Edwards rule, not an overbroad reading of the Sixth Amendment.
lb_rule_reference: Once a suspect invokes the right to counsel under Miranda, police may not question the suspect about any offense; but the Sixth Amendment right, once attached, only bars questioning about the charged offense
