Subtopic: Homicide
Outline: Murder
Question: Alejandro confronted his estranged brother, Tomás, and told Alejandro that he had secretly been having a sexual relationship with Alejandro's daughter for the past year and that the daughter was now pregnant. Alejandro, who had no prior knowledge of this, erupted in fury and strangled Tomás to death on the spot. Alejandro had no weapon and had not planned to harm Tomás when he arrived. Is Alejandro guilty of murder or voluntary manslaughter?
Answer A: Voluntary manslaughter, because Tomás's disclosure was objectively likely to inflame any parent's passions to the point of losing self-control
Answer B: Voluntary manslaughter, because the killing occurred immediately upon hearing the provocation, with no cooling-off period
Answer C: Murder, because words alone — even highly inflammatory disclosures — do not constitute legally adequate provocation at common law
Answer D: Murder, because Alejandro premeditated the killing by traveling to confront Tomás
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: At common law, adequate provocation requires conduct of a type that would cause a reasonable person to lose self-control to the point of killing. The traditional rule — still controlling on most MBE questions — is that words alone, regardless of their content or emotional impact, cannot satisfy the adequate provocation element. Tomás communicated entirely through verbal disclosure. However devastating, the information came through words. Alejandro's killing is murder, not voluntary manslaughter.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The adequate provocation standard at common law requires more than emotional devastation — it requires that the provocation involve conduct or information not merely conveyed by words. The traditional rule is that words alone, however extreme, cannot constitute adequate provocation. The controlling legal standard is not "most parents would be outraged" but "does verbal disclosure qualify as legally adequate provocation," and at common law it does not. B: Both elements must be satisfied: (1) adequate provocation and (2) no cooling-off period. Here, element (2) is satisfied, but element (1) — adequate provocation — fails because words alone do not qualify at common law. An absence of a cooling period does not save a legally deficient provocation. D: There is no evidence Alejandro planned or intended to kill Tomás before the revelation. Traveling to confront someone about a dispute does not constitute premeditation toward killing. D reaches the correct verdict (murder) but for the wrong reason. The correct basis is the inadequacy of verbal provocation, not premeditation.
lb_rule_reference: At common law, words alone — no matter how inflammatory, insulting, or emotionally devastating — are generally insufficient to constitute adequate provocation for voluntary manslaughter; physical conduct or its equivalent is required
