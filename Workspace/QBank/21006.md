Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Federal agents searched Torres's storage unit without a warrant and without any recognized exception. They found documents allegedly linking Torres to a tax fraud scheme. A federal grand jury was convened, and the prosecutor presented the illegally seized documents to the grand jury. The grand jury returned an indictment. Torres's attorney moved to dismiss the indictment, arguing that because the evidence was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment, the grand jury was not permitted to consider it. Should the court grant Torres's motion to dismiss the indictment?
Answer A: Yes, because evidence obtained through an unconstitutional search may never be presented to a grand jury
Answer B: Yes, because an indictment based substantially on illegally obtained evidence violates the Fifth Amendment
Answer C: No, because the Fourth Amendment exclusionary rule does not apply in grand jury proceedings and the indictment may stand
Answer D: No, because Torres's attorney should have been present in the grand jury room to object to the evidence
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: United States v. Calandra* (1974) squarely held that a witness in a grand jury proceeding — and by extension the grand jury itself — need not be shielded from evidence obtained through an unconstitutional search. The rationale is that the exclusionary rule is a judicially created remedy designed to deter police misconduct; its deterrent purpose is adequately served by exclusion at trial without extending it to the grand jury stage. Torres's remedy is suppression of the documents at trial, not dismissal of the indictment.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: In *United States v. Calandra* (1974), the Supreme Court held that the exclusionary rule does not apply to grand jury proceedings. The Court reasoned that the costs of excluding evidence before the grand jury outweigh the incremental deterrence benefit, given that the exclusionary rule already operates at trial. B: The Fifth Amendment grand jury guarantee does not import an exclusionary rule for unconstitutionally obtained evidence. The validity of an indictment depends on whether the grand jury had probable cause, not on whether the evidence it relied upon would be admissible at trial. D: There is no right to have defense counsel present inside the grand jury room. Grand jury proceedings are secret. Even if Torres's attorney had sought access, it would have been denied. The legal issue of exclusionary rule applicability is separate from the mechanics of grand jury participation.
lb_rule_reference: The Fourth Amendment exclusionary rule does not apply in grand jury proceedings; a grand jury may consider evidence obtained through an unconstitutional search, and grand jury proceedings are secret — witnesses have no right to have counsel present in the grand jury room
