Question: A farmer contracted with a miller to buy grain. The farmer told the miller that profits from the resale would help the farmer donate to a village charity led by Lydia. The contract did not mention Lydia or the charity and required the miller to deliver grain only to the farmer. When the miller failed to deliver, Lydia sued the miller as a third-party beneficiary. Is Lydia likely to recover?
Answer A: Yes, because the miller knew the farmer hoped to support Lydia's charity.
Answer B: Yes, because charity beneficiaries are automatically intended beneficiaries.
Answer C: No, because charitable gifts are never legally enforceable.
Answer D: No, because Lydia's expected benefit from the farmer's later use of profits was incidental to the grain contract.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: Lydia is likely only an incidental beneficiary. The contract did not require performance to Lydia or the charity; the expected donation would come from the farmer's later use of profits. That indirect benefit does not create enforcement rights under the grain contract.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because knowledge of hoped-for charity use does not show intent to confer a contractual right. B: B is wrong because charities are not automatically intended beneficiaries. C: C is wrong because some promises benefiting charities can be enforceable; this contract did not direct performance to Lydia.
lb_rule_reference: Common law third-party beneficiary — charitable/public benefit
