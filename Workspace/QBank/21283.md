Question: A state statute requires defendants who wish to appeal a criminal conviction to purchase a transcript of the trial proceedings at their own expense. Transcripts cost between $800 and $4,000 depending on trial length. An indigent convicted defendant moves for a free transcript to support her appeal, which the trial court denies, stating that the state has no obligation to subsidize appeals. The defendant argues the transcript requirement imposes a wealth-based barrier to the right to appeal her criminal conviction. Under the Equal Protection Clause, how should a court analyze the transcript fee requirement as applied to the indigent criminal defendant?
Answer A: No constitutional violation, because there is no constitutional right to appeal a criminal conviction; appeal is a statutory benefit the state may condition on any reasonable requirement, including payment
Answer B: Rational basis applies because the transcript fee is an economic regulation of a court service, not a suspect classification
Answer C: The transcript fee requirement, as applied to the indigent defendant seeking appeal of her criminal conviction, violates Equal Protection because the state cannot condition meaningful access to the appellate process on the ability to pay when criminal liberty is at stake
Answer D: The transcript fee requirement is constitutional because the state has a compelling interest in recovering the costs of court operations, and the transcript fee is narrowly tailored to recoup only the actual cost of producing the record
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The Supreme Court has held that the Equal Protection Clause forbids conditioning an indigent criminal defendant's access to the appellate process on the ability to purchase transcripts. When a state provides criminal appeals, it must provide indigent defendants with the tools necessary to make the appeal meaningful — including transcripts — because criminal convictions involve fundamental liberty interests. Wealth is not a permissible basis for denying access to the existing appellate process in criminal cases.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: While a state need not provide an appellate process, once it creates one, it cannot condition access in ways that create a wealth-based barrier to a fundamental interest. The Supreme Court has held that when a state provides appellate review, Equal Protection requires it to provide a defendant meaningful access regardless of wealth — including free transcripts for indigent defendants. B: Even though the transcript fee does not expressly classify by race or sex, it has a severe impact on the ability of indigent defendants to exercise the fundamental right to appeal a criminal conviction. When a wealth-based condition effectively blocks access to a fundamental interest in the criminal context, courts apply heightened review — not rational basis. D: Even if cost-recovery is a legitimate state interest, the state cannot use cost-recovery to effectively prevent indigent defendants from accessing the criminal appeals process. The solution is not to charge the fee to the indigent defendant; it is for the state to bear the cost of providing a record when an indigent defendant has no ability to pay. The means — charging the indigent defendant — is not narrowly tailored because it completely forecloses access for the poorest defendants.
lb_rule_reference: Denying an indigent criminal defendant meaningful access to the appellate process — for example by conditioning appeal on ability to pay for transcripts — violates Equal Protection; wealth-based barriers to fundamental rights receive heightened scrutiny.
