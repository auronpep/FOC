MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Civil Procedure
Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Personal Jurisdiction
Question: Kraken Tools Inc. is incorporated and headquartered in the State of Halven. Kraken manufactures power drills in Halven and sells them nationwide through a national retail chain. The drill is designed, assembled, quality-tested, and packaged entirely in Halven. A resident of the State of Mernick purchased a Kraken drill at a Mernick retail outlet and suffered a severe hand injury when the drill malfunctioned during use in Mernick. The resident sued Kraken in a Mernick federal court, asserting diversity jurisdiction and state-law product-liability claims. Mernick's long-arm statute provides: "A court of this state may exercise personal jurisdiction over a person who (a) is present in this state when served with process; (b) is domiciled in this state; (c) consents to jurisdiction; or (d) commits a tortious act within this state." Kraken moves to dismiss, arguing the statute does not reach it. Applying the Mernick long-arm statute, does Mernick have statutory authority to exercise personal jurisdiction over Kraken?
Answer A: Yes, because the drill malfunctioned in Mernick and caused injury there, satisfying the "tortious act within this state" provision
Answer B: Yes, because Kraken sells its products nationwide, which constitutes transacting business in Mernick
Answer C: No, because under a conduct-based interpretation of "tortious act within this state," Kraken's allegedly tortious conduct — design, manufacture, and quality testing — occurred entirely in Halven, not in Mernick
Answer D: No, because even if the statute reaches Kraken, exercising jurisdiction would violate due process since Kraken has no minimum contacts with Mernick
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Mernick's long-arm statute uses the "tortious act within this state" formula. Under the prevailing narrow interpretation, this provision requires that the defendant's wrongful conduct — the act constituting the tort — occurred in the state, not merely that the injury was suffered there. Kraken designed, manufactured, assembled, and tested the drill entirely in Halven. The allegedly defective act (negligent design or manufacture) was committed in Halven. Kraken satisfies none of the other three statutory prongs either. The statute does not reach Kraken, and the motion to dismiss should be granted on statutory grounds before any constitutional analysis.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Many states (including the majority following the Illinois "Gray" approach) construe "tortious act within the state" to require that the defendant's wrongful conduct — not merely the injury — occurred in the state. Kraken's conduct (design, manufacture, quality testing, packaging) all occurred in Halven. The injury occurred in Mernick, but the tortious act (negligent manufacture/design) was committed in Halven. Under the narrow interpretation of the statute, the "tortious act" provision requires the conduct to have occurred in Mernick, not merely the harm. B: Mernick's long-arm statute does not include a "transacting business" prong. The statute has four specific grounds: (a) present in state when served, (b) domiciled, (c) consent, (d) tortious act within the state. Kraken satisfies none of the first three, and whether it satisfies (d) depends on interpreting "tortious act." Importing a "transacting business" analysis from other statutes is a category error; this statute does not contain that language. D: The question asks about statutory authority — whether the long-arm statute reaches Kraken. The threshold question is the statutory one; if the statute doesn't reach the defendant, the constitutional analysis is never performed. This answer reverses the analytical sequence and reaches the constitutional step when the statutory step should be dispositive. Additionally, whether Kraken has minimum contacts with Mernick (sales through a national retailer into Mernick) is contestable — it might satisfy due process even if the statute doesn't reach it. The correct sequence is: statute first, constitution second.
lb_rule_reference: Long-arm statutes that authorize jurisdiction over persons who "commit a tortious act within the state" must be interpreted according to each state's approach — typically either (a) the conduct/act must occur in the forum state, or (b) the act includes out-of-state conduct causing in-state injury. When the statute is narrowly construed, out-of-state tortfeasors causing in-forum injury are not covered. Due process is irrelevant if the statute does not reach the defendant.
