Question: Abigail bought 2,000 “sealed bulk totes” of food-grade syrup from Jacob's refinery. The signed goods contract contained the phrase but did not define sealed. Jacob shipped totes closed with ordinary zip ties. Abigail rejected them, claiming that in the food-syrup trade “sealed bulk tote” means a tote closed with a tamper-evident numbered band. What is Abigail's best Content/Meaning defense to Jacob's suit for the price?
Answer A: The Statute of Frauds fails because the contract did not define sealed.
Answer B: The trade meaning may explain the technical term “sealed bulk tote,” and if adopted, the zip-tied totes did not conform.
Answer C: The parol evidence rule bars all trade evidence because the writing used an express word, “sealed.”
Answer D: Abigail wins only if the zip ties caused actual contamination before rejection.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that UCC trade usage may explain a technical term in a goods contract if the usage is established and reasonably consistent with the writing. The trigger facts are a goods sale, the specialized phrase “sealed bulk totes,” and an alleged trade meaning for sealed. The decisive lock is offered purpose: Abigail is not adding a new quantity or price term; she is explaining the quality description. If that meaning is adopted, Jacob's delivery may be nonconforming.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because the Statute of Frauds is not the meaning route; the issue is the content of an existing writing. C: C is wrong because an express technical word can be explained by trade usage. D: D is wrong because actual contamination is a damages/performance decoy; nonconformity can exist without proven contamination.
lb_rule_reference: UCC 2-202 / technical trade term as defense
