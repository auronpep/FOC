Question: A state funds its public schools through a combination of state aid and local property tax revenue. Because property values vary dramatically across districts, wealthy suburban districts spend approximately $18,000 per pupil annually while poor urban districts spend approximately $6,500 per pupil annually. Parents in the lower-spending district bring an Equal Protection challenge, arguing that the funding disparity deprives their children of an equal education. The state argues that local control of education and the tie between local tax revenue and local school funding are rational policy choices. Under Equal Protection analysis, what standard of review applies to the school-funding disparity, and what is the likely outcome?
Answer A: Strict scrutiny, because the funding disparity effectively denies poor children access to an equal education, which is a fundamental right
Answer B: Strict scrutiny, because the funding disparity has a disproportionate impact on poor children, who constitute a suspect class
Answer C: Rational basis review, and the school-funding system likely survives because local control of education and the nexus between local tax revenue and local school spending are conceivable legitimate government interests
Answer D: Intermediate scrutiny applies because the funding disparity reflects a state policy that systematically disadvantages children in lower-income districts, who overlap substantially with racial minority groups
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Rodriguez established that neither education as a fundamental right nor wealth as a suspect classification triggers strict scrutiny for school funding disparities. Rational basis review applies. The state's interest in local control of schools and the rational connection between local property wealth and local educational spending — while producing inequitable outcomes — satisfy rational basis review. The system is rationally related to the legitimate state interests of local control and fiscal responsibility.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Supreme Court held in San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez that education is not a fundamental right under the Equal Protection Clause. Strict scrutiny does not apply to education funding disparities merely because education is important. Only rights explicitly or implicitly recognized in the Constitution receive fundamental right protection. B: Rodriguez also held that poverty-based (wealth) classifications are not suspect. The poor are not a suspect class under federal equal protection doctrine. Accordingly, no heightened scrutiny is triggered by the fact that poor children receive less school funding. Rational basis applies. D: Rodriguez rejected this argument. Racial disparate impact alone — without proof of discriminatory purpose — does not trigger heightened scrutiny for facially neutral school funding formulas. Intermediate scrutiny is not the applicable standard. Rational basis governs absent a suspect classification, quasi-suspect classification, or fundamental right.
lb_rule_reference: The Supreme Court has held that education is not a fundamental right under the Equal Protection Clause (San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez). Funding disparities among school districts based on local property tax bases receive rational basis review.
