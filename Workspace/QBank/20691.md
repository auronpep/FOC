Question: During a bar altercation, Neil punched Victor once in the jaw. Victor had an undiagnosed and rare blood-clotting disorder. Because of the disorder, the punch caused internal bleeding that would not clot, and Victor died twelve hours later. Medical testimony established that a person without the disorder would have suffered only a bruised jaw from the same punch. Neil was unaware of Victor's condition. Is Neil the proximate cause of Victor's death?
Answer A: No, because the preexisting blood disorder was a superseding cause that broke the causal chain
Answer B: No, because Neil could not have foreseen that a single punch would kill Victor
Answer C: Yes, because the eggshell skull rule holds Neil responsible for the full extent of harm caused to Victor, including harm amplified by Victor's preexisting condition
Answer D: Yes, but only because twelve hours elapsed, which is close enough in time to establish proximate cause
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: In criminal law, the eggshell skull doctrine provides that a defendant takes the victim as he finds him. Neil's punch was the but-for cause of Victor's death — without the punch, the internal bleeding would not have occurred. Victor's blood disorder did not cause the punch; it merely amplified the punch's effect. The causal chain is intact. Neil is both the actual and proximate cause of Victor's death.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A preexisting condition in the victim is not a superseding intervening cause. The eggshell skull rule holds that a defendant takes the victim as he finds him. Victor's vulnerability pre-existed the assault and did not arise independently — it merely amplified the harm. A superseding cause must be an independent, unforeseeable intervening act that displaces the defendant's causation. A latent condition in the victim does not qualify. B: The eggshell skull doctrine specifically overrides the foreseeability inquiry for the victim's preexisting vulnerability. Neil did not need to foresee the severity of the outcome; he needed only to cause the harm. The doctrine holds defendants liable for results that are only as bad as they are because of the victim's particular vulnerability, even if those results were unforeseeable. D: While the year-and-a-day rule is relevant to some causation questions, temporal proximity alone is not what establishes proximate cause. The correct basis is the eggshell skull doctrine. D reaches the right result but through irrelevant reasoning — time proximity is not why Neil is the proximate cause here.
lb_rule_reference: The "eggshell skull" (thin skull) doctrine applies in criminal law: a defendant takes the victim as she finds her; a preexisting vulnerability that accelerates or worsens a death does not break the causal chain
