Question: Abraham contracted with Sarah to buy Sarah's farm. The contract required Abraham to “pay the farmer from whom Sarah buys replacement grain this season.” At the time of contracting, Sarah had not yet chosen that farmer. Sarah later contracted with Isaac for the replacement grain and told Isaac that Abraham would pay him directly under the farm-sale contract. Relying on that promise, Isaac delivered the grain and did not demand cash from Sarah. Abraham and Sarah then agreed to remove the payment clause. May Isaac enforce Abraham's promise?
Answer A: No, because Isaac was not identified when Abraham and Sarah made their contract.
Answer B: No, because Isaac's only remedy is against Sarah for the grain price.
Answer C: Yes, because Isaac was within the intended class and his rights vested when he relied by delivering grain without demanding immediate payment from Sarah.
Answer D: Yes, but only if Sarah assigned her farm-sale contract to Isaac.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: A beneficiary need not be specifically identified at formation if the contract intends to benefit a defined class. Isaac became the intended class beneficiary when Sarah bought replacement grain from him, and his reliance by delivering without demanding cash vested his rights before the attempted deletion.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because future or class beneficiaries can be intended. B: B is wrong because Isaac may enforce the direct payment promise once his beneficiary rights vested. D: D is wrong because Isaac's route is beneficiary status, not assignment.
