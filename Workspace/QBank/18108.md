Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Authentication and Identification
Question: In a federal criminal trial, the prosecutor wants to prove the exact words contained in a voicemail left on Caleb's phone. No witness heard the voicemail when it was left. The prosecutor offers a detective's testimony that she read a transcript prepared by another officer and remembers that it said, “Bring the stolen seal.” The voicemail file is available, but the prosecutor does not offer it. Caleb objects under the original-writing rule. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection because the prosecutor is trying to prove the contents of the voicemail without offering the recording or a proper substitute.
Answer B: Overrule the objection because a detective may testify from memory about a transcript.
Answer C: Overrule the objection because the words are relevant to the charge.
Answer D: Sustain the objection only if Caleb proves the transcript was inaccurate.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The prosecutor is offering testimony to prove the contents of a voicemail recording, and no witness heard the call independently. Because the recording is available, the original-writing rule requires the recording or a recognized substitute.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because reading and remembering a transcript does not avoid the original-writing requirement when contents of the recording are the target. C: C is wrong because relevance does not satisfy the content-proof requirement. D: D is wrong because Caleb need not prove inaccuracy before invoking the rule.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 1002
lb_student_diagnostic: Relevance-over-substitution trap: student lets probative value or transcript memory bypass the original-writing rule.
