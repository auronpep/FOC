Outline: Rape
Question: Wynn and Jada, former classmates, met at a party. Wynn asked Jada to accompany him to a private room. Once alone, Wynn attempted to initiate sexual intercourse. Jada clearly and repeatedly said "no" and "stop" but did not physically resist because she was afraid that struggling would provoke greater violence. Wynn proceeded despite Jada's verbal objections. There was no weapon, no prior threat of violence, and no physical injury to Jada. Wynn is charged with rape under the jurisdiction's modern rape statute, which defines rape as sexual intercourse without consent. Under the modern non-consent-based statute in this jurisdiction, is Wynn guilty of rape?
Answer A: No, because Jada did not physically resist, and rape requires the victim's utmost resistance at traditional common law
Answer B: No, because there was no physical force, weapon, or threat of violence, which are required elements of rape
Answer C: Yes, because Jada clearly communicated non-consent, and the statute requires only sexual intercourse without consent — physical resistance and force are not independent required elements
Answer D: Yes, but only because Jada's fear of violence is treated as equivalent to actual force exerted by Wynn
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The jurisdiction's rape statute defines the offense as sexual intercourse without consent. Jada's repeated verbal refusals ("no" and "stop") constitute clear non-consent. Wynn proceeded despite that non-consent. The statute does not require physical force as a separate element, nor does it require physical resistance by the victim. The modern approach, reflected in this statute, centers non-consent as the core element. Jada's subjective fear about physical resistance goes to the reasonableness of her non-resistance, but is not even a required showing under this statute. All elements are satisfied.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The question states this is a modern non-consent-based statute. Even if the historical utmost-resistance standard were the rule (which has been abandoned in virtually all jurisdictions), the question explicitly identifies the applicable law. Under a non-consent statute, the victim's level of physical resistance is not a required element. Jada clearly communicated non-consent verbally. B: The jurisdiction's statute defines rape as sexual intercourse without consent — it does not list force as a separate required element. Under modern non-consent statutes, force is relevant as evidence of non-consent but is not an independent element the prosecution must prove. The victim's repeated verbal objections established non-consent directly. D: This answer (1) reaches the right conclusion but (2) misstates the legal basis. Jada's internal fear is not imputed to Wynn as "force." The conviction rests on non-consent, which is the statute's core element. Force is not a required element under this modern statute, so there is no need to construct an equivalence between victim fear and defendant force. The correct basis is simply: sexual intercourse without consent.
lb_rule_reference: At traditional common law, rape required both force (or threat of force) and non-consent; many early formulations required "utmost resistance" by the victim; modern law generally treats non-consent as the central element with force as a means to show non-consent, not a separate requirement
