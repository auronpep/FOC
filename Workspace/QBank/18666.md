Question: Martha sold John an interior parcel that had no direct access to a public road. John demanded an easement by necessity over Elizabeth's neighboring land because crossing Elizabeth's land would be the shortest route to the road. Elizabeth's land was never part of Martha's tract and was never owned by Martha or John. Can John establish an easement by necessity over Elizabeth's land on these facts?
Answer A: No, because easement by necessity arises from severance of commonly owned land and does not burden a stranger's land merely because that route is shortest.
Answer B: Yes, because strict necessity allows access over whichever neighboring parcel gives the shortest route.
Answer C: Yes, because landlocked parcels receive access over any land that touches them.
Answer D: No, but only because an easement by necessity requires a recorded written grant.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: An easement by necessity arises from the severance of commonly owned land. It may burden land of the common grantor or grantee when strict necessity exists, but it does not automatically burden a stranger's land simply because that route is shorter.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because shortest route is not the test. C: C is wrong because landlock does not give access over any adjoining land. D: D is wrong because necessity easements are implied despite deed silence when the elements are met.
lb_rule_reference: Necessity over common grantor land only
lb_student_diagnostic: The repair signal is to ask whose land came from the common tract before choosing the access route.
