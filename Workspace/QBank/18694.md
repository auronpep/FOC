Subtopic: Presentation of Evidence
Outline: Burden of production and burden of persuasion
Question: In a federal collection action, Abraham offers invoices from his shop to prove that Solomon bought equipment. Abraham calls Ruth, the record custodian, who testifies that invoices are made at the time of each sale by employees with a duty to record sales and are kept in the ordinary course of the shop's business. Solomon argues that Abraham must also prove by a preponderance of the evidence that every invoice entry is true before the invoices can be admitted. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the invoices unless Abraham proves every entry true by a preponderance.
Answer B: Exclude the invoices because business records are never admissible for their contents.
Answer C: Admit the invoices if the business-record foundation is established; the jury decides what weight to give the entries.
Answer D: Admit the invoices only if Solomon first fails to produce contrary records.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The proponent must lay the foundation for the business-record exception. The judge does not require the proponent to prove every entry true before admission. Once admitted, the jury evaluates weight and credibility.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because admissibility of the record is not the same as persuading the jury that every entry is true. B: B is wrong because properly founded business records may be admitted for their contents. D: D is wrong because admissibility does not depend on Solomon's failure to produce contrary evidence.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 803(6)
