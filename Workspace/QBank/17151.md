Subtopic: General Principles
Outline: Self-defense
Question: After voluntarily consuming several drinks, Iris becomes loud and clumsy. She angrily swings her arm at a bartender who asks her to leave and strikes him in the face. Battery in the jurisdiction requires intentional offensive contact but no further purpose or objective. Iris argues that she was too drunk to form criminal intent. Is voluntary intoxication a defense to the battery charge?
Answer A: Yes, because intoxication negates any intent formed while drunk
Answer B: Yes, but only if Iris can show she does not remember the incident
Answer C: No, because voluntary intoxication is not a defense to a general-intent battery
Answer D: No, because battery is a strict-liability offense
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Battery is a general-intent crime on these facts. Iris voluntarily became intoxicated and then intentionally swung at the bartender. Voluntary intoxication cannot be used to defeat this general intent, even if it might matter for a separate specific-intent offense.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Voluntary intoxication does not negate general intent. Battery requires intent to make the contact, not an additional specific objective. B: Blackout or lack of later memory does not automatically show the required intent was absent at the moment of action, and voluntary intoxication is not a defense to general intent. D: Battery is not strict liability here; it requires intentional contact. The reason intoxication fails is that the crime is general intent, not strict liability.
lb_rule_reference: Voluntary intoxication is not a defense to general intent crimes; it may negate only specific intent
