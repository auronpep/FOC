Question: A singer contracts to perform at a theater. The theater later cancels the show to book a more profitable event. The singer sues the theater for intentional interference with the singer's contract with the theater, alleging the cancellation prevented performance. Is the intentional-interference claim against the theater likely proper?
Answer A: Yes, because the theater intentionally caused nonperformance of the contract
Answer B: Yes, because any breach of contract is also intentional interference
Answer C: No, because a party generally cannot tortiously interfere with its own contract
Answer D: No, because contracts for entertainment services are never enforceable
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The singer may have a breach claim, but interference requires a third-party defendant.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The theater is a contracting party, not an outside interferer. B: Breach and interference are distinct theories. D: The defect is the absence of an outsider, not unenforceability.
lb_rule_reference: Intentional interference with contract requires improper interference by a third party; a party to the contract is generally liable for breach, not for interfering with its own contract.
