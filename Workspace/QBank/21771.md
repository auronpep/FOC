Question: Samuel sold David a rear parcel. At the time of the sale, David also owned an adjoining strip that connected the rear parcel to a public road. Two years later, David sold that strip to Jacob. After the sale of the strip, David's rear parcel had no road access. David then demanded an easement by necessity over Samuel's retained land. Is David likely entitled to an easement by necessity over Samuel's land?
Answer A: Yes, because the rear parcel is currently landlocked.
Answer B: Yes, because any parcel that becomes landlocked after purchase receives access over the original seller's land.
Answer C: Yes, but only if David proves the prior road over the strip was visible and continuous.
Answer D: No, because necessity must exist when the common tract is severed and cannot be created later by David's voluntary sale of access.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: An easement by necessity depends on strict necessity existing at the time of severance. David had access when Samuel sold him the rear parcel. David's later voluntary sale of his access strip created the landlock, so the necessity route against Samuel fails.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because current landlock alone is not enough. B: B is wrong because later-created necessity does not automatically burden the original seller. C: C is wrong because it imports the prior-use test; this is a necessity timing problem. D is correct for the reasons above.
lb_rule_reference: Necessity must exist at severance
lb_student_diagnostic: The recurring miss is checking present access instead of access at the severance moment.
