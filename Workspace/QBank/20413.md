Question: Rachel hired Jacob to renovate a small bakery. The signed contract required laminate counters and stated that all changes had to be made by written change order. After work began, Rachel orally asked Jacob to install custom-cut stone counters instead. Jacob later billed the added cost, and Rachel refused to pay, citing the written-change-order clause. Which additional fact would most strengthen Jacob's argument that Rachel cannot rely on the written-change-order clause?
Answer A: Before the contract was signed, Rachel told Jacob that she liked the appearance of stone counters.
Answer B: After Rachel approved the stone supplier's written quote and told Jacob to proceed, Jacob canceled the laminate order and bought nonreturnable custom-cut stone.
Answer C: Because counters are goods, Article 2 automatically controls the entire renovation contract and makes the oral change enforceable.
Answer D: The signed contract also contained a merger clause stating that the writing was complete.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that a written-change-order clause in a services or construction contract may be overcome by waiver, estoppel, or reliance when the owner directs a later change and the contractor materially changes position. The trigger fact in B is Rachel's post-signing approval followed by Jacob's nonreturnable purchase. The decisive lock is reliance: the fact most directly explains why strict enforcement of the written-change-order clause would be unfair.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because a pre-signing preference risks a parol-evidence route and does not show reliance on a later change. C: C is wrong because incidental materials do not automatically make the renovation contract a goods contract. D: D is wrong because a merger clause strengthens integration of the original writing; it does not strengthen the later reliance route.
