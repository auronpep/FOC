Question: A city contracted with a contractor to maintain public lamps on a bridge. The contract stated that the purpose was “to improve public safety for all travelers.” David slipped on the bridge after several lamps went dark and sued the contractor as a third-party beneficiary of the city's maintenance contract. Is David likely to recover on the city-contractor contract?
Answer A: Yes, because the contract was intended to benefit the public and David is a member of the public.
Answer B: Yes, because David was injured by the contractor's poor performance.
Answer C: No, because no contract made by a city can ever create third-party beneficiary rights.
Answer D: No, because a public safety benefit alone usually makes David an incidental beneficiary, not an intended beneficiary with contract enforcement rights.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: David is likely an incidental beneficiary. A contract that benefits the public generally does not give every member of the public enforcement rights unless the contract shows an intent to confer a direct enforceable right on that person or class.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because general public benefit is not enough. B: B is wrong because injury does not itself create third-party beneficiary standing. C: C is wrong because government contracts can create intended-beneficiary rights when the contract so provides; this one does not.
