Question: Sarah orally hires Noah to restore a large house. Both expect the work to take about 18 months, but the agreement does not set a completion date and the work could be finished within one year if extra workers are used. Noah completes half the work and Sarah refuses to pay, asserting the Statute of Frauds because the project was expected to last more than one year. Is Sarah's Statute of Frauds defense likely to succeed on the one-year ground?
Answer A: Yes, because the parties expected performance to take longer than one year.
Answer B: No, because the agreement was capable of being fully performed within one year by its terms.
Answer C: Yes, because any oral services contract involving more than six months of work must be in writing.
Answer D: No, but only because Noah partially performed the contract.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The one-year provision applies only when the agreement, by its terms, cannot possibly be fully performed within one year. Here the contract had no term making completion within a year impossible; the parties merely expected a longer project. The SOF one-year category does not apply.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because expected duration is not enough; impossibility within one year must appear from the contract terms. C: C is wrong because there is no general six-month services SOF rule. D: D is wrong because the better answer is that the one-year category is not triggered, not that partial performance alone saves the agreement.
