Question: At a house party, Reggie consumed a large amount of alcohol and became visibly belligerent. He approached a stranger named Paul, who had said nothing to him, and punched Paul in the face, breaking Paul's nose. Reggie testified at trial that he was so intoxicated that he did not realize he was striking another person; he thought he was swinging at a lamp. Reggie's blood alcohol level, tested an hour after the incident, registered 0.19. Reggie is charged with battery. Which of the following best states the effect of Reggie's intoxication on his liability?
Answer A: Reggie is not guilty because his intoxication prevented him from forming the intent to harm Paul
Answer B: Reggie is not guilty because a reasonable person in his position might also have been confused about what he was hitting
Answer C: Reggie is guilty because voluntary intoxication is not a defense to a general-intent crime like battery
Answer D: Reggie is guilty only if the prosecution proves he specifically intended to injure Paul
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Battery is a general-intent crime requiring only that the defendant intended the act that constituted the harmful or offensive contact. The common-law rule is clear: voluntary intoxication may negate specific intent, but it does not negate general intent. Reggie chose to drink to excess and chose to swing his fist. The general intent to perform the physical act is established. His subjective belief about what he was swinging at is irrelevant to a general-intent analysis.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Battery is a general-intent crime. Voluntary intoxication is not a defense to general-intent crimes at common law. The defendant need only have intended to do the act that caused the harmful or offensive contact — not to cause harm specifically. Because Reggie voluntarily became intoxicated and voluntarily swung his fist, the general intent element is satisfied. B: The reasonable-person standard here is simply irrelevant. Battery's general intent element requires that Reggie intended to perform the act (swinging at something); whether a reasonable person would have been similarly confused is not the legal test. Moreover, voluntary intoxication is self-induced and courts do not treat voluntary impairment as an excuse for general-intent crimes. D: Battery does not require intent to injure. It requires only an intentional act causing harmful or offensive contact. Intent to injure is not an element. Requiring the prosecution to prove specific intent to harm would effectively convert battery into a specific-intent crime, which it is not at common law.
lb_rule_reference: Battery is a general-intent crime; voluntary intoxication does not negate general intent
