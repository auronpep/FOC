Question: Ruth Farms LLC bought greenhouse-monitoring sensors and a season of calibration visits from Benjamin's company. The agreement allocated most of the price to the sensors. The first page listed “Buyer: Ruth Farms LLC,” but the body sometimes referred to “Ruth” as the buyer. The signature line read only “Ruth,” without a title. After nonpayment, Benjamin sued Ruth personally. Ruth offered drafting emails showing that Benjamin agreed to look only to the LLC. What is Ruth's best Content/Meaning argument against personal liability?
Answer A: Because the transaction involved goods, Article 2 automatically prevents any signer from being personally liable.
Answer B: The party-capacity language is ambiguous, so evidence may be considered to explain whether Ruth signed personally or only for the LLC.
Answer C: The emails are admissible only if they show a collateral agreement unrelated to payment.
Answer D: A merger clause would automatically bar all evidence about the capacity in which Ruth signed.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that ambiguous party-identity or signing-capacity language may be explained by extrinsic evidence, even when the contract is otherwise integrated. The trigger facts are the LLC buyer line, mixed references to “Ruth,” and a bare signature. The decisive lock is term status: the evidence is offered to explain who is bound, not to add a new payment duty. Although the transaction may be predominantly goods, Article 2 does not automatically answer personal capacity.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because source law for goods does not itself decide that every individual signer is immune. C: C is wrong because the issue is not a collateral side agreement; it is the identity of the contracting party. D: D is wrong because merger language does not automatically bar evidence explaining an ambiguous signature capacity.
