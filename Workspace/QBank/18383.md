Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Consent
Question: Portsmith Ventures LLC, a Delaware company, was sued in a Grantville federal court. Portsmith's attorney timely filed a pre-answer motion under Rule 12(b)(6) arguing that the complaint failed to state a claim. The district court denied the motion. Portsmith then filed its answer, which included an affirmative defense asserting lack of personal jurisdiction — the attorney had concluded at the time of filing the 12(b)(6) motion that there were colorable grounds to challenge Portsmith's contacts with Grantville but decided to hold that defense for the answer. The plaintiff moves to strike the PJ defense as waived. How should the court rule on the plaintiff's motion to strike the personal jurisdiction defense?
Answer A: Deny the motion, because Portsmith raised the personal jurisdiction defense in its first responsive pleading, the answer, which is timely under Rule 12
Answer B: Deny the motion, because personal jurisdiction, like subject-matter jurisdiction, can be raised at any time and cannot be waived by litigation conduct
Answer C: Grant the motion, because Portsmith waived its personal jurisdiction defense by omitting it from the Rule 12(b)(6) pre-answer motion it filed
Answer D: Grant the motion, because Portsmith was required to raise all defenses simultaneously in a pre-answer motion and cannot reserve any for the answer
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Rule 12(g)(2) requires that a party consolidate all available Rule 12(b)(2)–(5) defenses into its first pre-answer motion. Rule 12(h)(1)(A) provides that these defenses are waived if omitted from that motion. Portsmith filed a Rule 12(b)(6) motion and deliberately withheld a personal jurisdiction defense it knew was available. That defense is now waived. The plaintiff's motion to strike should be granted.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Rule 12(h)(1) provides that certain defenses — including personal jurisdiction — are waived if the defendant made a Rule 12 motion and omitted the defense from it. Portsmith filed a 12(b)(6) motion and did not include the 12(b)(2) personal jurisdiction defense. Under Rule 12(g)(2), a party who makes a pre-answer motion cannot make a second pre-answer motion raising a defense that was available but omitted, and Rule 12(h)(1)(A) deems such defenses waived. The answer is too late once a pre-answer motion was made without including available 12(b)(2)–(5) defenses. B: Personal jurisdiction is fundamentally different from subject-matter jurisdiction. Subject-matter jurisdiction cannot be waived because it concerns the court's institutional power. Personal jurisdiction is a personal right of the defendant that can be waived — and is waived under the specific mechanics of Rule 12(h)(1) when omitted from a pre-answer motion. This is one of the most tested distinctions in procedural posture. D: The rule is more nuanced. A defendant who makes no pre-answer motion may include 12(b)(2)–(5) defenses in the answer. Portsmith's problem is not that it had to include all defenses in a pre-answer motion — it had no obligation to make a pre-answer motion at all. The problem is that once Portsmith chose to make a pre-answer motion (the 12(b)(6) motion), it was required to include all available 12(b)(2)–(5) defenses in that motion. Rule 12(b)(6) itself is not waivable and can still be raised later; it's 12(b)(2)–(5) that must be consolidated into the first motion made.
lb_rule_reference: Under FRCP 12(h)(1), a defense of personal jurisdiction is waived if omitted from a Rule 12 motion that was made, or if omitted from the first responsive pleading. A defendant who makes any pre-answer motion must include all available 12(b)(2)–(5) defenses in that motion or lose them. 12(b)(6) failure-to-state-a-claim is not subject to waiver and may be raised in any pleading or motion. *Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(2), 12(g)(2), 12(h)(1)*.
