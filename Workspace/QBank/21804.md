Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Police received a tip that a homeowner was growing marijuana in his backyard. The backyard was enclosed by a six-foot wooden privacy fence. Unable to see over the fence from ground level, officers hired a private plane to fly at 1,000 feet — well within navigable airspace — over the property. From the plane, officers plainly saw marijuana plants growing in a garden adjacent to the house. Officers used that observation to obtain a search warrant and later seized the plants. The homeowner moves to suppress, arguing the aerial observation was an unconstitutional search. Was the aerial observation a Fourth Amendment search?
Answer A: Yes, because the backyard is curtilage and the homeowner took steps to shield it from public view
Answer B: Yes, because police used technology (an airplane) not available to the general public to enhance their observation
Answer C: No, because the observation was made from publicly navigable airspace where any member of the public could fly, defeating the objective reasonableness of the homeowner's expectation
Answer D: No, because marijuana is contraband and therefore receives no Fourth Amendment protection
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: California v. Ciraolo is directly on point. The Fourth Amendment is not violated when police observe curtilage from publicly navigable airspace. The test is whether the vantage point is one the public has a right to occupy. At 1,000 feet, any private aircraft could fly over and observe the yard. Because the homeowner's expectation that no one above could see the plants is not one society recognizes as objectively reasonable, the observation was not a search and the warrant obtained from it was valid.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Although the backyard is curtilage and the subjective expectation is evident, the Katz objective prong fails. In California v. Ciraolo, the Supreme Court held that an individual has no objectively reasonable expectation that the curtilage will not be viewed from publicly navigable airspace. What any member of the public flying overhead could see is not constitutionally protected. B: Kyllo's enhancement principle applies to technology that reveals intimate details not otherwise obtainable (e.g., thermal imaging). A private airplane flying at 1,000 feet is available to the general public. Ciraolo involved the same factual situation and held it was not a search. Riley extended this even to helicopter flyovers at 400 feet. D: Contraband can still be suppressed if discovered through an unconstitutional search. The lawfulness of the substance searched for is irrelevant to the threshold question of whether a search occurred. The correct reason suppression is denied here is the publicly-navigable-airspace doctrine, not the nature of the evidence.
lb_rule_reference: Aerial observation from publicly navigable airspace does not constitute a Fourth Amendment search even when directed at curtilage (California v. Ciraolo; Florida v. Riley)
