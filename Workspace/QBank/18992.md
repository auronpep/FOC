Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Officers applied for a warrant to search a suspect's house based on an affidavit containing information from a confidential informant. A judge issued the warrant. Officers executed the warrant in good faith and found cocaine. At a suppression hearing, the court determined that the affidavit's probable cause was insufficient — the informant's tip, while specific, lacked adequate indicia of reliability under the Gates totality test. The suspect argues all evidence must be suppressed. Should the court suppress the cocaine?
Answer A: Yes, because if the warrant was based on insufficient probable cause, the evidence is always suppressible regardless of the officers' good faith
Answer B: Yes, because the good-faith exception applies only when officers believe they had probable cause; here, they knew the tip was from a single unverified source
Answer C: No, because officers reasonably relied on a facially valid warrant issued by a neutral magistrate, and none of the four Leon exceptions applies; suppression would not deter any police misconduct
Answer D: No, because once a judge issues a warrant, the evidence is always admissible regardless of the warrant's validity
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under United States v. Leon, evidence seized in objectively good-faith reliance on a facially valid warrant is not excluded even if the warrant is later found deficient. The four exceptions are: (1) magistrate misled by deliberate or reckless falsehoods; (2) magistrate wholly abandoned judicial role; (3) affidavit so lacking in indicia of probable cause that no reasonable officer could rely on it; (4) warrant so facially deficient in particularity that reliance was unreasonable. None applies here — the affidavit had specific details from a named source, the magistrate engaged in a judicial determination, and a minimally adequate affidavit was presented. The officers' reliance was objectively reasonable. Suppression would deter no future police misconduct and is unwarranted.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: United States v. Leon created the good-faith exception: when officers execute a search warrant in objectively reasonable good faith, the evidence is not suppressible even if the warrant is later found to lack probable cause, unless one of four exceptions applies. The exclusionary rule's purpose — deterring police misconduct — is not served by suppressing evidence when officers acted in reasonable reliance on a judicial determination. B: Leon's good-faith test is objective, not subjective. The question is whether a reasonably well-trained officer would have known the warrant was invalid. An affidavit sufficient to generate a facially valid warrant from a neutral magistrate creates a presumption of reasonableness. Officers are not required to question the magistrate's probable-cause determination; they are permitted to rely on it, absent the four Leon exceptions. D: Leon good faith is not absolute. The four exceptions are real limits. If officers deliberately misled the magistrate (Franks v. Delaware), the evidence is suppressible even though a warrant was obtained. The issuance of a warrant is not a universal shield.
lb_rule_reference: Evidence seized pursuant to a facially valid warrant is admissible even if the warrant is later found defective, unless (1) the magistrate was misled by deliberate or reckless falsehoods, (2) the magistrate wholly abandoned his judicial role, (3) the affidavit was so deficient that no officer could reasonably rely on it, or (4) the warrant was so facially deficient in particularity that officers could not reasonably rely on it (United States v. Leon)
