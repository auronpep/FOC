Outline: Larceny
Question: Theo takes his neighbor's car without permission to drive to a casino 60 miles away, intending to gamble and then buy gasoline to drive the car back. He parks the car at the casino, loses all his money, and cannot afford gas for the return trip. He abandons the car in the casino parking lot. At the time of taking, Theo genuinely believed he would return the car — but that return depended entirely on his winning at gambling. Did Theo commit larceny when he took the car?
Answer A: No, because Theo genuinely intended to return the car at the time of taking
Answer B: No, because Theo only used the car temporarily and it was found undamaged
Answer C: Yes, because Theo's intent to return was contingent on winning at gambling, making it equivalent to no genuine intent to return
Answer D: Yes, because taking a car without permission is always larceny regardless of intent
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The intent-to-return defense to larceny requires an unconditional or substantially certain intent to return the specific property. Theo's plan to return the car depended entirely on winning money at a casino — an uncertain event he could not control. Such a conditional intent is treated by courts as insufficient to negate the intent-to-permanently-deprive element. Moreover, by abandoning the car in a distant parking lot, Theo created a substantial risk that the owner would be permanently deprived, which independently satisfies the element. Theo is guilty of larceny.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The intent-to-return defense requires that the return be unconditional or at minimum not substantially contingent on an uncertain event. Theo's intent to return was entirely conditioned on winning at gambling — a speculative event he had no assurance of. An intent to return "if I win" is not the genuine, unconditional intent to return that negates larceny. Courts treat conditional intent to return as equivalent to no intent to return. B: Larceny's "intent to permanently deprive" is assessed at the moment of taking based on the defendant's state of mind, not the ultimate outcome. The fact that the car was eventually recovered does not retroactively negate larcenous intent. Furthermore, intent to expose property to substantial risk of permanent loss (by abandoning it in a distant parking lot) can itself satisfy the permanent-deprivation element. D: Larceny specifically requires intent to permanently deprive; without that element there is no larceny at common law (though "joyriding" statutes in many jurisdictions fill this gap). A defendant who takes a car with genuine unconditional intent to return it — for example, borrowing it for a short errand and actually returning it — has not committed larceny. The answer here is correct on outcome but for the wrong reason, which the MBE tests by requiring accurate legal reasoning.
lb_rule_reference: Larceny requires intent to permanently deprive; genuine intent to return the specific property negates this element, but intent conditioned on ability to return or intent to return in worthless or substantially different form does not.
