Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: Detective Lopez arrested Sol for aggravated battery. After giving Miranda warnings and obtaining a waiver, Lopez told Sol: "Look, if you tell me what happened right now, I can talk to the DA and make sure you don't get more than a year in county. If you don't cooperate, the DA is going to push for the maximum — eight years." Sol, fearing prison, immediately confessed. The DA ultimately offered no such deal and sought the maximum sentence. Sol moves to suppress the confession as involuntary. Is Sol's motion to suppress likely to succeed?
Answer A: No, because Miranda warnings were given and Sol executed a voluntary waiver before confessing
Answer B: No, because police are permitted to use deception and promises during interrogation as a matter of standard practice
Answer C: Yes, because the detective's specific sentencing promise was a significant inducement that likely overbore Sol's will, rendering the confession involuntary under the totality of circumstances
Answer D: Yes, because Sol's confession was the product of fear of imprisonment, and any fear-based confession is per se involuntary
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under the Due Process Clause, a confession is involuntary if police conduct overbore the suspect's will based on the totality of circumstances. Courts have consistently held that specific promises of leniency — particularly when a police officer promises a definite sentencing outcome in exchange for an immediate confession — constitute impermissible inducements. Detective Lopez promised Sol a sentence of one year against the threat of eight years in an attempt to overcome Sol's decision-making. This is strong evidence of overborne will. Coupled with the fact that Lopez had no authority to make a binding sentencing promise, the confession is likely inadmissible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Miranda compliance and Due Process voluntariness are independent inquiries. A Mirandized confession can be involuntary if police conduct overbore the suspect's will. Detective Lopez's specific promise of a one-year sentence in exchange for an immediate confession is the type of inducement that courts scrutinize closely under the totality of circumstances voluntariness analysis. B: While police may use certain forms of deception (e.g., falsely claiming they have fingerprints), a specific promise of a sentencing outcome by a law enforcement officer crosses into territory that can render a confession involuntary. Courts are particularly sensitive to promises that a confession will directly result in a specified lesser sentence because such promises directly engage the suspect's rational decision-making in a coercive way. D: Confessions made partly from fear of legal consequences are not automatically involuntary. The fact that a suspect fears punishment is not itself unconstitutionally coercive — the fear of legal consequences is the legitimate domain of criminal law. The constitutional issue is whether police conduct (here, the specific promise) overbore the will, not whether the suspect feared consequences independently. The answer is correct but for the wrong reason.
lb_rule_reference: A promise of leniency by law enforcement can render a confession involuntary under the Due Process totality of circumstances test; if the promise was significant enough to overbear the suspect's will, the resulting confession is inadmissible.
