Question: In a criminal trial, the prosecution offered a transcript of testimony that the victim had given at the preliminary hearing in the same case. The victim had since moved abroad and refused to return for trial. The defendant had been present at the preliminary hearing but, having only just been appointed counsel that morning, his attorney was present but had no meaningful opportunity to prepare and conducted only a brief, superficial cross-examination. The defendant now objects to admission of the transcript on Confrontation Clause grounds. Should the court admit the preliminary hearing transcript?
Answer A: Yes, because the victim's preliminary hearing testimony is a firmly rooted hearsay exception and is therefore presumptively reliable
Answer B: Yes, because the defendant was present at the preliminary hearing and his attorney had an opportunity to cross-examine
Answer C: No, because the defendant lacked a meaningful prior opportunity to cross-examine, making admission of the testimonial statement a Confrontation Clause violation
Answer D: No, because the victim's unavailability does not satisfy the hearsay unavailability requirement when the witness left the country voluntarily
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Crawford v. Washington*, testimonial statements — which prior judicial testimony clearly is — may be admitted only if the declarant is unavailable AND the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine. While a preliminary hearing can, in principle, provide a prior opportunity, that opportunity must be meaningful and adequate. On these facts, counsel was appointed the morning of the hearing, had no preparation time, and conducted only a brief, superficial cross-examination. Courts have held that such nominal cross-examination does not constitute the adequate prior opportunity Crawford requires, making admission of the transcript a Confrontation Clause violation.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Crawford v. Washington* (2004) overruled *Roberts*. Testimonial statements may not be admitted unless the defendant had an actual prior opportunity for cross-examination. Reliability-based analysis no longer governs testimonial statements. B: Crawford requires not just the opportunity to cross-examine but an adequate opportunity. A superficial cross-examination by underprepared counsel appointed that morning may not satisfy the meaningful opportunity requirement. The adequacy of the prior cross-examination is a factual issue, but on these facts — appointment the morning of the hearing, no preparation time — the cross-examination likely was not an adequate prior opportunity under Crawford. D: For Confrontation Clause purposes, a witness who refuses to return to testify qualifies as unavailable. The precise reason for unavailability (voluntary move abroad, refusal to return) does not make the Confrontation Clause analysis disappear — the issue is whether the prior cross-examination was adequate, not whether unavailability was "proper."
lb_rule_reference: Under Crawford v. Washington, testimonial statements by an unavailable declarant are barred by the Confrontation Clause unless the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine the declarant
