Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: During a traffic stop, an officer asked the driver for permission to search the car "for drugs." The driver said, "Fine, go ahead." The officer opened the glove box and found a small amount of marijuana. As the officer moved toward the trunk, the driver said, "Stop — I don't want you searching any more." The officer continued and opened the trunk, finding a kilogram of cocaine. The driver moves to suppress both the marijuana and the cocaine. Which of the following best describes the admissibility of the marijuana and cocaine?
Answer A: Both are suppressible because once the driver objected to continuing the search, all prior and subsequent evidence became tainted
Answer B: Both are admissible because once a person consents to a vehicle search, officers may complete that search regardless of any subsequent objection
Answer C: The marijuana is admissible (found during valid consent) and the cocaine is suppressible (found after consent was withdrawn)
Answer D: The marijuana is suppressible because the consent was limited to the glove box, and the cocaine is admissible because the driver's withdrawal came too late
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The driver gave valid consent to search "for drugs," which authorized the officer to search anywhere drugs could be found, including the glove box (Florida v. Jimeno — scope of consent measured by objective reasonableness). The marijuana found in the glove box was within both the scope and the time frame of valid consent. When the driver clearly withdrew consent before the trunk was opened, the officer's authority ended. The trunk search was warrantless and no exception applied. The cocaine found after withdrawal is suppressible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Withdrawal of consent operates prospectively, not retroactively. Evidence discovered before consent was withdrawn is admissible because officers were lawfully searching at that time. Only evidence found after the withdrawal is subject to suppression. B: A suspect retains the right to limit or withdraw consent at any time. When the driver clearly said "stop, I don't want you searching any more," consent was revoked. Officers had no authority to continue into the trunk without a separate warrant or exception. Evidence found in the trunk after revocation is suppressible. D: Under Jimeno, a general consent to search for drugs extends to any area where drugs might be found. The glove box was within scope. The driver's withdrawal before the trunk was opened was timely — there is no rule requiring a person to object before the officer reaches the area. The withdrawal occurred before any intrusion into the trunk.
lb_rule_reference: Consent to search is limited to the scope reasonably understood from the exchange, and a suspect may withdraw consent at any time; evidence found after withdrawal is inadmissible unless another exception applies (Florida v. Jimeno on scope; common-law withdrawal rule)
