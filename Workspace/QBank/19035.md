Outline: Burglary
Question: Marcus owned a vacation cabin that he rented out occasionally. After a long drive, he arrived at a neighboring cabin one night, thinking it was his. He broke a window to enter because he had forgotten his key, intending to sleep there for the week and take the flat-screen television he thought he had left behind on his last visit. The television he planned to take actually belonged to the cabin's true owner. At trial, evidence showed that the two cabins looked nearly identical and that Marcus had driven past his own cabin in the dark. If Marcus is charged with burglary, which of the following arguments most accurately describes his legal position?
Answer A: Marcus is guilty of burglary because a reasonable person would have realized he was entering the wrong cabin
Answer B: Marcus is not guilty of burglary because he lacked the intent to commit a felony inside the dwelling
Answer C: Marcus has a valid defense because his honest belief that the cabin was his negated the specific intent required for burglary
Answer D: Marcus is guilty of burglary because he actually broke a window, satisfying the breaking element
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Burglary is a specific-intent crime. The required specific intent is the intent to commit a felony inside the dwelling at the time of entry. Marcus's honest belief that the cabin was his negates the trespassory element (he believed he had a right to enter) and also negates any felonious intent regarding the television (he believed it was his property). For specific-intent crimes, an honest — even unreasonable — mistake of fact is a complete defense because it is inconsistent with having formed the required specific intent.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: For specific-intent crimes like burglary, the honest-but-unreasonable mistake of fact is still a defense. The reasonableness of the mistake goes to credibility — a jury may not believe the mistake was genuine — but it is not the legal standard. An honest, subjectively held mistake negates the specific intent element even if it was careless. B: Marcus did intend to commit what he understood to be a lawful retrieval of his own property (taking his own TV). Because he honestly believed the property was his, he lacked the intent to commit larceny. This choice gets the conclusion right for the wrong reason and misframes the analysis; the better framing is that honest mistake negates the specific intent for burglary. D: Completing the actus reus is necessary but not sufficient. Burglary also requires specific intent at the time of entry. Even with a completed breaking and entering, absence of the required specific intent (due to honest mistake) defeats the charge. Both actus reus and mens rea must be proven.
lb_rule_reference: Burglary requires breaking and entering a dwelling of another at nighttime with intent to commit a felony therein; an honest mistake of fact negates specific intent regardless of reasonableness
