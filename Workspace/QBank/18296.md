Question: A state statute defines second-degree murder as "knowingly causing the death of another person." The statute further provides: "The mental state of the defendant at the time of the killing is presumed from the act of killing. The defendant bears the burden of producing evidence that the killing was accidental." At trial for second-degree murder, the trial court instructs the jury in the exact words of the statute. The defendant does not present any evidence. The jury convicts. On appeal, the defendant argues that the instruction violated due process. Is the defendant's due process claim correct?
Answer A: No, because the state legislature has the authority to define the elements of crimes, including by setting presumptions that accompany the act of killing
Answer B: Yes, because any presumption in a criminal case is unconstitutional under the Due Process Clause
Answer C: Yes, because the instruction places the burden of producing evidence on an element of the offense on the defendant, relieving the prosecution of its constitutional duty to prove knowledge beyond a reasonable doubt
Answer D: No, because the instruction only shifts the burden of production, not the burden of persuasion, and shifting the burden of production is always permissible
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: In re Winship held that due process requires the prosecution to prove every element of a criminal offense beyond a reasonable doubt. Sandstrom v. Montana held that a jury instruction creating a mandatory presumption (even a rebuttable one) that shifts the burden of production or persuasion on an element to the defendant is unconstitutional. Here, "knowingly" is an element of second-degree murder. The instruction tells the jury to presume it from the act of killing and requires the defendant to produce evidence to rebut that presumption. This unconstitutionally shifts the burden on an element to the defendant, violating due process.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: While states may define crimes and place the burden of producing evidence on a defendant for affirmative defenses, they cannot use a presumption to relieve the prosecution of its constitutional obligation to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt. Here, mental state (knowledge) is an element of second-degree murder, not an affirmative defense. An instruction that presumes the element from the act of killing places the burden on the defendant to disprove an element—a direct violation of In re Winship. B: Not all presumptions are unconstitutional. Permissive inferences (the jury may, but need not, infer X from Y) are constitutional if there is a rational connection between the proven fact and the presumed fact. Mandatory conclusive presumptions and mandatory rebuttable presumptions that shift the burden of persuasion on an element are unconstitutional, but the rule is not absolute across all presumptions. D: Even shifting the burden of production on an element of the crime to the defendant is unconstitutional under Sandstrom and Francis v. Franklin. Once a mandatory presumption shifts any burden—production or persuasion—on an element, it violates due process. The distinction between production and persuasion burdens matters for affirmative defenses, not for elements of the crime.
lb_rule_reference: Under In re Winship and Sandstrom v. Montana, the Due Process Clause requires the prosecution to prove every element of the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt; a jury instruction that presumes or shifts the burden of proof on an element to the defendant is unconstitutional
