Question: In a federal civil trial, Sarah, who is ninety years old, is called to testify that she saw Daniel's truck enter an intersection against a red light. Sarah sometimes forgets names and dates, but she states that she remembers the traffic signal at the moment of the collision and understands that she must tell the truth. Daniel objects that Sarah's memory problems make her incompetent. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection because any memory weakness makes a witness incompetent.
Answer B: Sustain the objection unless Sarah can remember the exact date and time without hesitation.
Answer C: Overrule the objection only if Daniel cannot produce contrary eyewitnesses.
Answer D: Overrule the objection because Sarah's memory problems affect the weight of her testimony, not her general competency to testify.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: Competency is broad. If Sarah can understand the duty to tell the truth and provide testimony based on what she claims to remember, ordinary memory weaknesses are for cross-examination and jury evaluation. They do not automatically make her incompetent.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because memory weakness usually affects credibility, not competency. B: B is wrong because exact recall of every date and time is not required for competency. C: C is wrong because admissibility does not depend on whether the opponent has contrary witnesses.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 601 / memory weakness and age as credibility issues
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests the memory-weakness-as-incompetency trap and the weight/admissibility distinction.
