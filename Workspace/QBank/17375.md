Question: Mira visited a friend's cabin in a remote woodland area. The cabin's owner, Dalton, drugged Mira's drink after she arrived and she fell unconscious. Dalton then locked all the doors and windows from the inside, preventing anyone from entering or Mira from leaving. Mira remained in the same room she had entered voluntarily. She regained consciousness hours later and discovered she was locked in. Dalton never moved Mira from the room or from the cabin — she remained exactly where she had been when she arrived. The jurisdiction follows strict common-law kidnapping doctrine. Under strict common-law kidnapping doctrine, which requires asportation, what is the most accurate charge that fits Dalton's conduct?
Answer A: Kidnapping, because Dalton secretly confined Mira against her will in a location concealed from the outside world
Answer B: Kidnapping, because Dalton used force (drugging) to establish control over Mira
Answer C: False imprisonment, because Dalton intentionally confined Mira against her will without moving her, which satisfies false imprisonment but not common-law kidnapping's asportation requirement
Answer D: No crime was committed, because Mira entered the cabin voluntarily and was present by her own free will initially
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Common-law kidnapping requires: (1) unlawful confinement (2) of another person (3) accompanied by asportation — movement from one place to another. Dalton confined Mira but never moved her. The asportation element fails. However, Dalton did intentionally confine Mira within boundaries (the locked cabin) against her will, with no reasonable means of escape. These are the elements of false imprisonment, not kidnapping. False imprisonment is the appropriate charge on these facts under strict common-law doctrine. Had Dalton moved Mira — even slightly — to another room or outside the cabin, asportation might be satisfied.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Under strict common-law doctrine — which the question specifies — kidnapping requires asportation: the victim must be moved from one place to another. Dalton never moved Mira; she remained in the same location she voluntarily entered. Secret confinement without movement may satisfy some modern statutory kidnapping definitions, but not traditional common-law kidnapping. B: The method of establishing control — whether by drugging, physical restraint, or threats — does not substitute for the asportation requirement under common-law kidnapping. Force is relevant to showing the confinement was against Mira's will, but even forcible secret confinement without movement does not constitute common-law kidnapping. The missing element is asportation, not force. D: Initial voluntary presence does not constitute ongoing consent to confinement. When Dalton drugged Mira and locked the building, her prior voluntary entry became legally irrelevant — she had not consented to being rendered unconscious or imprisoned. Consent is evaluated at the time of the confinement, not at the time of initial entry. Dalton's conduct satisfies false imprisonment regardless of how Mira came to be in the cabin.
lb_rule_reference: Traditional common-law kidnapping required both confinement and asportation (movement); some modern statutes eliminate the asportation requirement and criminalize secret confinement alone; under strict common law, confinement without asportation is not kidnapping, though it may constitute false imprisonment
