Outline: Reliance damages
Question: Moses contracted with Rachel to paint a series of murals for $40,000. Rachel expected total performance costs of $52,000, so the contract would have produced a $12,000 loss if fully performed. Before Moses repudiated, Rachel reasonably spent $18,000 preparing and beginning the work. Moses proves the $12,000 expected loss with reasonable certainty. What is Rachel's reliance recovery?
Answer A: $18,000, because that is the amount Rachel actually spent before repudiation.
Answer B: Nothing, because Rachel would have lost money on the contract.
Answer C: $6,000, the reliance expenditures minus the proven losing-contract loss.
Answer D: $40,000, the contract price.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The remedy interest is reliance under common law. Reliance damages cannot put Rachel in a better position than full performance would have done, so the breacher may reduce reliance by the loss Rachel would have suffered if the contract had been performed. The formula is $18,000 expenditures − $12,000 proven losing-contract loss = $6,000. The limiting gate is offset, and the result scope is limited. The answer does not award expectation profit because the contract had no profit.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because it ignores the losing-contract offset and overcompensates Rachel under WAL-REM-008. B: B is wrong because a losing contract reduces reliance; it does not automatically create an all-or-nothing bar under WAL-REM-005. D: D is wrong because the contract price is an expectation measure, not a reliance measure.
lb_rule_reference: Common law reliance damages — expenditures reduced by loss breacher proves plaintiff would have suffered on full performance
