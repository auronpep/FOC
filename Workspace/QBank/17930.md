Outline: Battery
Question: During a heated argument at a crowded bus stop, Denton swung his fist intending to strike Marcus, who was standing directly in front of him. Marcus ducked at the last second, and Denton's fist struck Patrice, a bystander who was standing slightly behind Marcus. Patrice suffered a bruised cheekbone. Denton claims he had no intention of hitting Patrice and did not even notice her standing there. Is Denton guilty of battery against Patrice?
Answer A: No, because Denton lacked the specific intent to strike Patrice
Answer B: No, because Patrice assumed the risk of being struck by standing in a crowd near a fight
Answer C: Yes, because Denton's intent to strike Marcus transfers to Patrice under the doctrine of transferred intent
Answer D: Yes, but only because Denton acted recklessly by swinging in a crowd
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Common-law battery is the unlawful application of force to the person of another. When a defendant intends to commit a battery against one person but the force contacts a different person, the doctrine of transferred intent applies: the defendant's criminal intent transfers from the intended target to the actual victim. Denton had the requisite intent to commit battery (intent to strike Marcus); that intent transfers to Patrice. All other elements—harmful contact and lack of consent—are met.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Battery under the transferred-intent doctrine does not require that the defendant intend contact with the actual victim. The intent to strike Marcus transfers to Patrice. The mental state requirement is satisfied by the original unlawful intent. B: Assumption of risk is a civil tort defense and has no application to criminal battery. A bystander does not forfeit the protection of criminal law by being present near a dispute. D: While recklessness might independently support a battery conviction in some jurisdictions, it is not the reason Denton is guilty here. The correct basis is transferred intent, not a lesser mental state. Choosing the wrong legal theory still makes this answer incorrect.
lb_rule_reference: Under common law, the intent to commit battery transfers from the intended victim to the actual victim; the defendant need not have intended contact with the person actually harmed
