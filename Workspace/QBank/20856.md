Subtopic: Negligence
Outline: Proximate (legal) cause
Question: A driver negligently hit a pedestrian, breaking the pedestrian's arm. At the hospital, a doctor negligently set the arm, causing additional nerve damage. The pedestrian sues the driver for the nerve damage as well as the broken arm. Is the doctor's negligence likely a superseding cause relieving the driver of liability for the nerve damage?
Answer A: Yes, because any later negligence by a third person cuts off proximate cause
Answer B: Yes, because medical treatment is outside the risk of a traffic accident
Answer C: No, because negligent medical treatment of the original injury is ordinarily foreseeable
Answer D: No, because superseding cause never exists in negligence cases
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The driver's negligence remains a proximate cause of complications from ordinary treatment.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Foreseeable intervening negligence usually does not supersede. B: Medical treatment is a foreseeable consequence of causing a bodily injury. D: Superseding cause can exist, but not on these ordinary medical-treatment facts.
lb_rule_reference: Ordinary negligent medical treatment of an injury caused by the defendant is generally a foreseeable intervening cause, not a superseding cause.
