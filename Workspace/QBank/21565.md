Outline: Spousal Privileges
Question: Abraham is prosecuted in federal court for bank fraud. The prosecution subpoenas Sarah, Abraham's current wife, to testify that she saw Abraham hide deposit slips in their garage. Sarah states that she does not want to testify against Abraham and invokes spousal privilege. Should Sarah be compelled to testify?
Answer A: Yes, because she would testify only to an observation, not to a confidential communication.
Answer B: Yes, because Abraham has not personally asserted the privilege.
Answer C: No, because the current witness-spouse may refuse to testify adversely against the defendant-spouse in a federal criminal case.
Answer D: No, but only if Abraham also objects to Sarah's testimony.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The adverse spousal testimonial privilege permits the current witness-spouse to refuse adverse testimony in a federal criminal prosecution. Unlike the marital communications privilege, this testimonial privilege can cover observations. The decisive axis is willing versus unwilling witness-spouse.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because This imports the marital-communications limitation into the broader testimonial privilege. Observed acts are not marital communications, but they can still be adverse testimony Sarah may refuse to give. B: B is wrong because This repeats the holder error. Sarah herself holds the testimonial privilege and may claim it. D: D is wrong because Abraham's objection is unnecessary because the privilege belongs to Sarah.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 501 / adverse spousal testimonial privilege
lb_student_diagnostic: Student is treating the testimonial privilege as if it protects only confidential words or must be asserted by the accused spouse.
