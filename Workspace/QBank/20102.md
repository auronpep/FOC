Outline: Prior inconsistent statement
Question: In a federal civil-rights trial, Elijah testifies that the officer never pushed him. Two weeks after the event, Elijah signed a notarized affidavit under penalty of perjury stating, “The officer pushed me into the wall.” Elijah is subject to cross-examination at trial. The plaintiff offers the affidavit to prove that the officer pushed Elijah. The defendant objects. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Admit the affidavit as substantive evidence because it was signed under penalty of perjury.
Answer B: Admit the affidavit as substantive evidence only if the notary testifies.
Answer C: Admit the affidavit only for impeachment, not for the truth, because it was not given at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition.
Answer D: Exclude the affidavit for every purpose because it is inconsistent with Elijah's trial testimony.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: A prior inconsistent statement is substantively admissible under the federal rule only if it was made under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition. A notarized affidavit may be sworn, but it is not itself one of the listed proceeding or deposition settings. It may be used to impeach Elijah but not as substantive proof under Rule 801(d)(1)(A).
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because oath alone is not enough under the federal prior-inconsistent-statement rule. B: B is wrong because notarization/authentication does not change the hearsay route. D: D is wrong because inconsistency is what makes it useful for impeachment.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 801(d)(1)(A) / sworn affidavit not a proceeding
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests the common trap that any sworn writing becomes substantive prior inconsistent evidence.
