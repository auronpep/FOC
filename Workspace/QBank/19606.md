Question: Gideon orally told Lydia, “You may permanently use the south lane across my land for access to your house.” Lydia then sold her old access route, spent substantial money improving the south lane, and used it with Gideon's knowledge. Gideon later argued that the oral promise was unenforceable solely because it was not in a signed writing. What is Lydia's best response?
Answer A: The statute of frauds never applies to easements, so the oral promise is automatically an express easement.
Answer B: Even though an express easement normally requires a writing, Gideon may be estopped from revoking because Lydia substantially relied on the oral promise.
Answer C: Lydia has no possible right because reliance is irrelevant to land-use permission.
Answer D: Lydia owns Gideon's south lane because she improved it.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: An express easement ordinarily requires compliance with the statute of frauds, but substantial reliance on a landowner's permission can support estoppel or an irrevocable license in equity. Lydia sold her old access and improved the lane in reliance on Gideon's promise.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because express easements generally require a writing. B is correct for the reasons above. C: C is wrong because reliance is central to easement by estoppel. D: D is wrong because improvements support at most a limited use right, not fee title.
lb_rule_reference: Reliance can overcome statute-of-frauds defect in equity
lb_student_diagnostic: The trap is treating the statute of frauds as the end of analysis despite reliance facts.
