Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Discretionary exclusion (undue prejudice, confusion, waste of time)
Question: In a federal arson trial, the prosecutor offers evidence that Ezra recently bought a large insurance policy on the burned building. The prosecutor offers the evidence to show motive, not to prove that Ezra is a bad person. Ezra objects under Rule 403 and, alternatively, asks for a limiting instruction. Which ruling is most appropriate?
Answer A: Exclude the evidence because insurance evidence is always unfairly prejudicial.
Answer B: Admit the evidence for all purposes because any relevant evidence may be used however the jury wishes.
Answer C: Admit the evidence for motive if its probative value is not substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice, and give a limiting instruction on request.
Answer D: Exclude the evidence unless Ezra first testifies about his finances.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The insurance purchase is probative of motive. If the evidence is admitted for that limited purpose, the court should restrict it to its proper scope and instruct the jury on request. Rule 403 balancing considers whether the risk of misuse substantially outweighs probative value.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because insurance evidence is not categorically barred when offered for a permissible purpose such as motive. B: B is wrong because evidence admissible for one purpose may need a limiting instruction. D: D is wrong because admissibility does not depend on Ezra testifying first.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 403; FRE 105
lb_student_diagnostic: Student chooses all-or-nothing exclusion instead of limited-purpose control.
