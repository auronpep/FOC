MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: Police arrested Arlo for fraud. After Miranda warnings, Arlo clearly asked for a lawyer, and questioning stopped. He was released the same day. Twenty days later, police arrested him on new evidence, gave fresh Miranda warnings, and Arlo signed a waiver before confessing. No attorney was present. Does Arlo's earlier counsel invocation automatically suppress the later confession?
Answer A: Yes, because a request for counsel permanently bars all later police-initiated questioning
Answer B: Yes, because counsel had to be physically present for any later waiver
Answer C: No, because a twenty-day break in custody preceded fresh warnings and a new waiver
Answer D: No, because invoking counsel has no effect before formal charges are filed
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Arlo was released for more than fourteen days. The later custodial interrogation followed fresh warnings and waiver, so the earlier invocation does not automatically suppress the confession.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A substantial break in custody can end the Edwards bar. Here, twenty days passed after release. B: After a sufficient break in custody, fresh warnings and waiver can permit questioning. D: Miranda counsel invocation matters before charges during custodial interrogation. The reason the later statement is admissible is the break in custody, not lack of attachment.
lb_rule_reference: After a suspect invokes Miranda counsel, police generally may not reinitiate custodial interrogation, but a fourteen-day break in custody can end that protection for later warned questioning.
