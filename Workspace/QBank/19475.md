Question: Jacob hired Leah to provide a “launch package” for a new bakery. The signed services agreement stated that it was the complete and exclusive statement of the parties' obligations. It did not define “launch package.” Jacob offers two pieces of negotiation evidence: first, that both parties used “launch package” to mean a logo, landing page, and social-media graphics; second, that Leah separately promised six months of post-launch maintenance. Which evidence should be admitted?
Answer A: Both pieces, because both show what Jacob expected.
Answer B: Only the evidence explaining what “launch package” meant.
Answer C: Only the evidence about six months of maintenance.
Answer D: Neither piece, because the writing is complete and exclusive.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that a complete integration bars prior or contemporaneous evidence offered to add obligations, but it does not bar evidence offered to explain an ambiguous term already in the writing. The trigger facts are the complete-and-exclusive clause, the undefined phrase “launch package,” and the separate maintenance promise. The decisive locks are integration and purpose. The package-meaning evidence explains; the maintenance evidence adds.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because it treats all negotiation evidence as one package. C: C is wrong because the maintenance promise adds a separate obligation to a complete writing. D: D is wrong because complete integration bars adding terms, not explaining an ambiguous included term.
lb_rule_reference: Complete integration / ambiguity explanation versus added service
