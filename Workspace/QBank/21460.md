Outline: Common law
Question: Owen was swimming alone at a public beach when he noticed a stranger, Leon, struggling in the water about thirty yards away. Owen was a strong swimmer and could have reached Leon with minimal effort. Owen watched from the shore, made no attempt to help, and did not call for assistance. Leon drowned. Owen later told police he "just didn't want to get involved." The jurisdiction has no Good Samaritan statute requiring bystanders to render aid. Can Owen be held criminally liable for Leon's death based on his failure to act?
Answer A: Yes, because Owen had the ability to save Leon and chose not to
Answer B: Yes, because Owen's deliberate inaction was equivalent to an affirmative act of killing
Answer C: No, because Owen owed Leon no legal duty to act, and criminal liability for omission requires a legal duty
Answer D: No, because Leon's own decision to swim alone was a superseding cause that breaks the causal chain
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Criminal liability for a failure to act requires a pre-existing legal duty. The recognized bases for a legal duty are: statute, status relationship (parent-child, spouse, etc.), contractual obligation, voluntary assumption of care, or creation of peril. Owen and Leon were strangers. No statute required Owen to render aid. Owen had not undertaken any care of Leon and had not put Leon in danger. Owen had only a moral duty — which the common law does not treat as sufficient. Without a legal duty, the omission cannot form the actus reus of any crime.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Criminal law does not impose liability for omission based solely on the ability to help. There must be a legal duty to act. No special relationship existed between Owen and Leon (they were strangers). Owen had no contract requiring rescue, had not assumed care of Leon, and had not created the peril. Absent a statute imposing a duty on bystanders, moral capacity to help does not create a legal duty. B: The law distinguishes between acts and omissions. Omissions create criminal liability only when a legal duty to act exists. Treating all deliberate inaction as equivalent to affirmative acts would be an overbroad rule inconsistent with common-law principles. Courts have consistently refused to equate omission with commission absent a legal duty. D: Causation analysis is irrelevant unless the prosecution first establishes actus reus. If Owen had no duty to act, the omission itself cannot be the basis of liability, and there is nothing to connect to causation. The superseding-cause argument is technically a causation defense, not an actus reus defense, and the threshold question — whether Owen had any duty at all — must be answered first.
lb_rule_reference: Criminal liability for omission requires a legal duty to act, arising from statute, status relationship, contract, voluntary assumption of care, or creation of peril; a mere moral duty does not create criminal liability
