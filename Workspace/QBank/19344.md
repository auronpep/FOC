Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Specific and General Jurisdiction
Question: Declan is a freelance blogger based in the State of Orlin who writes about corporate governance. He publishes an article falsely accusing Vandela Corp. — a company incorporated and headquartered in the State of Rendell — of falsifying its quarterly earnings reports. Vandela Corp.'s entire executive leadership, accounting staff, and investor base are based in Rendell. Vandela's stock is traded on national exchanges. After publication, Vandela's stock price dropped 8% and several Rendell-based investors filed complaints with Rendell regulators. Vandela filed a defamation suit against Declan in Rendell federal court. Declan has never been to Rendell, has no business contacts there, and did not know the specific location of Vandela's headquarters when he wrote the article. Under the *Calder* effects test, does the Rendell court have personal jurisdiction over Declan?
Answer A: Yes, because the harmful effects of Declan's article were felt in Rendell, where Vandela is based and where the reputational and economic harm occurred
Answer B: Yes, because defamation about a corporation necessarily targets its principal place of business, and Declan should have known Vandela was based in Rendell
Answer C: No, because Declan did not expressly aim his conduct at Rendell — he lacked knowledge of Vandela's forum location and targeted no forum-specific audience, so the Calder effects test is not met
Answer D: No, because Declan is an individual blogger with no commercial presence anywhere, and individuals cannot be subject to personal jurisdiction outside their home state
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The *Calder* effects test requires (1) an intentional act, (2) expressly aimed at the forum state, (3) with knowledge that the brunt of the injury would be felt there. Declan committed an intentional act (publishing the article) but did not know Vandela was headquartered in Rendell, did not target a Rendell audience, and had no contacts with Rendell. Under *Walden v. Fiore*, jurisdiction cannot be based solely on the plaintiff's forum connections. Declan did not aim his conduct at Rendell; he aimed it at Vandela without regard to its location. The *Calder* test fails; the Rendell court lacks personal jurisdiction over Declan.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The *Calder* test requires that the defendant expressly aimed the conduct at the forum state — not merely that harm was felt there. The Supreme Court in *Walden v. Fiore* (2014) clarified that *Calder* does not allow jurisdiction based solely on the plaintiff's forum connections. The defendant's relationship to the forum — not the plaintiff's — supplies the constitutional basis. Declan did not know Vandela's headquarters location, did not target Rendell specifically, and had no forum contacts. The effects being felt where the plaintiff happens to be located does not satisfy *Calder*. B: The facts state that Declan "did not know the specific location of Vandela's headquarters when he wrote the article." A constructive-knowledge standard is not what *Calder* requires; it demands express aiming. More fundamentally, *Walden* rejected the proposition that a defendant's obligation to "know" where the plaintiff is located can substitute for actual forum-targeting conduct. Knowledge alone does not satisfy express aiming. D: There is no categorical rule that individuals are immune from personal jurisdiction outside their home state. Individuals can be subject to PJ in any state where they have sufficient contacts, consent to jurisdiction, or are validly served in-state. *Calder* itself involved an individual defendant (a journalist). The correct basis for denying jurisdiction is the failure of the *Calder* test, not some categorical immunity for individual defendants.
lb_rule_reference: Under the *Calder* effects test (*Calder v. Jones*, 1984), personal jurisdiction over an intentional-tort defendant is proper in the forum where the defendant (1) committed an intentional act, (2) expressly aimed at the forum state, (3) causing harm the defendant knew would be felt primarily there. The test is not satisfied merely because a plaintiff happens to live in the forum.
