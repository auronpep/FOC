Question: Fiona intended to shoot her ex-boyfriend Derek as he walked down the sidewalk. She fired once; the bullet missed Derek completely and shattered the plate-glass window of a storefront behind him. Derek was unharmed. The store owner, who was inside at the time, was startled but also unharmed. The only damage was to the window, valued at $3,500. The prosecutor wishes to use the transferred-intent doctrine to charge Fiona with malicious destruction of property for breaking the window. Which of the following correctly states the law?
Answer A: Fiona is guilty of malicious destruction of property under transferred intent because she intended to cause harm and did cause harm
Answer B: Fiona is not guilty of malicious destruction of property under transferred intent because the doctrine does not operate across different crime types
Answer C: Fiona is guilty of malicious destruction of property because she acted recklessly with respect to the window
Answer D: Fiona is not guilty of any crime because her intended victim was unharmed
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The transferred-intent doctrine applies when the defendant intends to commit a crime against victim A and instead commits the same crime against victim B. Here, Fiona intended to commit murder (a crime against a person) but the act resulted in property damage (a different crime against property). Because the intended crime and the resulting harm are categorically different offenses, transferred intent does not apply. The prosecution cannot use intent to kill to supply the mens rea for malicious destruction of property. Fiona might face charges for attempted murder of Derek, but not for the property offense via transferred intent.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Transferred intent does not extend across different crime types. Fiona's intent was to commit murder (a crime against a person). The result was property damage (a crime against property). The doctrine transfers intent only when the same crime is committed against an unintended victim — not when a completely different type of crime results from the act. Intent to kill does not transfer to create the mental state for malicious destruction of property. C: The question asks specifically about the transferred-intent theory. This choice answers a different legal question: whether recklessness satisfies the mens rea for property destruction independently of transferred intent. That may or may not be true depending on the jurisdiction's statute, but it does not validate the transferred-intent theory, which is what the question tests. The correct answer here is that transferred intent simply does not apply across crime types. D: Fiona fired a bullet at a specific person with intent to kill — that is attempted murder of Derek. The absence of a completed homicide does not eliminate criminal liability; attempt is a separate crime. This choice conflates "no completed harm to the intended victim" with "no crime," ignoring attempt liability entirely.
lb_rule_reference: Transferred intent does not operate across different crimes; if defendant intends to harm a person but accidentally damages property, the homicidal intent does not transfer to create malicious destruction of property
