Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Specific and General Jurisdiction
Question: GlassRidge Ceramics, incorporated in Vermont with its principal place of business in Vermont, sells handmade pottery exclusively through its website. The website allows customers to browse the catalog, create accounts, add items to a cart, enter a shipping address, and complete payment. GlassRidge ships products nationwide and has fulfilled 340 orders to customers in the State of Dunmore over the past two years, generating $47,000 in revenue from those orders. GlassRidge has no office, employees, agents, or warehouse in Dunmore. A Dunmore consumer purchased a ceramic serving dish, which arrived cracked. After the dish shattered during use, the consumer suffered a hand laceration. She sued GlassRidge in a Dunmore federal district court, invoking diversity jurisdiction. Dunmore's long-arm statute authorizes jurisdiction over any defendant that "transacts business within the state or contracts to supply goods or services in the state." GlassRidge moves to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction. Applying federal due-process doctrine, how should the court rule on GlassRidge's motion?
Answer A: Grant the motion, because GlassRidge has no physical presence in Dunmore
Answer B: Grant the motion, because the long-arm statute does not reach a defendant that sells goods only through the internet
Answer C: Deny the motion, because GlassRidge purposefully directed repeated commercial activity into Dunmore, satisfying both the long-arm statute and due-process minimum contacts
Answer D: Deny the motion, but only because GlassRidge's website is accessible in Dunmore, making all forum residents potential plaintiffs
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: GlassRidge's interactive website — accepting orders, processing payments, and shipping to Dunmore customers 340 times for $47,000 — constitutes deliberate, repeated targeting of forum residents, satisfying purposeful availment. The claim arises directly from a Dunmore sale. The long-arm statute covers "transacting business" in the state. Both steps of the PJ analysis are satisfied, and exercise of jurisdiction is reasonable given the volume of intentional contacts.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: International Shoe* replaced the physical-presence requirement with a minimum-contacts standard. A defendant can be subject to specific jurisdiction without any physical presence if it purposefully availed itself of the forum through deliberate commercial activity directed there. Physical presence is sufficient but not necessary. B: Dunmore's long-arm statute reaches any defendant that "transacts business within the state or contracts to supply goods in the state." GlassRidge accepted 340 orders from Dunmore addresses and shipped goods there — this satisfies both prongs of the statute. Courts consistently read "transacts business" to include recurring e-commerce directed at forum residents. D: Mere accessibility of a website in the forum is not enough. *Zippo Manufacturing* and subsequent doctrine distinguish interactive commercial sites from passive ones. Jurisdiction is sustainable here because GlassRidge repeatedly accepted and fulfilled orders from Dunmore — not merely because the site could be viewed there. An accessibility-only rationale would impermissibly extend jurisdiction over every defendant with a public website.
lb_rule_reference: Specific personal jurisdiction requires (1) that the applicable long-arm statute reach the defendant, and (2) that the defendant purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities in the forum state such that it could reasonably anticipate being haled into court there. *Int'l Shoe v. Washington* (1945); *Hanson v. Denckla* (1958).
