Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Officers knocked on a suspect's door and told her: "We have a search warrant for your home; please step aside." The suspect said, "Okay, come in." Officers found narcotics in the kitchen. In fact, police had no warrant. The suspect moves to suppress, arguing her consent was not voluntary because it was obtained by misrepresentation. Should the court grant the motion to suppress?
Answer A: No, because the suspect said "okay, come in," which constitutes express verbal consent to the search
Answer B: No, because police may always enter a home if they reasonably believe they have authority to do so
Answer C: Yes, because consent procured by a false claim of warrant authority is coerced as a matter of law and cannot be voluntary
Answer D: Yes, because the Fourth Amendment categorically prohibits any warrantless entry into a home, and consent can never substitute for a warrant
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Bumper v. North Carolina held that when law enforcement asserts it has a warrant, a homeowner's capitulation is not voluntary consent — it is mere submission to claimed legal authority. The government cannot rely on consent to justify a search when the consent itself was obtained through a false assertion that legal compulsion already existed. The narcotics were obtained through an invalid consent, and no other exception applies. Suppression is required.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Voluntariness requires that consent be free from coercion, deception, or misrepresentation of legal authority. In Bumper v. North Carolina, the Supreme Court held that when an officer falsely claims to have a warrant, "consent" obtained in response is not truly voluntary — the resident is simply acquiescing to claimed legal authority. The suspect here was told police had a warrant; she stepped aside in response to that false claim, not as a free exercise of will. B: The apparent authority doctrine protects officers who reasonably but mistakenly believe a third party has authority over a space — it does not protect officers who lie about having a warrant. Bumper specifically rejected the idea that a false claim of legal authority can constitute valid consent. D: Voluntary consent is a fully recognized exception to the warrant requirement. Had the suspect genuinely and freely consented — without the false warrant claim — the search would have been lawful. The defect here is not that consent can never work; it is that this specific consent was involuntary.
lb_rule_reference: Consent must be voluntary under the totality of the circumstances; consent induced by an officer's false claim of authority (e.g., falsely claiming to have a warrant) is not voluntary and is constitutionally invalid (Bumper v. North Carolina)
