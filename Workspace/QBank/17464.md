Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Definition
Question: In a federal slip-and-fall trial, Ruth claims that Levi negligently left water on the floor of his shop. Ruth offers evidence that Levi lied on a personal tax return five years ago. Ruth is not offering the evidence to impeach Levi as a witness; Levi has not testified. Levi objects. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Admit the evidence because any dishonest act by a party is relevant in any civil trial.
Answer B: Admit the evidence because a party's character is always a fact of consequence.
Answer C: Exclude the evidence only if Levi promises not to testify.
Answer D: Exclude the evidence because, for the stated purpose, the old tax lie does not make the condition of the shop floor or Levi's notice more or less probable.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: The offered purpose controls relevance. Ruth is not using the evidence for impeachment, and Levi has not testified. The old tax lie does not make negligence, notice, causation, or damages in the slip-and-fall case more or less probable.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because dishonesty is not automatically relevant to every merits issue. B: B is wrong because party character is not generally a consequential fact in ordinary negligence. C: C is wrong because the evidence is being offered now for a nonconsequential purpose.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401 / unrelated misconduct not consequential
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests purpose-lock discipline: a fact that might matter for one purpose may be irrelevant for the purpose actually offered.
