Subtopic: Negligence
Outline: Negligence per se
Question: A city ordinance requires rental scooters used after dark to display a front light "to protect pedestrians and riders from nighttime collisions." A rider used a rental scooter at night with no light and struck a pedestrian in a crosswalk. The rider had no excuse for the violation. The pedestrian sues the rider for negligence. What effect should the ordinance have in the pedestrian's negligence claim?
Answer A: The violation can establish duty and breach because the pedestrian and collision fit the ordinance's protected class and risk
Answer B: The violation proves the entire negligence claim, including causation and damages
Answer C: The ordinance is irrelevant because only common-law duties matter in negligence
Answer D: The rider is strictly liable because any ordinance violation creates automatic tort liability
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The ordinance protects pedestrians from nighttime collisions, and the unlit scooter caused that kind of harm to that class of plaintiff. The violation can supply duty and breach, leaving causation and damages to prove.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Negligence per se can establish duty and breach, but the plaintiff still must prove causation and damages. C: A safety statute can define the standard of care when class, risk, violation, and excuse requirements are met. D: Negligence per se is not strict liability. It supplies duty and breach only when the statute fits, and other negligence elements remain.
lb_rule_reference: An unexcused violation of a safety statute can establish duty and breach when the plaintiff is in the protected class and the injury is the type the statute was designed to prevent.
