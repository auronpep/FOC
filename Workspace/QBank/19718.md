Subtopic: Presentation of Evidence
Outline: Offers of proof
Question: In a federal civil trial, the judge excludes a line of questioning to Ruth about a meeting with Peter. Ruth's lawyer says only, “Her testimony would help my case,” and makes no further record of what Ruth would say. The substance of the excluded testimony is not apparent from the question or surrounding context. If Ruth loses and challenges the exclusion on appeal, what is the likely result?
Answer A: The claim is preserved because counsel told the court the testimony would be helpful.
Answer B: The claim is not preserved because counsel did not inform the court of the substance of the excluded testimony by an offer of proof.
Answer C: The claim is preserved because offers of proof are required only in criminal cases.
Answer D: The claim is not preserved only if the judge expressly requested a question-and-answer offer of proof.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: When evidence is excluded, the proponent must inform the court of its substance by an offer of proof unless the substance is apparent. A vague statement that the testimony would help does not create an adequate record.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because helpfulness is not the substance of the proof. C: C is wrong because offer-of-proof preservation rules apply in civil trials too. D: D is wrong because the duty to make an adequate offer does not depend on the judge requesting a special format.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 103(a)(2) / offer of proof after exclusion
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests preservation after exclusion and offer-of-proof omission.
