Question: Congress enacts an annual appropriations statute that gives grants directly to religious schools for devotional chapel construction. A federal taxpayer sues, alleging that the appropriation violates the Establishment Clause. The government moves to dismiss for lack of standing. What is the taxpayer's strongest standing argument?
Answer A: Taxpayer standing exists for any claim that federal money is spent unlawfully
Answer B: Standing is plausible because the plaintiff challenges a congressional taxing-and-spending appropriation alleged to violate the Establishment Clause
Answer C: Standing is impossible because taxpayers can never challenge Establishment Clause spending
Answer D: Standing depends only on whether the schools are public or private
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The classic narrow exception covers federal taxpayer challenges to congressional exercises of the taxing and spending power alleged to violate the Establishment Clause.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Federal taxpayer standing is ordinarily barred. The exception is narrow and not available for all illegal spending claims. C: The Establishment Clause spending exception is narrow but real. D: The standing question turns on the taxpayer-standing exception, not the ultimate merits of the funding program.
lb_rule_reference: Federal taxpayer standing is narrow and can apply to a challenge to congressional taxing and spending alleged to violate the Establishment Clause, not to every executive expenditure.
