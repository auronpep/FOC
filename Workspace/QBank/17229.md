Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Definition
Question: In a federal premises-liability trial, Hannah claims that a staircase railing was loose for weeks before she fell. Hannah offers evidence that the store cannot produce any weekly inspection logs for the month before the fall, although its manager says such logs are normally kept. The store objects that the missing logs do not prove the railing was loose. How should the court rule on relevance?
Answer A: Exclude the evidence because absence of records can never be relevant.
Answer B: Exclude the evidence unless Hannah first proves the store intentionally destroyed the logs.
Answer C: Admit the evidence only if the missing logs themselves described the loose railing.
Answer D: Admit the evidence because the absence of expected inspection logs tends to make inadequate inspection or notice issues more probable.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: The absence of expected inspection records has some tendency to make inadequate inspection, lack of maintenance, or notice issues more probable. It need not prove the exact defect by itself.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because absence evidence can be probative. B: B is wrong because intentional destruction is not required for threshold relevance. C: C is wrong because the point is the absence of expected inspection documentation, not the contents of missing logs.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401 / missing records and notice
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests negative evidence and relevance to notice/maintenance.
