Outline: Constitutional limitations and justiciability
Question: A federal disclosure statute requires an agency to publish quarterly reports about grants made to private contractors. The agency misses one quarterly deadline. A citizen who follows government spending sues in federal court, alleging that the missed report violated the statute. She does not allege that she requested the report for a concrete use, was denied a statutory entitlement directed to her, or suffered any personal consequence beyond being upset that the agency missed the deadline. What is the strongest Article III standing objection?
Answer A: The plaintiff lacks standing because a bare statutory violation and public interest in lawful government do not by themselves show concrete personal injury
Answer B: The plaintiff has standing because Congress may convert any procedural violation into automatic injury
Answer C: The plaintiff has standing because every taxpayer is injured when an agency misses a reporting deadline
Answer D: The plaintiff loses only because disclosure claims are political questions
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Article III requires a concrete and particularized injury. A missed reporting deadline can support standing only when the plaintiff is personally denied information to which the statute gives a concrete entitlement.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Congress can identify injuries, but it cannot eliminate the constitutional requirement of concrete harm. C: Taxpayer or citizen status does not create standing to police general executive compliance. D: The threshold problem is lack of concrete injury, not textual commitment to another branch.
lb_rule_reference: A statutory reporting violation does not create Article III standing unless the plaintiff was denied information to which the statute gives that plaintiff a concrete entitlement.
