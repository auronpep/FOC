


BARMATRIX Q#: 16173
Outline_code: 75090100
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Right to Jury Trial
Question: In a trial of a defendant for armed bank robbery, the prosecutor presented overwhelming evidence of guilt. Three tellers identified the defendant as the robber, a latent fingerprint found on the bank counter linked the defendant to the scene, and bank money had been found in the defendant's car. The police had arrested the defendant immediately after the robbery, as the defendant was driving away from the bank. When the prosecution rested its case, the judge directed the jury to return a verdict of guilty. The defense attorney did not object at the time. The jury withdrew, discussed the case, and agreed to a guilty verdict. The jury returned to the courtroom and announced the guilty verdict. The defense attorney then voiced an objection to the judge's having directed the verdict. The court overruled the objection and sentenced the defendant to 20 years in prison. On appeal, what should the appellate court do?
Answer A: A: Affirm the conviction, because the defense attorney failed to timely object to the judge's action.
Answer B: B: Affirm the conviction, because the prosecution presented both circumstantial and direct evidence conclusively establishing the defendant's guilt.
Answer C: C: Reverse the conviction, because the defense attorney's failure to timely object to the judge's action constituted ineffective assistance of counsel.
Answer D: D: Reverse the conviction, because the judge's action in directing the verdict denied the defendant his constitutional right to a trial by jury.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: (D)is the best response, because for a criminal verdict in a jury trial to be constitutional, the jury, not the judge, must find that all elements of the crime were proved beyond a reasonable doubt. A criminal defendant has a Sixth Amendment right to a jury trial. This right includes the right not to be convicted unless the jury finds that each element of the crime has been proved beyond a reasonable doubt. In Re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970). Consequently, the Supreme Court has said, “a judge may not direct a verdict of guilty no matter how conclusive the evidence.” United Brotherhood of Carpenters & Joiners v. United States, 330 U.S. 395 (1947). That is, if the judge were to direct the jury to find the defendant guilty — or even direct it to find that a single one of the elements of the crime charged had been established beyond a reasonable doubt — it would not be the case that the jury had made the requisite finding-beyond-a-reasonable-doubt as to all elements of the crime, as the Sixth Amendment guarantees to every criminal defendant. (A)is not the best response, because the Sixth Amendment right of a criminal defendant to have the jury find every element of the crime to be proven beyond reasonable doubt is considered to be fundamental and thus not easily waived. The judge's error here in taking the case away from the jury so clearly violated the defendant's right to a jury trial that the appellate court would likely hold that this was “plain error,” of a sort that should not be deemed waived by the lawyer's failure to object before the jury rendered its verdict. That's especially true given that the lawyer made the objection even before sentence was pronounced (and presumably even before the jury was discharged), so that once the lawyer made the objection there was still time for the judge to give a curative instruction to the jury, or at least order a new trial. (B)is not the best response, because the defendant had a constitutional right to have the jury decide the case, regardless of the strength of the prosecution's evidence. As we discuss in Choice (D), the directed verdict here was a complete violation of the defendant's Sixth Amendment right not to be convicted except upon a jury's unanimous finding that the prosecution proved each element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. This Sixth Amendment right is essentially absolute — if the court is convinced that the jury's ability to make its own findings of fact has been interfered with, the defendant will be entitled to a new trial no matter how overwhelming the evidence against the defendant seems to have been. (C)is not the best response, because an ineffective assistance claim would require the defendant to show that but for the lawyer's error, there is a reasonable probability that the result would have been different. For the defendant to succeed with an ineffective assistance of counsel (“IAC”) claim, the defendant has to show both that (1)counsel's performance was so deficient that counsel was not functioning as the sort of counsel guaranteed to the defendant by the Sixth Amendment; and (2)“there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different” (i.e., the defendant would not have been convicted). Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984). The examiners have carefully told you that the evidence against the defendant was “overwhelming”; therefore, you can infer that the defendant would not be able to satisfy condition (2), a showing that absent counsel's failure to object in a timely way, there was a “reasonable probability” that the defendant would have been acquitted or at least achieved a hung jury. Furthermore, nearly all appellate courts hold that an IAC claim should be raised either at the trial court in the form of a motion for a new trial, or as a post-appeal collateral proceeding, not as a request for relief as part of the main appeal.
