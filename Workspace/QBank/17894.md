Question: Police investigating a robbery suspect attached a pen register to the suspect's home telephone line without obtaining a warrant. The pen register recorded only the phone numbers dialed from the suspect's phone, not the content of any conversations. Based on the numbers dialed to a known associate's phone, police built their case. The suspect moves to suppress the pen register data. Should the court grant the motion to suppress the pen register records?
Answer A: Yes, because the suspect had an objectively reasonable expectation that the numbers he dialed would remain private
Answer B: Yes, because a pen register is equivalent to wiretapping the content of conversations
Answer C: No, because numbers dialed are voluntarily conveyed to the phone company and thus not protected under the Fourth Amendment
Answer D: No, because the Carpenter exception makes cell-site location information (CSLI) the only category of phone records that receives Fourth Amendment protection
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Smith v. Maryland is controlling. When a person dials a telephone number, they expose that number to the telephone company's equipment as a necessary part of placing the call. This voluntary conveyance to a third party eliminates any Fourth Amendment protection under the third-party doctrine. The pen register recorded only numbers, not content. No warrant was required and suppression is denied.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Smith v. Maryland held that a person who dials a phone number voluntarily conveys that number to the telephone company. By sharing the information with the phone company (a third party), the subscriber assumes the risk the company will disclose it to the government. No REE exists in numbers dialed, so no Fourth Amendment event occurred. B: A pen register captures only numbers dialed, not content. Wiretapping captures content — which is independently protected. Smith v. Maryland specifically distinguished these two scenarios and held only the content is protected under the Fourth Amendment. D: Carpenter narrowly held that comprehensive, long-term CSLI (7 days or more of location tracking) reveals a detailed chronicle of a person's movements and receives Fourth Amendment protection — unlike bank records or pen registers. Carpenter explicitly reaffirmed Smith v. Maryland for numbers dialed. This question involves a pen register, not CSLI, so Carpenter is inapplicable.
lb_rule_reference: Numbers dialed voluntarily conveyed to the telephone company receive no Fourth Amendment protection (Smith v. Maryland); call content does receive protection; Carpenter limits the doctrine for comprehensive CSLI
