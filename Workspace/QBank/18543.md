Subtopic: Separation of Powers
Outline: Taxing and spending power
Question: Five members of a 100-member legislative chamber sue federal officials, alleging that the officials are spending funds in a way that violates a statute the chamber enacted. The chamber has not authorized the lawsuit, and the legislators do not allege that their votes were nullified. They assert only that executive noncompliance injures the legislature as an institution. What is the strongest standing objection?
Answer A: The legislators lack standing because they assert an institutional injury without authorization from the chamber or vote nullification
Answer B: The legislators have standing because elected officials always may sue to enforce statutes they voted for
Answer C: The legislators have standing because any statutory violation nullifies every prior vote for the statute
Answer D: The legislators lack standing only because spending disputes are always political questions
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Individual legislators ordinarily cannot sue to vindicate an abstract institutional interest of the legislature. Authorization or a concrete vote-nullification injury is needed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Voting for a statute does not give each legislator a personal Article III injury when the executive allegedly violates it. C: Vote nullification is narrower than alleged executive underenforcement after a statute becomes law. D: The problem is plaintiff injury and authority to sue, not a categorical bar on adjudicating spending-related disputes.
lb_rule_reference: Individual legislators generally lack standing to assert an institutional injury to the legislature unless they have authorization or their votes were effectively nullified.
