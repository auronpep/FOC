Question: After a federal fraud verdict against Zacchaeus, juror Esther signs an affidavit stating two things: first, that during deliberations a court clerk told the jurors that Zacchaeus had been accused of fraud before; second, that Esther personally voted against Zacchaeus because she misunderstood the judge's burden-of-proof instruction. Zacchaeus moves for a new trial and offers the affidavit. How should the court treat the affidavit?
Answer A: Exclude the entire affidavit because jurors may never testify about anything after a verdict.
Answer B: Consider the statement about the clerk's outside information, but not Esther's statement about her own mental process.
Answer C: Consider both statements because the verdict's validity is being challenged.
Answer D: Consider only Esther's statement about misunderstanding the law because it directly explains her vote.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: During an inquiry into a verdict's validity, a juror generally may not testify about deliberations, vote effects, or mental processes. But a juror may testify about extraneous prejudicial information or outside influence. The clerk's statement is outside information; Esther's misunderstanding of the instruction is a mental-process matter.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because outside information may be considered. C: C is wrong because the mental-process portion remains barred. D: D is wrong because a juror's reason for voting is precisely the kind of mental-process evidence the rule excludes.
