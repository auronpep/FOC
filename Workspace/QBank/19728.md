Question: Mary was sued after a warehouse accident. At her lawyer's request, Mary wrote a confidential chronology describing what she saw, what she did, and what questions she wanted legal advice about. She sent the chronology only to her lawyer. If the plaintiff requests the chronology in discovery, how should the court rule?
Answer A: Compel production, because written narratives are documents rather than communications.
Answer B: Compel production, because the plaintiff is entitled to discover all facts Mary knows.
Answer C: Compel production unless Mary's lawyer wrote the chronology.
Answer D: Deny production of the chronology, while allowing discovery of the underlying facts through proper nonprivileged questions.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: A client's confidential written narrative prepared for the purpose of obtaining legal advice is a privileged communication. The privilege does not shield underlying facts, so the opposing party may ask Mary what happened, but cannot compel the confidential legal-advice communication itself. The decisive axis is document-as-communication versus facts-inside-document.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because This is a format error. A writing can be a privileged communication when it conveys information to counsel for legal advice. B: B is wrong because This correctly identifies discoverable facts but uses the wrong channel. The facts may be discovered without compelling the privileged chronology. C: C is wrong because This wrongly requires lawyer authorship. Client-to-lawyer communications can be privileged.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 501 / confidential client communication
lb_student_diagnostic: Student is failing to separate the protected vehicle from independently discoverable facts.
