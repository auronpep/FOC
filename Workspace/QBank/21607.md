Subtopic: Homicide
Outline: Murder
Question: After voluntarily drinking for hours, Reed fires a gun several times into the ceiling of a crowded apartment. One bullet ricochets and kills a guest. Reed is charged with second-degree murder based on extreme recklessness, often called depraved-heart malice. Reed argues he was too drunk to appreciate the risk. How does Reed's voluntary intoxication affect the murder charge?
Answer A: It defeats murder because intoxication prevents malice
Answer B: It reduces the charge to involuntary manslaughter automatically
Answer C: It generally does not defeat depraved-heart murder because voluntary intoxication does not negate malice recklessness
Answer D: It is a complete defense only if Reed was too drunk to remember firing the gun
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Depraved-heart murder rests on extreme recklessness or malice, not a specific intent to achieve a further objective. Voluntary intoxication is not a defense to that mental state. Reed voluntarily became drunk and then fired into a crowded apartment, conduct from which malice can be inferred.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Voluntary intoxication generally does not negate malice or recklessness. It is not a defense to depraved-heart murder based on conscious disregard of grave risk. B: Voluntary intoxication does not automatically reduce a malice homicide. Firing into a crowded apartment can show extreme recklessness despite intoxication. D: Later memory loss is not the test. The doctrine asks whether voluntary intoxication negates the required mental element, and it generally does not negate malice recklessness.
lb_rule_reference: Voluntary intoxication generally does not negate malice, recklessness, negligence, or general intent; it is limited to specific-intent mental elements
