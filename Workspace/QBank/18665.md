Question: In a federal tax-fraud case, the government offers bank records showing that Rachel deposited large unexplained cash payments during the months of the alleged fraud. Rachel objects under Rule 403, arguing that the records are embarrassing and will make the jury dislike her. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the records because embarrassment to a party is the same as unfair prejudice.
Answer B: Admit the records if their probative value on unreported income is not substantially outweighed by a danger of unfair prejudice.
Answer C: Exclude the records because financial records are always private.
Answer D: Admit the records only if Rachel first testifies about each deposit.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The records are probative of unreported income. Embarrassment or ordinary dislike caused by probative evidence does not equal unfair prejudice. The court should apply the substantial-outweighing test.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because embarrassment is not automatically unfair prejudice. C: C is wrong because privacy alone is not the Rule 403 standard for relevant bank records in a tax-fraud case. D: D is wrong because the proponent may offer relevant records without waiting for Rachel to testify.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 403
lb_student_diagnostic: Student converts embarrassment into unfair prejudice without an improper-decision risk.
