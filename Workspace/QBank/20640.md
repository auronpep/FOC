MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Other Crimes
Outline: Robbery
Question: Officers responding to a report of an armed robbery chased a suspect into a grocery store. Officer Chen apprehended the suspect, handcuffed him, and immediately noticed an empty shoulder holster. Concerned that a loaded firearm was somewhere in the store with shoppers nearby, Chen asked, "Where's the gun?" The suspect nodded toward some cartons and said, "The gun is over there." Officers retrieved a loaded pistol. No Miranda warnings had been given before Chen's question. The suspect moves to suppress both his statement and the gun. Should the court suppress the statement and the gun?
Answer A: Yes, because the suspect was in custody when questioned without Miranda warnings, making both the statement and gun inadmissible
Answer B: Yes as to the statement but no as to the gun, because physical evidence derived from a Miranda violation is admissible under *United States v. Patane*
Answer C: No, because the public safety exception permitted questioning about the gun's location without Miranda warnings, making both the statement and gun admissible
Answer D: No, because the suspect's statement was voluntary, which independently renders it admissible regardless of Miranda
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: In *New York v. Quarles* (1984), the Supreme Court recognized a public safety exception to Miranda. When an officer faces an objectively reasonable need to protect the public from immediate danger, the officer may ask questions necessary to address that danger without first giving Miranda warnings. Here, an empty holster on an apprehended suspect in a crowded grocery store created exactly the kind of immediate threat *Quarles* contemplated. Officer Chen's question was narrowly tailored to locate the weapon. Both the statement and the gun are admissible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: This analysis ignores the public safety exception established in *New York v. Quarles* (1984). When police face an objectively reasonable immediate threat to public safety, Miranda warnings are not required before asking questions necessary to address that threat. The threat of an unsecured loaded weapon in a crowded store is the paradigmatic *Quarles* scenario. B: While *Patane* is a correct statement of law for ordinary Miranda violations, it is unnecessary here. The *Quarles* public safety exception applies, which means there was no Miranda violation in the first place. Both the statement and the gun are admissible on that basis. Reaching *Patane* skips the threshold question of whether a violation occurred. D: Voluntariness under the Due Process Clause is a separate inquiry from Miranda compliance. A voluntary statement can still be excluded for a Miranda violation. The correct basis for admissibility here is the *Quarles* public safety exception, not voluntariness. Relying solely on voluntariness would be an incomplete and misleading analysis.
lb_rule_reference: Under *New York v. Quarles* (1984), police may ask questions necessary to address an objectively reasonable immediate public safety threat without first giving Miranda warnings; the resulting statement and any evidence obtained are admissible.
