Question: In a federal robbery trial, seven-year-old Samuel is called to testify that he saw the robber run from the store. Before Samuel testifies, the judge questions him outside the jury's presence. Samuel says he knows the difference between a true statement and a false statement, understands that he must tell the truth in court, and can describe what he saw. The defendant objects that Samuel is too young to be a witness. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection because a child under ten is automatically incompetent to testify.
Answer B: Overrule the objection because Samuel can understand the duty to tell the truth and can describe matters he perceived.
Answer C: Sustain the objection unless another witness corroborates Samuel's testimony before he testifies.
Answer D: Overrule the objection only if the jury first decides that Samuel is mature enough to testify.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Every person is competent to testify unless a rule provides otherwise. A child is not automatically incompetent because of age. Samuel's ability to understand the duty to tell the truth and describe what he perceived supports allowing him to testify. His age may affect weight and credibility, but it is not a categorical bar.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because there is no federal age cutoff for competency. C: C is wrong because corroboration is not a prerequisite to a child witness's competency. D: D is wrong because the court screens competency; the jury later assesses weight and credibility.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 601 and 603 / child witness with truth-duty understanding
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests the age-as-incompetence trap and the judge-versus-jury split for witness competency.
