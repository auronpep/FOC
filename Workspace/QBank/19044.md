Subtopic: Privileges and Other Policy Exclusions
Outline: Attorney-client
Question: Gideon is charged with burglary. During a private meeting with his defense lawyer, Deborah, Gideon says, “I broke into the shop last month. I need advice about whether to plead guilty and what defenses I may have.” Gideon does not ask Deborah to hide evidence, threaten witnesses, create false documents, or continue any crime. The prosecutor subpoenas Deborah to testify about Gideon's statement. Should the court compel Deborah to disclose Gideon's statement under the crime-fraud exception?
Answer A: No. A confidential communication about completed wrongdoing, made to obtain legal defense advice, remains privileged absent a future or ongoing crime-fraud objective.
Answer B: Yes. Any communication admitting a crime falls outside the attorney-client privilege.
Answer C: Yes. The privilege applies only if the client is innocent or denies the charged offense.
Answer D: Yes. The statement is highly relevant to the prosecution, so the need for the evidence defeats the privilege.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: The crime-fraud exception targets communications seeking legal advice or services to further future or ongoing crime or fraud. A client may confidentially tell a lawyer about past wrongdoing to obtain legal representation. Gideon's statement concerns a completed burglary and seeks defense advice, so the exception does not defeat privilege.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because crime-fraud is not an all-crimes exception. Admitting past crime to a defense lawyer is a core privileged communication. C: C is wrong because attorney-client privilege does not depend on the client's factual innocence. Guilty clients may seek confidential defense advice. D: D is wrong because relevance or prosecutorial need does not override attorney-client privilege absent waiver or exception.
lb_rule_reference: Past wrongdoing disclosed to obtain legal defense remains privileged
lb_student_diagnostic: Student treats crime-fraud as triggered by criminal content rather than by the client's intended future misuse of legal services.
