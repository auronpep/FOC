Subtopic: Negligence
Outline: Falling below the standard of care
Question: A theater had an internal policy requiring employees to inspect rolling ladders before each use. An employee skipped the checklist and used a ladder that appeared stable. A patron was injured when a wheel lock failed. The patron offers the skipped checklist as evidence of negligence. The patron sues the theater for negligence. What is the best use of the internal policy in the patron's negligence claim?
Answer A: The skipped checklist is irrelevant because private policies can never matter in negligence
Answer B: The skipped checklist is evidence of breach, but it is not automatically negligence by itself
Answer C: The skipped checklist conclusively establishes negligence per se
Answer D: The skipped checklist proves breach only if every other theater uses the same checklist
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The policy may help show reasonable care required inspection, but the factfinder still decides breach and the plaintiff must prove causation and damages.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Internal policies can be relevant evidence of what the defendant considered safe, even if they do not set the legal standard by themselves. C: Negligence per se generally requires a statute or regulation, not merely a defendant's internal policy. D: Internal policy can be relevant even without industry-wide adoption, though its weight may vary.
lb_rule_reference: A defendant's internal safety policy can be evidence of breach, but violating it does not automatically establish negligence without the full reasonable-care and causation analysis.
