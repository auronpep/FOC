Subtopic: Presentation of Evidence
Outline: Materiality and probative value
Question: In a federal negligence action, Leah claims that a fall in a store caused her back injury. The store offers testimony from Leah's coworker that Leah regularly carried heavy crates at work without back complaints before the fall, but complained of back pain immediately afterward. Leah objects that many other things could have caused her back pain. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the testimony because it does not conclusively prove that the fall caused the injury.
Answer B: Exclude the testimony because alternative causes make the evidence speculative and irrelevant.
Answer C: Admit the testimony because it has some tendency to make injury causation more probable, though alternative causes affect weight.
Answer D: Admit the testimony only if the coworker is qualified as a medical expert.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Relevance requires only a tendency to make a consequential fact more or less probable. The before-and-after observation bears on causation. Alternative causes and lack of medical expertise affect weight and possible scope, not threshold relevance.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because admissibility does not require conclusive proof. B: B is wrong because alternate explanations usually go to weight. D: D is wrong because the coworker is describing observed condition and complaints, not giving a medical diagnosis.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401 / weak but probative evidence
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests the low relevance threshold and weight-versus-admissibility split.
