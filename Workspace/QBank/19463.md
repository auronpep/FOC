Question: Officers arrested a suspect for armed robbery inside his two-bedroom apartment. After handcuffing the suspect in the living room, one officer stayed with him while the other walked through the apartment, searching each bedroom, closets, and under the beds. In a bedroom closet ten feet from where the suspect was detained, officers found a firearm and stolen cash. The suspect moves to suppress. Should the court suppress the firearm and cash found in the bedroom closet?
Answer A: No, because SITA authorizes a full search of the premises incident to any lawful arrest
Answer B: No, because the officers had probable cause to believe the apartment contained evidence of the robbery
Answer C: Yes, because the bedroom closet was outside the area of immediate control; the handcuffed suspect could not access it, and no other exception authorized the search
Answer D: Yes, but only because the officers should have conducted a protective sweep of the entire apartment first
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under Chimel v. California, SITA extends to the area within the arrestee's immediate control — the place from which he might grab a weapon or destroy evidence at the moment of arrest. Once the suspect was handcuffed in the living room, a bedroom closet ten feet away was beyond his reach and outside the permissible SITA zone. No other exception (exigency, consent, protective sweep) is present in the facts. The firearm and cash found in the closet must be suppressed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Chimel v. California specifically rejected a full-premises search incident to arrest. SITA extends only to the person arrested and the area within his immediate control — the wingspan area from which he could reach a weapon or destroy evidence. A bedroom closet ten feet away, with the arrestee handcuffed in another room, is far outside the area of immediate control. B: Probable cause alone does not justify a warrantless search of the home. Police must either obtain a warrant or satisfy a recognized exception. SITA does not extend to the bedroom, and probable cause without more is not an exception. D: A protective sweep (Maryland v. Buie) is a quick, cursory inspection of spaces where a person might hide — not a full search. It must be based on articulable facts suggesting danger from a third party. It does not authorize opening closets to look for evidence. Even a valid protective sweep would not make the closet search lawful here.
lb_rule_reference: A search incident to lawful arrest extends to the person and the area within the arrestee's immediate control — the area from which he might grab a weapon or destroy evidence — at the time of arrest; it does not authorize a general search of the premises (Chimel v. California)
