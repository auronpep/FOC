Outline: False pretenses
Question: At 11:30 p.m., Felix goes to the front door of Maria's home dressed in a fake utility company uniform. He knocks and tells Maria there is an emergency gas leak in the neighborhood and he needs to check her lines immediately. Maria, believing him, opens the door and lets him in. Once inside, Felix ties Maria up and steals her jewelry and cash. Felix had no utility connection and had planned the ruse entirely to gain entry to commit robbery. Is Felix guilty of common-law burglary?
Answer A: No, because Felix did not physically break anything — Maria opened the door willingly
Answer B: No, because Felix entered through the front door, not through a window or other unusual point of entry
Answer C: Yes, because Felix's fraudulent inducement of Maria to open the door constitutes constructive breaking, and all other elements of common-law burglary are satisfied
Answer D: Yes, because Felix's actions constituted both burglary and robbery, and the greater offense controls
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Common-law burglary's breaking element is satisfied by constructive breaking — gaining entry through fraud or coercion. Felix used a fabricated emergency to deceive Maria into opening her own door; her consent was procured by fraud and is therefore legally void. The other elements are met: (1) constructive breaking, (2) entering, (3) Maria's home is a dwelling, (4) the entry was at 11:30 p.m. (nighttime), and (5) Felix intended to commit robbery (a felony) inside at the time of entry. All five elements of common-law burglary are satisfied.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Common-law burglary recognizes constructive breaking: gaining entry through fraud, deception, or threats counts as a breaking even without physical force. Felix used a false pretense (fake utility emergency) to induce Maria to open the door. Consent induced by fraud is vitiated — it is not genuine consent. The deceptive procurement of entry is a constructive breaking, satisfying the breaking element. B: The point of entry is irrelevant to the breaking element. A front door can be "broken" in the constructive sense just as easily as a window. The breaking element is about how entry was obtained (physical force, fraud, or coercion), not where the entry occurred on the structure. D: The question asks whether Felix is guilty of burglary, not which crime is greater. Burglary and robbery are separate crimes with independent elements, and a defendant can be convicted of both. "Greater offense controls" is not a rule of criminal liability — it is sometimes a rule of sentencing in merger doctrines, but it does not negate or replace either charge. The correct answer identifies the specific burglary analysis, not a merger principle.
lb_rule_reference: "Breaking" in common-law burglary includes constructive breaking — gaining entry by fraud, threats, or by inducing an occupant to open the door under false pretenses. Physical force or damage to the structure is not required if the defendant obtained entry through deception or coercion.
