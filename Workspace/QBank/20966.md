Question: A merchant seller contracted to sell 300 heaters to Abigail. The signed contract stated that delivery was due June 1 and that modifications had to be in a signed writing. In May, Abigail told the seller by phone that June 15 delivery would be acceptable. Relying on that statement, the seller scheduled shipment for June 15. Abigail then demanded strict June 1 delivery and refused the heaters when they arrived June 15. Which source-law route is most accurate?
Answer A: Common law controls because any no-oral-modification clause removes a goods contract from Article 2.
Answer B: The parol evidence rule bars Abigail's phone statement because it was not included in the original writing.
Answer C: The seller must lose because Article 2 never recognizes oral waivers when a signed no-oral-modification clause exists.
Answer D: Article 2 controls; even if the oral modification fails under the signed-writing clause, the attempted modification may operate as a waiver, and retraction may be ineffective if unjust after reliance.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: This is a goods contract governed by Article 2. Article 2 enforces certain signed no-oral-modification clauses, but an attempted modification that fails the writing requirement can still operate as a waiver. A waiver affecting an executory portion may be retracted by reasonable notice unless retraction would be unjust because of material reliance. The seller relied on Abigail's phone waiver.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because a no-oral-modification clause does not remove a goods contract from Article 2. B: B is wrong because the phone statement was a later attempted modification or waiver, not prior or contemporaneous parol evidence. C: C is wrong because Article 2 expressly recognizes that an ineffective attempted modification can operate as a waiver.
