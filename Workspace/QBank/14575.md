Question: A defendant was charged with murder. Several witnesses testified that the crime was committed by a person of the defendant's general description who walked with a severe limp. The defendant in fact walks with a severe limp. He objected to a prosecution request that the court order him to walk across the courtroom in order to display his limp to the jury to assist it in determining whether the defendant was the person that the witnesses had seen. The defendant's objection will most likely be
Answer A: A: sustained, because the order sought by the prosecution would violate the defendant's privilege against self-incrimination.
Answer B: B: sustained, because the order sought by the prosecution would constitute an illegal search and seizure.
Answer C: C: denied, because the order sought by the prosecution is a legitimate part of a proper courtroom identification process.
Answer D: D: denied, because a criminal defendant has no legitimate expectation of privacy.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: C is correct. The defendant's mode of walking is relevant to aid with the in-court identification, and the court can properly order the defendant to give such a demonstration without running afoul of his constitutional rights.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A is incorrect. Evidence of how the defendant walks is non-testimonial, similar to ordering blood samples or handwriting exemplars, so the privilege against self-incrimination is not implicated. B is incorrect. Having the court order the defendant to walk in front of the jury is not an "illegal" search. It is done pursuant to a properly obtained court order. D is incorrect. It is not true that a criminal defendant has no legitimate expectation of privacy. However, here, even if there had been a reasonable expectation of privacy, the valid court order ensured that anything amounting to a "search" was constitutional. The objection should nevertheless be overruled because the walking demonstration is relevant and non-testimonial, as explained above.
