Outline_code: 44040504
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Constitutional Law
Subtopic: Individual Rights
Outline: Freedom of association
Question: A state adopted a rule denying admission to its bar to anyone who was currently or had previously been a member of a subversive group. The state's bar application form was modified to ask applicants whether they were or had previously been members of any subversive organization. An applicant refused to answer the question and was denied bar admission on that basis. The applicant challenged the decision, arguing that the question infringed upon his freedom of association. Is the applicant likely to prevail?
Answer A: A: No, because membership in a subversive group constitutes endorsement of the group's illegal activities.
Answer B: B: No, because the Constitution does not apply to the bar.
Answer C: C: Yes, because denying bar admission based on any association with a subversive organization violates the First Amendment.
Answer D: D: Yes, because denying bar admission based solely on past membership in a subversive organization violates the First Amendment.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: D is correct. The freedom of association under the First Amendment prevents a state from inquiring about an applicant's associations for the purpose of withholding a right or benefit due to the individual's beliefs. Despite the legitimate interest in determining the character and professional competence of bar applicants, the state may use other means to determine this, which are less restrictive of First Amendment freedoms. See Shelton v. Tucker, 364 U.S. 479 (1960).
Wrong Answer Explanations: A is incorrect. A person's prior membership in an organization is not an endorsement of illegal activities of that organization unless that person had a specific intent to further those illegal activities. B is incorrect. The fact that the state created the rule at issue means that the state action requirement is satisfied and thus the rule must comply with the First Amendment. C is incorrect. This answer misstates the law; a state may in fact deny bar admission to anyone who is a member of a subversive organization if they have a specific intention to further the illegal activities of the organization. See Scales v. United States, 367 U.S. 203 (1961).
additional_info: Forcing disclosure of First Amendment activities as a condition of bar membership may have a chilling effect. Thus, the state may not force every prospective employee to disclose every membership because such a broad disclosure has insufficient relation to loyalty and professional competence, and the state has less drastic means available to achieve its purpose. See Shelton v. Tucker, 364 U.S. 479 (1960). The state may inquire only into those activities that are relevant to the position, and if the candidate refuses to answer relevant questions, employment or bar admission may be denied. Konigsberg v. State Bar of California, 366 U.S. 36 (1961). Furthermore, the U.S. Supreme Court has held that states may deny bar admission to individuals who are members of subversive organizations if, but only if, they have a specific intention to further the illegal activities of the organization. See Scales v. United States, 367 U.S. 203 (1961). D is correct. B is incorrect. The rule at issue was adopted by the state, and thus it constitutes state action and must comply with the First Amendment.
