Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Specific and General Jurisdiction
Question: Lydian Press, a small Vermont publisher, maintains a website listing its staff, mission, and email address. The site does not accept orders, run ads targeted by state, collect payments, or ship products. A reader in Arizona saw an article excerpt on the website and sued Lydian Press in Arizona for defamation. Arizona's long-arm statute extends to the due-process limit. Is website accessibility alone enough for specific jurisdiction in Arizona?
Answer A: Yes, because any public website is accessible in Arizona
Answer B: Yes, because Arizona's long-arm statute reaches due process
Answer C: No, because a passive, informational website without forum targeting or transactions is not enough by itself
Answer D: No, because internet contacts are categorically ignored in personal-jurisdiction analysis
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The site was passive and untargeted. Arizona access alone does not create defendant-created suit-related contacts with Arizona.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Mere accessibility does not show purposeful targeting of Arizona. Otherwise every public website would support jurisdiction everywhere. B: A full long-arm statute still requires minimum contacts. D: Interactive, commercial, or targeted internet contacts can support jurisdiction.
lb_rule_reference: Passive online accessibility alone does not show purposeful availment of every state where the site can be read.
