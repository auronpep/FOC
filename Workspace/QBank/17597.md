Question: A K-9 unit alerted to the odor of narcotics from the exterior of a car parked on a public street. Officers had probable cause based solely on the dog alert. They searched the vehicle without a warrant and found heroin in a paper bag in the trunk. The driver moves to suppress, arguing that because the vehicle was stationary on the street and not moving, the exigency rationale for the automobile exception was absent. Was the warrantless search of the vehicle constitutional?
Answer A: No, because the automobile exception requires both probable cause and exigency, and a parked car presents no exigency
Answer B: No, because a K-9 alert is not sufficient probable cause on its own
Answer C: Yes, because the K-9 alert provided probable cause to believe contraband was in the vehicle; the automobile exception requires only probable cause and no separate exigency showing
Answer D: Yes, but only because the trunk is a separate compartment that requires independent probable cause beyond the K-9 alert to the exterior
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The automobile exception, rooted in Carroll v. United States, authorizes a warrantless search whenever police have probable cause to believe a vehicle contains contraband or evidence of a crime. Florida v. Harris confirms that a trained K-9 alert constitutes probable cause. The fact that the vehicle was stationary is irrelevant — the Supreme Court has expressly rejected the requirement of a separate exigency for parked vehicles. The search of the trunk (and the bag within it — California v. Acevedo) was constitutional. Suppression is denied.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Supreme Court has made clear that the automobile exception does not require a separate showing of exigency. Once there is probable cause to believe a vehicle contains contraband, no warrant is needed — regardless of whether the vehicle is parked or moving. The reduced expectation of privacy in automobiles and their inherent mobility are the categorical reasons for the exception; individual cases need not show actual exigency. B: Florida v. Harris held that a reliable, trained K-9's alert to the odor of narcotics from a vehicle provides probable cause. A qualified dog alert is not merely a tip — it is direct evidence of narcotics. No additional corroboration is required once the dog's reliability is established. D: When probable cause to search a vehicle arises (here, from the K-9 alert), officers may search the entire vehicle and any containers within it that could conceal the contraband — including the trunk. California v. Acevedo allows search of containers in a vehicle when there is probable cause to believe the container holds contraband. No separate probable cause to the trunk is required.
lb_rule_reference: The automobile exception allows a warrantless search of a vehicle when police have probable cause to believe it contains contraband or evidence; no separate showing of exigency is required (Carroll v. United States; California v. Acevedo; Collins v. Virginia limits exception to areas with REE)
