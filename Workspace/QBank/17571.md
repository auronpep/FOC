MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Intentional Torts
Outline: Battery
Question: A shed owner was tired of nighttime tool thefts. Without warning signs, he rigged a spring device inside the shed to fire a sharpened dart at anyone opening the door. A trespasser opened the door to steal tools and was seriously injured by the dart. The trespasser sues the shed owner for battery. Can the shed owner defeat the trespasser's battery claim by relying on defense of property?
Answer A: Yes, because the trespasser was committing a theft
Answer B: No, because a spring dart that risks serious injury is excessive force to protect property alone
Answer C: Yes, because no person was present to apply force directly
Answer D: No, because property owners may never use any force to protect property
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Mechanical traps are judged by the same limits as personal force. Serious force is not privileged when used solely to protect tools.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Defense of property does not permit deadly or seriously injurious force solely to protect property. C: A mechanical device set by the defendant can be an intentional application of force, and it cannot exceed the force the owner could lawfully use in person. D: Reasonable nondeadly force may be privileged. This owner loses because the force was seriously injurious and mechanical, not because all force is forbidden.
lb_rule_reference: A property owner may use reasonable nondeadly force to protect property, but may not use deadly or seriously injurious mechanical force solely to protect property.
