Question: A broker contracted with Aaron to buy a rare lamp from Aaron. The broker knew that Joseph, a collector, wanted the lamp and planned to sell it to Joseph at a profit. The broker told Aaron, “Joseph will be thrilled if this deal closes,” but the contract required Aaron to deliver the lamp only to the broker and did not mention Joseph. Aaron failed to deliver. Joseph sued Aaron as a third-party beneficiary. Is Joseph likely to recover on the broker-Aaron contract?
Answer A: Yes, because Aaron knew Joseph was the broker's expected resale customer.
Answer B: Yes, because Joseph would have received the lamp if the broker's plan succeeded.
Answer C: No, because contracts involving rare goods can never benefit third parties.
Answer D: No, because Joseph was only an incidental downstream beneficiary of the broker's purchase contract.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: Joseph is likely an incidental beneficiary. Aaron's duty was to deliver the lamp to the broker, and Joseph's expected resale benefit was a byproduct of the broker's separate plan. Knowledge of a resale customer does not show intent to give that customer enforcement rights.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because knowledge of the resale customer is not enough. B: B is wrong because expected practical benefit is not intended-beneficiary status. C: C is wrong because rare-goods contracts can create intended beneficiaries if performance is directed to the third party; this one was not.
