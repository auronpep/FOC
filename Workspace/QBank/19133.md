Question: Marcus had been quietly seething for weeks after his business partner, Terrence, embezzled funds from their shared company. One afternoon, Marcus received a bank statement confirming the theft, then sat at his kitchen table for twenty minutes reading through the documents. He then retrieved a handgun from his bedroom, drove thirty minutes to Terrence's office, waited in the parking lot for Terrence to exit, and shot him twice in the chest. Terrence died at the scene. Marcus was charged with first-degree murder. Is Marcus guilty of first-degree murder?
Answer A: No, because Marcus acted out of anger and was not calm when he pulled the trigger
Answer B: No, because twenty minutes is too short a time period to constitute deliberation
Answer C: Yes, because Marcus formed the intent to kill after a period of cool reflection and then carried out a planned killing
Answer D: Yes, but only because Marcus used a firearm, which elevates the charge to first-degree murder
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: First-degree murder at common law requires a willful, premeditated, and deliberate killing. Premeditation means prior thought; deliberation means the intent was formed after cool reflection rather than in the heat of passion. Here, Marcus sat quietly for twenty minutes reviewing documents, retrieved a weapon, drove to the victim's location, and waited — all indicators of advance planning and cool reflection. This is the paradigm case of first-degree murder.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Deliberation does not require the defendant to be emotionally detached at the moment of the killing. It requires cool reflection at the time the decision to kill was formed — here, the twenty minutes of document review and methodical travel satisfy that standard. B: Common law does not require any minimum time period. Even a brief interval of cool reflection satisfies deliberation. The quality of thought, not its duration, controls. Twenty minutes of focused review followed by deliberate preparation easily satisfies the standard. D: At common law, use of a firearm does not by itself elevate homicide to first-degree murder. The elevation turns on premeditation and deliberation, not the weapon used. D reaches the right result for the wrong reason.
lb_rule_reference: First-degree murder requires both premeditation and deliberation — meaning the defendant must have thought about the killing beforehand AND formed the intent after a period of cool, calm reflection, however brief
