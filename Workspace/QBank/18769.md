Subtopic: Negligence
Outline: Damages
Question: A competitor falsely emailed several grocery buyers that a jam maker's new jars contained metal fragments. The competitor knew the claim was false and sent it to steer buyers to its own brand. Three named stores canceled purchase orders because of the email, causing the jam maker a specific lost profit. Is the jam maker likely to prevail on a trade libel claim against the competitor?
Answer A: No, because the statement criticized the product rather than the owner's personal reputation
Answer B: Yes, because the competitor knowingly published a false product-disparagement statement causing specific lost sales
Answer C: Yes, because trade libel uses the same presumed-damages rule as libel
Answer D: No, because only statements to the general public count as publication
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The statement disparaged the product, was false, was published with improper motive, and caused identified pecuniary loss.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Trade libel specifically protects economic interests in products or business property. C: Trade libel generally requires proof of special pecuniary loss; the claim works here because lost orders are identified. D: Publication to third-party buyers is enough for trade libel if it causes economic loss.
lb_rule_reference: Trade libel protects economic interests in property or products and generally requires falsity, publication, malice or improper motive, and special damages.
