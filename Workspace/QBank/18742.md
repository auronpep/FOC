Outline: Use of impeachment material
Question: In a federal negligence trial, Rachel testifies for the plaintiff that she saw the defendant drive through a stop sign. The defendant offers a certified judgment showing that Rachel was convicted of perjury three years ago. The defendant argues that the conviction proves Rachel is lying in this case and also proves that the defendant did not run the stop sign. Rachel objects. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the conviction because convictions may be used only in criminal cases.
Answer B: Admit the conviction to attack Rachel's credibility, but not as substantive proof of whether the defendant ran the stop sign.
Answer C: Admit the conviction as substantive proof that Rachel's testimony about the stop sign is false.
Answer D: Exclude the conviction unless Rachel first testifies that she is a truthful person.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: A perjury conviction is admissible to attack a witness's character for truthfulness under FRE 609(a)(2). But the conviction does not substantively prove what happened at the intersection. On timely request, the court should limit the evidence to credibility under FRE 105.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because conviction impeachment applies in civil cases as well. C: C is wrong because impeachment material does not automatically prove the opposite of the witness's testimony. D: D is wrong because a conviction may attack credibility without prior truthful-character bolstering.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 609 and 105 / conviction used for impeachment only
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests limited-purpose use of conviction impeachment material and the substantive/impeachment split.
