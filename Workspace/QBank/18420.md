Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Double Jeopardy
Question: Ned was prosecuted in state court for assault with a deadly weapon based on an attack that also violated federal law criminalizing assault on a federal officer. The state jury acquitted Ned of all charges. The federal government then indicted Ned for the federal offense of assault on a federal officer arising from the identical incident. Ned moves to dismiss the federal indictment on double jeopardy grounds. Should the federal court dismiss the indictment?
Answer A: Yes, because Ned was acquitted by a jury of charges arising from the same conduct, and the Double Jeopardy Clause absolutely bars any subsequent prosecution for the same conduct
Answer B: Yes, because the state acquittal has preclusive effect on federal prosecution under principles of collateral estoppel
Answer C: No, because the dual sovereignty doctrine permits both federal and state prosecution for the same conduct, and a state acquittal does not bar a federal prosecution
Answer D: No, because the federal offense of assault on a federal officer requires elements not present in the state assault charge, satisfying the *Blockburger* test
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The dual sovereignty doctrine is well established under *United States v. Lanza* (1922) and reaffirmed in *Gamble v. United States* (2019). The Double Jeopardy Clause prohibits successive prosecutions by the same sovereign for the same offense. The federal government and state governments are separate sovereigns, each with independent legislative authority. A state prosecution and acquittal do not bar a subsequent federal prosecution for a violation of federal law, even if the conduct was identical. The federal indictment stands.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Double Jeopardy Clause bars successive prosecutions by the same sovereign. Under the dual sovereignty doctrine, the United States and the individual states are separate sovereigns, each deriving its authority from a different source. A prosecution by one sovereign does not bar prosecution by the other, even for the same underlying conduct. *United States v. Lanza* (1922) and *Bartkus v. Illinois* (1959) confirm this doctrine. B: Collateral estoppel under *Ashe v. Swenson* precludes relitigation of issues necessarily resolved in a prior proceeding — but this doctrine applies to prosecutions by the same sovereign. The dual sovereignty doctrine prevents its extension across sovereign lines. Federal courts are not bound by state jury acquittals because the federal government was not a party to the state proceeding and derives independent sovereignty. D: The *Blockburger* test is the correct tool when analyzing successive prosecutions by the same sovereign. Here, the threshold issue is that different sovereigns are involved. The correct analysis is the dual sovereignty doctrine, not *Blockburger*. While the offenses may also differ under *Blockburger*, reaching *Blockburger* implies the dual sovereignty issue does not exist, which is doctrinally misleading.
lb_rule_reference: The dual sovereignty doctrine permits both federal and state governments to prosecute a defendant for the same underlying conduct without violating the Double Jeopardy Clause; each sovereign's laws define separate offenses.
