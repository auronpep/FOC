Question: The United States ratifies a treaty promising that signatory nations will create a private cause of action for workers injured by certain cross-border pollutants. The treaty text says each nation "shall enact appropriate implementing legislation." Congress never enacts such legislation. A worker sues a private company in federal court relying only on the treaty. What is the company's strongest defense?
Answer A: The treaty automatically creates a private federal cause of action because treaties are part of federal law
Answer B: The worker cannot rely on the treaty alone because the treaty calls for implementing legislation that Congress has not enacted
Answer C: The treaty is invalid because treaties may never affect private parties
Answer D: The treaty is enforceable only if the President signs a separate executive order after ratification
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The treaty binds internationally but directs each nation to enact implementing legislation. Without self-executing text or implementing statute, it does not itself create the asserted private cause of action.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Not every treaty is self-executing. Text calling for implementing legislation usually indicates that domestic enforcement requires Congress to act. C: Treaties and implementing statutes can affect private rights. The issue is self-execution and implementation, not a categorical private-party bar. D: The required implementation here is congressional legislation creating the cause of action; a general presidential order cannot supply missing statutory authorization.
lb_rule_reference: A treaty may bind the United States internationally, but domestic enforceability can require self-execution or implementing legislation.
