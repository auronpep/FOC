Question: Crawford was charged with second-degree murder. The evidence against her was strong, including two eyewitness accounts and cell-phone records placing her at the scene. Crawford's attorney negotiated a plea to voluntary manslaughter, avoiding a potential life sentence. Crawford consistently maintained she did not commit the killing but agreed to the plea to avoid the risk of a harsher sentence. At the hearing, Crawford told the judge, "I am not saying I did this, but I understand the evidence against me and I am choosing to plead guilty to take advantage of the plea offer." The judge accepted the plea. Was the judge's acceptance of Crawford's guilty plea while she maintained her innocence constitutionally permissible?
Answer A: No, because a defendant must personally admit guilt for a guilty plea to be valid under the Constitution
Answer B: No, because accepting a plea from a defendant who maintains innocence violates the defendant's privilege against self-incrimination
Answer C: Yes, because under *North Carolina v. Alford*, a defendant may enter a knowing and voluntary guilty plea while maintaining innocence if there is a strong factual basis for the charge in the record
Answer D: Yes, but only because Crawford's attorney recommended the plea; a defendant acting pro se cannot enter an Alford plea
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: North Carolina v. Alford* (1970) held that a trial court may accept a guilty plea from a defendant who simultaneously maintains innocence. The Constitution does not bar such a plea provided it meets two conditions: (1) the plea is knowing, intelligent, and voluntary; and (2) there is a strong factual basis on the record supporting the guilt of the defendant (sufficient evidence the jury could find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt). Both conditions are satisfied here — Crawford understood the proceedings and strong evidence existed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: North Carolina v. Alford* (1970) held that a defendant may enter a valid guilty plea even while protesting innocence, as long as the plea is knowing and voluntary and is supported by strong evidence of guilt in the record. Crawford's statement is a classic Alford plea. B: The privilege against self-incrimination is relevant to the voluntariness of confessions and guilty pleas, but an Alford plea is not compelled self-incrimination — it is a voluntary choice by the defendant to accept a plea bargain while declining to admit the underlying acts. *Alford* addressed this directly: a knowing voluntary plea is not compelled. D: Alford* does not condition the validity of an innocence-maintaining plea on representation by counsel. The requirements are knowing and voluntariness of the plea and a sufficient factual record, not the presence of counsel per se. A counseled plea may be easier to show was knowing, but the Alford doctrine does not bar pro se defendants.
lb_rule_reference: Under *North Carolina v. Alford*, a defendant may enter a constitutionally valid guilty plea while simultaneously maintaining innocence, provided the plea is knowing and voluntary and there is a strong factual basis on the record supporting the charge
