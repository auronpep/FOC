Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: After Miranda warnings, Trent said, "I do not want to talk about this." The detective immediately replied, "This is your only chance to explain yourself," and continued questioning. Five minutes later, Trent confessed to the burglary. Should Trent's confession be suppressed from the prosecution's case-in-chief?
Answer A: Yes, because police failed to honor Trent's clear invocation of the right to remain silent
Answer B: Yes, because any suspect who receives Miranda warnings can never be questioned again
Answer C: No, because Trent did not ask for a lawyer
Answer D: No, because Trent eventually answered voluntarily
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Trent clearly invoked silence. The detective immediately continued interrogation instead of stopping, so the resulting confession is suppressible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Police may question after valid waiver and may sometimes reapproach after honoring silence. The defect here is immediate continued interrogation after a clear invocation. C: A clear invocation of silence also requires police to stop and scrupulously honor the choice. D: Continued police pressure after invocation undermines reliance on the later answer as a valid waiver.
lb_rule_reference: When a suspect clearly says he does not want to talk, police must stop interrogation and may not immediately continue pressing for a statement.
