Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Discretionary exclusion (undue prejudice, confusion, waste of time)
Question: In a federal products-liability trial, Hannah claims a pressure cooker exploded because its locking valve was defective. Hannah offers evidence that a different brand of pressure cooker exploded ten years earlier after a user removed its safety valve. The defendant objects under Rule 403. Which ruling is most appropriate?
Answer A: Admit the evidence because all other accidents involving pressure cookers are automatically relevant.
Answer B: Admit the evidence because the prior accident resulted in an injury.
Answer C: Exclude the evidence if the dissimilar conditions make it likely to mislead the jury or create a side trial with little probative value.
Answer D: Exclude the evidence only if the prior injured user is unavailable to testify.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Other-accident evidence depends on purpose-sensitive similarity. A different brand, different cause, and remote timing may make the evidence weak and misleading, creating a risk of confusion or a collateral mini-trial that substantially outweighs probative value.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because other accidents are not automatically relevant in a useful way without sufficient similarity. B: B is wrong because the fact of injury does not establish probative similarity. D: D is wrong because witness availability is not the Rule 403 similarity-and-confusion test.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 403
lb_student_diagnostic: Student treats the label 'other accident' as enough and misses similarity/confusion balancing.
