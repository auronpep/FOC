Outline: Right to Confrontation
Question: During a violent confrontation, Serge intentionally sliced off Carlton's left ear with a knife. Carlton, who was right-handed, retained full use of both arms, his legs, his right eye, and all other limbs. He suffered no functional impairment of any capacity useful to fighting. He was, however, permanently and severely disfigured. Serge is charged with common-law mayhem under the traditional formulation, which the court defines as follows: "Unlawful and malicious removal of a body member that renders the victim less able to fight." Under the court's stated definition — which tracks the narrow traditional common-law fighting-capacity formulation — is Serge guilty of mayhem?
Answer A: Yes, because an ear is part of the body and Serge intentionally removed it with malice
Answer B: Yes, because the injury was permanent and disfiguring, and all permanent disfigurements qualify as mayhem
Answer C: No, because under the court's narrow fighting-capacity definition, removal of an ear does not render a person less able to fight — no functional combat impairment was caused
Answer D: No, because Carlton was the initial aggressor and assumed the risk of injury during a violent confrontation
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The court defined mayhem as unlawful and malicious removal of a body part that renders the victim less able to fight. This is the traditional, narrow common-law formulation under which mayhem covered eyes, arms, hands, legs, and feet — body parts with fighting utility. An ear has no recognized fighting function; its removal causes disfigurement but not a diminution of fighting capacity. Carlton retained full physical fighting ability. Under the definition stated, the charge fails because the element — rendering less able to fight — is not met. Serge would have been guilty under the expanded disfigurement formulation, but the court applied the narrow one.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The court defined mayhem narrowly as removal of a body part that renders the victim less able to fight. An ear does not affect fighting capacity under the traditional formulation — it does not aid in striking, defending, or physically resisting attack. Under this specific definition, the injury must impair fighting, not merely the body. This question turns on the precise scope of the court's stated definition. B: The question states the court applies the narrow fighting-capacity formulation, not the expanded disfigurement formulation. The disfigurement expansion was a later development and is not part of the definition provided. The answer erroneously applies a broader modern or expanded definition the court has not adopted. D: Assumption of risk is a civil tort doctrine with no direct application to criminal mayhem. Voluntary participation in a fight does not constitute consent to permanent disfigurement or dismemberment; criminal law does not recognize consent to mayhem as a defense in most formulations. Moreover, the stated reason for no liability is the failure to satisfy the fighting-capacity element, not anything Carlton did.
lb_rule_reference: Traditional common-law mayhem covers two categories: (1) dismemberment or disablement of a body part useful in fighting (eye, arm, leg, hand, foot), and (2) later-expanded disfigurement of the face or body; cutting off an ear historically qualified only as disfigurement, not fighting-capacity mayhem
