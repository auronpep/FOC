Subtopic: Presentation of Evidence
Outline: Materiality and probative value
Question: Jonah is sued after a car collision at 10 p.m. The plaintiff offers evidence that Jonah's blood-alcohol level was above the legal limit in a test taken at midnight. Jonah objects that the test is irrelevant because it was taken two hours after the collision. How should the court rule, assuming no other exclusion applies?
Answer A: Exclude the test because blood-alcohol evidence is relevant only if taken immediately at the scene.
Answer B: Exclude the test unless the plaintiff proves Jonah had nothing to drink after the collision.
Answer C: Admit the test only if the plaintiff has an eyewitness who saw Jonah drinking before the collision.
Answer D: Admit the test because it has some tendency to show Jonah's condition at the time of the collision; the two-hour delay affects weight.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: A later blood-alcohol test can be probative of the driver's condition at the relevant time, even if the time gap creates questions about intervening drinking or metabolism. Those questions ordinarily affect weight.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because immediate testing is not required for threshold relevance. B: B is wrong because the proponent need not eliminate all intervening-drinking possibilities before admission. C: C is wrong because eyewitness drinking testimony is not required for the test to have probative value.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401 / FRE 402
lb_student_diagnostic: Treating time-gap weakness as a relevance bar.
