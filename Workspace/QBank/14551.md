Question: A builder borrowed $10,000 from a lender to finance a small construction job under a contract with a homeowner. The builder gave the lender a writing that stated, "Any money I receive from the homeowner will be paid immediately to the lender, regardless of any demands from other creditors." The builder died after completing the job but before the homeowner paid. The lender demanded that the homeowner pay the $10,000 due to the builder directly to the lender. The homeowner refused, saying that he would pay directly to the builder's estate everything that he owed the builder. Is the lender likely to succeed in an action against the homeowner for $10,000?
Answer A: A: No, because the builder's death terminated the lender's right to receive payment directly from the homeowner.
Answer B: B: No, because the writing the builder gave to the lender did not transfer to the lender the right to receive payment from the homeowner.
Answer C: C: Yes, because the builder had manifested an intent that the homeowner pay the $10,000 directly to the lender.
Answer D: D: Yes, because the lender is an intended beneficiary of the builder-homeowner contract.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: B is correct. There was no effective assignment of the builder's rights to the lender. The writing did not sufficiently establish the builder's intent to immediately and completely extinguish the builder's rights under the contract with the homeowner and transfer them to the lender. A
Wrong Answer Explanations: A is incorrect. Without an effective assignment, there were no rights transferred from the builder to the lender that could have been extinguished by the builder's death. Therefore, the builder's death is irrelevant. C is incorrect. Even if the builder subjectively intended for the homeowner to pay the $10,000 directly to the lender, the writing was not sufficiently clear to create a valid assignment of rights. D is incorrect. Any benefit to the lender from the builder-homeowner contract would have been merely incidental, not intentional, given that there is no evidence the homeowner intended to benefit any third party's interest with payment of the $10,000.
