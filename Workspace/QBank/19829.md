Subtopic: Jurisdiction and Venue
Outline: Specific and General Jurisdiction
Question: Caldex Logistics Inc. is a freight company incorporated in Texas with its principal place of business in Houston. To comply with the State of Verano's Foreign Corporation Act, Caldex registered to do business in Verano and designated a registered agent for service of process located in Verano's capital. The Verano statute expressly states: "A foreign corporation that registers to transact business in Verano consents to the exercise of general jurisdiction by courts of this state with respect to any cause of action, whether or not arising from activities in Verano." Caldex operates a single distribution center in Verano employing 60 workers, but all of its significant executive decisions are made in Houston. A Texas resident who was injured in an accident in New Mexico by a Caldex truck sued Caldex in Verano state court. Caldex moves to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction, arguing it is not "at home" in Verano under *Daimler AG v. Bauman*. How should the Verano court rule on Caldex's motion to dismiss?
Answer A: Grant the motion, because *Daimler* limits general jurisdiction to the state of incorporation and the principal-place-of-business state, and Caldex's Verano registration cannot expand that limit
Answer B: Grant the motion, because Caldex's 60-employee distribution center is insufficient to establish minimum contacts with Verano for purposes of a New Mexico accident
Answer C: Deny the motion, because Caldex consented to general jurisdiction in Verano by registering under a statute that expressly conditions registration on such consent, and this consent-based jurisdiction is constitutional under Mallory
Answer D: Deny the motion, because Caldex's voluntary maintenance of a Verano distribution center creates continuous and systematic contacts that independently satisfy general jurisdiction
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The Verano Foreign Corporation Act explicitly provides that registration constitutes consent to general jurisdiction. Caldex registered to comply with the Act and designated an agent. Under *Mallory v. Norfolk Southern* (2023), a state statute that expressly links registration to general-jurisdiction consent is constitutional. *Daimler*'s at-home analysis governs contacts-based general jurisdiction, not consent-based jurisdiction. Caldex voluntarily submitted to Verano's courts for all claims by complying with the registration statute; the motion should be denied.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Daimler* analyzed general jurisdiction predicated on the defendant's contacts with the forum, not consent. The Supreme Court in *Mallory v. Norfolk Southern* (2023) held that *Daimler* does not foreclose consent-based general jurisdiction. When a corporation voluntarily registers under a statute that expressly conditions registration on consent to general jurisdiction, it may be sued in that state on any claim. The *Daimler* at-home test governs unilateral-contact jurisdiction; it does not limit what a defendant may contractually or statutorily consent to. B: This answer applies the specific-jurisdiction test to a general-jurisdiction consent theory. The plaintiff's claim does not need to arise from Caldex's Verano contacts when consent is the basis for jurisdiction. If Caldex validly consented to general jurisdiction by registration, the court may hear any claim regardless of where the underlying events occurred, without running a minimum-contacts analysis. D: After *Daimler*, a single in-state distribution center with 60 employees is not sufficient for contacts-based general jurisdiction. The correct basis for denying the motion is Caldex's statutory consent under Verano law — not the scope of its physical operations. An answer that reaches the right procedural outcome for a legally incorrect reason tests whether students understand the doctrine, not just the conclusion.
lb_rule_reference: A state may require foreign corporations to designate an in-state agent for service of process as a condition of doing business, and such registration may constitute consent to general jurisdiction in that state if the statute so provides. *Pennsylvania Fire Ins. Co. v. Gold Issue Mining Co.* (1917); *Mallory v. Norfolk Southern Ry. Co.* (2023). Questions should note the post-*Mallory* framework and state the applicable statute clearly.
