Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: After arresting Sheila for an alleged domestic assault, officers transported her to the police station and began booking her. The booking officer asked standard questions: name, address, date of birth. The officer then asked, "Are there any weapons at your home address?" Sheila responded, "There's a knife in my bedroom I used tonight." Officers retrieved the knife, which bore the victim's blood. Sheila had not been given Miranda warnings before the booking questions. She moves to suppress her statement about the knife. Should the court suppress Sheila's statement about the knife?
Answer A: No, because all questions asked during the booking process fall within the booking exception to Miranda
Answer B: No, because the question about weapons was asked for officer safety, which is a legitimate non-investigative purpose
Answer C: Yes, because the question about weapons was reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response and fell outside the routine booking exception
Answer D: Yes, because Sheila was in custody at the moment of booking and any question by police requires Miranda warnings
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Miranda requires warnings before custodial interrogation. The booking exception exempts only routine administrative questions (name, address, DOB) from the interrogation definition. The question here — whether there were weapons at the home address following a domestic assault — was not administrative; it was reasonably likely to elicit incriminating information about the crime charged. Because Sheila was in custody and the question constituted interrogation without Miranda warnings, her response must be suppressed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The booking exception covers only routine administrative questions designed to process the arrestee, not questions that are reasonably likely to elicit incriminating responses. "Are there any weapons at your home address?" is not a routine administrative question — it is designed to elicit information about the crime and is reasonably likely to produce incriminating evidence. It falls outside the booking exception. B: The public safety exception under *Quarles* applies when there is an objectively reasonable need to address an immediate public safety threat. A question about weapons at a home address when the suspect is already in custody and no officers are entering the home imminently does not satisfy the *Quarles* standard. Furthermore, the answer obtained concerned the crime itself, not an imminent safety threat. D: Even in custody, not every question by police is interrogation. The booking exception is an established doctrine allowing routine administrative questions without warnings. The correct analysis identifies which questions fall within the exception (name, DOB, address) and which do not (the weapons question). The answer is correct only as to the weapons question, not as an absolute rule.
lb_rule_reference: Routine booking questions — name, address, date of birth, height, weight, occupation — are exempt from Miranda because they are administrative, not interrogation; but a question reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response falls outside the exception even during booking.
