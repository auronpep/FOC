Subtopic: General Principles
Outline: Defense of property
Question: Nina sees a teenager trying to steal her parked motorcycle from her driveway. The teenager is unarmed and is moving away from Nina, pushing the motorcycle toward the street. Nina yells for him to stop. When he keeps moving, Nina fires a rifle at his back and seriously injures him. Nina is charged with aggravated assault. Is Nina's use of force justified as defense of property?
Answer A: Yes, because the motorcycle was valuable and the thief ignored Nina's warning
Answer B: Yes, because deadly force is allowed to stop any felony theft
Answer C: No, because deadly force is not justified solely to protect personal property
Answer D: No, because defense of property is never recognized in criminal law
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Nina was protecting a motorcycle, not herself or another person. The teenager was unarmed and moving away. Nondeadly measures might be available to protect property, but firing a rifle at a fleeing thief is deadly force. Deadly force is not justified solely to prevent property loss.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Property value and a warning do not authorize deadly force. Deadly force is reserved for person-safety threats, not property protection alone. B: Modern defense-of-property doctrine does not permit deadly force merely to stop theft of property. The fact that a theft may be a felony does not by itself create a deadly person-safety threat. D: Defense of property exists, but it is limited. Reasonable nondeadly force can be justified; deadly force for property alone cannot.
lb_rule_reference: A person may use reasonable nondeadly force to protect property, but deadly force is not justified solely to protect property
