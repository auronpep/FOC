MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Other Crimes
Outline: Larceny by trick
Question: Sylvia tells a used-car dealer that she is a licensed dealer herself and wants to buy his entire inventory of ten vehicles to resell. Based on this representation, the dealer signs over the titles to all ten cars and accepts Sylvia's personal check. Sylvia is not a licensed dealer, the check is drawn on a closed account, and she has no intention of paying. She drives away with all ten cars, title documents in hand. Of which crime is Sylvia most likely guilty?
Answer A: Larceny by trick, because Sylvia used fraudulent misrepresentations to obtain the cars
Answer B: Embezzlement, because Sylvia was entrusted with the cars in a commercial transaction
Answer C: False pretenses, because Sylvia obtained title to the vehicles by a knowingly false representation of present fact
Answer D: False pretenses, because Sylvia's check was drawn on a closed account, constituting a false promise of future payment
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: False pretenses requires: (1) a false representation of a present material fact, (2) made knowingly, (3) with intent to defraud, (4) causing the victim to pass title to the defendant. All elements are met: Sylvia falsely represented that she was a licensed dealer (present fact — her current status); she knew this was false; she intended to defraud; and the dealer signed over legal title (not merely possession) to the vehicles in reliance on that representation. Title passing is the critical distinguishing element that places this in false pretenses rather than larceny by trick.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Larceny by trick applies when the victim, induced by fraud, transfers only possession (not title) to the defendant. Here, the dealer signed over the titles — transferring legal ownership — in reliance on Sylvia's false representations. When title passes as a result of the fraud, the correct crime is false pretenses, not larceny by trick. The distinction between the two crimes turns entirely on whether possession only or full title was transferred. B: Embezzlement requires that the defendant lawfully receive property in a position of trust and then fraudulently convert it. Here, Sylvia's initial receipt of the cars was not lawful — it was obtained through fraud. Moreover, the dealer transferred title, not just possession for a limited purpose. This is not an entrustment scenario; it is a fraud-induced transfer of ownership, which is the domain of false pretenses. D: False pretenses requires a false representation of a present material fact, not a false promise of future payment. A promise to pay in the future (even one the promisor knows is false) is generally treated as a future promise, not a present fact misrepresentation, and traditionally does not satisfy false pretenses. The correct basis for false pretenses here is Sylvia's false representation of her current status as a licensed dealer — a present fact. The bad check is supporting evidence of fraudulent intent but is not itself the operative false representation of present fact.
lb_rule_reference: False pretenses is committed when a defendant obtains title to property of another by making a knowingly false representation of a present material fact, with intent to defraud. If the victim passes only possession (not title) in reliance on the fraud, the crime is larceny by trick, not false pretenses.
