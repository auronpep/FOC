MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Intentional Torts
Outline: Intentional infliction of emotional distress
Question: A supervisor knew an employee had recently returned from treatment for a severe elevator phobia. For two weeks, the supervisor repeatedly assigned the employee to deliver empty folders to the top floor, then mocked the employee over the office speaker when she shook and cried. The employee had severe panic attacks and needed additional treatment. The employee sues the supervisor for intentional infliction of emotional distress. Is the employee likely to prevail in an intentional infliction of emotional distress claim against the supervisor?
Answer A: No, because assigning deliveries is ordinarily a normal workplace task
Answer B: Yes, because repeated exploitation of a known vulnerability caused severe emotional distress
Answer C: No, because IIED is unavailable unless the employee was physically touched
Answer D: Yes, because any workplace teasing that upsets an employee is outrageous
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The supervisor knowingly targeted the employee's severe vulnerability, repeated the conduct, publicly mocked her, and caused severe distress requiring treatment.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Context matters. Repetition, abuse of workplace authority, public mockery, and known vulnerability can make otherwise ordinary acts outrageous. C: IIED does not require physical touch or impact. Severe emotional distress is the relevant harm. D: Not every workplace slight is outrageous. Liability depends on the specific aggravating facts, especially known vulnerability and severe distress.
lb_rule_reference: Repeated harassment exploiting a known vulnerability can make conduct extreme and outrageous when it causes severe emotional distress.
