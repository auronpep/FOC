Question: Ruth contracted with a builder to repair Ruth's house and install safety rails for Ruth's father, Isaac. The contract stated that any claim for defective work had to be made in writing within 30 days after completion. Isaac discovered defective rails after completion but waited four months before sending any written notice. He then sued the builder as an intended beneficiary. What is the builder's best defense?
Answer A: Isaac's claim is barred by the contract's written-claim requirement if that requirement is valid and applies to the rail work.
Answer B: Isaac cannot enforce any contract term because he was not a party.
Answer C: Isaac may ignore the notice requirement because it was agreed to by Ruth, not Isaac.
Answer D: Isaac wins automatically because the rails were installed for his benefit.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A beneficiary who enforces a contract generally must accept the contract's conditions and procedural limits. If the written-claim requirement validly applies, Isaac's late notice bars the claim even though he was the intended recipient of the safety rails.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because intended beneficiaries may enforce contract promises. C: C is wrong because Isaac cannot enforce the benefit while avoiding applicable claim procedures. D: D is wrong because intended status does not override the contract's notice condition.
