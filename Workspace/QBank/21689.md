Question: Detectives investigating an arson-murder deliberately employed a two-step strategy: they questioned Joyce for ninety minutes without Miranda warnings until she admitted she planned the fire. Then they gave Miranda warnings, obtained a written waiver, and resumed questioning. They confronted Joyce with her earlier admissions and asked her to repeat and confirm them. Joyce repeated the confession. The trial record shows the strategy was intentional departmental policy. Joyce moves to suppress the post-warning confession. Should the post-warning confession be suppressed?
Answer A: No, because Joyce received Miranda warnings before the second confession and waived her rights in writing
Answer B: No, because under *Oregon v. Elstad*, a subsequent warned confession is admissible even if a prior unwarned statement was obtained
Answer C: Yes, because the police deliberately used a two-step tactic to undermine Miranda, rendering the post-warning confession inadmissible under *Missouri v. Seibert*
Answer D: Yes, because any unwarned statement automatically taints all subsequent statements regardless of warnings given
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: In *Missouri v. Seibert* (2004), the Supreme Court suppressed a post-warning confession obtained through a deliberate two-step interrogation strategy. The plurality held that when police intentionally withhold Miranda warnings to obtain an unwarned confession, then administer warnings and exploit the prior confession to obtain a repetition, the warnings are ineffective. The post-warning confession is the product of the tainted unwarned confession, not of a fresh, independent decision. Because the record here demonstrates deliberate departmental policy, *Seibert* controls and the second confession is suppressed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Missouri v. Seibert* (2004) specifically addressed this scenario and held that a deliberate two-step interrogation tactic renders the post-warning statement inadmissible. When police intentionally extract an unwarned confession and then exploit it to secure a Mirandized repetition, the warnings are not effective because the suspect already "let the cat out of the bag." A written waiver does not cure a deliberately exploitative two-step tactic. B: Elstad* applies when the first Miranda violation was inadvertent or based on a good-faith mistake — not when police deliberately used the two-step tactic to circumvent Miranda. *Missouri v. Seibert* is the controlling case when the record shows a deliberate strategy to undermine warnings. The distinction between inadvertent violation (*Elstad*) and deliberate tactic (*Seibert*) is critical. D: This is precisely what *Oregon v. Elstad* rejected. An inadvertent Miranda violation followed by a properly warned statement does not automatically taint the subsequent statement. The rule is not categorical; it depends on whether the initial failure was deliberate (*Seibert*) or inadvertent (*Elstad*). The correct answer here is yes, but for the specific *Seibert* deliberate-tactic reason, not for a blanket automatic-taint rule.
lb_rule_reference: Under *Missouri v. Seibert* (2004), when police deliberately use a two-step interrogation tactic — obtaining an unwarned confession, then administering Miranda warnings and re-eliciting the same confession — the post-warning statement is inadmissible; under *Oregon v. Elstad*, an inadvertent Miranda violation followed by a properly warned statement does not require suppression of the second statement.
