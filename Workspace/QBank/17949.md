Question: Mary hired John to maintain elevators in her apartment building under a signed services agreement for “priority support.” The agreement was complete and exclusive but did not define that phrase. In an evidentiary ruling, the court admitted proof that both parties used “priority support” to mean dispatching a technician by the next business day. John then waited four business days to dispatch a technician after an elevator stopped working. Assuming the court adopts that meaning of “priority support,” what is the likely legal effect?
Answer A: Mary cannot recover because a complete and exclusive writing omitted a specific dispatch deadline.
Answer B: Mary cannot recover because ambiguity evidence may be considered only for admissibility, never for interpreting the parties' duties.
Answer C: Mary can recover if the late dispatch breached the priority-support duty as interpreted by the court.
Answer D: Mary can recover only if John fraudulently omitted the dispatch deadline from the written agreement.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that evidence admitted to explain an ambiguous term can affect the legal content of the contract once the court adopts that interpretation. The trigger facts are the undefined “priority support” term, the admitted meaning evidence, and the four-business-day dispatch. The decisive lock is call type: this question asks legal effect after interpretation, not merely whether the evidence is admissible. If “priority support” means next-business-day dispatch, John's later dispatch can breach that duty.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because complete integration bars added duties but does not erase an interpreted duty contained in an ambiguous term. B: B is wrong because adopted meaning evidence informs the contract's legal content; it is not a sterile admissibility exercise. D: D is wrong because fraud is not required when the route is ambiguity explanation.
