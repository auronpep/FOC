MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: At 6 a.m., six officers entered Marco's apartment with an arrest warrant for his roommate. They separated Marco from his family, kept him in the kitchen with two armed officers blocking the only doorway, took his phone, and told him he could not leave until they finished. Without warnings, a detective questioned Marco for an hour about drugs found in the apartment. Marco confessed that the drugs were his. Is Marco's confession admissible in the prosecution's case-in-chief?
Answer A: Yes, because questioning in a person's own home is never custodial for Miranda purposes
Answer B: Yes, because the officers had a warrant for the roommate, not Marco
Answer C: No, because Marco was in custody and interrogated without warnings
Answer D: No, because any statement made while police execute a warrant is automatically coerced
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: A reasonable person in Marco's position would not feel free from formal-arrest-like restraint. The detective asked questions designed to elicit incriminating responses. Because both custody and interrogation were present, Miranda warnings were required.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Location is not dispositive. Police blocked the exit, took Marco's phone, isolated him, and told him he could not leave, creating formal-arrest-like restraint. B: Miranda custody depends on Marco's actual restraint and interrogation, not on whose warrant authorized entry. D: The Miranda problem here is unwarned custodial interrogation. Coercion is a separate due process issue and is not automatic from warrant execution.
lb_rule_reference: Miranda custody exists when a reasonable person in the suspect's position would experience restraint comparable to formal arrest, even if the questioning occurs at home.
