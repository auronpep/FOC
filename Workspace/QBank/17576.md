Question: Harold was indicted for burglary and an attorney was appointed. While in pretrial detention on the burglary charge, a detective came to the jail and, after giving Miranda warnings, asked Harold about an unsolved arson that police suspected Harold had committed. The arson had not been charged. Harold waived his Miranda rights and made incriminating statements about the arson. Harold was later charged with arson and moved to suppress the statements on Sixth Amendment grounds. Should the court suppress Harold's statements about the arson?
Answer A: Yes, because Harold had appointed counsel on the burglary and any police-initiated interrogation was therefore barred
Answer B: Yes, because Harold's attorney should have been notified before any interrogation in jail
Answer C: No, because the Sixth Amendment right to counsel is offense-specific and had not attached to the uncharged arson
Answer D: No, because Harold waived his Sixth Amendment right to counsel by responding to questions without requesting an attorney
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *McNeil v. Wisconsin*, the Sixth Amendment right to counsel is offense-specific. Harold's right to counsel had attached on the burglary charge, but arson was a separate, uncharged offense. The Sixth Amendment therefore did not bar police from questioning Harold about the arson. Because Miranda warnings were given and Harold voluntarily waived, the statements were properly obtained and are admissible.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Sixth Amendment right is offense-specific. Counsel on the burglary charge does not extend to the uncharged arson. Police could lawfully question Harold about the arson after giving Miranda warnings. B: There is no constitutional requirement that police notify retained or appointed counsel before questioning about an uncharged, unrelated offense. The Sixth Amendment is charge-specific; Miranda governs here, and the waiver was valid. D: The Sixth Amendment right had never attached to the arson charge, so there was nothing to waive. The better analysis is that the Sixth Amendment simply did not apply. Waiver analysis is only necessary when the right has first attached.
lb_rule_reference: The Sixth Amendment right to counsel is offense-specific; police may question a represented defendant about an uncharged offense without violating the Sixth Amendment
