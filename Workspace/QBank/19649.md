Outline: Common objections to questions
Question: In a federal securities trial, Martha calls Andrew, a clerk who organized boxes of company emails for trial but did not participate in the events described in them. Martha's lawyer asks, “After reading all those emails, what do you think the directors intended to do?” The defendants object. What is the best ruling?
Answer A: Overrule the objection because Andrew read the emails.
Answer B: Overrule the objection because anyone may state an opinion after reviewing documents.
Answer C: Sustain the objection only if the emails themselves are privileged.
Answer D: Sustain the objection because the question calls for speculation and an improper lay opinion not based on Andrew's personal perception of the events.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: Andrew may testify to clerical matters he personally handled, but he did not perceive the underlying events and is not shown to be qualified to infer the directors' intent from a mass of documents. The question calls for speculation and an improper lay opinion.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because reading documents does not create personal knowledge of the events or intent described. B: B is wrong because lay opinion must be rationally based on the witness's perception and helpful. C: C is wrong because privilege is not the only objection; the witness lacks a proper basis for the opinion.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 602/FRE 701 / lay speculation from documents
