MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Other Crimes
Outline: Rape
Question: Orion and Petra have been married for three years and are separated but not yet divorced. On a night when Petra had returned to the marital home to collect belongings, Orion forced Petra to engage in sexual intercourse against her will, using physical force to overcome her resistance. Orion is charged with rape. At trial, Orion argues that a husband cannot be guilty of raping his wife under the common-law rule. How should the court rule on Orion's argument?
Answer A: Orion is correct; the common-law marital exemption provides a complete defense to rape
Answer B: Orion may have a partial defense because the parties are still legally married, which could reduce the charge to a lesser offense
Answer C: The court should reject Orion's argument because the marital exemption has been abolished; a spouse can be convicted of rape
Answer D: The court should reject the argument, but only because Orion and Petra were separated at the time — the exemption would apply if they lived together
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The traditional common-law marital exemption, based on Hale's implied-consent theory, has been abrogated in all American jurisdictions either by explicit statute or judicial abolition. Marriage does not constitute permanent irrevocable consent to sexual intercourse. A husband who forces intercourse on his wife without her consent can be convicted of rape on the same elements applicable to any rape: (1) sexual intercourse (2) without consent (3) by force or threat. Orion's separation from Petra does not affect this analysis, as the abolition applies to married couples generally, not only to those separated or divorcing.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: While the marital exemption was indeed the traditional common-law rule (derived from Hale's proposition that a wife gives irrevocable consent to intercourse upon marriage), virtually every American jurisdiction has abolished it by statute or judicial decision. The MBE tests modern law on this point. Orion cannot invoke a rule that no longer exists. B: While a small number of jurisdictions historically maintained diminished penalties for spousal rape (a relic of partial reform), the modern and majority rule treats spousal rape identically to rape of a stranger. The marital relationship is not a mitigating factor that reduces the charge. The court should reject Orion's argument entirely. D: The abolition of the marital exemption applies to all married couples — separated or cohabiting. The separation is irrelevant to whether the exemption applies. Courts abolishing the exemption do so on the ground that marriage does not imply consent at any time, not only upon separation. This answer preserves a remnant of the exemption that does not exist.
