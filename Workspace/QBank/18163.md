Question: During a domestic dispute, Renata called 911 and, while still on the phone with the dispatcher, described her boyfriend striking her and stated his name and address. Officers arrived within minutes. Renata fled before trial and could not be located. The prosecution sought to admit the 911 recording. The defendant objected, arguing that Renata's statements were testimonial under *Crawford* and that he had never had an opportunity to cross-examine her. Should the court admit the 911 recording?
Answer A: No, because the recording contains out-of-court statements by an unavailable declarant and the defendant never cross-examined her
Answer B: No, because a victim's 911 statements are formal accusations that must be subject to cross-examination
Answer C: Yes, because Renata's statements were made during an ongoing emergency and are therefore non-testimonial under Davis v. Washington
Answer D: Yes, because excited utterances are a firmly rooted hearsay exception and therefore presumptively reliable under Ohio v. Roberts
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Davis v. Washington*, the primary purpose test distinguishes testimonial from non-testimonial statements. Statements made to a 911 dispatcher while an attack is ongoing, seeking police help in an immediate emergency, are non-testimonial. Renata called while being struck — the emergency was ongoing. Because the statements are non-testimonial, the Confrontation Clause does not apply, and the recording is admissible subject only to ordinary hearsay rules (here, likely as an excited utterance).
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The threshold Crawford inquiry is whether the statement is testimonial. Under *Davis v. Washington*, statements made during an ongoing emergency to a 911 dispatcher are non-testimonial because their primary purpose is to obtain police assistance, not to create a record for prosecution. The Confrontation Clause does not bar non-testimonial hearsay. B: The primary purpose test under *Davis* looks at circumstances at the moment of the statement, not at the statement's later use. If the declarant was describing an ongoing emergency, the statement is non-testimonial regardless of whether it is later used to identify the defendant at trial. D: The correct doctrinal basis is *Davis v. Washington* (non-testimonial under the primary purpose test), not *Ohio v. Roberts* (the pre-Crawford reliability framework). *Crawford* overruled *Roberts* for testimonial statements, but the better analysis here is that the statement is non-testimonial under *Davis*, making Crawford inapplicable altogether. Citing *Roberts* as the operative rule misidentifies the governing doctrine.
