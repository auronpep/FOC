Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: An officer sought a warrant to search Jordan's apartment. The affidavit said only, "I believe drugs are inside based on experience," with no informant details, surveillance, controlled buy, dates, or facts tying drugs to the apartment. A magistrate issued the warrant, and officers found cocaine. Jordan moves to suppress. Does the good-faith exception save the cocaine?
Answer A: Yes, because a magistrate issued the warrant
Answer B: Yes, because the officer subjectively believed drugs were inside
Answer C: No, because the affidavit was so bare bones that reliance on the warrant was objectively unreasonable
Answer D: No, because good faith never applies to drug cases
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The affidavit contained only a conclusory belief. No reasonable officer could rely on a warrant supported by no meaningful facts tying evidence to the apartment.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Good faith has limits. A bare-bones affidavit so lacking probable cause cannot support objectively reasonable reliance. B: Good faith is objective. A reasonable officer needs facts, not only personal belief. D: Good faith depends on objective reliance and warrant defects, not the offense category.
lb_rule_reference: The good-faith exception fails when a warrant affidavit is bare bones and no reasonably well-trained officer could rely on it.
