Outline: Rape
Question: Trent, age 24, met Abby at an 18-and-over nightclub where she had been admitted after presenting a state-issued identification card showing she was 18. Abby told Trent she was a college sophomore. Trent had sexual intercourse with Abby, believing in good faith that she was 18 or older. Abby was actually 15; she had obtained a fake identification. The jurisdiction's statutory rape statute provides: "Any person who engages in sexual intercourse with a person under the age of 16 is guilty of a felony." The statute is silent on intent. Trent is charged with statutory rape. Which of the following best describes his liability?
Answer A: Trent is not guilty because he reasonably believed Abby was 18 based on her ID and statements
Answer B: Trent is not guilty because Abby's use of a fake ID was an intervening cause
Answer C: Trent is guilty because statutory rape is a strict liability offense and his belief about Abby's age, however reasonable, is not a defense
Answer D: Trent is guilty only if the prosecution proves he was reckless or negligent in ascertaining Abby's age
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Statutory rape is the canonical example of a strict liability crime. The legislature has determined that the protection of minors is so important that no mens rea as to age is required. A statute that is silent on intent, combined with the nature of the protected interest (child welfare), signals strict liability. Trent's good-faith, reasonable mistake about Abby's age is simply not a defense. He engaged in the prohibited act with a person below the statutory age; that is sufficient for liability.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Statutory rape is a strict liability offense as to the victim's age. At common law and in most jurisdictions, the reasonableness of the defendant's belief about the victim's age is irrelevant. The statute contains no mens rea requirement, which signals strict liability. A defendant who engages in the prohibited act is guilty regardless of his subjective belief about the victim's age, no matter how reasonable that belief was. B: Intervening-cause analysis applies to causation of a physical result (death, injury), not to the formation of mens rea. More fundamentally, mens rea is irrelevant to strict liability crimes. Strict liability means the defendant is liable for the act itself, regardless of how he was deceived into committing it. Abby's deception may be morally relevant but has no legal effect on Trent's strict liability. D: Strict liability means no mens rea of any kind is required — not even negligence. This choice imports a culpability requirement where the legislature has chosen to impose none. If the prosecution had to prove recklessness or negligence, the crime would not be strict liability. The whole point of strict liability is that the defendant's level of care is irrelevant.
lb_rule_reference: Statutory rape is a strict liability offense as to the victim's age; even a reasonable, good-faith belief that the victim was of age is not a defense
