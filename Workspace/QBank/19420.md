Outline: Receipt of stolen property
Question: Gene buys a new-looking laptop from a stranger in a parking lot for $75. Gene thinks "this seems suspicious — it might be stolen," but tells himself "I'll just say I didn't know if anyone asks." He does not investigate further. The laptop is, in fact, stolen. At trial, the prosecution can prove the laptop was stolen and that Gene had significant suspicion. The prosecution cannot prove Gene had actual certainty the laptop was stolen. Is Gene guilty of receiving stolen property under the common-law standard?
Answer A: No, because Gene only suspected the laptop might be stolen and suspicion is insufficient for the knowledge element
Answer B: Yes, because the circumstances were highly suspicious — $75 for a new laptop from a stranger in a parking lot — and Gene should have known it was stolen
Answer C: Yes, because Gene's deliberate decision not to investigate — while recognizing the strong likelihood the laptop was stolen — constitutes willful blindness, which satisfies the knowledge element
Answer D: No, because Gene did not have specific knowledge of who the true owner was
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Receipt of stolen property requires that the defendant know the property is stolen. The willful-blindness doctrine (deliberate ignorance) holds that a defendant who is subjectively aware of a high probability that a fact exists and deliberately avoids learning the truth is treated as having knowledge of that fact. Gene's own internal monologue — acknowledging suspicion and deciding to avoid investigation so he could claim ignorance — is the paradigmatic case of willful blindness. Courts treat this as equivalent to actual knowledge, satisfying the mens rea of receipt of stolen property.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: This answer is partially correct but incomplete. Gene's deliberate decision to avoid investigation — telling himself "I'll just say I didn't know" — crosses from mere suspicion into willful blindness (deliberate ignorance), which most jurisdictions equate with actual knowledge for the receipt-of-stolen-property mens rea. The willful-blindness doctrine prevents defendants from escaping liability by deliberately avoiding learning the truth. B: Receipt of stolen property does not use a negligence ("should have known") standard. The knowledge element requires actual knowledge or willful blindness — a subjective standard. Even highly suspicious circumstances supply only an objective inference, not proof of subjective knowledge. The prosecution must prove Gene actually knew or was willfully blind to the fact the laptop was stolen. D: Receipt of stolen property does not require knowledge of who the true owner is. The required knowledge is that the property was stolen — i.e., that someone other than the seller has superior title to it. Gene does not need to know the victim's name or identity; he needs only to know (or be willfully blind to the fact) that the laptop was taken from someone without their consent.
lb_rule_reference: Receipt of stolen property requires that the defendant (1) receive stolen property, (2) knowing it was stolen, (3) with intent to permanently deprive the owner. Mere suspicion, even strong suspicion, is insufficient; the defendant must actually know the property is stolen (or be willfully blind to that fact).
