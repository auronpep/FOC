MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Officers arrested a suspect for DUI and impounded his vehicle pursuant to department policy. The department had a written policy requiring officers to inventory all items in the passenger compartment and the trunk before towing. An officer inventoried the passenger compartment but did not open the trunk because he "didn't want to bother." A second officer, curious about a suspicious-looking duffel bag in the trunk, opened it and found cocaine. The suspect moves to suppress. Should the court suppress the cocaine found in the trunk?
Answer A: No, because the vehicle was lawfully impounded and any search of an impounded vehicle is per se reasonable
Answer B: No, because the second officer's observation of the duffel bag gave him probable cause to open it under the automobile exception
Answer C: Yes, because the search of the trunk and the duffel bag deviated from the department's standardized inventory procedures, converting an administrative search into an investigative search without a warrant
Answer D: Yes, because inventory searches are never constitutionally valid and evidence found during one is always suppressible
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: South Dakota v. Opperman and Colorado v. Bertine require inventory searches to follow standardized, non-discretionary departmental procedures to be constitutionally valid. The policy here required inventorying the trunk. The first officer's unilateral decision to skip the trunk was a deviation. The second officer's targeted search of the duffel bag based on suspicion was investigative, not administrative. A deviation from standardized procedures — especially one motivated by investigative curiosity — renders the inventory exception unavailable. No other exception covers the trunk search. Suppression is required.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Inventory searches are constitutionally valid only when conducted according to standardized procedures. The procedures exist precisely to prevent the exception from becoming a general license to search. Here, the first officer deviated from the mandatory policy by not opening the trunk, and the second officer opened the duffel bag based on curiosity — not pursuant to any policy. Neither action was a valid inventory search. B: Looking "suspicious" is not probable cause. The automobile exception requires probable cause to believe the specific container holds contraband — a generalized suspicion about a duffel bag without specific articulable facts falls short of probable cause. The automobile exception cannot rescue a constitutionally invalid inspection. D: Inventory searches conducted pursuant to standardized, non-discretionary procedures following a lawful impoundment are constitutionally valid. The Supreme Court has affirmed them in Opperman and Bertine. This suppression is warranted not because inventory searches are categorically invalid, but because this specific search deviated from the required procedures.
lb_rule_reference: Inventory searches of impounded vehicles are constitutional if conducted pursuant to standardized, non-discretionary procedures; a search conducted without following those procedures, or as pretext for an investigative search, is unconstitutional (Colorado v. Bertine; South Dakota v. Opperman)
