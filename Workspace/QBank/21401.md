Outline: Presumptions and inferences
Question: Moses is charged in federal court with arson. The government proves that Moses owned the burned building and had recently increased the insurance coverage. The prosecutor requests this instruction: “If you find that Moses recently increased the insurance on the building, you must presume that he intentionally set the fire unless Moses proves otherwise.” Moses objects. What should the court do?
Answer A: Give the instruction, because insurance evidence is relevant to motive.
Answer B: Give the instruction only for the limited purpose of proving motive, not identity.
Answer C: Refuse the instruction, because it would require the jury to presume an element of the offense unless Moses disproves it.
Answer D: Refuse the instruction only if Moses offers evidence that someone else started the fire.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The instruction is framed as a mandatory presumption on intent in a criminal case. The prosecution must prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt; the court may not require the defendant to disprove the element.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because relevance to motive does not justify a mandatory presumption on intent. B: B is wrong because a limiting-purpose instruction does not cure burden-shifting language. D: D is wrong because Moses need not prove an alternative cause before objecting to an unconstitutional mandatory presumption.
lb_rule_reference: Due process / mandatory criminal presumption
