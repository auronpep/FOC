Outline: Burglary
Question: Sam breaks a window and enters his neighbor Ted's house at 11:00 p.m. to retrieve a jacket Sam believes Ted borrowed and never returned. Sam genuinely believes the jacket belongs to him and has a legal right to it. Once inside, Sam finds the jacket, but also notices Ted's expensive watch on the nightstand. Sam decides on the spot to steal the watch and takes it. Is Sam guilty of common-law burglary?
Answer A: Yes, because Sam broke and entered Ted's dwelling at night and did in fact commit a felony inside
Answer B: No, because Sam is not guilty of any crime — he had a legitimate claim to the jacket
Answer C: No, because Sam lacked felonious intent at the time of entry — the intent to steal the watch arose only after he was already inside
Answer D: Yes, because breaking into someone's home at night is inherently felonious regardless of stated purpose
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Common-law burglary requires that the intent to commit a felony inside exist at the moment of breaking and entering. Sam entered Ted's home intending only to retrieve what he believed was his own jacket — a claim-of-right purpose that does not constitute felonious intent. The intent to steal the watch arose spontaneously after Sam was already inside and saw the watch. Because felonious intent was not present at the time of entry, the intent element of burglary is not satisfied. Sam may be guilty of larceny (the watch) and criminal trespass, but not burglary.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Burglary requires that the felonious intent exist at the time of the breaking and entering — not that a felony merely occurs afterward. Sam entered with the intent to retrieve his own jacket (or what he believed to be his property), which reflects a claim-of-right belief, not felonious intent. The decision to steal the watch was formed after entry. Intent formed after entry does not satisfy the contemporaneous intent requirement for burglary. B: Sam is guilty of larceny of the watch, which he stole with no claim of right. He is also potentially liable for criminal trespass and breaking-and-entering offenses even if burglary is not established. He is not without criminal liability merely because his initial entry purpose (the jacket) was arguably non-criminal. D: Breaking and entering at night satisfies only two of the five elements of common-law burglary. The crime also requires (1) a dwelling, (2) of another, and (3) intent to commit a felony therein. The intent element is independently required and is not supplied merely by the act of breaking and entering. The entry itself — even at night — is not the felony the intent element refers to; the required intent must be toward an additional felony to be committed inside.
lb_rule_reference: Common-law burglary requires that the defendant intend to commit a felony inside at the time of the breaking and entering. If the defendant enters without felonious intent and forms that intent only after entry, the burglary element of intent at time of entry is not satisfied.
