MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Torts
Subtopic: Negligence
Outline: Damages
Question: A print shop installed a nighttime compressor that vibrated through the wall of an adjoining apartment building. Several tenants lost sleep. The landlord sued for private nuisance and requested a permanent injunction shutting the compressor down entirely. The shop proved it could move the compressor to an interior room for a moderate cost, which would end nearly all vibration without closing the shop. The landlord refused to discuss relocation and demanded closure because the tenants had been miserable for months. What is the most likely remedy in the landlord's private nuisance claim against the print shop?
Answer A: A total shutdown injunction, because any substantial nuisance requires the defendant to stop the activity
Answer B: No remedy, because the landlord refused to mitigate by accepting the shop's first proposal
Answer C: A tailored injunction requiring reasonable abatement, with damages limited to nonavoidable loss-of-use harms
Answer D: Only punitive damages, because the shop continued operating after complaints
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The vibration substantially interferes with residential use, but a full shutdown is more burdensome than necessary. A court likely orders abatement through relocation and limits damages using avoidable-consequences principles.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Injunctions are equitable and require balancing. If relocation can abate the nuisance at moderate cost, a narrower injunction or damages is more likely than total shutdown. B: Mitigation limits avoidable damages; it does not erase the underlying nuisance or bar all relief. The court can tailor relief to the reasonable abatement option. D: The facts point to equitable abatement and compensatory loss, not punishment. Nothing establishes the kind of malicious or outrageous conduct needed for punitive damages.
lb_rule_reference: Nuisance remedies require balancing the seriousness of the interference, hardship of an injunction, adequacy of damages, and reasonable mitigation; mitigation does not require the plaintiff to accept unreasonable burdens.
