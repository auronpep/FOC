Question: Lydia signed and delivered a deed stating, “I convey my meadow to Paul.” The deed identified the meadow accurately but contained no clause reciting payment, love and affection, or any other consideration. Paul accepted the deed. Lydia later argued that the deed failed because it contained no consideration clause. Who owns the meadow?
Answer A: Lydia, because a deed must recite consideration on its face.
Answer B: Paul, because a deed need not contain or be supported by consideration if the required deed formalities and delivery are satisfied.
Answer C: Lydia, because a deed without a consideration clause is merely an unenforceable promise.
Answer D: Paul only if he proves that he paid consideration outside the deed.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The title source is a deed, and the decisive validity gate is the deed-formality and delivery checklist, not a consideration clause. Lydia signed and delivered a writing with present transfer words, an identifiable grantee, and a sufficient description. Paul accepted, so title passed even though the deed did not recite consideration.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because no consideration recital is required for deed validity. C: C is wrong because the writing used present conveyance language and was delivered, so it was not merely a promise. D: D is wrong because outside payment is unnecessary to satisfy the title-transfer lock.
lb_rule_reference: No consideration clause required
