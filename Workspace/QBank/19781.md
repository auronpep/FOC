Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: A college student stored documents on a laptop he kept in his dormitory room. He told his roommate about the documents but asked his roommate to keep the contents confidential. Campus police, without a warrant, took the laptop from the student's room while the student was in class and searched its files, discovering evidence of academic fraud. The student moves to suppress the evidence, arguing the search violated his Fourth Amendment rights. Should the court grant the motion to suppress?
Answer A: No, because the student disclosed the documents to his roommate, eliminating any expectation of privacy
Answer B: No, because the Fourth Amendment does not protect items stored electronically
Answer C: Yes, because the student had both a subjective expectation of privacy and an objectively reasonable one
Answer D: No, because the police had probable cause to believe the laptop contained evidence of a crime
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under Katz, the Fourth Amendment protects people when they (1) manifest a subjective expectation of privacy and (2) that expectation is one society recognizes as objectively reasonable. The student kept the laptop in his private dorm room and asked his roommate not to share the contents — demonstrating a subjective expectation. A dorm room is constitutionally analogous to a home; a resident has an objectively reasonable expectation that police cannot enter without a warrant. Because police took and searched the laptop without a warrant and no exception applies, suppression is required.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Telling one person in confidence is not a voluntary disclosure to the world. The third-party doctrine applies when information is conveyed to a third party in a context where sharing is expected (e.g., bank records, pen registers). A private confidence to a single roommate does not eliminate the objectively reasonable expectation that police cannot seize the laptop. B: The Fourth Amendment fully protects electronic data on personal devices. Katz extended protection beyond physical intrusion to anywhere a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy, including digital files. D: Probable cause is a prerequisite for a warrant, not a substitute for one. Without a warrant or a recognized exception, even well-founded probable cause does not authorize a warrantless entry and seizure in a constitutionally protected space.
lb_rule_reference: Fourth Amendment protection requires both a subjective expectation of privacy and one that society recognizes as objectively reasonable (Katz v. United States)
