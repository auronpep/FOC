


BARMATRIX Q#: 22330
Outline_code: 75070000
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: An informant told a police officer that a woman was dealing drugs from her apartment. The informant told the officer that three months ago the informant had bought drugs from the woman and that a bartender had recently told him that the woman was still dealing drugs. The informant had given the officer reliable information many times over the years, and the officer knew that the informant, the woman, and the bartender all knew each other and lived in the same neighborhood. The officer secured a search warrant from a magistrate for the woman's apartment. During the subsequent search, the police discovered a large supply of cocaine. At trial, the woman moved for the court to suppress the evidence against her. Should the court grant the woman's motion?
Answer A: A: No, because the informant had provided reliable information to the officer before.
Answer B: B: No, because the informant had personally bought drugs from the woman.
Answer C: C: Yes, because the informant's information was based on a sale that occurred three months before.
Answer D: D: Yes, because the informant's information was inadmissible hearsay.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under the Fourth Amendment, warrants can only be issued upon sufficient probable cause. Here, while the informant has proven reliable in the past, the drug sale itself took place three months before. This likely makes the information stale and too remote in time to support a finding of probable cause that the woman is dealing drugs now and that drugs will be found in her apartment. The fact that the bartender told the informant that the woman was still dealing drugs does not change the analysis because there is no information regarding the reliability of the bartender or the information he passed on to the informant, and the warrant is based on the informant's statements, not the bartender's statements. A is incorrect because of the staleness of the informant's information. B is incorrect because the fact that the informant bought the drugs personally does not change the analysis. D is incorrect because the issue is the validity of the warrant. There's no indication that the informant's statement is going to be admitted into evidence.
