Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: Petra was a key witness in a pending assault prosecution against Leon. Before trial, Leon shot and killed Petra during an argument about a personal debt unrelated to the assault case. Prosecutors sought to introduce Petra's prior statements identifying Leon as the assailant in the assault case, arguing that Leon had forfeited his Confrontation Clause rights by killing the witness. The defendant offered evidence that the shooting was impulsive and had nothing to do with preventing Petra from testifying. Does the forfeiture-by-wrongdoing doctrine allow admission of Petra's statements?
Answer A: Yes, because any wrongdoing that causes a witness to be unavailable constitutes forfeiture of the Confrontation Clause right
Answer B: Yes, because forfeiture applies whenever the defendant's crime and the underlying prosecution involve the same victim
Answer C: No, because forfeiture requires that the defendant's wrongdoing was specifically intended to prevent the witness from testifying, which is not established here
Answer D: No, because Petra's prior statements are non-testimonial and therefore not subject to the Confrontation Clause at all
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Giles v. California*, equitable forfeiture of Confrontation Clause rights requires that the defendant engaged in conduct designed to prevent the witness from testifying. It is not enough that the defendant committed wrongdoing that incidentally rendered the witness unavailable. Here, the killing was impulsive, motivated by a debt dispute, and unrelated to preventing Petra from testifying in the assault case. The prosecution has not established the requisite intent to silence, so the forfeiture doctrine does not apply and Petra's prior statements are barred by the Confrontation Clause.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Giles v. California* narrowed the forfeiture doctrine. The defendant must have acted with the *purpose* of making the witness unavailable to testify. Causing unavailability through a killing motivated by an entirely unrelated reason (here, a debt dispute) does not satisfy the *Giles* intent requirement. B: Giles* does not create a same-victim rule. The forfeiture doctrine requires purposeful conduct designed to prevent testimony. The overlap in victim identity is coincidental; the killing was motivated by the debt, not by a desire to silence a witness. D: The facts describe Petra's statements as identifying Leon as the assailant in an assault case — a paradigmatic testimonial identification. The correct analysis is not to re-characterize the statements as non-testimonial but rather to address whether the forfeiture doctrine applies. It does not, for the reason stated in C.
lb_rule_reference: Under Giles v. California, a defendant forfeits Confrontation Clause rights by wrongdoing only when the wrongdoing was designed to prevent the witness from testifying; accidental or passion-driven killing alone does not satisfy the forfeiture doctrine
