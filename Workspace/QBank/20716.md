Outline: Invasion of privacy
Question: A newspaper published an article about a city marathon and included a crowd photo showing hundreds of runners. One runner appeared in the background for a small portion of the photo. The newspaper did not use the runner's image in an advertisement or imply endorsement. Is the runner likely to prevail on an appropriation claim against the newspaper?
Answer A: Yes, because any publication of a person's image requires consent
Answer B: No, because the runner's image was incidental to a newsworthy editorial photo
Answer C: Yes, because newspapers are commercial businesses
Answer D: No, because appropriation requires the photo to be false
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The photo illustrated a newsworthy event and used the runner only incidentally, not as an endorsement or advertisement.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Newsworthy and incidental editorial use generally does not constitute appropriation. C: A commercial publisher can still make editorial newsworthy uses that are not appropriation. D: Appropriation does not require falsity; the actual reason is newsworthy and incidental use.
lb_rule_reference: Newsworthy, editorial, or incidental uses of identity generally are not appropriation, even if a person's image appears.
