Outline: Offers of proof
Question: Sarah sues Peter after a collision. Peter offers a repair invoice to prove the cost of replacing Sarah's bumper. Sarah's lawyer says only, “Objection,” without stating a ground. The court overrules the objection and admits the invoice. Nothing in the exchange indicates whether Sarah objects based on hearsay, authenticity, relevance, or original-writing grounds. On appeal, Sarah argues that the invoice was hearsay. Is the hearsay claim preserved?
Answer A: Yes, because any objection preserves every possible evidentiary ground.
Answer B: Yes, because the judge should have guessed the hearsay ground from the invoice itself.
Answer C: No, because a party challenging admitted evidence must timely object or move to strike and state the specific ground unless it is apparent from context.
Answer D: No, but only because Sarah failed to make an offer of proof.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: When evidence is admitted, the opponent preserves error by timely objecting or moving to strike and stating the specific ground unless the ground is apparent from context. Sarah's bare “Objection” did not preserve a hearsay ground that was not apparent.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because a general objection does not preserve all evidentiary theories. B: B is wrong because the court need not infer a specific objection from a document when multiple grounds are possible. D: D is wrong because an offer of proof preserves claims about excluded evidence, not objections to admitted evidence.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 103(a)(1) / timely specific objection after admission
