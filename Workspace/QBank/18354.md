Question: In a federal medical-malpractice trial, Luke, a qualified orthopedic expert, is asked for his opinion on whether Paul's spinal injury was caused by delayed treatment. Luke did not treat Paul and did not personally examine him, but he reviewed X-rays, hospital records, and deposition testimony of treating physicians, materials reasonably relied on by orthopedic experts. The defendant objects that Luke lacks personal knowledge of Paul's injury. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Overrule the objection because the personal-knowledge requirement for ordinary fact witnesses does not bar a qualified expert from basing an opinion on reasonably relied-upon data.
Answer B: Sustain the objection because an expert must personally observe every fact underlying the opinion.
Answer C: Sustain the objection unless Luke first admits all reviewed records into evidence.
Answer D: Overrule the objection only if Luke testifies that he personally saw Paul at the hospital.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: Rule 602's personal-knowledge requirement does not apply to expert testimony under Rule 703. A qualified expert may base an opinion on facts or data made known to the expert if experts in the field would reasonably rely on them. The admissibility of the underlying materials is a separate issue.
