Outline: Homicide
Question: During an argument, Reed stabbed Gloria in the abdomen, inflicting a wound that was serious but survivable with proper medical treatment. Gloria was transported to a hospital, where a surgeon negligently failed to follow standard post-operative protocols, leading to a preventable infection. Gloria died four days later from septic shock caused by the infection. An expert testified that Gloria would have survived the stab wound with competent medical care. Is Reed the proximate cause of Gloria's death?
Answer A: No, because the surgeon's negligence was an independent superseding cause that broke the causal chain
Answer B: No, because the original wound was not itself fatal, so Reed did not cause Gloria's death
Answer C: Yes, because ordinary medical negligence is a foreseeable consequence of inflicting a serious wound and does not sever the causal chain
Answer D: Yes, but only if the surgeon's conduct rose to the level of gross negligence rather than ordinary negligence
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Reed's stab wound required medical intervention, making medical care (and ordinary medical error) a foreseeable dependent consequence. A dependent intervening cause does not break the causal chain unless it is wholly independent and unforeseeable — such as an intentional act by a third party directed at the victim. Ordinary medical negligence falls short of that threshold. Reed's act was the but-for cause of Gloria's hospitalization and thus her exposure to the negligent treatment. He is the proximate cause of her death.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Ordinary medical negligence is a foreseeable consequence of inflicting a serious wound. When a defendant stabs someone, it is foreseeable that the victim will receive medical treatment and that treatment may not be optimal. A dependent intervening cause — one set in motion by the defendant's own act — does not sever the causal chain unless it is both unforeseeable and grossly abnormal. Medical negligence (as opposed to intentional misconduct) does not meet that standard. B: The causation inquiry does not require that the defendant's act be independently sufficient to cause death. It requires that the defendant's act be a but-for cause of the death that actually occurred, and that there be no superseding intervening cause. Reed's wound required medical treatment; that treatment was negligently provided; Gloria died. Reed's act set in motion the chain of events that led to her death, satisfying actual causation. D: The common-law rule is that even grossly negligent medical treatment generally does not sever causation when it is treatment sought for a wound the defendant inflicted. The threshold for a superseding medical cause is generally intentional misconduct by a treating physician, not mere gross negligence. D misstates the standard for when medical treatment severs causation.
lb_rule_reference: A dependent intervening cause (one set in motion by the defendant's act) does not break the causal chain unless it is both unforeseeable and independent; medical malpractice is generally foreseeable and dependent, so it does not sever causation
