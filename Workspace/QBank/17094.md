Question: Noah bought 500 “clinic-ready storage carts” from Sarah's medical-supply company. The signed goods contract included a merger clause but did not define “clinic-ready.” Noah offered established trade-usage evidence that, in medical-supply sales, “clinic-ready” carts must have antimicrobial coating certification. The court admitted the usage and adopted that meaning. Sarah delivered carts without the certification. Assuming the court adopts the trade meaning, what is the likely legal effect?
Answer A: Noah has no contract-content argument because the merger clause bars the trade meaning from affecting the parties' duties.
Answer B: Noah may treat the carts as nonconforming if the adopted trade meaning made certification part of “clinic-ready.”
Answer C: Noah may complain only if he proves the carts caused actual patient harm.
Answer D: Sarah wins because a trade usage can never affect an express goods description.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that an established UCC trade usage may give particular meaning to a technical goods term when it can be reasonably read with the writing. The trigger facts are a goods sale, an undefined technical phrase, the admitted usage, and the missing certification. The decisive lock is call type: the question asks legal effect after interpretation, not admissibility. If “clinic-ready” includes certification, uncertified carts fail to conform to the interpreted term.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because a merger clause does not erase a trade meaning adopted to explain a term under the UCC. C: C is wrong because actual patient harm is a performance/damages decoy; nonconformity turns on the term as interpreted. D: D is wrong because trade usage may explain or qualify express terms when reasonably consistent.
