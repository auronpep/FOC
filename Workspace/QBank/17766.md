Question: A professional football player signed a written consent for his team's physician to perform a knee operation. After the athlete was under a general anesthetic, the doctor asked a world famous orthopedic surgeon to perform the operation. The surgeon's skills were superior to the doctor's, and the operation was successful. In an action for battery by the athlete against the surgeon, the athlete will In an action for battery by the athlete against the surgeon, the athlete will
Answer A: prevail, because the athlete did not agree to allow the surgeon to perform the operation.
Answer B: prevail, because the consent form was in writing.
Answer C: not prevail, because the surgeon's skills were superior to the doctor's.
Answer D: not prevail, because the operation was successful.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A is correct. Battery requires harmful or offensive contact to the plaintiff's person, intent, and causation. Contact to the plaintiff's person is considered offensive if it is not expressly or impliedly consented to by the plaintiff. Unlike a medical malpractice claim, a prima facie case for battery does not require proof of damages to prevail. In this case, the athlete signed a consent form specifically allowing the team doctor to perform the surgery, not the surgeon. Regardless of any superior skill level, the surgeon touched the athlete without his consent, which constitutes offensive contact, and thus meets the requirements for a battery.

The reason the plaintiff will prevail is not that the consent for the doctor to perform the surgery was in writing, but rather, because of the lack of any consent - express or implied - for the surgeon to perform the surgery.", "C": "C is incorrect. The fact that the surgeon had superior skills has no bearing on his liability for battery, which does not require a showing of damages.", "D": "D is incorrect. As explained above, without any need to show damages, the surgery's success does not preclude a battery claim."}
