Outline: Mistake
Question: Simone sees two men in a parking lot. One man (Reed) has the other (Carter) pinned against a car and appears to be pummeling him. Simone does not know that Carter had attacked Reed minutes earlier and that Reed is defending himself. Simone reasonably concludes she is witnessing an assault and punches Reed to stop him. In fact, Reed had a valid self-defense claim against Carter. The jurisdiction follows the majority rule on defense of others. Simone is charged with battery. Under the majority rule, does Simone have a valid defense-of-others claim?
Answer A: No, because Reed actually had the right to use force, making Simone's intervention unjustified
Answer B: No, because Simone did not personally witness the original attack on Reed
Answer C: Yes, because Simone reasonably believed Carter was being unlawfully attacked and that intervention was necessary
Answer D: Yes, but only if Carter specifically asked Simone for help
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under the majority rule for defense of others, an intervener is justified if she reasonably believes (1) the person she is defending would have been entitled to use self-defense, and (2) her intervention was necessary to protect that person. The majority rule does not penalize an intervener who acts on a reasonable mistake about who the actual aggressor is. Simone's perception — that Carter was a victim being beaten — was objectively reasonable from the outside. Her honest, reasonable belief satisfies both elements, and the fact that Reed was actually justified does not defeat her defense under the majority approach.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The question specifies the majority rule, which does not use the alter-ego approach. Under the majority rule, the intervener's liability turns on whether her belief was reasonable, not on the actual facts about who had the right to use force. Simone's belief was reasonable based on what she observed. B: Defense of others does not require the intervener to have witnessed the origin of the conflict. It requires a reasonable belief that intervention is necessary to protect the third party from imminent unlawful force. A reasonable person watching what Simone saw — one person pinning and hitting another — would believe the victim needed protection. Simone's absence from the earlier events does not defeat her claim. D: Defense of others requires no request, invitation, or pre-existing relationship. A stranger may invoke the defense for any person she reasonably believes needs protection. A consent or request requirement does not exist in the common-law defense-of-others doctrine.
lb_rule_reference: Under the majority rule, a defendant may use force to defend a third party if she reasonably believes (1) the third party was entitled to use self-defense and (2) intervention was necessary; a reasonable mistake does not forfeit the defense
