Subtopic: Inchoate Crimes
Outline: Conspiracy
Question: Kim and Park were jointly indicted for conspiracy to commit wire fraud and for separate substantive wire fraud counts. Kim's defense was that Park acted alone; Park's defense was that Kim was the mastermind. At trial, the government introduced recorded conversations between Kim and Park that were admissible against Park as party admissions but constituted inadmissible hearsay as to Kim. The trial court gave a limiting instruction telling the jury to consider the recordings only as to Park. Kim was convicted. On appeal, Kim argues the joint trial denied him a fair trial and that severance was required. Was the trial court's denial of Kim's severance motion a reversible error?
Answer A: Yes, because whenever co-defendants present mutually antagonistic defenses, severance is required as a matter of constitutional law
Answer B: Yes, because Kim had a right to exclude all evidence that was inadmissible against him, and a joint trial made that impossible
Answer C: Probably not, because the trial court gave a limiting instruction and severance is required only when the prejudice is so compelling that no instruction can adequately protect the defendant
Answer D: No, because the government has an absolute right to try co-conspirators together and the defendant has no right to severance
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under *Zafiro v. United States* (1993) and Rule 14, a severance is warranted only when there is a serious risk that a joint trial would prevent the jury from making a reliable judgment, and that risk cannot be cured by a less extreme measure. Trial courts may presume that juries follow limiting instructions. Unless Kim can show that the recording so powerfully and directly implicated him — as in a *Bruton* situation — that the instruction was insufficient to neutralize the prejudice, the denial of severance is within the court's discretion and is not reversible error.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The Supreme Court held in *Zafiro v. United States* (1993) that mutually antagonistic defenses are not per se prejudicial. Severance is not constitutionally required whenever co-defendants blame each other; the defendant must show that the joint trial subjected him to compelling prejudice that could not be remedied by a less drastic measure such as a limiting instruction. B: Joint trials routinely involve evidence admissible against one defendant but not another. Limiting instructions are the prescribed remedy. The Court in *Bruton v. United States* (1968) found a Confrontation Clause problem when a non-testifying co-defendant's confession directly implicated Kim and could not be effectively limited — but where a limiting instruction can plausibly work, the trial court may rely on it. The question is whether the limiting instruction was sufficient, not whether Kim had an absolute right to exclusion. D: Rule 14 expressly gives courts discretion to sever even properly joined defendants when prejudice is shown. There is no absolute government right to joint trial, and there is no absolute defendant right to severance. Both are subject to the court's discretion under the prejudice standard.
lb_rule_reference: Under Fed. R. Crim. P. 14, a court may order severance if joinder appears to prejudice a defendant or the government; severance is not granted merely because separate trials would improve the defendant's chances — the prejudice must be compelling and not curable by limiting instruction
