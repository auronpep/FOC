Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Officers stopped a suspect for matching the general description of a shoplifter: male, jeans, gray hoodie. The suspect was cooperative, kept his hands visible at all times, and made no threatening gestures. The officer had no information suggesting the shoplifting suspect was armed. Out of "routine caution," the officer conducted a pat-down and discovered a knife. The suspect moves to suppress the knife. Should the court suppress the knife?
Answer A: No, because any lawful Terry stop automatically authorizes a frisk for officer safety
Answer B: No, because the officer's subjective concern for safety is sufficient to justify a protective pat-down
Answer C: Yes, because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion that the suspect was armed and dangerous; a valid stop alone does not authorize a frisk
Answer D: Yes, because shoplifting suspects are inherently dangerous and the officer was entitled to frisk as a matter of law
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under Terry v. Ohio, a pat-down for weapons requires reasonable suspicion, based on articulable facts, that the person is armed and presently dangerous. Here, the facts point entirely the other way: the suspect was cooperative, kept his hands visible, made no threatening gestures, and there was no information suggesting a shoplifting suspect was armed. "Routine caution" is a subjective hunch, not an articulable fact. Without the required suspicion of being armed and dangerous, the frisk was unconstitutional and the knife must be suppressed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Terry held that the stop and the frisk are analytically distinct. A stop requires reasonable articulable suspicion of criminal activity. A frisk additionally requires reasonable suspicion — based on specific, articulable facts — that the person is currently armed and dangerous. One does not flow automatically from the other. B: Terry requires an objective standard. The officer must be able to point to specific, articulable facts from which a reasonably prudent person in the officer's position would conclude the suspect poses an armed danger. A general, subjective feeling of caution — without objective facts supporting it — is insufficient. D: No categorical rule makes all shoplifting suspects presumptively dangerous. Reasonable suspicion of being armed must be based on specific facts about the particular encounter, not on generalizations about the type of offense. Shoplifting, without more, does not generate a presumption of dangerousness.
lb_rule_reference: A Terry frisk is authorized only if the officer has reasonable suspicion, separate from the stop justification, that the detainee is currently armed and dangerous; a valid stop does not automatically justify a frisk (Terry v. Ohio)
