Question: Rachel hired Miriam, a site planner, under a signed agreement requiring Miriam to prepare a “basic site plan.” The agreement stated that it was the complete and exclusive statement of the parties' obligations, but it did not define “basic site plan.” Rachel offers two pieces of negotiation evidence: first, that both parties used “basic site plan” to mean a boundary drawing plus parking layout; second, that Miriam separately promised to represent Rachel for free at any zoning appeal. Which evidence should be admitted?
Answer A: Both pieces, because both relate to the project.
Answer B: Only the evidence explaining that “basic site plan” meant a boundary drawing plus parking layout.
Answer C: Only the evidence about free zoning-appeal representation.
Answer D: Neither piece, because a complete and exclusive writing bars all negotiation evidence.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that a complete and exclusive integration bars prior or contemporaneous evidence offered to add obligations, but it does not bar evidence offered to explain an ambiguous term already included in the writing. The trigger facts are the complete-integration clause, the undefined “basic site plan” term, and the separate zoning-appeal promise. The decisive lock is purpose. The first item explains the included term; the second adds a separate service duty.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because it treats the two pieces of negotiation evidence as one package. C: C is wrong because the appeal-representation promise adds an obligation to a complete writing. D: D is wrong because complete integration does not bar evidence that explains an ambiguous included term.
lb_rule_reference: Complete integration / explanation versus added obligation
