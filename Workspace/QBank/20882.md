Subtopic: Homicide
Outline: Manslaughter
Question: Dr. Marsh, a licensed internist, saw a patient, Owen, who complained of chest pain and fatigue. Dr. Marsh conducted a brief physical exam and concluded Owen had acid reflux without ordering an EKG or cardiac workup. A standard-of-care review later concluded that a reasonable internist would have ordered an EKG given Owen's age and risk factors. Owen went home and died that evening from a myocardial infarction that an EKG would likely have detected. There was no evidence Dr. Marsh was distracted, intoxicated, or indifferent to Owen's presentation. Is Dr. Marsh guilty of involuntary manslaughter?
Answer A: Yes, because a reasonable internist would have ordered an EKG, and Dr. Marsh's failure to do so caused Owen's death
Answer B: Yes, because Dr. Marsh had a duty to his patient and breached that duty by failing to order standard diagnostic tests
Answer C: No, because failing to order one diagnostic test without additional egregious circumstances constitutes ordinary professional negligence, not the gross deviation required for criminal negligence
Answer D: No, because Dr. Marsh did not know Owen would die and lacked the intent to cause harm
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Involuntary manslaughter based on criminal negligence requires a gross deviation from the standard of care — conduct substantially more culpable than ordinary professional negligence. Dr. Marsh examined Owen, reached a diagnosis, and made a professional judgment that turned out to be wrong. Without additional facts showing deliberate indifference, intoxication, a pattern of gross misconduct, or extreme disregard for obvious risks, a single diagnostic error does not satisfy the criminal negligence threshold. Owen's death is tragic, but it gives rise to civil malpractice liability, not criminal homicide.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Ordinary professional negligence — even negligence that causes death — does not constitute criminal negligence. Criminal negligence for involuntary manslaughter requires a gross deviation from the standard of care, not merely a breach of the professional standard. A single failure to order a diagnostic test, without more, typically does not rise to the gross-deviation threshold absent aggravating circumstances (intoxication, deliberate indifference, egregious pattern). B: Civil negligence elements (duty, breach, causation, damages) are not the same as criminal negligence. The criminal standard requires more than a breach of duty — it requires a gross deviation from the standard of care sufficient to constitute criminal recklessness or criminal indifference to the risk of death. Dr. Marsh conducted an exam and formed a differential diagnosis; his error was ordinary professional misjudgment, not gross deviation. D: Involuntary manslaughter does not require intent to harm. It requires criminal negligence — gross deviation from reasonable care. D correctly concludes no liability but for the wrong reason. The correct basis is that the negligence was not gross enough, not that Dr. Marsh lacked intent to harm.
lb_rule_reference: Not every professional breach of duty constitutes criminal negligence; the act must be a gross deviation from the standard of care — something more than ordinary professional negligence — before criminal liability for involuntary manslaughter attaches
