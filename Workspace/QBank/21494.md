Outline: Homicide
Question: Frank beat his roommate, Hector, so severely that Hector suffered permanent brain damage and entered a persistent vegetative state. Fourteen months later, Hector's family agreed to withdraw life support and Hector died. The medical examiner testified that the brain damage from the beating was the cause of Hector's death and that no independent intervening cause contributed. The jurisdiction had abolished the year-and-a-day rule by statute three years before Frank's attack. Can Frank be charged with homicide for Hector's death?
Answer A: No, because Hector died more than a year and a day after the attack, which bars a homicide charge
Answer B: No, because Hector's death resulted from withdrawal of life support, which is an independent intervening cause
Answer C: Yes, because the jurisdiction has abolished the year-and-a-day rule and causation is established by the medical examiner's testimony
Answer D: Yes, but the jury cannot consider the beating as the cause of death since the immediate cause was withdrawal of life support
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The common-law year-and-a-day rule has been abolished in this jurisdiction by statute. The only remaining issues are causation and the applicable mens rea. The medical examiner testified that the brain damage from Frank's beating caused Hector's death, with no independent intervening cause. Both actual cause (but-for) and proximate cause are established. Frank can be charged with homicide.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The year-and-a-day rule was a common-law rule, not a constitutional protection. Most modern jurisdictions — including this one — have abolished it by statute. Once abolished, there is no time bar to prosecution. Frank cannot use a rule that no longer exists in this jurisdiction. B: Withdrawal of life support following terminal injury inflicted by the defendant is not an independent intervening cause. The medical examiner established that the brain damage from the beating was the cause of death. The withdrawal of life support from a patient who is in a persistent vegetative state due to the defendant's assault is a foreseeable, dependent consequence of the assault. It does not sever causation. D: Causation analysis looks at but-for causation and proximate causation across the entire chain of events, not only the last event before death. Frank's beating caused the brain damage; the brain damage caused the vegetative state; the vegetative state caused the withdrawal decision; the withdrawal caused the death. The beating is the but-for cause throughout the chain, and no superseding cause exists.
lb_rule_reference: The common-law year-and-a-day rule barred homicide prosecution if the victim died more than a year and a day after the defendant's act; most modern jurisdictions have abolished this rule, allowing prosecution regardless of time elapsed, provided causation can be proved
