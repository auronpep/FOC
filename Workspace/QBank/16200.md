


BARMATRIX Q#: 16200
Outline_code: 75100000
MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Exclusionary Rule
Question: Two state troopers, a man and a woman driving a single patrol car, lawfully stopped the driver of a van on the turnpike for exceeding the speed limit by four miles per hour. The male trooper approached the van to warn the driver to drive within the speed limit. The female trooper remained in the patrol car and ran a computer check of the license number of the driver's van; the computer check indicated that there was an outstanding warrant for the driver's arrest for unpaid traffic tickets. The male trooper therefore told the driver she was under arrest pursuant to the warrant, and searched her person. Then, while the driver waited next to the van, the trooper searched the van's front seat. He saw and seized ten glassine bags of heroin in a paper bag on that seat. Later, it was learned that the driver had paid the outstanding traffic tickets ten days earlier and the warrant had been quashed, but the clerk of the court had failed to update the computer, which continued to list the warrant as outstanding. The driver was charged with unlawful possession of heroin. Her attorney filed a motion to suppress the use as evidence of the heroin found in the van. Should the motion be granted?
Answer A: A: No, because the troopers could reasonably rely on the computer report and the search was incident to arrest.
Answer B: B: No, because troopers may lawfully search the passenger compartment of a car incident to a valid traffic stop.
Answer C: C: Yes, because there was no arrest for the traffic violation and no lawful arrest could be made on the basis of the warrant.
Answer D: D: Yes, because there was no probable cause or reasonable suspicion to believe drugs were in the car.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: (A)is the best response, because evidence generally will not be suppressed where police reasonably held a good-faith belief that their action leading to its discovery was authorized by a valid warrant. See Arizona v. Evans, 514 U.S. 1 (1995). The computer check on the license number of the driver's car revealed that there was an outstanding warrant for the driver's arrest based on unpaid parking tickets. The police had no reason to believe that the warrant was invalid, so the arrest will be treated as valid for purposes of the search-incident-to-arrest doctrine. See Arizona v. Evans, 514 U.S. 1 (1995). That being the case, the troopers were entitled to search, incident to the arrest, both the driver's person and the part of the van's passenger compartment that was accessible to the driver at that moment. When this passenger-compartment search yielded the heroin, the heroin became evidence validly seized incident to a lawful arrest, and thus admissible. (But let's assume, instead, that after the troopers searched the driver's person pursuant to the warrant, they handcuffed her, and placed her in the patrol car; in that event the ensuing search of the van's passenger compartment would not have been incident to the arrest, since the compartment would not have been accessible to the driver at the moment of the search. See Arizona v. Gant, 129 S.Ct. 1710 (2009). In this alternate scenario, the heroin would not be the result of a proper search or seizure, and would therefore be inadmissible because made without a valid search warrant and without any applicable exception to the requirement of such a warrant.) Note: This question originally appeared on the MBE, with slightly different facts, before the 2009 decision in Arizona v. Gant, supra. In the original question, the troopers handcuffed the driver after the arrest; once Gant was decided, under the original fact pattern the handcuffing would have prevented the van-search from being incident to the arrest, since the passenger compartment would not have been accessible to the driver during the search. We have modified the facts to make the passenger-compartment search properly incident to the arrest, thereby making the heroin admissible as fruit of a proper search even post-Gant. (B)is not the best response, because when the police make a traffic stop but do not make an arrest, they are not permitted to make a full-blown search of the passenger compartment. See Knowles v. Iowa, 525 U.S. 113 (1998). In this case, however, the troopers did arrest the driver of the car pursuant to an arrest warrant that they believed in good faith to be valid. This made the arrest valid for purposes of applying the search-incident-to-arrest exception to the requirement of a search warrant. Furthermore, the driver had access to the van's front seat at the time the trooper searched the passenger compartment, making the search properly incident to this valid arrest. Therefore, the driver's motion to dismiss will be denied. See Arizona v. Evans, 514 U.S. 1 (1995) (applying the good-faith exception to the requirement of a search warrant under similar circumstances). (C)is not the best response, because evidence generally will not be suppressed where police reasonably held a good-faith belief that their actions leading to its discovery were authorized by a valid warrant. Here, the arrest was made in the good-faith belief that there was a valid outstanding warrant. That made the arrest valid despite the court's clerical error. See Arizona v. Evans, 514 U.S. 1 (1995) (applying the good-faith exception to the requirement of an arrest warrant under similar circumstances). Since the arrest was valid, the search of the front seat was incident to this valid arrest, making the heroin admissible as the fruit of a valid search-incident-to-arrest. (D)is not the best response, because neither probable cause nor reasonable suspicion was required, as the search was incident to an arrest. It is true that the arrest warrant turned out to be invalid, but evidence generally will not be suppressed where, as in this case, police (a) reasonably held a good-faith belief that they were entitled to make an arrest of a driver; and (b) searched the vehicle's passenger compartment incident to that arrest, while the compartment was accessible to the arrestee. See Arizona v. Evans, 514 U.S. 1 (1995) (applying the good-faith exception to the requirement of an arrest warrant under similar circumstances).
