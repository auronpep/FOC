Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Statements and Confessions
Question: Officers arrested Ray, gave Miranda warnings, and obtained a written waiver. Officers then interrogated Ray for sixteen hours without a break, denying his requests for food, sleep, and bathroom access. Ray was kept in a windowless room with bright lights. Near the end of the interrogation, Ray confessed to murder. The officers' conduct violated departmental policy but did not constitute physical violence. Ray moves to suppress the confession as involuntary. Is Ray's confession likely to be suppressed as involuntary?
Answer A: No, because Ray received Miranda warnings and executed a written waiver, establishing that his statements were voluntary
Answer B: No, because there was no physical violence, and courts require physical coercion to find a confession involuntary
Answer C: Yes, because the totality of circumstances — sixteen-hour interrogation, denial of food and sleep, sensory deprivation — likely overbore Ray's will, rendering the confession involuntary
Answer D: Yes, because the officers violated departmental policy during the interrogation, which independently renders the confession inadmissible
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under the Due Process Clause (14th Amendment), a confession is involuntary when police conduct, considered under the totality of circumstances, overcame the suspect's capacity for self-determination. Courts examine duration, physical conditions, psychological pressure, and the suspect's personal characteristics. Here, a sixteen-hour interrogation denying food, sleep, and bathroom breaks combined with sensory-controlled conditions constitutes the type of psychological overreaching that courts have found sufficient to overbear the will. Miranda compliance is irrelevant to this independent Due Process analysis. The confession is subject to suppression.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Miranda compliance and Due Process voluntariness are independent requirements. A confession that follows Miranda warnings can still be involuntary if coercive police conduct overbore the suspect's will. The two doctrines address different concerns: Miranda addresses the inherent compulsion of custody; Due Process addresses actual police overreaching. Satisfying Miranda does not immunize a confession from a voluntariness challenge. B: The Supreme Court has long held that psychological coercion can render a confession involuntary. Deprivation of sleep, food, and bathroom access over sixteen hours in a sensory-controlled environment constitutes the kind of psychological overreaching that can overbear a suspect's will. Physical violence is not a prerequisite for a finding of involuntariness. D: Violation of departmental policy does not itself make a confession inadmissible under federal constitutional law. The constitutional standard is whether police conduct overbore the suspect's will under the totality of circumstances. A policy violation is evidence of problematic conduct but does not independently establish constitutional inadmissibility.
lb_rule_reference: A confession is involuntary under the Due Process Clause if, under the totality of circumstances, police conduct overbore the suspect's will; Miranda compliance does not immunize a confession from a separate voluntariness challenge.
