Outline: Self-defense
Question: Evelyn suffers from severe schizophrenia. During a psychotic episode, she strangles her neighbor, believing she is squeezing the juice from a giant orange. She has no awareness that she is harming a human being. She is charged with murder and pleads not guilty by reason of insanity in a M'Naghten jurisdiction. Under the M'Naghten test, is Evelyn's insanity defense likely to succeed?
Answer A: No, because Evelyn knew she was performing a physical act (squeezing)
Answer B: No, because Evelyn had a history of mental illness and should have sought treatment
Answer C: Yes, because Evelyn did not know the nature and quality of her act — she believed she was squeezing an orange, not harming a person
Answer D: Yes, because Evelyn was unable to control her impulse due to mental disease
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The M'Naghten test has two disjunctive prongs: the defense is established if the defendant, due to a mental disease, either (1) did not know the nature and quality of the act, or (2) did not know the act was wrong. Evelyn satisfies the first prong. Because of her schizophrenic delusion, she did not know she was strangling a human being — she believed she was handling fruit. She lacked the cognitive understanding of what she was actually doing. That is precisely the type of cognitive defect M'Naghten is designed to address. The defense succeeds on the first prong alone.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The "nature and quality" prong under M'Naghten asks whether the defendant knew what she was actually doing — that she was killing a human being, not squeezing an orange. If a defendant, due to psychosis, does not understand the true nature of her act, the first prong is satisfied regardless of whether she was aware of the physical motion. B: M'Naghten focuses entirely on the defendant's cognitive state at the moment of the act — specifically whether she knew the nature of the act or that it was wrong. Prior treatment history, failure to seek help, or prior knowledge of one's condition are not elements of the M'Naghten test. The analysis is about the mental state at the time of the crime. D: The irresistible-impulse test is a separate, supplemental insanity standard not included in the basic M'Naghten test. M'Naghten is a purely cognitive test — it asks what the defendant knew, not whether she could control herself. Evelyn's defense succeeds under M'Naghten's knowledge-of-nature prong, not the volitional control prong of a different test. Selecting this answer misidentifies the operative standard.
lb_rule_reference: Under M'Naghten, a defendant is not guilty by reason of insanity if, at the time of the act, due to a mental disease, she either (1) did not know the nature and quality of the act, or (2) did not know the act was wrong (legally wrong in most jurisdictions)
