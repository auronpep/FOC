Question: In a federal theft trial, Peter is called to testify that he saw the defendant enter a storeroom. Peter has a prior felony conviction for fraud and expects the prosecutor to recommend leniency in an unrelated case if he testifies truthfully. The defendant objects that Peter is incompetent because he is a convicted felon and has an interest in pleasing the prosecutor. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection because convicted felons are incompetent to testify in federal criminal trials.
Answer B: Sustain the objection because a witness with a motive to please one side is incompetent.
Answer C: Overrule the objection only if the prosecutor promises not to mention Peter's conviction.
Answer D: Overrule the objection because conviction and interest generally affect credibility and impeachment, not competency.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: The Federal Rules broadly treat persons as competent unless a rule provides otherwise. A prior conviction and an interest or motive to favor a party do not automatically make the witness incompetent. Those facts may be used to impeach credibility, but they do not bar Peter from testifying.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because a felony conviction is not a categorical competency bar. B: B is wrong because bias or interest is impeachment material, not automatic incompetency. C: C is wrong because suppressing the conviction is not a condition of competency.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 601 / conviction and interest do not automatically destroy competency
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests the conviction/interest-as-incompetency trap and routes those facts to impeachment instead.
