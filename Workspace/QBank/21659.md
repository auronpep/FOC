Subtopic: Presentation of Evidence
Outline: Materiality and probative value
Question: Joanna is charged with breaking into a storeroom through a window. A small piece of green fabric was found on the broken glass. The prosecutor offers evidence that Joanna owned a green cloak with a small tear the next morning. Joanna objects that many green fabrics exist and that no test proved the fragment came from her cloak. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the evidence because the fabric fragment was not scientifically matched to Joanna's cloak.
Answer B: Exclude the evidence because many green fabrics exist.
Answer C: Admit the evidence only if Joanna admits her cloak was torn during the break-in.
Answer D: Admit the evidence because the matching color, tear, and timing have some tendency to connect Joanna to the break-in; weaknesses affect weight.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: The evidence is circumstantial and imperfect, but it has some tendency to connect Joanna to the broken window. Scientific certainty and uniqueness are not required for relevance.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because scientific matching would strengthen the evidence, but it is not required for threshold admissibility. B: B is wrong because commonness weakens the evidence but does not necessarily eliminate probative value. C: C is wrong because Joanna's admission is not required.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401; FRE 402
lb_student_diagnostic: Student treats weak circumstantial comparison evidence as inadmissible unless scientifically conclusive.
