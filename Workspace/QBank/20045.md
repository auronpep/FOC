Outline: Personal knowledge
Question: In a federal civil trial arising from a collision in a store aisle, Nathan is called to testify that Sarah's cart struck Jacob's cart. Nathan testifies outside the jury's presence that he was standing about twenty-five feet away, looking through a glass wall, and saw the carts collide. Sarah objects that Nathan could not have seen clearly because it was raining outside and the glass wall reflected light. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection because Nathan's view was imperfect and therefore he lacks personal knowledge as a matter of law.
Answer B: Overrule the objection because Nathan's testimony is sufficient to support a finding that he perceived the collision; the weaknesses affect weight.
Answer C: Sustain the objection unless Nathan first produces a photograph showing his exact line of sight.
Answer D: Overrule the objection only if the jury first decides that Nathan actually saw the collision.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: A witness may testify if evidence is introduced sufficient to support a finding that the witness has personal knowledge. Nathan's own testimony that he was present, looking toward the carts, and saw the collision supplies that threshold foundation. Rain, distance, and reflection are proper subjects for cross-examination and jury weight, not automatic exclusion.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because imperfect perception does not automatically defeat personal knowledge. C: C is wrong because corroborating physical proof is not required before a witness may testify from perception. D: D is wrong because the judge screens the threshold foundation; the jury later weighs credibility and accuracy.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 602 / eyewitness perception foundation / weight not admissibility
lb_student_diagnostic: Tests the foundation-over-demand trap and the difference between threshold personal knowledge and jury weight.
