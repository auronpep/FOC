Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Appeal
Question: A state prisoner filed a federal habeas petition in 2018 and lost on the merits. In 2024, the Supreme Court announces a new rule of constitutional law holding that a particular type of eyewitness identification procedure violates due process. The prisoner's conviction rested heavily on eyewitness identification using that exact procedure, which was not challenged as a constitutional matter in the 2018 habeas petition. The prisoner seeks to file a second federal habeas petition raising the new due process rule. The court of appeals must decide whether to authorize the successive petition. Under AEDPA, may the prisoner file the successive petition?
Answer A: No, because having litigated one habeas petition to final judgment, the prisoner is barred from ever filing a second habeas petition on any ground
Answer B: Yes, if the Supreme Court has made the new due process rule retroactive to cases on collateral review, because that is one of the two exceptions permitting a successive petition under AEDPA
Answer C: Yes, because a new Supreme Court decision always entitles a prisoner to reopen a previously decided habeas petition regardless of whether the Court declared the rule retroactive
Answer D: No, because the prisoner waived the due process argument by failing to raise it in the 2018 habeas petition, and AEDPA's abuse-of-writ doctrine bars claims that could have been raised in prior petitions
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Under 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(2)(A), a second or successive habeas petition is not barred if it relies on a new rule of constitutional law that was previously unavailable and that the Supreme Court has made retroactive to cases on collateral review. The prisoner's new claim—based on a due process rule announced after the first petition—fits this exception, but only if the Supreme Court has expressly declared the rule retroactive. Under Teague v. Lane, new constitutional rules generally do not apply retroactively on collateral review unless they are either (a) substantive rules or (b) watershed rules of criminal procedure. The prisoner must also obtain authorization from the court of appeals before the district court may consider the petition. If both conditions are met, the successive petition may proceed.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: AEDPA does not impose an absolute bar on successive habeas petitions. Section 2244(b) bars successive petitions as a default rule but provides two exceptions: (1) a new constitutional rule made retroactive by the Supreme Court, and (2) newly discovered facts that establish innocence by clear and convincing evidence. A successive petition that qualifies under one of those exceptions may proceed after the petitioner obtains authorization from the court of appeals. C: AEDPA requires two things: (1) the rule must be new and previously unavailable, and (2) the Supreme Court must have made it retroactive to cases on collateral review. A new Supreme Court ruling does not automatically apply retroactively on collateral review—Teague and § 2244(b) require an explicit retroactivity declaration. If the Supreme Court announces a new rule but has not made it retroactive, the successive petition exception does not apply. D: The abuse-of-writ principle (which AEDPA codified in § 2244(b)(1)) bars claims that were raised in a prior petition. It does not automatically bar a claim that was not raised but could not have been raised, such as a claim based on a new constitutional rule that did not exist in 2018. The question is whether the exception in § 2244(b)(2)(A) applies—and it does, if the Supreme Court made the rule retroactive. The prisoner's failure to raise the claim in 2018 is understandable because the constitutional rule did not exist then. The "could have been raised" bar addresses claims available but omitted, not claims based on post-petition legal developments.
lb_rule_reference: Under AEDPA (28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)), a second or successive habeas petition must be dismissed unless it relies on (1) a new rule of constitutional law made retroactive to cases on collateral review by the Supreme Court, or (2) newly discovered facts that, if proven, establish actual innocence by clear and convincing evidence; petitioners must obtain leave from the court of appeals before filing a successive petition in the district court
