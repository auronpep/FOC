Question: A city uses a written civil service examination to select candidates for promotion to supervisory positions in its fire department. A statistical analysis shows that Black and Hispanic firefighters pass the exam at significantly lower rates than white firefighters. There is no evidence that city officials designed the exam with the intent to disadvantage minority firefighters. A group of minority firefighters who failed the exam challenge the promotion process. Which of the following accurately distinguishes the correct frameworks for a constitutional Equal Protection claim versus a Title VII statutory claim arising from the exam?
Answer A: The constitutional Equal Protection claim succeeds based on disparate impact alone, because a government employer administering a racially disparate test is engaged in a race-based classification triggering strict scrutiny
Answer B: The Title VII disparate impact claim and the constitutional claim are analyzed identically; if the city cannot show the test is job-related, both claims succeed
Answer C: The constitutional Equal Protection claim requires proof of discriminatory purpose, which is absent here; the Title VII disparate impact claim does not require proof of intent — it is established by statistical disparity that the employer cannot justify as job-related and consistent with business necessity
Answer D: The constitutional claim succeeds because the city, as a state actor, has an affirmative duty to ensure that its employment practices produce racially proportionate outcomes
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Washington v. Davis established the constitutional EP rule: discriminatory purpose is required; disparate impact alone is insufficient. The constitutional claim fails absent evidence that city officials acted with racial animus. Title VII, by contrast, establishes a disparate impact theory under which statistical evidence of racially disproportionate outcomes shifts the burden to the employer to demonstrate the test is job-related and consistent with business necessity. These are two different frameworks with different elements and burdens.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Washington v. Davis held that a facially neutral government employment test does not violate the Equal Protection Clause solely because it has a racially disparate impact. Discriminatory purpose must be proven. A test that was not designed to discriminate and has no history of discriminatory intent does not become a racial classification just because of statistical disparity in outcomes. B: Constitutional Equal Protection and Title VII are separate frameworks with different elements. Constitutional EP requires proof of discriminatory purpose (intent); Title VII disparate impact does not — it shifts the burden to the employer to show business necessity once disparate impact is established statistically. The claims have different elements, different burdens, and different outcomes depending on the evidence. D: The Constitution imposes no affirmative duty to achieve racially proportionate outcomes in government employment. The Equal Protection Clause prohibits intentional racial discrimination; it does not require racial balance. Statistical underrepresentation, without discriminatory purpose, is not a constitutional violation.
lb_rule_reference: A constitutional Equal Protection racial claim requires proof of discriminatory purpose, not just disparate impact. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act provides a statutory disparate impact theory that applies to employment. These are separate frameworks with different elements and different sources of law.
