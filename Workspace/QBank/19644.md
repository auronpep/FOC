Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Definition
Question: Elizabeth sues a bus company after a collision, claiming the driver was speeding. The bus company offers evidence that Elizabeth spends weekends gardening. It argues that the jury should know what kind of person Elizabeth is. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the evidence, because Elizabeth's gardening does not make speeding, causation, or damages more or less probable on these facts.
Answer B: Admit the evidence, because all background facts about a party are relevant.
Answer C: Admit the evidence only if Elizabeth denies gardening.
Answer D: Exclude the evidence only if gardening is viewed positively by most jurors.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A fact must have some tendency to make a consequential fact more or less probable. Elizabeth's gardening has no stated link to the collision, speeding, causation, or damages.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because background facts still need a logical connection to an issue or proper purpose. C: C is wrong because denial would not create consequential relevance unless the fact mattered. D: D is wrong because relevance does not turn on whether the fact is flattering or unflattering.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401 / FRE 402
lb_student_diagnostic: The trap is confusing narrative color with a fact of consequence.
