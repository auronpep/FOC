Question: Paul hired Ruth to install a “premium sound package” in a small theater. The final signed services agreement contained a merger clause and did not define the phrase. During negotiations, Ruth had sent Paul a written proposal stating that her “premium sound package” included two wireless microphones and wall-mounted speakers. The proposal was not attached to the final agreement. After Ruth installed only the speakers, Paul offered the proposal to explain what the phrase meant. Should the court admit the proposal for that purpose?
Answer A: No, because prior written proposals are always barred once a final agreement contains a merger clause.
Answer B: Yes, because a prior writing is never parol evidence.
Answer C: No, because the proposal proves a condition precedent rather than a meaning issue.
Answer D: Yes, if the final phrase is reasonably susceptible to Paul's meaning; the proposal is offered to explain the ambiguous package term, not merely to add an unrelated obligation.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that a prior writing can be parol evidence, but it may still be admissible to explain an ambiguous term in the final agreement. The trigger facts are the undefined phrase “premium sound package,” the unincorporated proposal, and Paul's limited explanatory purpose. The decisive locks are timing and purpose: the proposal predates the final writing, but it is offered to explain a term rather than to contradict it.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because merger clauses do not bar all ambiguity evidence. B: B is wrong because prior writings can be parol; written form alone does not exempt them. C: C is wrong because the proposal defines package scope; it does not gate contract effectiveness as a condition precedent.
