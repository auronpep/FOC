Question: A federal officer had probable cause to believe that a woman had participated in a bank robbery. Two days after the robbery, the woman checked into a local hotel. When the woman left her room for the evening, the hotel manager opened the hotel room door so that the officer could enter the room and look inside. The officer did not find any of the stolen money but did see, lying open on the bed, the woman's diary. The diary contained an entry describing the woman's involvement in robbing the bank. The woman was charged in federal court with bank robbery. She has moved to suppress the diary. Should the court suppress the diary?
Answer A: A: Yes, because the officer had no warrant.
Answer B: B: Yes, because admitting the diary would violate the woman's privilege against self-incrimination.
Answer C: C: No, because the hotel manager had actual authority to allow the officer into the hotel room.
Answer D: D: No, because the officer reasonably relied on the hotel manager's apparent authority to allow the officer into the hotel room.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A is correct. The Fourth Amendment protects the woman's expectation of privacy in her dwelling, including her temporary hotel room dwelling. Absent exigent circumstances, which were not present in this situation, the Fourth Amendment protections require the officer to have obtained a warrant before entering the hotel room. See Minnesota v. Olson, 495 U.S. 91 (1990). Because the officer did not have a warrant to enter, the woman's motion to suppress the diary should be granted.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B is incorrect. This answer reaches the correct answer with the wrong reasoning. Admitting the diary would not violate the woman's self-incrimination privilege because the woman was not compelled to write (or to produce) the diary. See United States v. Hubbell, 530 U.S. 27, 35-36 (2000). C is incorrect. The hotel manager did not have the authority to allow the officer to enter the woman's hotel room. Stoner v. California, 376 U.S. 483 (1964). D is incorrect. The hotel manager did not have actual authority to allow the entry under Stoner, and any reliance by the officer upon the manager's unauthorized consent would not have been reasonable. Illinois v. Rodriguez, 497 U.S. 177, 187-89 (1990) (discussing Stoner).
