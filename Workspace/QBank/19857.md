Question: Abraham promised Sarah that he would pay Ruth $10,000 if Sarah completed a catalog of Abraham's inventory. Ruth was an intended beneficiary of the promise. Sarah completed only a small portion of the catalog and then abandoned the job. Ruth sued Abraham for the $10,000. What is Abraham's strongest defense?
Answer A: Abraham may assert Sarah's material breach because his duty to pay Ruth was dependent on Sarah's performance.
Answer B: Abraham must pay Ruth because an intended beneficiary's rights are always free of defenses between the original contracting parties.
Answer C: Abraham must pay Ruth unless Ruth personally breached a duty owed to Abraham.
Answer D: Abraham has no defense because third-party beneficiary contracts are governed only by remedies law.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: An intended beneficiary's rights are generally subject to defenses the promisor could assert against the promisee. Abraham's promise to pay Ruth depended on Sarah completing the inventory catalog. Sarah's material nonperformance prevents Abraham's payment duty from maturing against Ruth.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is wrong because intended beneficiaries do not automatically take free of promisor defenses against the promisee. C: C is wrong because the relevant breach is Sarah's breach of the condition or constructive condition tied to Abraham's payment duty. D: D is wrong because third-party status does not erase the performance/consequence route.
lb_rule_reference: Third-party beneficiary derivative defenses / constructive condition
