Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Authentication and Identification
Question: In a federal assault trial, Rachel testifies that she received a call from the number saved in her phone as Samuel's. The caller did not give a name, but he referred to the exact location of a private argument Rachel had with Samuel an hour earlier and said, “You should not have told the guard.” Rachel offers the call as Samuel's. Samuel objects that Rachel did not hear the caller say his name. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection because a caller must state his name to authenticate a phone call.
Answer B: Sustain the objection because Rachel must produce a certified phone-company record.
Answer C: Overrule the objection because the saved number and distinctive content provide sufficient evidence for a jury to find Samuel was the caller.
Answer D: Overrule the objection only if Rachel is certain beyond doubt that the caller was Samuel.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Telephone identity can be shown by circumstantial evidence, including the number called or displayed and distinctive content known to the alleged caller. These facts support a finding that Samuel was the caller; any uncertainty goes to weight.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because self-identification is not required when other identifying circumstances exist. B: B is wrong because certified phone records are not the only route. D: D is wrong because certainty beyond doubt is not the authentication threshold.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 901(b)(4)-(6)
lb_student_diagnostic: Name-statement and certified-record over-demand; ignores circumstantial authentication.
