Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Officers followed a drug suspect into an apartment building. They lost sight of him but smelled marijuana coming from one of two apartments. They knocked loudly on the left apartment door and announced "Police!" They heard shuffling sounds and what seemed like running inside. Believing evidence was being destroyed, officers kicked the door down without a warrant and found cocaine. The left apartment turned out to be occupied by an uninvolved tenant, not the suspect. The tenant moves to suppress. Should the court grant the motion to suppress the cocaine?
Answer A: No, because the officers had probable cause to believe drugs were being destroyed and were justified in entering immediately
Answer B: No, because officers may always enter to prevent destruction of evidence whenever they hear sounds suggesting movement inside
Answer C: Yes, because officers lacked sufficient probable cause specific to this apartment and the sounds of shuffling were insufficient to establish imminent destruction of evidence
Answer D: Yes, because the officers created the exigency by announcing their presence and therefore may not rely on any resulting exigency to justify entry
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The smell of marijuana in a hallway between two apartments does not provide particularized probable cause directed at the left apartment specifically. Beyond that, shuffling sounds after a knock are equally consistent with someone walking to the door as with evidence destruction. The exigency exception for imminent destruction requires both probable cause that the specific premises contain contraband and specific articulable facts that destruction is actually imminent. Neither condition is adequately established here. Suppression is required.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Under Kentucky v. King, the key question is whether police created the exigency through a Fourth Amendment violation. If they did, they cannot rely on that exigency to justify warrantless entry. The analysis requires examining what the officers did before entry. Here, however, the officers' knock-and-announce was not itself a Fourth Amendment violation (officers may knock). The real issue is whether smelling marijuana + shuffling sounds provided probable cause and a genuine exigency — which requires a fact-specific analysis showing the shuffling was destruction, not mere movement. B: Sounds of movement (shuffling, footsteps) alone do not establish probable cause that evidence is being destroyed. Officers need specific, articulable facts suggesting imminent destruction — not just the general sounds of someone moving around in an apartment after hearing a knock. D: Kentucky v. King held that police announcing their presence with a knock is not itself a Fourth Amendment violation and does not count as creating the exigency. Police become unable to rely on the resulting exigency only if they created it by engaging in conduct that violated the Fourth Amendment. A lawful knock does not cross that line.
lb_rule_reference: Police may enter without a warrant when they have probable cause plus exigent circumstances; however, police may not deliberately create the exigency through a Fourth Amendment violation in order to justify a warrantless entry (Kentucky v. King)
