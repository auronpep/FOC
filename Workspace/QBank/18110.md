Question: At a private hospital meeting, a rival doctor falsely said that the plaintiff doctor had no medical license. The statement was heard by several hospital administrators. The plaintiff doctor sued for slander but could not prove any specific lost patient. Can the plaintiff avoid proving special damages under common-law slander rules?
Answer A: Yes, because the spoken charge harmed the plaintiff in her profession and is slander per se
Answer B: No, because all spoken defamation requires special damages
Answer C: No, because only accusations of crime can be slander per se
Answer D: Yes, but only because the accusation was written down
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A false statement that a doctor lacks a license directly impugns professional competence and fits a per se category.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: Ordinary slander requires special damages, but slander per se is an exception. C: Business or professional incompetence is also a per se category. D: The facts describe spoken words; the correct theory is slander per se.
lb_rule_reference: Spoken words that impugn a plaintiff's business or profession can be slander per se, allowing presumed damages under common-law rules unless constitutional limits apply.
