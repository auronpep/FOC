MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Inchoate Crimes
Outline: Conspiracy
Question: Agent Reyes, working undercover, approached Leonard and proposed that they jointly sell stolen electronics. Leonard agreed enthusiastically, suggested a meeting location to exchange the goods, and provided Agent Reyes with a list of items he could supply. At no point did Agent Reyes actually intend to sell anything. Leonard was arrested and charged with conspiracy to sell stolen property under common law. Leonard argues the conspiracy charge must fail. Under the common-law bilateral conspiracy rule, is Leonard correct?
Answer A: No, because Leonard's intent and overt acts are sufficient for conspiracy regardless of Reyes's intent
Answer B: Yes, because conspiracy requires a genuine agreement between at least two persons who both intend to commit the crime
Answer C: Yes, because government agents cannot be co-conspirators as a matter of law
Answer D: No, because Leonard committed solicitation when he approached Reyes
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: Common-law conspiracy demands a true bilateral agreement — a meeting of the minds between two or more people, each of whom genuinely intends to advance the criminal scheme. Agent Reyes was feigning agreement; she never intended to complete any crime. Because one party to the purported agreement lacked genuine criminal intent, no conspiracy was formed under the bilateral rule. Leonard's charge fails. (This would be different under the MPC unilateral approach, where only Leonard's agreement matters.)
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: At common law, conspiracy is inherently bilateral. The crime is the agreement of two or more minds to pursue a criminal objective. Because Agent Reyes never genuinely agreed, there was no meeting of criminal minds. Leonard's intent alone cannot supply the bilateral agreement the crime requires. C: The reason is not a special immunity rule for government agents. The reason is the bilateral agreement requirement: because Reyes lacked genuine criminal intent, the bilateral agreement element is not met. Framing it as agent immunity misstates the doctrinal basis. D: It was Reyes who approached Leonard, not the other way around, so the solicitation framing is factually wrong. Moreover, the question asks about conspiracy. Even if solicitation were charged, that would not establish conspiracy liability.
lb_rule_reference: At common law, conspiracy requires a genuine bilateral agreement between two or more persons who each intend to further the criminal objective; an agreement with an undercover officer who feigns consent does not satisfy this requirement
