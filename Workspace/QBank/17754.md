Question: Benjamin agreed to sell a beverage company “all aluminum cans required for its regional seltzer line,” with an estimate of 500,000 cans per quarter. After the beverage company obtained a national grocery account, it demanded 2,100,000 cans for the next quarter. Benjamin offered the company's precontract production records and the written estimate to show normal or comparable requirements. The beverage company objects that the records are outside the final writing. Should the court consider Benjamin's evidence?
Answer A: No, because prior production records are parol evidence that varies the final writing.
Answer B: No, because estimates are never relevant once a requirements term is enforceable.
Answer C: Yes, because normal or comparable prior requirements and a stated estimate are relevant to whether the demand is unreasonably disproportionate.
Answer D: Yes, but only after breach is established, because the evidence can be used only to calculate damages.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: A requirements term allows actual good-faith requirements, but no quantity unreasonably disproportionate to a stated estimate or normal/comparable prior requirements may be demanded. The trigger facts are the 500,000-can estimate, the sharp jump to 2,100,000 cans, and the records offered to show comparable prior requirements. The decisive lock is offered purpose: the evidence measures the contractual quantity limit and is not offered to contradict the writing.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because the records help apply the requirements term rather than vary it. B: B is wrong because estimates and comparable requirements are relevant to the statutory limit. D: D is wrong because the evidence affects the content and legal limit of the demand, not merely damages after breach.
lb_rule_reference: UCC 2-306(1) requirements contracts; stated estimate and comparable prior requirements
