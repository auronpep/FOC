Outline: Defamation
Question: A restaurant owner organized rallies, gave interviews, and ran a website opposing a proposed downtown curfew. During that debate, a columnist falsely wrote that the owner had fabricated curfew crime statistics. The columnist relied on one careless source but had no serious reason to doubt the source. Is the owner likely to recover in a defamation claim against the columnist without proving actual malice?
Answer A: Yes, because the owner is not famous generally
Answer B: No, because the owner was a limited-purpose public figure for the curfew controversy
Answer C: Yes, because careless reliance on one source is actual malice
Answer D: No, because public figures cannot sue for defamation
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The owner voluntarily led public advocacy on the curfew issue, and the statement concerned that controversy, so actual malice is required.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Limited-purpose public figure status can arise from voluntary prominent participation in a particular public controversy. C: Actual malice requires knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard, not merely negligent investigation. D: Public figures can sue if they prove falsity and actual malice.
lb_rule_reference: A plaintiff who voluntarily becomes a prominent participant in a public controversy is a limited-purpose public figure for statements related to that controversy.
