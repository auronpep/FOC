MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Inchoate Crimes
Outline: Attempt
Question: Greta believed that it was illegal to purchase a particular imported cheese without a special government permit. Acting on this belief, she purchased a wheel of the cheese from a local gourmet shop, believing she was committing a crime. In fact, no law prohibited the purchase of that cheese; Greta had misread a trade regulation that had been repealed years earlier. The prosecutor charges Greta with attempted violation of the (nonexistent) permit law. Greta raises legal impossibility as a defense. Under common law, how should the court rule?
Answer A: The defense fails because Greta's intent to commit a crime is sufficient for attempt
Answer B: The defense succeeds because the completed act would not have been a crime
Answer C: The defense fails because Greta took a substantial step toward what she believed to be a crime
Answer D: The defense fails because Greta's mistake about the law does not negate criminal intent
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: True legal impossibility exists when the defendant's completed conduct — taken exactly as she believed it to be — would not constitute a crime. Greta's acts (buying cheese) are entirely lawful. The nonexistent permit law cannot supply the criminal element. Common law recognizes this as a complete defense to attempt, unlike factual impossibility (where the act would have been criminal had facts been as the defendant believed). The MPC largely abolishes this distinction, but the question specifies common law.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: At common law, true legal impossibility is a complete defense. If the acts as actually performed — buying cheese lawfully — do not constitute any crime, there is nothing to "attempt." Criminal attempt requires that the completed conduct would be criminal; here it is not. Greta's mistaken belief cannot transform a lawful purchase into a crime. C: The question calls for common-law analysis. Common law recognizes legal impossibility as a defense regardless of how many steps the defendant took toward the (nonexistent) offense. The substantial-step standard is the MPC test and does not apply here. D: The "mistake of law is no defense" maxim applies to defendants who know the facts but are ignorant of the law making those facts criminal. Legal impossibility is conceptually different: the acts themselves, even as believed, are not criminal. It is not that Greta didn't know the law — it is that the law simply does not exist.
lb_rule_reference: At common law, true legal impossibility — where the defendant's completed acts, even as she believed them to be, would not constitute a crime — is a defense to attempt
