MBE Subjects (1 of 7): Criminal Law and Procedure
Subtopic: Inchoate Crimes
Outline: Attempt
Question: Lena discovered her roommate, Cam, asleep in the apartment. Enraged over a dispute about rent money, Lena raised a ceramic vase over Cam's head intending to strike him. She stopped herself at the last moment and set the vase down after deciding it was not worth the trouble. Cam never stirred and had no awareness of what had occurred. Lena is charged with assault. Under the attempted-battery theory of assault, which is recognized at common law, is Lena guilty of assault?
Answer A: No, because Cam was never placed in apprehension of imminent contact, an essential element of assault
Answer B: No, because Lena voluntarily abandoned her plan, negating criminal intent
Answer C: Yes, because under the attempted-battery theory, Lena took a substantial step toward committing battery with the intent to complete it, and victim awareness is not required
Answer D: Yes, but only because Lena had the apparent ability to complete the battery
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: At common law, assault may be grounded in either (1) causing reasonable apprehension of imminent battery, or (2) an attempted battery. The attempted-battery theory treats assault as an inchoate crime: the defendant must have the intent to commit a battery and take a substantial step toward its completion. Raising a vase directly over a person's head with intent to strike is a substantial step. Because the theory is premised on the defendant's act and intent — not the victim's mental state — the fact that Cam was asleep and unaware is irrelevant. Lena's stopping does not constitute true abandonment on these facts.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: The question asks specifically about the attempted-battery theory, under which apprehension by the victim is NOT required. Under this theory, assault is an attempted battery — the defendant's act of raising the vase with intent to strike constitutes an attempt regardless of whether the victim was aware. The apprehension requirement belongs to the alternative, separate theory. B: Voluntary abandonment can be a defense to attempt crimes in many jurisdictions, but it requires true renunciation — not merely stopping because the act was inconvenient. "Not worth the trouble" is not a genuine change of heart based on recognition of wrongdoing; it is a pragmatic pause. Moreover, some jurisdictions do not recognize abandonment as a defense once the defendant has taken a substantial step. D: Apparent ability matters primarily under the apprehension theory (the victim must perceive the defendant as capable of completing the threatened act). Under the attempted-battery theory, it is the defendant's intent and substantial step that matter, not whether the victim perceived apparent ability. This answer misstates which element is doing the decisive work.
lb_rule_reference: Under the attempted-battery theory of assault, no apprehension by the victim is required; under the apprehension theory, the victim must be aware of the threatened contact — most jurisdictions require awareness, but the attempted-battery theory does not
