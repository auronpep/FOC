Outline: Statute of Frauds
Question: David owes a supplier $20,000 for tools. David's father, Jacob, orally tells the supplier, “If David does not pay you, I will.” Jacob receives no business benefit from the promise; he says he is trying to help his son. David defaults, and the supplier sues Jacob. What is Jacob's strongest defense?
Answer A: No contract was formed because a father cannot contract for a child's debt.
Answer B: The promise is unenforceable because all family-related promises are gifts.
Answer C: The promise is within the suretyship Statute of Frauds and is unenforceable absent a sufficient writing.
Answer D: The promise is enforceable because the supplier gave consideration to David.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: A promise to answer for the debt of another is within the suretyship provision of the Statute of Frauds unless an exception applies. Jacob's promise is collateral and motivated by family affection, not primarily by his own economic advantage.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because family relationship does not prevent contract formation. B: B is wrong because the issue is not gift status alone but the suretyship writing requirement. D: D is wrong because consideration to the principal debtor does not eliminate the suretyship SOF defense against Jacob.
lb_rule_reference: Common-law SOF — suretyship
