Question: A town contracted with a builder to repair a public bridge. The contract did not mention any nearby stores or promise performance directly to them. David, who owned a nearby store, heard about the project and spent money advertising that his store would be easier to reach after the repairs. The town and builder later postponed the bridge work for six months. David sued as a third-party beneficiary. Does David's advertising expense give him enforceable beneficiary rights?
Answer A: Yes, because he relied financially on the contract.
Answer B: Yes, because bridge repairs were likely to benefit his store.
Answer C: No, but only because public contracts can never create intended beneficiaries.
Answer D: No, because reliance cannot vest rights for a person who is only an incidental beneficiary.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: David was only incidentally benefited by the bridge contract. Reliance can vest rights only for an intended beneficiary; it does not transform an incidental beneficiary into one. The contract showed a public project, not a direct enforceable promise to David.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because reliance does not create intended-beneficiary status by itself. B: B is wrong because foreseeable benefit is not enough. C: C is wrong because some public contracts could expressly create enforceable beneficiary rights, but this one did not.
