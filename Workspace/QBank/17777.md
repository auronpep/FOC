Outline: Rule of completeness
Question: In a federal fraud trial, the prosecutor offers the first sentence of an email written by Daniel to his accountant: “I knew the reported income was too low.” The prosecutor stops reading there. The next sentence in the same email says, “But I told the bookkeeper to correct it before the return was filed.” Daniel asks the court to require the prosecutor to introduce the second sentence immediately. The prosecutor objects that the second sentence is hearsay. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Exclude the second sentence because Daniel is offering his own out-of-court statement for its truth.
Answer B: Require the second sentence to be introduced at that time if fairness requires it to avoid a misleading impression from the first sentence.
Answer C: Admit the entire email, because introduction of one sentence makes every statement in the email admissible.
Answer D: Require Daniel to wait until his case-in-chief to introduce the second sentence, because completeness can never interrupt the proponent's proof.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: When one party introduces part of a statement, the adverse party may require introduction, at that time, of another part or related statement that fairness requires to be considered with it. The second sentence directly changes the meaning of the excerpt by explaining Daniel's claimed corrective instruction. If fairness requires completion, the hearsay objection does not bar the completing statement.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because Rule 106 can permit a completing statement over a hearsay objection when fairness requires completion. C: C is wrong because completeness does not admit the entire email; only the part needed to correct the misleading impression comes in. D: D is wrong because the rule is designed to permit completion at the time the partial statement is introduced when fairness requires contemporaneous context.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 106 / fairness-required remainder
