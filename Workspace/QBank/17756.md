Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Authentication and Identification
Question: In a products-liability action, Samuel offers a cracked helmet after a crash. The helmet bears a molded mark reading 'Solomon Shield Co.' and a serial sticker in the same name, both appearing to be affixed during manufacture. Solomon Shield objects that the markings are out-of-court statements and cannot authenticate the helmet. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Sustain the objection because a product inscription is hearsay whenever it identifies the maker.
Answer B: Sustain the objection unless Samuel calls the person who placed the sticker on the helmet.
Answer C: Overrule the objection because trade inscriptions and labels affixed in the course of business can authenticate origin.
Answer D: Overrule the objection only if Solomon Shield admits it made the helmet.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Trade inscriptions and similar labels indicating origin, ownership, or control are self-authenticating. They may identify the source of a product without testimony from the person who affixed them.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because the trade inscription functions as an authentication/source marker. B: B is wrong because the person who placed the sticker is not required for authentication. D: D is wrong because a party admission is not required.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 902(7)
lb_student_diagnostic: Student treats product identity marks as hearsay and misses self-authentication.
