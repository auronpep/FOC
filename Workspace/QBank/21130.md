Question: Dena learned that her neighbor, Carl, had reported her to animal control for keeping illegal animals. Furious, she drove to a hiking trail she knew Carl used each morning, parked a half-mile away, hiked in before dawn, and crouched behind a boulder near the trail's midpoint. When Carl jogged past an hour later, Dena emerged and stabbed him three times with a knife she had brought from home. Carl died from the wounds. Dena told police she had been "waiting to confront him" but admitted she had the knife ready before Carl arrived. What is the most serious charge for which Dena can be convicted?
Answer A: Voluntary manslaughter, because Dena acted out of anger over Carl's report to animal control
Answer B: Second-degree murder, because there is no direct evidence Dena planned to kill Carl in advance
Answer C: First-degree murder, because lying in wait provides circumstantial evidence of premeditation and deliberation
Answer D: First-degree murder, but only because the killing was committed with a deadly weapon
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: First-degree murder requires willful, premeditated, and deliberate killing. Premeditation need not be proven by direct evidence; lying in wait is a recognized category of circumstantial evidence from which premeditation is inferred. Dena drove to the trail before dawn, hiked in, concealed herself, brought a weapon, and waited over an hour — this is paradigmatic lying in wait and satisfies both premeditation (prior thought) and deliberation (cool, planned action, not impulse).
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Voluntary manslaughter requires heat of passion arising from adequate provocation — typically sudden, in-the-moment provocation. Here, Dena had time to plan an ambush. Learning about a report is legally insufficient provocation, and the planning gap defeats any heat-of-passion claim entirely. B: Premeditation may be proven by circumstantial evidence. Concealment before dawn at a known route, possession of a weapon, and waiting over an hour are classic lying-in-wait facts from which a jury may infer premeditation and deliberation. D: Use of a deadly weapon does not, by itself, establish first-degree murder at common law. The correct basis for first-degree murder here is the lying-in-wait evidence of premeditation, not the type of weapon.
lb_rule_reference: Lying in wait is circumstantial evidence from which premeditation and deliberation may be inferred; the act of concealment and positioning to strike demonstrates advance thought and cool reflection
