Outline: Spousal Privileges
Question: In a federal criminal trial for bank fraud, the prosecution calls the defendant's current spouse. The spouse would testify that she saw the defendant place bank files in a locked cabinet at home. The spouse refuses to testify against the defendant. The defendant also objects. How should the court rule on the prosecution's request to compel the spouse's testimony?
Answer A: Compel the testimony because the spouse would testify only to an observation, not to a confidential marital communication.
Answer B: Refuse to compel the testimony because the witness-spouse holds the adverse spousal testimonial privilege in a federal criminal case.
Answer C: Exclude the testimony only if the defendant invokes the privilege, because the defendant-spouse controls adverse testimony.
Answer D: Compel the testimony because spousal privileges apply only in civil cases.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The federal adverse spousal testimonial privilege belongs to the witness-spouse. In a federal criminal case, a current spouse may refuse to testify adversely. The fact that the proposed testimony concerns an observation rather than a confidential marital communication defeats the separate marital-communications route, but it does not eliminate the witness-spouse's testimonial privilege.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because it correctly rejects marital-communications privilege but misses the separate testimonial privilege. C: C is wrong because it gives control to the defendant. Under the federal rule, the witness-spouse decides whether to testify adversely. D: D is wrong because the federal adverse spousal testimonial privilege is a criminal-case privilege, not a civil-only rule.
lb_rule_reference: Federal adverse spousal testimonial privilege belongs to the witness-spouse
lb_student_diagnostic: Student collapses spousal testimonial privilege with marital communications or assigns the holder to the wrong spouse.
