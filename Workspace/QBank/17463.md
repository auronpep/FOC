Outline: Prior inconsistent statement
Question: In a California state civil trial governed by the California Evidence Code, Ruth testifies that she did not see the delivery truck enter the intersection. On cross-examination, Ruth is asked about a statement she made to an investigator the day after the crash: “I saw the truck enter the intersection.” Ruth admits making the statement but says she was wrong then. The opponent offers the investigator's testimony about Ruth's prior statement for its truth. How should the court rule under California law?
Answer A: Exclude the testimony because only prior inconsistent statements made under oath at a proceeding may be used for their truth.
Answer B: Admit the testimony because California permits evidence of a witness's prior inconsistent statement for its truth if it is offered in compliance with the witness-examination requirement.
Answer C: Admit the testimony only to impeach Ruth, never for its truth.
Answer D: Exclude the testimony because Ruth admitted making the prior statement.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: California Evidence Code section 1235 provides that evidence of a witness's prior inconsistent statement is not made inadmissible by the hearsay rule if the statement is inconsistent with the witness's testimony and is offered in compliance with section 770. Ruth was examined about the statement while testifying, giving her an opportunity to explain or deny it. That California route differs from the federal rule for unsworn prior inconsistent statements.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because it imports the federal 801(d)(1)(A) oath-and-proceeding limitation into a California-law question. C: C is wrong because California permits substantive use when the statutory conditions are met. D: D is wrong because admitting the statement does not bar its use; her admission satisfies the opportunity framework.
lb_rule_reference: California Evidence Code §§ 1235 and 770 / California substantive PIS contrast
