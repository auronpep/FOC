Subtopic: Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons (Criminal Procedure)
Outline: Fourth Amendment
Question: Officers investigated Davis for a series of armed robberies and developed probable cause to believe he committed them. At 7 a.m. on a Tuesday, they went to Davis's apartment, knocked on the door, and when Davis answered, they stepped inside and arrested him without an arrest warrant. Davis moved to suppress all statements he made following the arrest, arguing the entry was unconstitutional. The government contends that probable cause alone justified the in-home arrest. Was the warrantless in-home arrest of Davis constitutional?
Answer A: Yes, because police had probable cause to believe Davis committed the robberies
Answer B: Yes, because Davis opened the door and was visible from a public vantage point
Answer C: No, because police needed an arrest warrant to arrest Davis inside his home and no exigent circumstances existed
Answer D: No, because the Fourth Amendment requires a search warrant, not an arrest warrant, to enter a home
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Payton v. New York* (1980) holds that the Fourth Amendment prohibits warrantless, non-consensual entry into a suspect's home to make a routine felony arrest. Because there were no exigent circumstances alleged and no arrest warrant, the entry and arrest were unconstitutional. Statements obtained as a direct result are subject to suppression under the fruit-of-the-poisonous-tree doctrine.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Under *Payton v. New York*, probable cause is necessary but not sufficient for an in-home arrest; an arrest warrant (or exigent circumstances) is also required. Probable cause alone does not authorize entry into a residence. B: An individual who answers a door remains in their home for Fourth Amendment purposes. *Payton* still requires a warrant; merely opening the door does not transform the space into a public area. D: Payton* specifically held that an arrest warrant — not a search warrant — is required to arrest someone in their own home. The two warrants serve different functions; getting the type wrong produces the wrong legal conclusion.
lb_rule_reference: Under *Payton v. New York*, police must have an arrest warrant to arrest a suspect inside the suspect's own home absent exigent circumstances; no warrant is needed for a public arrest based on probable cause
