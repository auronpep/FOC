Subtopic: Inchoate Crimes
Outline: Attempt
Question: Vince was furious at his neighbor Hector and decided to shoot him. Vince fired his gun at Hector from across the street. The bullet missed Hector but struck and killed Clara, a bystander who happened to be walking past Hector at that moment. Vince did not know Clara and had no grievance with her. Under the doctrine of transferred intent, which charges may the prosecution bring against Vince arising out of this incident?
Answer A: Murder of Clara only, because Vince's intent merged into the completed homicide
Answer B: Attempted murder of Hector only, because Vince never intended to harm Clara
Answer C: Both murder of Clara and attempted murder of Hector, because transferred intent transfers to the unintended victim while leaving attempt liability for the intended target intact
Answer D: Involuntary manslaughter of Clara only, because Vince acted recklessly in firing a gun in a populated area
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: Under the doctrine of transferred intent, Vince's intent to kill Hector transfers to Clara because Clara was the actual victim of the same type of crime (homicide). Vince is therefore liable for Clara's murder. Simultaneously, Vince is liable for attempted murder of Hector because he took a substantial step toward killing Hector with the required specific intent. Double jeopardy does not bar both charges because they involve different victims. The transferred-intent doctrine does not merge or eliminate the attempt charge; it adds liability for the unintended victim.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: Transferred intent does not eliminate liability for the attempt against the intended target. Vince may be charged with both (1) murder of Clara (transferred intent) and (2) attempted murder of Hector (the intended target). The doctrine transfers the intent to the unintended victim but does not extinguish liability for what was attempted against the intended victim. B: The transferred-intent doctrine exists precisely to address this situation. When a defendant's act aimed at victim A accidentally harms victim B, the intent transfers to victim B. Limiting charges only to the intended victim would create an unjust outcome where causing an actual death produces less liability than an unsuccessful attempt. D: Vince harbored premeditated intent to kill a specific person. That intent — purposeful, deliberate — transfers to the unintended victim. The transferred-intent doctrine upgrades the mental state: Vince is guilty of murder (with transferred intent to kill), not merely the lower culpability of involuntary manslaughter. Recklessness analysis would apply only if there were no specific intent to kill; here there clearly was.
lb_rule_reference: Under transferred intent, if defendant intends to commit a crime against person A but the act harms person B, the intent transfers to person B; defendant is liable for the crime against B and may also be liable for attempt against A
