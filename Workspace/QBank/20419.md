Question: A retailer bought ten commercial printers from a manufacturer under a contract that included ordinary setup services. Most of the price was allocated to the printers. The writing required each printer to arrive with a “starter cartridge kit,” but did not define that phrase. In five prior printer purchases between the same parties, the manufacturer supplied four cartridges per printer under the same phrase. In the broader office-equipment trade, a starter cartridge kit usually means one cartridge per printer. The manufacturer shipped one cartridge per printer. What is the retailer's best Content/Meaning argument?
Answer A: Because setup services were included, common-law services rules apply and no UCC course evidence may be considered.
Answer B: Because the trade usage is industry-wide, it controls over the parties' prior dealings.
Answer C: The predominant purpose was goods, and the parties' prior course of dealing supplies a stronger meaning source than inconsistent general trade usage.
Answer D: The retailer must lose unless it proves the printers could not function with one cartridge.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that for a goods-dominant mixed contract, UCC meaning rules can apply, and when meaning sources cannot reasonably be reconciled, a course of dealing between the parties is stronger than general trade usage. The trigger facts are the price allocation toward printers, the repeated prior four-cartridge transactions, and the conflicting industry usage. The decisive locks are source law and hierarchy. C selects the correct source and ranks the private course over general usage.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because incidental setup services do not necessarily make the transaction a services contract. B: B is wrong because it reverses the UCC hierarchy by preferring general usage over the parties' own repeated course. D: D is wrong because functionality is a performance/damages decoy; the issue is what the starter-kit term means.
