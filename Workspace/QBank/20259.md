Subtopic: Separation of Powers
Outline: Taxing and spending power
Question: A federal statute requires any individual or entity owning more than 100 cars to ensure that at least 10 percent of those cars are electric-powered. A city has sued the federal official responsible for enforcing this statute in federal district court, seeking an injunction prohibiting enforcement of the statute on the ground that it is unconstitutional. Should the court grant the injunction?
Answer A: No, because the statute is valid under the commerce clause and does not violate the Tenth Amendment.
Answer B: No, because the federal government has sovereign immunity and cannot be sued without its explicit consent.
Answer C: Yes, because the statute violates the reserved rights of the states under the Tenth Amendment.
Answer D: Yes, because as applied to state and local governments, the statute exceeds Congress's power under the commerce clause.
Correct Answer: A
Correct Answer Explanation: A is correct. The statute falls within the scope of congressional power to regulate commercial activity that, in the aggregate, has a substantial effect on interstate commerce.
Wrong Answer Explanations: B: B is incorrect. Although the federal government does enjoy sovereign immunity from being sued as a party, this immunity applies only to suits seeking compensatory monetary relief and not to suits seeking only prospective injunctive relief. The suit here seeks only prospective injunctive relief. C: C is incorrect. The statute does not violate the Tenth Amendment, which protects local governments from being commandeered by the federal government. This statute both regulates commercial activity having a substantial effect on interstate commerce and applies consistently to all entities engaging in the same type of conduct. D: D is incorrect. On the contrary, this statute falls squarely within congressional commerce powers to regulate activities having a substantial effect on interstate commerce, as explained above.
additional_info: Article I, Section 8, Clause 3 empowers Congress to regulate commerce with foreign nations and among the several states, and with the Indian tribes. Commerce is defined broadly and includes activity involving or affecting more than one state. To be within Congress's power under the Commerce Clause, a federal law must regulate channels of interstate commerce, instrumentalities of interstate commerce and persons and things in interstate commerce, articles moving in interstate commerce, or activities that have a substantial effect on interstate commerce. If regulated intrastate activity is not commercial or economic, aggregation is generally unavailable absent a direct substantial economic effect. Transportation or traffic has consistently been treated as commerce. The Tenth Amendment reserves undelegated powers to the states or the people, but the Supreme Court's broad reading of enumerated federal powers leaves little state power exclusive. The anti-commandeering doctrine prevents Congress from requiring states or state officials to regulate private citizens, but Congress may directly regulate states and local governments on the same terms as other regulated entities when acting within an enumerated power. Here, the electric-car ownership requirement regulates car ownership/transportation/economic activity affecting interstate commerce and does not commandeer the city to regulate others.
