Subtopic: Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence
Outline: Definition
Question: Jacob is on trial for failing to pay a supplier under a written contract. The supplier offers evidence that Jacob's cousin had once been convicted of theft. The supplier argues that the conviction shows that Jacob's family was not trustworthy. How should the court rule?
Answer A: Admit the evidence, because any evidence about dishonesty in Jacob's family has some probative value.
Answer B: Admit the evidence only if Jacob calls a character witness.
Answer C: Exclude the evidence only if Jacob proves that he had no contact with his cousin.
Answer D: Exclude the evidence, because the cousin's conviction does not make any fact of consequence in the contract-payment dispute more or less probable.
Correct Answer: D
Correct Answer Explanation: The cousin's conviction has no logical bearing on whether Jacob failed to pay under the contract. It does not concern a fact of consequence and is not relevant merely because it sounds negative.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because relevance requires a connection to a consequential fact in the case. B: B is wrong because the supplier has not identified a permissible relevance route; possible character mechanics do not supply one here. C: C is wrong because the absence or presence of contact is not the core problem; the offered conviction still lacks a consequential link.
lb_rule_reference: FRE 401 / FRE 402
lb_student_diagnostic: The trap is treating negative background information as relevance.
