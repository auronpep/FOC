Question: Leah granted a written easement to “John Electric Company, its successors and assigns, to install and maintain underground electric lines across Leah's land.” John Electric Company later merged into Peter Power Company, which continued operating the same lines in the same location. May Leah bar Peter Power Company from using the easement solely because the original grantee no longer exists?
Answer A: Yes, because all easements in gross are personal and nontransferable.
Answer B: Yes, because underground utility rights can exist only as licenses.
Answer C: No, because a commercial easement in gross is generally transferable, especially when the grant refers to successors and assigns.
Answer D: No, because Peter Power Company now owns Leah's land in fee simple.
Correct Answer: C
Correct Answer Explanation: A commercial easement in gross is generally transferable unless the grant provides otherwise. The grant expressly referred to successors and assigns, and the successor company is using the same utility easement in the same place.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because it overstates the nontransferability of easements in gross; commercial easements are commonly transferable. B: B is wrong because a written utility easement may be a property interest, not merely a license. C is correct for the reasons above. D: D is wrong because an easement is a limited use right, not fee ownership.
lb_rule_reference: Commercial easement in gross / assignability
lb_student_diagnostic: The trap is treating every in-gross easement as personal and nonassignable.
