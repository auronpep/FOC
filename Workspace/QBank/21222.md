Question: Rachel hired David to design a donor website. The signed services agreement required David to deliver a “final site acceptable to Rachel's campaign committee.” The agreement did not define acceptable. David delivered a site that worked technically, but it omitted the donation-tracking dashboard discussed in committee meetings. Rachel refused to pay the final installment. David argues that acceptability means only technical functionality. What is Rachel's best Content/Meaning argument?
Answer A: Because David substantially performed a working site, the acceptability language has no legal effect.
Answer B: The acceptability phrase is ambiguous, so evidence of the committee's communicated design requirements may be used to determine what acceptance required.
Answer C: Rachel may refuse payment only if she proves fraud in the drafting of the agreement.
Answer D: The court should ignore the committee evidence because the dispute concerns performance, not contract meaning.
Correct Answer: B
Correct Answer Explanation: The narrow rule is that an ambiguous approval or satisfaction term must be interpreted before the court can decide whether performance satisfied it. The trigger facts are the undefined “acceptable to Rachel's campaign committee” phrase and the omitted dashboard. The decisive locks are term status and stage crossover: this is a performance dispute, but the primary route is Content/Meaning because the court first must decide what the approval clause required. Evidence of communicated committee requirements can explain the acceptance standard.
Wrong Answer Explanations: A: A is wrong because substantial performance cannot be measured until the contractual acceptance standard is interpreted. C: C is wrong because fraud is not required when evidence is offered to explain ambiguity. D: D is wrong because performance facts do not displace the meaning question; they make the meaning question decisive.
