### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** The clean foreseeability target is not the handcart accident, the precise chain of collisions, or Stephen’s own impaired driving. The target is whether a person might foreseeably be in the lane Stephen chose to drive through.

**Gold Key:** For negligence by an active operator, ask what person or class of persons was foreseeably put at risk by the defendant’s conduct. Do not require foreseeability of the exact prior event that placed the plaintiff there, and do not require foreseeability of the defendant’s own negligent breach.

**Silver Key:** In a “foreseeable that ___” answer array, compare the noun phrase after “that.” The credited answer usually names the general plaintiff/risk target, while the traps name the old set-stage event, the exact chain, or the defendant’s own wrongdoing.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells the exact-chain instinct. It asks whether Stephen had to foresee a person clipped by a prop cart would suffer a second collision within minutes. The breaker is the target. Liability does not turn on that specific sequence; it turns on whether a person in the loading lane was foreseeable.

**C.** This choice sells the “negligence itself must be foreseeable” instinct. It points to Stephen’s drowsy operation, which feels morally central because that is the careless act. The breaker is the Gold Key: the plaintiff does not need to show Stephen’s own negligent breach was foreseeable; the plaintiff needs the risk to a person in the lane to be foreseeable.

**D.** This choice sells the set-stage instinct. It focuses on Timothy’s earlier careless handcart conduct and the fact that Timothy left Lydia in the lane. The breaker is the timing frame. Lydia’s presence in the lane preexisted Stephen’s tortious act, so Stephen’s liability does not turn on whether Timothy’s earlier conduct was foreseeable to Stephen.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The transformed item preserves the original legal issue and credited outcome. The original row credits the answer asking whether a person would be in the roadway, and explains that the prior circumstances placing the pedestrian there do not control; the same structure is preserved here with the loading lane. 

The lawyer-confirmation rule is the Palsgraf-style foreseeable-plaintiff / foreseeable-risk frame: negligence is relational, and the relevant duty/risk inquiry asks whether the plaintiff or class of persons was within the foreseeable range of danger. Palsgraf states the classic formulation that the reasonably perceived risk defines the duty, and risk is relational to persons within the range of apprehension. ([vLex][1])

Modern Restatement framing can describe the same result through ordinary duty and scope of liability: an actor ordinarily has a duty of reasonable care when conduct creates a risk of physical harm, and liability is limited to harms resulting from the risks that made the conduct tortious. ([Justia][2])

No authority found contradicts the supplied key. The doctrinal vocabulary may be duty, breach, or scope-of-risk depending on jurisdictional framing, but the MBE result remains unchanged.
