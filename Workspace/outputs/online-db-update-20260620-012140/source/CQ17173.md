**Gold Key:** When the plaintiff fully performs and the only thing left is the defendant’s payment of a definite contract price, the recovery is the bargain price. Do not relabel a higher market value as “reliance” to get more.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B.** This choice sells a true-sounding instinct: “reliance” is not the right label once the contract price is fixed. The breaker is that the answer jumps from “not a reliance measure” to “nothing,” skipping the contract-price recovery that the call asks for.

**D.** This is the dominant trap. ### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule is unchanged from the source item: a fully performing service provider under a valid fixed-price contract recovers the agreed contract price, not a higher reasonable value by calling the claim “reliance.” Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 344 distinguishes expectation, reliance, and restitution interests; expectation protects the benefit of the bargain, reliance reimburses loss from relying on the contract, and restitution restores benefits conferred. ([Open Casebook][1]) Restatement § 347 supplies the expectation measure; Restatement § 349 describes reliance as an alternative to expectation, not as an automatic reasonable-value override. ([Open Casebook][1]) Restatement § 373(2) also confirms the core restitution limit: no restitution right exists when the injured party has fully performed and the only remaining duty is payment of a definite sum. ([Open Casebook][2]) That confirms the credited answer: Mary gets $12,600, not $20,400, $0, or $7,800.
