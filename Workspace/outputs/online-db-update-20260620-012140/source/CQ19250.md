### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** The clause asks whether Lydia is satisfied that the printer produces a stated commercial output. That is a measurable performance target, not a personal-aesthetic taste term. **Gold Key:** a satisfaction clause about commercial utility or mechanical performance is judged objectively when a reasonable-person test is practicable. **Silver Key:** underline what the party must be satisfied *about* before honoring “I was not satisfied.” Here, the target is 3,200 finished cards per hour, and the printer produced 3,360. The rent condition is satisfied.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** The trap sounds lawyerly because restitution can matter when an express condition really fails. The breaker is earlier: the condition did not fail once the measurable performance clause is judged by the commercial-output standard.

**C.** The trap sells the student myth that the word “satisfied” always makes the buyer the sole judge. The breaker is the absolute wording: “any satisfaction clause” and “unlimited subjective discretion” overclaim before the facts even matter.

**D.** The trap sells a familiar honesty move: Lydia truly dislikes the printer, so satisfaction feels absent. **Gold Key:** honest personal dissatisfaction matters for taste or aesthetics, but this clause targets measurable commercial output. **Silver Key:** do not let “honest dislike” move you off the object of the clause.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The source row is Q#19250, a Contracts express-conditions item testing a satisfaction condition tied to objective commercial utility; the supplied original credited answer is D, and the official explanation says commercial/mechanical satisfaction is generally judged by objective reasonableness.  The transform preserves that issue and outcome: rent is due because the measurable performance condition was satisfied.

Legal confirmation: Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 228 prefers a reasonable-person interpretation when a satisfaction condition can practicably be judged by whether a reasonable person in the obligor’s position would be satisfied. ([Open Casebook][1]) Courts also describe commercial value, quality, operative fitness, and mechanical utility satisfaction clauses as governed by a reasonableness standard rather than pure subjective whim. ([Justia][2])

The new question keeps the same hard trap: the word “satisfied” invites a subjective-discretion answer, but the object of satisfaction is measurable commercial performance. The facts exceed the stated metric and introduce an honest but legally insufficient personal dislike.
