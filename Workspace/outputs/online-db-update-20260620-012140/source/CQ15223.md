**BARMATRIX QID:** `15223_lydias_loaves`

Lydia’s Loaves, a for-profit food company, contracted with Barnabas Couriers to deliver boxed meals to remote retreat centers around the world. The contract between Lydia’s Loaves and Barnabas Couriers was terminable at will.

Barnabas Couriers then entered into a contract with Peter’s Pilots to provide an airplane to deliver the meals. The contract between Barnabas Couriers and Peter’s Pilots was also terminable at will.

Lydia’s Loaves was displeased with Peter’s Pilots because of a previous business dispute between them. Upon learning of Barnabas Couriers’ contract with Peter’s Pilots, Lydia’s Loaves terminated its contract with Barnabas Couriers in order to cause Peter’s Pilots to lose the business. After Lydia’s Loaves terminated Barnabas Couriers’ contract, Barnabas Couriers had no choice but to terminate the Peter’s Pilots contract.

**If Peter’s Pilots sues Barnabas Couriers for tortious interference with contract, will Peter’s Pilots prevail?**

### 5. Correct answer

**A. No, because Peter’s Pilots and Barnabas Couriers were the parties to the contract.**

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

A is correct.

**Gold Key:** Tortious interference with contract is an outsider tort. The defendant must interfere with a contract between the plaintiff and a third person. Here, Peter’s Pilots sued Barnabas Couriers, but Peter’s Pilots and Barnabas Couriers were the two parties to the very contract at issue. A claim against Barnabas would sound in contract, not tortious interference.

**Silver Key:** Circle the defendant named in the call. The call asks whether Peter’s Pilots can sue **Barnabas Couriers** for tortious interference. Do not drift to Lydia’s Loaves, even though Lydia’s Loaves caused the business loss.

The transformed result matches the supplied source row: the original item also keyed A because the plaintiff sued its own contracting party rather than the outside interferer. 

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B is wrong.**
This reaches the correct outcome but gives the wrong reason. Peter’s Pilots did not sue Lydia’s Loaves. It sued Barnabas Couriers. Whether Peter’s Pilots was in privity with Lydia’s Loaves does not answer the call.

**Silver Key:** The trap is wrong-defendant drift. The answer talks about the Peter–Lydia relationship, but the lawsuit is Peter v. Barnabas.

**C is wrong.**
This answer focuses on whether Peter’s Pilots performed poorly. That is not the decisive frame. Peter’s Pilots is suing its own contracting party for tortious interference with that same contract.

**Gold Key:** The plaintiff’s counterparty is not the outside interferer for that contract. A poor-performance reason might matter in a contract dispute, but it does not make the counterparty a tortious-interference defendant.

**D is wrong.**
This invents a participation rule. Barnabas Couriers did not become part of Lydia’s Loaves’ acts merely because Barnabas terminated the downstream contract after Lydia terminated the upstream contract. The stem says Barnabas had no choice after Lydia pulled the job.

### 8. Black-letter verification and legal reasoning

The supplied official explanation cites **Restatement (Second) of Torts § 766** and states that tortious interference provides a cause of action against one who improperly interferes with performance of a contract between the plaintiff and a third person; it also states that the proper tort defendant in the original fact pattern would have been the outside food company, not the contracting delivery service.  External legal confirmation likewise describes § 766 as requiring interference with a contract “between another and a third person,” which is the same outsider structure. ([Trepanier Law][1])

The legal rule, answer, and issue category are unchanged. The Christian variation only changes the names and surface setting.
