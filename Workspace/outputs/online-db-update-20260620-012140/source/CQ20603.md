### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Daniel is the person Ruth sued. The answer must explain why Daniel’s estate is or is not responsible for Ruth’s injury. The stem says Daniel was acting with reasonable care. That fact blocks the rescue-doctrine route against Daniel’s estate.

**Gold Key:** Rescue doctrine needs a negligent peril-maker. A rescuer’s injury is charged to the person whose negligence created the peril, not merely to the person who needed help.

**Silver Key:** Lock the target actor. When the rescuer sues the imperiled person’s estate, ask what the imperiled person did wrong. Do not start with how noble, reasonable, or unsuccessful the rescue was.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells the moral-policy move: courts do not want to discourage rescue. The breaker is that policy is not the operative test against Daniel’s estate; the answer never connects Daniel to a negligent creation of the peril.

**C.** This choice sells the rescuer-conduct move: Ruth’s attempt sounds careful and socially valuable. **Gold Key:** A reasonable rescue does not create liability against a careful imperiled person; the rescue doctrine needs negligent peril by the defendant.

**D.** This choice sells the failed-benefit move: because Ruth did not save Daniel, it feels as though there is no basis for recovery. The breaker is that rescue success is not the liability axis; if Daniel had negligently created the peril, failure alone would not cut off the rescuer’s claim.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The legal issue and credited outcome are unchanged from the supplied row: the rescuer may recover only if the defendant negligently created the peril; the row’s additional information states that a defendant is liable when he negligently puts himself or another in peril and the plaintiff is injured attempting rescue, but the hiker was acting with reasonable care and therefore breached no duty. 

External lawyer-confirmation check: Cornell’s Wex summary states the common-law starting point that there is generally no duty to rescue, but a duty may arise where an individual negligently creates the need for rescue. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) Wagner v. Applied to the variation, Daniel’s peril did not arise from his own failure to exercise reasonable care. Ruth’s reasonableness and the failed outcome do not supply Daniel’s missing negligence. The official result remains **No**.

## review_truth

A rescuer’s claim needs a negligent peril-maker; noble rescue alone does not create liability against a careful person.
