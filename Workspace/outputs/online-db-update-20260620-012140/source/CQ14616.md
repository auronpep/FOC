### 5. Correct answer

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**D is correct.** The first search was Lydia’s private action. The second search came after a federal agent encouraged Lydia and offered a reward for additional evidence. That timing changes the layer.

**Gold Key:** A private search does not trigger the Fourth Amendment. But when law enforcement encourages or participates enough that the private person acts as a government instrument, the Fourth Amendment applies. Here, the second search is the government-induced search.

**Silver Key:** Split the timeline. Do not choose “all” or “none” when the stem gives two searches with a government-contact event between them.

The court should suppress only the second set.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A is wrong.** It flattens the two searches into one. The first set can survive because Lydia acted privately. But the second set cannot be treated the same way because the agent encouraged and rewarded the second search.

**B is wrong.** “No probable cause” is the wrong all-purpose frame. A private person does not need probable cause before turning over what she found. Once Lydia became a government instrument for the second search, the problem was the government-induced warrantless search, not a universal probable-cause defect covering everything.

**C is wrong.** This is the dominant trap. The statute violation matters, but the stipulated private statutory violation does not itself answer the suppression question for all images. **Gold Key:** violation and remedy are separate links. The suppression result turns on the Fourth Amendment agency shift for the second search.

### 8. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule and result are unchanged from the original: the first set is not suppressed; the second set is suppressed. The Fourth Amendment reaches searches by government actors or agents, not purely private searches. LII summarizes that a Fourth Amendment search occurs when a governmental employee or government agent violates a reasonable expectation of privacy. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) In *United States v. Jacobsen*, the Supreme Court held that private carrier employees’ search, even if it would have been impermissible for government agents, did not violate the Fourth Amendment because it was private; later government action is tested by how far it exceeds the private search. ([Justia Law][2]) In *Skinner*, the Court stated that a private party may become an instrument or agent of the government depending on the degree of government participation. ([Justia Law][3])

Here, Lydia’s first search was private. The federal agent’s later encouragement and reward offer created government participation for the second search. For an ordinary criminal search, a warrantless search is generally unreasonable absent an exception. ([Legal Information Institute][1])

The statutory trap is also preserved. Federal law prohibits certain interceptions of wire, oral, or electronic communications. ([GovInfo][4]) But the federal suppression provision, 18 U.S.C. § 2515, is textually framed around intercepted “wire or oral communication.” ([Legal Information Institute][5]) On the supplied official explanation, the stipulated private statutory violation alone does not require suppressing all evidence.

**This item teaches:** private search vs government-agent search; violation vs remedy.
**Fills:** computer-search suppression timing; government participation.
**Adjacent to master:** private search doctrine, exclusionary rule, statutory suppression.

### review_truth
