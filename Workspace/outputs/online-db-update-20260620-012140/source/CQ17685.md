## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**Gold Key: Rule 12(h)(3) Section  the court's independent, non-waivable duty.**

Under FRCP 12(h)(3), lack of subject matter jurisdiction is one of the three defenses that can **never** be waived. A court has an independent obligation to notice and act on its own lack of subject matter jurisdiction at any stage Section  even after trial, even on appeal. Neither party silence nor procedural delay creates federal adjudicatory power that does not exist. The court must dismiss.

Here, the federal court has only state-law claims between nondiverse parties and no supplemental-jurisdiction anchor. The court lacks subject matter jurisdiction. Under Rule 12(h)(3), the court must dismiss Section  the one-week-before-trial posture is irrelevant.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A (new) / D (original) Section  "Proceed to trial because delay = waiver"**

*What makes it attractive:* Students know that most defenses can be waived by delay or failure to raise them. This choice weaponizes that general intuition and applies it to SMJ Section  it feels like a natural extension of the waiver rules they have just learned.

*The breaker:* Subject matter jurisdiction is categorically different from personal jurisdiction and venue. Rule 12(h)(3) expressly exempts SMJ from the waiver framework. No amount of delay, silence, or procedural progress can create federal jurisdiction where none exists. The court must dismiss on its own motion.

**Silver Key:** The cut move is to flag the word "waiver" Section  if you see a waiver-based answer for SMJ, it is always wrong. SMJ is one of the three non-waivable defenses under Rule 12(h)(3).

**B (new) / A (original) Section  "Proceed to trial because only a party can raise it"**

*What makes it attractive:* This is the dominant trap. In most of civil procedure, the parties drive the litigation Section  the court does not inject defenses the parties have not raised. Personal jurisdiction, for example, is waived if the defendant does not raise it. This choice exploits that pattern and makes it feel like SMJ works the same way.

*The breaker:* The court has an **independent duty** to examine subject matter jurisdiction. Rule 12(h)(3) does not say "a party may move to dismiss" Section  it says the court "must dismiss" whenever it determines it lacks SMJ. The judge's own obligation is non-delegable. Neither party's silence binds the court.

**Gold Key:** Rule 12(h)(3) places the duty on the **court**, not the parties. When a choice says "only a party can raise it," that is the tell Section  the rule says the opposite.

**D (new) / C (original) Section  "Transfer to state court instead of dismissing"**

*What makes it attractive:* This choice appeals to common sense and fairness. Dismissing a case that could be heard in state court seems wasteful Section  why not just send it where it belongs? The word "transfer" sounds like a reasonable judicial remedy.

*The breaker:* Rule 12(h)(3) mandates **dismissal**, not transfer. While 28 U.S.C. Section  1406(a) allows transfer to cure venue defects, it does not override the Rule 12(h)(3) mandate when the federal court lacks subject matter jurisdiction. The proper course is dismissal; the plaintiff may refile in state court.

**Silver Key:** The cut move is to distinguish **venue** from **subject matter jurisdiction**. Transfer is a venue remedy; dismissal is the SMJ remedy. When the call is about SMJ, "transfer" is a misfit.
