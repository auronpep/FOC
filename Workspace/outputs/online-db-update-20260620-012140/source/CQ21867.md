### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**A is correct.** The charged theory is misdemeanor manslaughter based on the unpermitted puzzle booth. The booth did not produce, set up, or increase the risk of Ruth’s later fall from a defective stepstool at a different location. **Gold Key:** misdemeanor manslaughter still needs actual and proximate causation; a misdemeanor that merely happened earlier is not enough. **Silver Key:** when one answer links the misdemeanor to the death and another only names the misdemeanor, choose the cause-link answer.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B.** This choice sells the cleanest first-step trap: Peter did commit a misdemeanor, so the answer feels like it has found the unlawful act. The breaker is that it stops before the cause link; it proves the label, not the death-causing connection.

**C.** This choice sells a sophisticated-sounding limitation: “violent misdemeanors” feels like a plausible way to narrow an overbroad homicide rule. The breaker is that it answers with a fabricated threshold instead of the fact that decides this case, and the **Gold Key:** causation, not a violent-only rule, defeats liability here.

**D.** This choice sells the broadest student myth: if a misdemeanor happened first and a death happened later, the homicide label attaches. The breaker is the word “any”; the answer turns a cause requirement into pure chronology, and the **Silver Key:** should steer the student back to the cause-link answer.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The transformed item preserves the tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome from the source row: misdemeanor manslaughter requires the unlawful act to actually and proximately cause death, and the misdemeanor label alone is insufficient.  The Model Penal Code’s general causation provision states that conduct causes a result only if it is a but-for antecedent and also satisfies any additional causal requirements imposed by the offense-defining law. ([Criminal Law Web][1]) In *People v. Penny*, the California Supreme Court treated the causation link as essential in an unlawful-act manslaughter analysis and quoted the rule that the death must be the probable consequence naturally flowing from the unlawful act. ([Justia Law][2]) Oklahoma’s criminal jury-instruction commentary similarly limits misdemeanor manslaughter to cases in which the underlying misdemeanor is the direct and proximate cause of the homicide, expressly rejecting a misdemeanor “in no way connected with the death.” ([Justia Law][3])

Applied here, Peter’s permit violation was neither a but-for cause nor a proximate cause of Ruth’s stepstool fall. The correct answer remains “No,” for lack of causation.
