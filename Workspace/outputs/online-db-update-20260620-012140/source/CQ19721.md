### 6. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. The motion should be granted because the complaint’s own facts defeat the claim it chose. The complaint says the technician negligently latched the hatch without realizing Mary was inside. That pleaded fact does not support false imprisonment as an intentional confinement claim.

**Gold Key:** False imprisonment is an intentional-confinement claim. If the complaint pleads only negligent confinement, the complaint itself knocks out the claim.

**Silver Key:** Do not pick by outcome alone. A “grant” answer with the wrong jurisdictional reason is still a trap; the credited answer must match the ground that defeats the complaint.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This answer sells a sensible damages instinct: a night in a float with no physical harm may not sound like a six-figure verdict. The breaker is that the amount-in-controversy test is not an “unlikely award” test, and the motion is won on claim sufficiency, not damages probability. **Gold Key:** The amount alleged controls unless it is legally certain that the claim is below the threshold.

**B.** This answer uses a real amount-in-controversy move: nothing in the facts shows that recovery above $75,000 is impossible. The breaker is responsiveness. The company’s winning motion is not about subject-matter jurisdiction; it is about the complaint pleading negligence while asserting an intentional confinement claim.

**D.** This answer uses a real pleading idea: a complaint must identify the claim and give notice. The breaker is that notice is not enough when the pleaded facts themselves negate the needed claim element. The complaint’s own “negligently latched” fact defeats the false-imprisonment theory.
