Lydia, who was 20 years old, purchased a new, high-powered sports car called the **Galilee GT**, which was marketed with an intended and recognized appeal to youthful drivers, including ads showing college students driving to Christian music festivals. The car was designed with the capability to attain speeds in excess of 100 miles per hour. It was equipped with tires designed and tested only for a maximum safe speed of 85 miles per hour.

Lydia took her new car out for a drive on a straight, smooth country road where the posted speed limit was 55 miles per hour. **Will Lydia prevail?**

## 6. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.**

**Gold Key: Foreseeable misuse / warning defect.** A manufacturer cannot escape a warning-defect strict-liability claim merely by calling the plaintiff’s use “misuse” when the use was foreseeable from the product’s design, marketing, and manual. If the product’s danger could have been reduced by a clearer warning, the warning failure can make the product defective.

**Silver Key:** When the answer set fights between **“misuse”** and **“inadequate warning,”** go back to the stem and ask whether the manufacturer invited or forecasted the dangerous use. Here, the car could exceed 100 mph, was marketed to youthful drivers, and the manual itself mentioned high-speed tires without telling Lydia that the installed tires were not high-speed-capability tires.

Lydia’s speeding may affect damages under pure comparative fault, but it does not defeat liability at the “will she prevail?” stage.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A is incorrect.**
This answer says “misuse,” but it does not answer the decisive question: whether the use was unforeseeable. **Gold Key:** foreseeable misuse is not a complete bar to a warning-defect strict-liability claim. **Silver Key:** the stem’s design/marketing/manual facts point away from a complete “misuse” bar.

**B is incorrect.**
This answer treats “defective” as only a physical design or manufacturing problem. That misses the warning-defect lane. A product can be defective because the warning or instructions are inadequate, even if the object itself performs as designed.

**D is incorrect.**
This answer imports negligence-per-se language and makes it a complete bar. But the call asks whether Lydia will prevail in strict product liability, and the stem gives pure comparative fault. At most, her fault can reduce damages; it does not automatically defeat the claim.

## 8. Black-letter verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule is unchanged from the source item: strict products liability may rest on inadequate warnings where the foreseeable risks of harm could have been reduced by reasonable warnings, and defects in marketing include improper instructions or failures to warn. ([Open Casebook][1]) The original row’s official explanation likewise identifies the failure to warn about tire speed capacity as the defect and treats high-speed use as foreseeable from the car’s design and marketing. 

Comparative-fault treatment is also consistent with the supplied pure-comparative-fault assumption. Daly v. General Motors confirms that comparative principles can reduce strict-products recovery while leaving strict liability intact for injuries caused by a defective product. ([Justia Law][2]) A products-liability authority from Illinois pattern instructions states the same exam-useful distinction: foreseeable misuse is not a defense to strict products liability, while misuse concepts may operate through comparative fault or damages reduction. ([Illinois Courts][3])

## 15. outline_mastery_map

**Placement:** Torts → Strict Liability and Products Liability → Warning Defect.

**This item teaches:**
The plaintiff-conduct defense does not automatically defeat products liability when the dangerous use was foreseeable and the product warning was incomplete.

**Adjacent mastery cells:**

* design defect vs warning defect,
* foreseeable misuse,
* comparative fault,
* negligence per se as fault evidence.

## 16. crossover_intersection_map

* **Products liability × comparative fault:** fault can reduce recovery without defeating the claim.
* **Products liability × negligence per se:** speeding may be fault, but it is not the strict-products answer.
* **Design/marketing × warning:** marketing can make a high-risk use foreseeable.
* **Manual warning × hidden product condition:** vague warning can be inadequate where the installed component has undisclosed limits.

## 17. review_truth

Foreseeable high-risk use does not automatically bar warning-defect strict liability; comparative fault goes to reduction, not automatic defeat.
