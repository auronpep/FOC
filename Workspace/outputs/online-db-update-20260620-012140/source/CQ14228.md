### 1. Final question — revised stem + call

A city ordinance in the City of Bethany requires a **Road-to-Emmaus shuttle operator’s license** to drive a paid shuttle van in the city. The ordinance states that the sole criteria for issuing such a license are driving ability and knowledge of the city’s streets. An applicant is tested for these qualifications with a detailed questionnaire, written and oral examinations, and a practical behind-the-wheel demonstration.

The ordinance does not limit the number of licenses that may be issued. It does, however, allow any citizen to file an objection to issuing a particular license, but only on the ground that an applicant does not possess the required qualifications. City licensing officials are also authorized by the ordinance to determine, in their discretion, whether to hold an evidentiary hearing on an objection before issuing a license.

Peter demands a hearing before the license is issued so that he may prove his claim.

City licensing officials refuse to hold a hearing and issue the license to Mary. Peter petitions for review in an appropriate court, alleging that the Constitution requires city licensing officials to grant his request for a hearing before issuing Mary’s license.

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

D is correct. Peter is asking for a hearing, but the threshold question is whether the city action threatens a protected property interest of his. The ordinance gives a person a licensing path based on driving ability and city-street knowledge. It does not cap licenses. It permits objections only to an applicant’s qualifications. Peter’s objection is not about Mary’s qualifications; it is about competition.

**Gold Key:** Procedural due process begins only if the claimant has a protected liberty or property interest in the specific thing being threatened. A license does not automatically include a protected right to be free from new competitors unless the governing law gives that protection.

**Silver Key:** Do not start with “Was the hearing fair?” Start with “Was there a protected interest that required a hearing at all?”

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A is wrong.** It overclaims. “All persons whose property may be adversely affected” is too broad. Economic disappointment is not enough. The question is narrower: did this ordinance give Peter a protected no-competition interest?

**B is wrong.** It answers the wrong layer. It talks about who may decide whether to hold a hearing. That matters only after Peter shows that he is constitutionally entitled to a hearing.

**C is wrong.** It invents a waiver/estoppel rule. Peter’s prior use of the licensing system does not, by itself, mean he can never challenge government action under that system.

### 8. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The rule and outcome are unchanged from original QID 14228. Procedural due process protects against deprivation of life, liberty, or property without adequate process, but property interests are not created by the Constitution; they are created and defined by independent sources such as state law, ordinances, or rules. Board of Regents v. Roth states that a claimant needs more than an abstract desire or unilateral expectation; the claimant needs a legitimate claim of entitlement. ([Justia Law][1]) Loudermill confirms the same threshold framing: once a protected property interest exists, the Constitution determines what process is due, but the existence and dimensions of the interest come from the underlying law. ([Justia Law][2])

Here, the ordinance gives Peter a license to operate. It does not give him a capped market, a veto over additional qualified applicants, or a hearing right for competition-based objections. In analogous taxi-license litigation, courts have treated the right to operate as distinct from a claimed right to a competition-free marketplace. Checker Cab Operators v. Miami-Dade County, for example, rejected the claim that medallion holders had an entitlement to a competition-free market. ([Justia Law][3])
