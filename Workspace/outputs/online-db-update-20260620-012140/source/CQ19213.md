## 6. Full right-answer explanation — exam register

**C** is the best answer.

**Silver Key:** Lock the exact duty. The question is not “was Esther a paying customer?” It is “did these facts trigger a duty to post security guards against a random third-party attack?”

**Gold Key:** A business is not an insurer against random third-party crime. Customer status matters, but a guard/security duty needs a foreseeability trigger: prior similar incidents, created risk, imminent danger, or comparable notice.

The stem gives six quiet years, an unknown stranger, and no created danger. That makes **C** the only answer tied to the duty the lawsuit actually claims.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** The attraction is that Esther was a paying participant, so the business-customer relationship feels established. The breaker is that paid status answers only a status element; it does not explain why guards were required for this random attack. **Silver Key:** separate “customer status” from “security-guard trigger.” **Gold Key:** customer status alone does not make a business an insurer against unforeseeable stranger crime.

**B.** The attraction is the protective instinct: businesses should keep customers safe. The breaker is the answer’s absolute wording — “every business” and “all crimes” convert reasonable care into crime insurance.

**D.** The attraction is the lawyerly phrase “as a matter of law.” The breaker is that the answer invents an automatic inherent-danger category for all exit breezeways, even though the stem supplies no history or condition making this breezeway predictably dangerous.

## 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

Lawyer-confirmation layer only: Restatement (Second) of Torts § 344 recognizes a business-premises duty to use reasonable care regarding third-party acts, but comment f limits that duty: the possessor is not an insurer and generally must have notice or reason to anticipate such conduct before precautions such as guards are required. Sources quoting § 344 comment f state that the duty may arise from the place or character of the business or from past experience showing likely criminal conduct; they also emphasize that a business owner is not automatically liable for unforeseeable third-party crime. ([American Bar Association][1])

The original issue, rule, and credited outcome are unchanged: no guard-duty on facts showing a random stranger attack, no prior similar danger, no created risk, and no other guard-duty trigger. The source row states the same black-letter reference: no duty to protect from third-party criminal acts absent a special relationship, prior similar danger, created risk, or other facts making harm sufficiently foreseeable.
