### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.** Timothy’s strongest listed argument is that Lydia acted unreasonably by testing the cable rig in an open driveway beside a public sidewalk. **Gold Key:** negligence needs an unreasonable-risk theory; here, the argument supplies a fault basis instead of merely pointing to a product, a cause, or Lydia’s professional title. **Silver Key:** on a “most effective argument” call, do not demand a guaranteed win. ### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells a status shortcut. A professional role can make the fact pattern sound more blameworthy, but the breaker is that the choice gives no unreasonable act, no breach, and no link between the professional standard and Timothy’s injury.

**B.** This choice sells the intuitive move that “she helped cause it, so she loses.” The breaker is that cause alone is only one piece; it does not say the conduct was negligent, intentional, or otherwise within a strict-liability category. **Silver Key:** for a best-argument call, prefer the answer that supplies a liability basis over the answer that supplies only a chain-of-events fact.

**D.** This choice sells the defective-product trap. The product-liability words are real and attractive, but **Gold Key:** strict products liability for a defective product targets commercial sellers or distributors, not a mere user of the product.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule and outcome are unchanged from the source row: the credited answer is the reasonableness/negligence argument, not strict product liability, causation-only language, or professional status. The supplied official explanation says negligence is a failure to act reasonably and identifies the unreasonable-front-yard argument as the only listed argument that could support the walker’s claim. 

Lawyer confirmation: Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical and Emotional Harm § 3 defines negligence as a failure to exercise reasonable care under the circumstances, considering foreseeable likelihood of harm, severity, and burden of precautions. Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability § 1 imposes product-defect liability on one engaged in the business of selling or otherwise distributing defective products. Those authorities support the transformed key: Lydia’s unreasonable use of the rig near the sidewalk can support negligence, while strict products liability does not fit Lydia as a user rather than a product supplier.
