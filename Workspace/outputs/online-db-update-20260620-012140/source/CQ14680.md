## 1. Final question

In a jurisdiction that has abolished the felony-murder rule, but otherwise follows the common law of murder, Peter and Ruth, both armed with loaded hunting rifles, entered a Christian bookstore during a busy Saturday sale to rob the safe. Peter told everyone inside to lie face down and put their hands behind their heads. When several customers hesitated, Ruth, not intending to hit anyone, fired several rounds into the air. One round struck an iron display bracket, ricocheted, and killed a customer in the store. Peter and Ruth were charged with murder of the customer. Which of the following is correct?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

When one armed robber fires a gun into a crowded place and a ricochet kills a bystander in a no-felony-murder jurisdiction, can both robbers be convicted of murder?

## 3. Final answer choices (A–D, post-shuffle)

- **A:** Both Peter and Ruth can be convicted of murder.
- **B:** Ruth can be convicted of murder, but Peter cannot be convicted of either murder or manslaughter.
- **C:** Neither Peter nor Ruth can be guilty of murder, but both can be convicted of manslaughter based upon an unintentional homicide.
- **D:** Ruth can be convicted only of manslaughter, but Peter cannot be convicted of murder or manslaughter.


## 5. Correct answer

**A** is correct.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

A is correct. Ruth's decision to fire loaded rifles into the air in a crowded bookstore shows implied malice: she did not need to intend to hit the customer for murder if her conduct displayed depraved indifference to human life. Peter is not off the hook because he aided and encouraged the armed robbery, and the ricochet killing was a foreseeable result of that armed crime. Gold Key: implied malice can exist without intent to kill, and accomplice liability can make the armed partner liable for the foreseeable killing. Silver Key: separate the shooter's mental state from the partner's derivative liability before comparing murder and manslaughter.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

B sells the dominant trap: it gets Ruth right, then wrongly treats Peter as if he were legally separate from the homicide. The breaker is accomplice liability; Peter intentionally aided the armed robbery, and a ricochet death from gunfire in the store was foreseeable.

C is attractive because it translates "no intent to hit anyone" into "only manslaughter." That misses implied malice. An unintentional homicide can still be murder when the defendant acts with depraved indifference to human life, and Peter's derivative liability follows from the armed robbery.

D pushes the no-intent-to-kill fact too far and adds a no-direct-shot rule for Peter. Ruth can have implied malice without intending to kill, and Peter can be liable even though Ruth fired the fatal shot.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning
