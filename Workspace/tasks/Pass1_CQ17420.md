# Pass-1 Transformation Report: CQ17420

## Final Question
Elias, a former volunteer at a historic downtown mission, is charged in federal court with knowingly possessing a stolen 19th-century pulpit Bible belonging to the mission. At trial, the prosecution offers evidence that when mission directors approached Elias in the parking lot, he quickly shoved the Bible into a bag and covered it with his choir robe. 

At the close of evidence, the judge proposes the following jury instruction: "If you find that Elias intentionally hid the Bible, you may consider that as evidence of a consciousness of guilt. Furthermore, if you find that he hid the Bible, you must find that Elias knew the Bible was stolen."

Elias's counsel objects to the proposed instruction. Which of the following rulings is correct?

## Distilled Core Question
In a criminal case, is a mandatory presumption instruction that requires the jury to find a necessary element of the crime (knowledge) based on a predicate fact (hiding) constitutional?

## Final Choices (Post-Shuffle)
(A) Sustain the objection as to the mandatory "must-find" language, but allow a properly framed permissive inference instruction.
(B) Overrule the objection because the act of hiding is a recognized "proof channel" for establishing the defendant's state of mind.
(C) Overrule the objection because the defendant's purpose in hiding the property is a collateral matter rather than a direct element of the offense.
(D) Sustain the objection in its entirety because evidence of hiding is inadmissible character evidence under the "prior bad acts" rule.

## Correct Answer
**Correct Answer: A** (Original Key: C)

## Right-Answer Explanation
**A is correct.** In criminal cases, mandatory presumptions—instructions that tell the jury they *must* find an element of the crime if a predicate fact is proven—violate the Due Process Clause because they shift the burden of proof to the defendant or relieve the prosecution of its burden to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt (*Sandstrom v. Montana*). However, permissive inferences—instructions that tell the jury they *may* find an element based on a fact—are generally constitutional. Therefore, the judge must strike the "must-find" sentence while the "may consider" (permissive) portion remains valid.

## Wrong-Answer Explanations
- **(B) is incorrect.** While evidence of hiding (flight/concealment) is indeed a valid "proof channel" for showing consciousness of guilt, it does not justify a *mandatory* instruction. The jury must remain free to reject the inference.
- **(C) is incorrect.** Knowledge that the property was stolen is a central element of the charged offense, not a "collateral" issue. A mandatory instruction on a load-bearing element is a constitutional violation.
- **(D) is incorrect.** Evidence of hiding the specific property that is the subject of the charge is not "prior bad acts" character evidence; it is direct circumstantial evidence of the defendant's mental state regarding the charged crime. While the mandatory instruction is improper, the evidence itself is admissible.

## Black-Letter Verification
- **Authority:** *Sandstrom v. Montana*, 442 U.S. 510 (1979); *Francis v. Franklin*, 471 U.S. 307 (1985).
- **Rule:** A jury instruction in a criminal case that creates a mandatory presumption (even a rebuttable one) regarding an element of the offense violates Due Process.
- **Verification:** The instruction "you must find that Elias knew the Bible was stolen" if he hid it creates a mandatory presumption on the element of knowledge. This is unconstitutional.

## Rigor/Difficulty Note
**Rigor: Core.** This tests the fundamental distinction between permissive inferences (allowed) and mandatory presumptions (prohibited) in criminal trials. The difficulty lies in recognizing that the *evidence* is good, but the *instruction* is bad.
