# 1. FINAL QUESTION

Silas and Timothy were roommates sharing a mission house owned by their church. While both were standing at the front door, two police officers arrived and asked to search the premises for a laptop reported stolen from a local library. Silas said, "I have nothing to hide; you are welcome to search our house." Timothy immediately interjected, "No, I do not consent to any search of this home." Despite Timothy's objection, the officers entered the house and discovered the stolen laptop on a coffee table in the shared living room.

Timothy moved to suppress the laptop as evidence, arguing that the warrantless search was unconstitutional.

Does Silas's permission validate the officers' warrantless entry over Timothy's objection?

(A) Yes, because any occupant with common authority over the premises can provide valid consent that binds all other residents.
(B) No, because Timothy was physically present at the threshold and expressly refused to permit the search.
(C) Yes, because the laptop was found in a common area where neither Silas nor Timothy had an expectation of privacy from each other.
(D) No, but only if Timothy could prove that he had exclusive possession of the specific area where the laptop was found.

# 7. RIGHT-ANSWER EXPLANATION

(B) is correct. Under the Fourth Amendment, a warrantless search of a shared dwelling is generally valid if police obtain the voluntary consent of an occupant who possesses common authority over the premises (*United States v. Matlock*). However, in *Georgia v. Randolph*, the Supreme Court established a narrow but clear exception: if a co-occupant is physically present at the threshold and expressly refuses to permit the search, that refusal overrides the consent given by another co-occupant. Here, because Timothy was at the door and explicitly voiced his objection, the officers' entry was unreasonable and the search was invalid as to Timothy. **Gold Key:** A physically present co-occupant's express refusal of consent overrides a co-tenant's permission.

# 8. WRONG-ANSWER EXPLANATIONS

(A) This choice is attractive because it reflects the general rule of "common authority" used when a co-occupant is absent. However, it is incorrect because it fails to account for the *Randolph* exception triggered by the objector's physical presence and express refusal.

(C) This choice is attractive because it correctly notes that roommates have reduced privacy from *one another* in shared spaces. However, it is incorrect as a matter of constitutional law; the lack of a warrant or valid consent makes the *government's* entry unreasonable, regardless of the roommates' internal sharing arrangements.

(D) This choice is attractive because it sounds like a technical property/privacy rule. However, it is incorrect because the *Randolph* rule applies to the shared dwelling as a whole. An objector does not need to prove exclusive control over a specific sub-area to prevent the government from using another's consent to bypass his own refusal at the front door.

# 9. BLACK-LETTER-LAW VERIFICATION

**Tested Rule:** Fourth Amendment Consent — Co-occupant refusal overrides consent when physically present.
**Authority:** *Georgia v. Randolph*, 547 U.S. 103 (2006); *Fernandez v. California*, 571 U.S. 292 (2014).
**Reasoning:** The Supreme Court held that a physically present co-occupant's stated refusal to permit entry prevails over another's consent, rendering a warrantless search unreasonable. The key factual triggers are (1) physical presence at the threshold and (2) express refusal. Both are present in the stem. The outcome (No/Invalid) is consistent with the source item and established precedent.
