### 2. The 17-section student case study

## program_frame
This item tests the specific limitation of the third-party consent exception to the warrant requirement. It focuses on the clash between a consenting occupant and an objecting occupant who are both physically present.

## student_access_test
The student can solve this by recognizing the "Georgia v. Randolph" fact pattern: two people at the door, one says "come in," the other says "stay out." The "no" wins in this specific constitutional context.

## question_data
Silas and Timothy are at the door of their mission house. Silas says police can search; Timothy says no. Police search anyway and find a stolen laptop.

## distilled_core_question
Two co-occupants are both physically present at the door of their shared residence. One consents to a police search, while the other expressly refuses. Does the consent validate a warrantless search?

## call_and_prediction
"Does Silas's permission validate the officers' warrantless entry...?" Predict "No" because Timothy was present and objected.

## trigger_facts
- **"both were standing at the front door":** Physical presence trigger for *Randolph*.
- **"No, I do not consent":** Express refusal trigger.
- **"Silas's permission":** The competing consent.

## governing_c3_lane
Lane: Fourth Amendment (Search). The lane cuts for the specific clash of consent rights at the threshold.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### Choice A
- **C3 Signal:** CLASH.
- **Student Label:** "common authority binds all".
- **Responsive Version:** "Yes, if Timothy had been arrested and removed from the scene before the search."
- **Lawyer Confirmation:** This overclaims the *Matlock* rule and ignores the *Randolph* exception.

### Choice B
- **C3 Signal:** WIN.
- **Student Label:** "refusal overrides consent".
- **Responsive Version:** N/A (Credited).
- **Lawyer Confirmation:** Directly applies the *Georgia v. Randolph* holding to the facts.

### Choice C
- **C3 Signal:** CUT.
- **Student Label:** "privacy != consent".
- **Responsive Version:** "No, because police needed probable cause even with consent." (False statement).
- **Lawyer Confirmation:** Privacy arrangement between residents is not a substitute for a valid warrant exception against the state.

### Choice D
- **C3 Signal:** CUT.
- **Student Label:** "exclusive possession req".
- **Responsive Version:** "No, unless Silas was a landlord and not a roommate." (Irrelevant hierarchy).
- **Lawyer Confirmation:** Proof of exclusive possession is not required to exercise the right to refuse entry at the threshold.

## residual_answer
A present co-occupant's refusal overrides another's consent.

## legal_leak_audit
The rule is narrow. If Timothy had been in the back room and didn't hear the interaction, Silas's consent would be enough (*Matlock*). If Timothy had been arrested and put in the squad car, Silas's consent would be enough (*Fernandez*). But because he was "at the threshold" and spoke up, he wins.

Drift audit: The item remains strictly on the *Randolph* rule.

## final_student_script
Two roommates are at the door. One says yes, one says no. The "no" wins. Silas's consent isn't enough to override Timothy while Timothy is standing right there saying "stay out." Choice A is a trap that uses the general rule for *absent* roommates. B is the right application of the *Randolph* case. Choose B.

**Title:** Fourth Amendment: Co-occupant Consent
**Signal:** Two residents at the door, one says yes, one says no.
**Student Move:** Favor the objector if they are physically present.
**Tiny Rule:** Consent + Silence/Absence = SEARCH. Consent + Present Refusal = NO SEARCH.

## Gold Key(s)
**GK-CRIM-FOURTH-CONSENT-REFUSAL:** In a shared residence, a physically present co-occupant's express refusal to permit entry overrides another occupant's consent.
