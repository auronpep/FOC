### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** A deed recorded *before the grantor has title* is a "wild deed" — it falls outside the grantor's chain of title and does **not** give constructive notice to a subsequent purchaser, even though it is technically on the record.

C is correct. The jurisdiction's recording statute is a notice statute: a subsequent purchaser for value and without notice prevails over a prior unrecorded (or, as here, a recorded-but-wild) conveyance. Luke paid valuable consideration, recorded, and had no actual, constructive, or inquiry notice of Silas's deed. Although Silas recorded his deed, that deed was a **wild deed** — Timothy did not own the farm when he conveyed to Silas, so the deed was not connected to the chain of title originating from Barnabas. A subsequent purchaser searching Luke's chain of title (starting from Barnabas → Timothy → Luke) would never encounter the Timothy-to-Silas deed. Wild deeds do not provide constructive notice. Therefore, Luke is a bona fide purchaser protected by the recording act, and Luke prevails in the quiet-title action.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap):** This choice is attractive because Timothy genuinely did not own the farm when he conveyed to Silas — the grantor lacked title at the time of the deed. A student who spots this fact may leap to "nothing to convey" as the reason Luke wins. But this ignores **estoppel by deed** (also called after-acquired title): when a grantor later acquires the title they purported to convey, that title automatically inures to the benefit of the earlier grantee. So Silas *did* acquire valid title through Timothy's later inheritance. Luke wins not because Silas got nothing, but because Silas's deed was outside Luke's chain of title and Luke had no notice of it. **Gold Key:** Estoppel by deed means the grantor's later acquisition inures to the first grantee — the "nothing to convey" framing is a trap.

**B:** This choice suggests the first deed was somehow delayed in taking effect. But there is no fact in the stem supporting any delay — the deed was executed, delivered, and recorded. Under estoppel by deed, Silas's interest vested automatically when Timothy inherited. No timing rule postpones the first deed's effect. This distractor invents a timing mechanism that does not exist.

**D (original C):** This choice tempts students who conflate possession with priority. While possession can sometimes give inquiry notice (a buyer who sees someone else in possession may be put on notice to investigate), here Luke took possession *after* his purchase and had no knowledge of Silas's claim. Under a notice statute, the priority question turns on notice, not on who was in possession first. Possession alone does not determine the outcome of a quiet-title action.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule — Notice Recording Statute + Wild Deed Doctrine:**

The jurisdiction's statute — "No conveyance or mortgage of real property shall be good against subsequent purchasers for value and without notice unless the same be recorded according to law" — is a **pure notice statute**. Under a notice statute, a subsequent bona fide purchaser (BFP) for value and without notice prevails over a prior conveyance, regardless of whether the prior conveyance was recorded. *See* 11 Thompson on Real Property § 82.02 (David A. Thomas ed., 2d ed., 2002); Powell on Real Property § 82.02[1][d] (1997); Wikipedia, "Recording (real estate)" (accessed 2026) (notice statute protects subsequent BFP who takes without notice of prior conveyance).

**Constructive notice is limited to the chain of title.** Recording provides constructive notice only of instruments that are properly connected to the chain of title. A deed recorded by a grantor who does not yet hold title — a "wild deed" — is not connected to any chain of title and does not provide constructive notice to subsequent purchasers. *See* Matthew Miner, Property II Outline – Recording Act ("Wild Deed — A wild deed is one that is not properly connected to the chain of title because a predecessor failed to record his deed. Courts have uniformly ruled that a wild deed does not provide constructive notice to subsequent purchasers."); South Texas College of Law, Property II Ortiz Outline ("Wild Deed Rule — A wild deed, unconnected to the chain of title, does not give constructive notice to subsequent purchasers."); Mindli Explore, "Wild Deed and Chain of Title Problems" ("The recording system's constructive notice is limited to documents within the chain of title; therefore, a wild deed generally does not provide notice to subsequent bona fide purchasers.").

**Estoppel by deed (after-acquired title):** When a grantor conveys by warranty deed and later acquires the title purportedly conveyed, the after-acquired title passes automatically to the grantee by operation of law. The grantor is estopped from denying the deed's validity. *See* DetailedPedia, "Estoppel by deed" ("The doctrine of estoppel by deed (also known as after-acquired title) ... the grantor of a deed is estopped from denying the truth of the deed."); *BCML Holding LLC v. Wilmington Trust, N.A.*, Fla. 3d DCA (2015) (after-acquired title doctrine held applicable to subsequent purchaser).

**Application:** Timothy did not own the farm when he conveyed to Silas, but he later inherited it from Barnabas. By estoppel by deed, Silas's title vested automatically upon Timothy's inheritance. However, the Timothy-to-Silas deed was a wild deed — recorded before Timothy had title, it was not connected to the chain of title running from Barnabas → Timothy → Luke. Luke, searching the record, would trace title from Barnabas to Timothy to himself and would never encounter the Silas deed. Luke had no actual, constructive, or inquiry notice of Silas's claim. Luke is a subsequent BFP protected by the notice statute and prevails in the quiet-title action.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Barnabas owned a small farm. His son Timothy lived on it. Two years ago, Timothy conveyed the farm by warranty deed to his friend Silas, who recorded. One year ago, Barnabas died and left the farm to Timothy. One month ago, Timothy conveyed the farm by warranty deed to Luke, who recorded and had no knowledge of the Silas deed. Timothy delivered possession to Luke. Timothy then fled. Luke sues Silas to quiet title.

**Recording act:** "No conveyance or mortgage of real property shall be good against subsequent purchasers for value and without notice unless the same be recorded according to law."

**Call:** "If Luke wins, it will be because"

**Choices:**
- A: Timothy had nothing to convey to Silas two years ago.
- B: Timothy's deed to Silas was not to take effect until after Timothy's deed to Luke.
- C: Timothy's deed to Silas was not in Luke's chain of title.
- D: Luke was first in possession.

#### Distilled Core Question

A grantor conveyed land by warranty deed to a grantee who recorded, but the grantor did not yet own the land. The grantor later acquired title by inheritance, then conveyed the same land to a second purchaser who recorded and had no notice of the first deed. Under a notice recording statute, who wins a quiet-title action?

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks for the *reason* Luke wins. The student should look for the answer that identifies why Luke, the subsequent purchaser, prevails over Silas, the prior grantee who recorded. The recording act is a notice statute — so the answer must relate to notice.

5. **Notice recording statute** (visible: quoted in stem)

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "Timothy had nothing to convey to Silas two years ago."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Timothy didn't own the farm when he conveyed to Silas — that's true. But the stem also says Timothy later inherited the farm. When a grantor later acquires the title they tried to convey, the law says the earlier grantee gets the title anyway (estoppel by deed). So "nothing to convey" is too absolute — it ignores this doctrine.
2. **Student label:** The grantor-had-nothing trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Although Timothy lacked title when he conveyed to Silas, his later inheritance passed title to Silas by estoppel; however, Silas's deed was outside Luke's chain of title."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Estoppel by deed (after-acquired title) means the grantor's later acquisition inures to the first grantee. Silas got valid title when Timothy inherited. The "nothing to convey" framing is an overclaim that omits this doctrine. **Gold Key:** A deed recorded before the grantor holds title is a wild deed — it falls outside the grantor's chain of title and does NOT give constructive notice to a subsequent purchaser.

**Choice B: "Timothy's deed to Silas was not to take effect until after Timothy's deed to Luke."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The stem says the deed was executed, delivered, and recorded two years ago. There's no condition, no future date, no contingency. This choice invents a delay that doesn't exist in the facts.
2. **Student label:** The phantom-delay trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Timothy's deed to Silas took effect upon delivery and related back to the date of conveyance when Timothy later acquired title."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** No legal authority supports a rule that a warranty deed's effectiveness is delayed when the grantor later acquires title. The deed was valid upon delivery.

**Choice C: "Timothy's deed to Silas was not in Luke's chain of title."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Silas recorded his deed, but Timothy didn't own the farm yet. If you search the record starting from Barnabas (the original owner), you'd find Barnabas → Timothy → Luke. The Silas deed, recorded before Timothy owned anything, wouldn't show up in that chain. Luke had no way to find it.
2. **Student label:** The wild-deed / chain-of-title answer.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This choice is already true and responsive. It correctly identifies that the recorded deed was outside the chain of title and thus provided no constructive notice to Luke.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Wild deed doctrine: a deed recorded by a grantor who does not yet hold title is not connected to the chain of title and does not provide constructive notice. Under the notice statute, Luke as a subsequent BFP without notice prevails. **Gold Key:** A deed recorded before the grantor holds title is a wild deed — it falls outside the grantor's chain of title and does NOT give constructive notice to a subsequent purchaser.

**Choice D: "Luke was first in possession."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The stem says Timothy delivered possession to Luke after the purchase. But the recording act is a notice statute — the question is about notice, not possession. Possession can sometimes put a buyer on inquiry notice, but here Luke had no knowledge of Silas. The call asks why Luke wins, and possession priority isn't the reason under a notice statute.
2. **Student label:** The possession-priority trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Luke had no notice of Silas's deed, and under the notice recording statute, a subsequent BFP without notice prevails."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Under a notice recording statute, priority turns on notice, not possession. Possession is not the basis for quiet-title priority here.
