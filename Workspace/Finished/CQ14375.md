## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Mary hired Timothy, a scenic designer, under a written agreement to design and install a traveling Nativity play set for Mary’s private Advent festival at a fixed price of $18,400. The agreement attached detailed plans and provided:

> Before or during installation, this agreement may be modified by mutual agreement of the parties as to “extras” or other departures from the attached plans. Such modifications, however, may be authorized only in a writing signed by both parties.

During the work, Timothy added a Bethlehem-star pulley, painted sheep stalls, hand-lettered Scripture banners, and other extras that Mary orally requested for orally agreed prices in addition to the contract price. Timothy installed the extras. Mary later refused to pay anything for the extras, totaling $4,700 at the agreed prices, solely because no written, signed authorization was ever made.

If Timothy sues Mary for the extras, which, if any, of the following will effectively support Mary’s defense?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A written services contract required signed written authorization for extras, but the buyer orally requested extras, agreed to prices, and the contractor performed them. Can the buyer avoid paying solely because the written authorization was missing?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. Failure of an express condition.
B. Mary does not have a valid defense.
C. The parol evidence rule.
D. The preexisting duty rule.

### 5. Correct answer

**B**. Original key: **D**.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

Mary’s best answer is **B**. The clause required signed written authorization for extras, but Mary orally requested the extras, Timothy installed them, and Mary refused payment only after receiving the benefit. **Gold Key:** a no-oral-modification clause is not an automatic shield when the requesting party’s later words and conduct induce the other party to perform the extra work. **Silver Key:** lock the call to Mary’s stated defense: she is relying only on missing signed authorization, not on price, quality, or nonperformance.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Failure of an express condition.**
This is the strongest trap because it points straight at the signed-writing clause. The breaker is reliance/waiver: Mary requested the extras, Timothy performed them, and the missing writing does not effectively support Mary’s defense on these facts.

**C. The parol evidence rule.**
This is attractive because the case starts with a written agreement, and “parol evidence” feels like the rule that protects writings. The breaker is timing: the extra-work agreement happened later, during performance, so the parol-evidence frame is aimed at the wrong time period.

**D. The preexisting duty rule.**
This sounds like a contract-modification rule, so it looks nearby. The breaker is the call: Mary’s sole defense is missing signed authorization, and the extras are departures from the attached plans rather than Timothy’s already-owed base work.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 14375, a Contracts item involving a written construction agreement, orally requested extras, no signed authorization, and official key D, “The owner does not have a valid defense.”  The original stem and official explanations state that the contractor performed orally requested extras and that the owner refused payment solely because no signed authorization existed. 

For lawyer confirmation: the parol evidence rule addresses prior or contemporaneous agreements, not later modifications; Cornell’s Wex description states that the rule bars prior or contemporaneous extrinsic evidence that contradicts an integrated writing. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 213 likewise states the rule in terms of an integrated agreement discharging prior agreements. ([Open Casebook][2]) For the relied-on modification point, Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 89(c) states that a promise modifying an executory contract is binding to the extent justice requires enforcement because of a material change of position in reliance. ([Open Casebook][3]) Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 150 also recognizes enforcement where reinstating the original terms would be unjust because of a material change of position in reliance on an oral modification. ([Law Insider][4]) This confirms the transformed outcome: Mary has no effective defense based solely on the missing signed authorization.

This item teaches: no-oral-modification defense after orally requested extras and reliance.
Fills: modification timing, signed-writing clause, waiver/estoppel anchor.
Adjacent to master: parol evidence rule, preexisting duty, express conditions.

## crossover_intersection_map

Contracts × Evidence: parol evidence sounds evidentiary but functions as substantive contract scope.
Contracts × Remedies: reliance/performance prevents unfair use of the writing clause.
Contracts × Conditions: condition language can be waived or neutralized by later conduct.

## review_truth

A party who orally requests and receives extra work cannot usually use the no-oral-modification clause as a defense after the other party materially relies by performing the requested extras.
