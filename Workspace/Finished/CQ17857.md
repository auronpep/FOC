### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Esther is **not** likely to prevail. The competition privilege protects competitors who seek prospective business through lawful means, including truthful price competition. Esther had no signed renewal contract, so her "expectancy" was at most an unwritten course-of-dealing, not a contractual right. Paul's means were a truthful lower quote — that is the textbook lawful competition. **Gold Key:** competition for prospective business through truthful pricing is privileged; liability for interference with prospective economic advantage requires improper means, not lawful price competition. **Silver Key:** when a stem pairs "no signed renewal" with "truthful lower price," the privilege line is the deciding axis — cut the "tortious because lost a deal" answers first.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. Yes, because any expected annual commission is tortious interference.**

This is the dominant trap and the classic student overclaim. The student sees that Esther expected the work and that Paul took it, and concludes that interference must have happened. The breaker is that interference with **prospective** advantage requires **improper means** (or interference with an existing contract); lawful price competition is privileged. "Any expected commission" overclaims the rule.

**C. Yes, because the competition privilege applies only to existing contracts.**

This is the reversed-rule trap. The student half-remembers that there is a competition privilege and inverts its scope. The privilege applies to competition for **prospective** business, including the renewal-type relationship Esther had. The privilege is not limited to existing contracts; it is in many ways most important in the prospective-deal context.

**D. No, because business expectancies are never protected.**

This is the overclaim-in-the-other-direction trap. The student tries to explain why Esther loses by saying expectancies are "never" protected. The breaker is that "never" is overbroad: business expectancies **can** be protected when interference is by improper means or with an existing contract. The right framing is that the privilege defeats the claim on these facts, not that expectancies are categorically unprotected.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17857, a Torts item involving a software subscription renewal, a competing vendor's lower truthful offer, and a suit for interference with prospective economic advantage. The official key is C: no recovery where the means were lawful price competition. The substantive rule sits in the Restatement (Second) of Torts § 768 (competition privilege for persuasion, advertising, and similar conduct) and the Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Economic Harm § 4 (privilege to compete for prospective economic advantage through proper means). The MBE-style black-letter statement is that interference with prospective economic advantage requires (a) the existence of a valid business expectancy, (b) the defendant's knowledge of that expectancy, (c) intentional and improper interference causing the loss, and (d) resulting damages. The competition privilege applies where the competitor's means are lawful — truthful pricing, persuasion, and ordinary advertising. The Restatement (Third) clarifies that "proper means" includes truthful comparative claims and ordinary price competition, and the privilege is broader for prospective advantage than for existing-contract interference because society generally favors robust competition for not-yet-vested business.

The "no signed renewal" fact is load-bearing: without a written renewal, Esther's interest is an expectancy, not a contract right. That removes the "interference with existing contract" theory entirely and forces the analysis into the prospective-advantage lane, where the competition privilege does most of the work. The transformed outcome is identical: Esther loses against Paul because the only means alleged (a truthful lower quote) is privileged competition, not improper interference.
