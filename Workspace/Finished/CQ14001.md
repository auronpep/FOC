## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question
At a citywide Christmas outreach, several volunteers were hurt when a rented lighting truss fell from a private production company's stage. The volunteers sued the company in federal court for negligent setup. The company moved to dismiss, arguing that deciding the suit would require a court to second-guess municipal crowd-control policy and therefore presented a political question that federal courts could not hear. The district court denied the motion. The company immediately appealed and argued that the court of appeals had jurisdiction under the collateral order doctrine to decide whether the political-question objection barred the case. Is the court of appeals likely to have jurisdiction under the collateral order doctrine?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
A defendant loses a motion to dismiss on political-question grounds and immediately appeals under the collateral order doctrine; is there jurisdiction when the ruling can be reviewed after final judgment?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. The collateral order doctrine is narrow, and the issue must be effectively unreviewable after final judgment. Gold Key: collateral-order review requires all three gates: conclusive ruling, important issue separate from the merits, and effective unreviewability after final judgment. The company can challenge the denial of its political-question motion after final judgment, so the court of appeals likely lacks immediate jurisdiction. Silver Key: when the answers name separate collateral-order elements, do not treat one satisfied element as the whole test.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is the dominant trap because it sells a real part of the doctrine. A political-question objection may be separate from whether the company negligently set up the stage, but separateness is only one gate. The breaker is the missing effective-unreviewability requirement.

C lands on the no-jurisdiction side but uses the wrong procedural reason. The 30-day rule is a notice deadline after judgment, not a command that a party must wait 30 days before appealing.

D is attractive because the district court did make a conclusive ruling on the motion to dismiss. The breaker is that conclusiveness alone does not create collateral-order jurisdiction. Gold Key: all three Cohen gates must be satisfied.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: the denial of a motion to dismiss on political-question grounds is not immediately appealable under the collateral order doctrine when the issue can be reviewed after final judgment. Under 28 U.S.C. 1291, federal courts of appeals generally review final decisions of district courts. Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp. recognized a small collateral-order category, and Mohawk Industries, Inc. v. Carpenter states the modern three-gate formulation: the order must conclusively determine the disputed issue, resolve an important issue separate from the merits, and be effectively unreviewable after final judgment. Mohawk also emphasizes that postjudgment review usually defeats the third gate and that the doctrine remains narrow. Sources checked: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/1291 and https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/08-678.ZO.html.
