## 1. Final question

A youth group from Grace Community Church is on a weekend retreat at Cedar Hollow State Park. While the group is hiking a marked trail, a state park ranger stops them and claims they lack a required trail permit. When the youth pastor, **Stephen**, tries to calmly explain the group's reservation, the ranger shoves him to the ground, fracturing his wrist. Stephen sues the ranger in federal court under a federal civil-rights statute, alleging excessive force. The state where the park is located has a physician-patient privilege statute. Stephen moves to exclude Dr. Lois's testimony by invoking that state statute. Which ruling is most proper on Stephen's privilege objection?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**A is correct.** The deciding move is to apply FRE 501's source-law gate before doing anything else. The claim is filed in federal court under a federal civil-rights statute, so it is a federal-question case. FRE 501 says federal common law — interpreted "in the light of reason and experience" — governs claims of privilege in federal-question cases; state privilege law supplies the rule of decision only for a claim or defense for which state law supplies the rule of decision. None of that describes Stephen's §1983 excessive-force claim. A state physician-patient statute therefore does not control the privilege question in this case.

Once the source-law gate has routed to federal common law, the second move is the privilege recognition step. Federal common law recognizes a psychotherapist-patient privilege (*Jaffee v. Redmond*, 518 U.S. 1 (1996)) but does **not** recognize a general physician-patient privilege for ordinary medical treatment. Theurgently, that is consistent with a long line of federal authority declining to recognize such a privilege (see *Whalen v. Roe*, 429 U.S. 589, 602 n.28 (1977)). So even though Stephen made statements to Dr. Lois in the course of treatment, those statements are not shielded by a general federal physician-patient privilege. The objection is overruled, and Dr. Lois may testify.

**Gold Key (woven in):** *FRE 501 is a source-law gate. In a federal-question claim, federal privilege law governs. Federal common law recognizes the psychotherapist-patient privilege (Jaffee v. Redmond, 1996) but NOT a general physician-patient privilege for ordinary treatment.*

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

### B (re-skinned original A — dominant trap)

B's cognitive move is to point the source-law test at the geographic location of the treatment and stop there: treatment happened in the state, so the state's privilege law should control. The breaker is that location is not the source-law test. FRE 501 routes the privilege question to whichever body of substantive law supplies the rule of decision for the claim. For a federal civil-rights claim, that is federal law, not the law of the forum state. The forum state happens to be where the treatment occurred, and the privilege statute happens to be a state statute; both facts are red herrings once the source-law gate is applied. (Gold Key: the source-law gate, not the forum, decides.)

### C (re-skinned original B)

C sounds lawyerly because it names federal law and an "incorporation" idea. The cognitive move is to assume that because FRE 501 routes to federal common law for federal-question claims, federal law then "imports" every state medical privilege as a matter of course. The breaker is that the move is fabricated: FRE 501 does not incorporate state privilege statutes wholesale; it directs federal courts to develop privilege law under the "common law as interpreted by the United States courts in the light of reason and experience." There is no general federal physician-patient privilege to import, and no rule that state medical privileges travel with it. (Gold Key: federal common law governs; no general federal physician-patient privilege exists for ordinary treatment.)

### D (re-skinned original C — the "right result, wrong reason" trap)

D reaches the correct result — overrule — but routes it through the wrong doctrinal door. D's reasoning is that "state law governs, but the privilege is only a rule of professional confidentiality." The breaker is that state law does **not** govern the privilege question in a federal-question claim; the source-law gate routes to federal common law. The professional-ethics framing is a plausible-sounding policy appeal, but it is a different rule from the one that actually controls. A student who picks D has reached the right outcome for the wrong reason and would also reach the wrong outcome if the federal claim were absent (where state privilege law would govern a state-law claim) or if the asserted privilege were the psychotherapist-patient privilege (which federal common law does recognize under *Jaffee*). (Silver Key: when two answers reach the same result, the one that uses the correct doctrinal route is the better answer.)

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**Governing rule.** Federal Rule of Evidence 501, titled "Privilege in General," provides:

> "The common law — as interpreted by United States courts in the light of reason and experience — governs a claim of privilege unless any of the following provides otherwise: the United States Constitution; a federal statute; or rules prescribed by the Supreme Court. But in a civil case, state law governs privilege regarding a claim or defense for which state law supplies the rule of decision."

For a federal-question civil-rights claim such as a §1983 excessive-force action, federal law supplies the rule of decision, so the second sentence's state-law branch is not triggered; the first sentence directs the court to federal common law of privilege.

**Authority floor (real, not fabricated).**

- **FRE 501** (28 U.S.C. app., Pub. L. 93–595, §1, Jan. 2, 1975, 88 Stat. 1933; Apr. 26, 2011, eff. Dec. 1, 2011) — the source-law gate itself.
- ***Jaffee v. Redmond***, 518 U.S. 1 (1996) — Supreme Court held, under FRE 501, that federal common law recognizes a psychotherapist-patient privilege. The Court emphasized the Rule "did not freeze the law governing privileges at a particular point in our history, but rather directed the courts to 'continue the evolutionary development of testimonial privileges.'" 518 U.S. at 8 (quoting *Trammel v. United States*, 445 U.S. 40, 47 (1980)). The Court stopped at the psychotherapy boundary; it did not recognize a general physician-patient privilege.
- ***Whalen v. Roe***, 429 U.S. 589, 602 n.28 (1977) — footnote 28 notes that "the physician-patient privilege does not exist in the federal common law" in the relevant sense. (Sometimes cited for the broader proposition that no general federal physician-patient privilege exists. The proposition is well accepted across the circuits, even though the scope of the footnote has been debated.)
- ***Trammel v. United States***, 445 U.S. 40, 47 (1980) — the "reason and experience" standard, which *Jaffee* later applied.
- **House Report No. 93-650** (1974) — the legislative history confirming that FRE 501 leaves privilege law to the federal courts, with state law supplying the rule of decision only for state-law claims/defenses.

**Affirmative right-answer reasoning.** For Stephen's §1983 excessive-force claim, federal substantive law supplies the rule of decision. FRE 501 therefore directs the court to federal common law of privilege. Federal common law recognizes a psychotherapist-patient privilege (*Jaffee*) but does not recognize a general physician-patient privilege for ordinary medical treatment. The state physician-patient privilege statute has no operative effect in this federal-question case. Dr. Lois's testimony is admissible. (Counsel should still consider other evidentiary limits, e.g., relevance under FRE 401–402 and unfair-prejudice under FRE 403, but those are not the gating questions on these facts.)
