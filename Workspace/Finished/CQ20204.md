### 1. Final question
A small Christian school hires a school bookkeeper under a written handbook stating that all staff serve at will and may be dismissed without cause. After a year of good evaluations, the headmaster decides the bookkeeper is not a good fit for the school's Christian mission and fires her without a hearing. ### 2. Distilled Core Question
Does a state-action school employer with a written at-will handbook owe procedural due process when it fires a bookkeeper without a hearing?

### 6. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. Procedural due process under the Fourteenth Amendment protects only a state-created property interest in continued employment. Bd. of Regents v. Roth, 408 U.S. 564, 577 (1972). Such an interest requires a legitimate claim of entitlement created by state law, contract, or rules that limit the government's power to dismiss, not merely a subjective expectation. Perry v. Sindermann, 408 U.S. 593, 601-603 (1972). Here, the written at-will handbook expressly preserves the right to dismiss without cause, so no legitimate entitlement exists and no pretermination hearing was due. Cleveland Bd. of Educ. v. Loudermill, 470 U.S. 532, 538 (1985) (procedural protections attach only where a property interest exists).

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations
B is wrong because hiring at a state-action school does not, by itself, create a property interest; the controlling question is whether rules limit removal, and the handbook here expressly preserves dismissal without cause. Roth, 408 U.S. at 577.

A is wrong because a paycheck and the loss of reputation are not automatically a liberty deprivation; reputation-based liberty claims require a stigma plus a tangible alteration of legal status such as loss of a right or opportunity, neither of which the facts show. Paul v. Davis, 424 U.S. 693, 708-712 (1976); Roth, 408 U.S. at 573-575.

D is wrong because it asks the court to second-guess the wisdom of the firing. The threshold issue is whether a property interest exists, not whether the firing was sound policy. Roth, 408 U.S. at 579 n.2 ("[T]he Constitution does not require 'judicial second-guessing' of administrative decisions.").
