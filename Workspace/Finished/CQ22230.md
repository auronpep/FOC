## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**C is correct.** A witness's credibility may be impeached extrinsically — by evidence that does not come from the witness's own mouth — by evidence tending to show the witness was incapable of perceiving accurately. The witness testified he identified the defendant by the light of the full moon. Expert testimony that there was no moon that night directly tends to show the witness could not have seen what he claimed to see. This goes to the witness's capacity to perceive, which is always a material issue — never collateral. The expert's testimony is admissible to show the witness's testimony is not worthy of belief.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (Yes — res gestae):** This choice is attractive because "res gestae" sounds like it could apply to anything connected to the event. The breaker: the res gestae doctrine (now largely replaced by the excited utterance concept under FRE 803(2)) applies to statements made under the stress of a startling event. The expert's testimony is an opinion offered at trial, not a statement made under stress. Res gestae has no application here. Even if the doctrine applied, it would be a hearsay exception, not an impeachment doctrine — the question is about impeaching the witness's capacity to perceive.

**B (No — extrinsic evidence of a collateral matter):** This is the dominant trap. The attraction is real: the general rule is that extrinsic evidence is inadmissible to prove a collateral matter. Students learn this rule and overapply it. The breaker: a witness's capacity to perceive or remember is **never** regarded as collateral. It is always material because it goes directly to whether the witness's testimony is credible. The extrinsic-evidence bar for collateral matters does not apply when the evidence tends to show the witness was incapable of perceiving the events testified to.

**D (No — no opportunity to explain):** This choice is attractive because it invokes a fairness intuition — the witness should get a chance to respond. The breaker: except for evidence of prior inconsistent statements (which must be disclosed to the witness under FRE 613), the Federal Rules do not require that a witness be given an opportunity to explain before extrinsic evidence is introduced to impeach. The defendant's attorney was not required to recall the witness before offering the expert's testimony.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**FRE 607 — Who May Impeach:** "Any party, including the party that called the witness, may attack the witness's credibility."

**FRE 613 — Witness's Prior Statement:** Except for prior inconsistent statements (which require an opportunity to explain), the FRE do not impose a general confrontation requirement for extrinsic impeachment evidence.

**Extrinsic Evidence and the Collateral-Matter Bar:** The general rule is that extrinsic evidence is inadmissible to prove a collateral matter. However, a witness's capacity to perceive, remember, or communicate is **never** considered collateral because it goes directly to the reliability of the witness's testimony. Evidence tending to show the witness was incapable of perceiving accurately — such as evidence that no moonlight existed when the witness claims to have seen by moonlight — is admissible as extrinsic impeachment. See 2 McCormick on Evidence § 37 (8th ed.); FRE 607.

**Res Gestae:** The common-law "res gestae" doctrine — statements made during or immediately after a startling event while under stress — has been largely superseded by the excited utterance exception in FRE 803(2). It does not apply to expert opinion testimony offered at trial.
