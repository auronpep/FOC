# CQ14799 — Pass 1: Divergent Christian Transform Report

## 1. Final Question

In a civil trial for fraud arising from the sale of a wooden fishing boat, the defendant (Paul) claimed that he had not been the one to sell the boat to the plaintiff. The plaintiff (Lydia) called Stephen, a longtime friend, to testify about Paul's involvement in misrepresenting the boat's hull condition at the time of sale. To Lydia's surprise, Stephen testified on the stand that Paul had not been involved and denied ever having made any statement to the contrary. Lydia has now called Mary to testify that Stephen told her, during a church picnic, that Paul had personally misrepresented the boat's hull condition. Is Mary's testimony admissible?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A party's own witness surprises that party by flatly denying the case. A second witness is offered to prove the first witness's earlier out-of-court statement (not made under oath) that the first witness had in fact said the opposite. Is the second witness's testimony admissible — and if so, only to impeach, or also for the truth?

- **D.** No, because it is hearsay not within any exception.

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**A is correct.** A prior inconsistent statement offered only to impeach the in-court witness's credibility is **not hearsay** — it is not offered for the truth of the matter asserted, only to show that the witness has told two different stories. Under **FRE 607**, "the credibility of a witness may be attacked by any party, including the party that called the witness," so Lydia is allowed to use the prior statement to impeach Stephen even though Stephen is her own witness. Under **FRE 613(b)**, extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement is admissible once the witness has been given an opportunity to explain or deny it (satisfied here because Stephen already took the stand).

The statement, however, is **not admissible for the truth** (i.e., not admissible to prove Paul was actually involved) unless it qualifies under **FRE 801(d)(1)(A)**. That exemption requires the prior statement to have been given "under oath subject to the penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, or other proceeding, or in a deposition." Stephen's comment to Mary at a church picnic was not under oath and was not made at any formal proceeding. So the statement is admissible **only to impeach**, not substantively.

> **Silver Key (SK-EVID-IMPEACHSUB-01):** When a "Yes" answer adds "and to prove the matter asserted," check the oath. If the prior inconsistent statement was not given under oath at a formal proceeding or deposition, the answer is impeachment only.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### C is incorrect — "No, because a party cannot impeach the party's own witness."

What makes it attractive: this is the old **common-law voucher rule** that students sometimes carry over from bar prep outlines. It is intuitive that you should not be able to "vouch" for a witness you yourself called. The trap pulls in the small minority of test-takers who have not internalized the modern rule. **The breaker:** **FRE 607** expressly abolishes the voucher rule: "The credibility of a witness may be attacked by any party, including the party that called the witness." (Picked by 1% in the source row — the weakest distractor, but kept because the misconception is real and the rule is exam-tested.)

### D is incorrect — "No, because it is hearsay not within any exception."

What makes it attractive: the out-of-court statement *would* be hearsay if offered for the truth, and students conditioned to scan "out-of-court statement → hearsay rule → exception" sometimes skip the dispositive **purpose-of-offer** question. The cognitive move this choice sells is "if it's an out-of-court statement, hearsay must apply; no exception fits; therefore inadmissible." **The breaker:** hearsay is defined as an out-of-court statement **offered for the truth of the matter asserted.** When a statement is offered only to impeach credibility, the hearsay rule is not in play at all. (Picked by 8% in the source row.)

### B is incorrect — "Yes, to impeach Stephen and to prove Paul's involvement." (Dominant trap.)

What makes it attractive: this is the **most dangerous wrong answer** on the page. The first half is correct (impeachment is allowed), so students under time pressure extend the same logic to the substantive use. The cognitive move this choice sells is "the prior statement shows Paul was involved, so the jury should be able to use it for that too." **The breaker:** a prior inconsistent statement is admissible as **substantive** proof only when it qualifies under **FRE 801(d)(1)(A)**, which requires the statement to have been given **under oath at a trial, hearing, or other proceeding, or in a deposition.** A casual remark at a church picnic fails that threshold. The substantive half of the answer collapses.

> **Gold Key (GK-EVID-OATHTHRESHOLD-01):** A prior inconsistent statement is admissible as substantive evidence over a hearsay objection only if it was given under oath at a formal proceeding or deposition. Without the oath, it is impeachment-only.

> **Silver Key (SK-EVID-IMPEACHSUB-01):** When a "Yes" answer says "impeach AND prove," check the oath. No oath → impeachment only.

## 8. Black-Letter Verification & Legal Reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Governing rule.** A prior inconsistent statement is admissible to impeach the credibility of a witness who has just testified. The impeachment use is **not** an offer for the truth and therefore is **not hearsay** under FRE 801(c). Any party may impeach any witness, including a witness the party called (FRE 607). Extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement is admissible once the witness has had an opportunity to explain or deny it (FRE 613(b)).

A prior inconsistent statement is admissible **as substantive evidence** of the matter asserted only if it falls within the hearsay exemption in **FRE 801(d)(1)(A)**: the declarant testifies and is subject to cross-examination, and the prior statement was "given under oath subject to the penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, or other proceeding, or in a deposition." The oath-at-a-formal-proceeding requirement is the dividing line between impeachment-only and impeachment-plus-substantive.

**Application to the variant.** Mary will testify that Stephen, during a church picnic conversation, said Paul had misrepresented the boat's hull condition. (1) The statement is admissible to impeach Stephen, who has just testified on the stand that Paul was not involved and that he never said otherwise. FRE 607 and FRE 613(b) both permit this. (2) The statement is **not** admissible to prove that Paul was actually involved, because the picnic statement was not under oath and was not made at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition. FRE 801(d)(1)(A) is not satisfied.

**Real authority.** Federal Rule of Evidence 607 (attack by any party); Federal Rule of Evidence 613(b) (extrinsic evidence of prior inconsistent statement); Federal Rule of Evidence 801(d)(1)(A) (prior inconsistent statement as non-hearsay, oath-at-formal-proceeding condition). Sources: Cornell LII Rule 801 text and Advisory Committee notes (https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_801) and the Judicial Conference Committee Notes, both confirming the oath requirement has been the controlling federal formulation since the 1975 adoption of the Federal Rules of Evidence.

**Doctrinal-currency flag (for attorney review).** The Standing Committee on Rules of Practice and Procedure has approved an amendment to **FRE 801(d)(1)(A)** that **eliminates the oath-at-a-formal-proceeding requirement**; the amendment is scheduled to take effect **December 1, 2026** (see Vanderbilt Law / Jeff Bellin summary at https://my.vanderbilt.edu/jeffrey-bellin-publications/proposed-rule-801d1a-effective-december-1-2026). **As of the date of this case (June 16, 2026), the amendment is not yet in force**, and the current federal rule (oath required for substantive use) governs. The official key C of the source row is therefore correct under the rule currently in effect. After December 1, 2026, the same fact pattern would yield choice D (admissible to impeach **and** to prove the matter asserted). Outcome under the current rule: **unchanged.** `attorney_review: true` — note for legal team to re-check on/after December 1, 2026.

#### distilled_core_question

A party's own witness surprises that party by flatly denying the case. A second witness is offered to prove the first witness's earlier out-of-court statement (not made under oath) that the first witness had in fact said the opposite. Is the second witness's testimony admissible — and if so, only to impeach, or also for the truth?

#### call_and_prediction

Call: "Is Mary's testimony admissible?" Two `Yes` answers and two `No` answers on the page. The dispute between the two `Yes` answers is **scope of use**: impeachment only vs. impeachment + substantive proof. The dispute between the two `No` answers is **which old rule to invoke**: voucher rule (C) or hearsay-without-exception (D). Prediction: **Yes, impeachment only** (A).

#### trigger_facts

1. **Lydia is the party who called Stephen.** → FRE 607 is in play; the voucher rule is not a bar. *(structural)*
2. **Stephen just testified on the stand that Paul was not involved and that he never said otherwise.** → Surprise adverse in-court testimony; the predicate for a prior inconsistent statement is satisfied. *(structural)*
3. **Mary will testify that Stephen said the opposite at a church picnic.** → Extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement, admissible under FRE 613(b) on these facts. *(structural)*
4. **The picnic statement was not under oath and was not made at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition.** → FRE 801(d)(1)(A)'s substantive-use threshold fails. *(structural via stem; the threshold itself is the Gold Key)*
5. **Civil fraud trial — party-vs-party posture.** → No constitutional fact; no standing, jurisdictional, or statutory-line issue. *(structural)*

**A. Yes, but only to impeach Stephen. (KEY)**

**B. Yes, to impeach Stephen and to prove Paul's involvement in the fraud. (DOMINANT TRAP)**
