## 1. Final Question Stem + Call

A state uses the Model Penal Code substantial capacity test for insanity and requires the prosecution to prove sanity, when it is placed in issue, beyond a reasonable doubt. Barnabas, a worship leader at a local congregation, is on trial for aggravated assault stemming from an altercation during a Sunday morning service. He pleads insanity. The prosecution calls a forensic psychiatrist who examined Barnabas and opines that Barnabas had the substantial capacity to appreciate the wrongfulness of his conduct. Barnabas's attorney offers no expert psychiatric testimony; instead, he presents several congregation members who testify that, in their view, Barnabas lacked the capacity to understand what he was doing at the time of the incident. At the close of evidence, each side moves for a directed verdict on the issue of sanity.

**Which of the following best describes the judge's options?**

In an MPC state where the prosecution must prove sanity beyond a reasonable doubt, after both sides rest (prosecution presented an expert saying sane; defense presented lay witnesses saying insane), can the judge grant a directed verdict for either party — and on what standard?

## 7. Right-Answer Explanation (Choice C)

**Gold Key — GK-CRIMINAL-SANITY-BURDEN-01:** When the state places the burden of proving sanity on the prosecution beyond a reasonable doubt, the directed verdict inquiry asks only whether the prosecution's evidence was sufficient for a reasonable jury to find sanity — not whether the defendant's witnesses were believable, and not whether the judge personally credits the prosecution's expert.

**Silver Key — SK-CRIMINAL-BURDEN-FOCUS-01:** When all four choices describe different directed verdict standards, anchor on who bears the burden, then ask which choice correctly measures the burden-holder — any choice that measures the other side's witnesses or asks whether the judge is personally convinced is tracking the wrong party or the wrong standard.

Choice C states the correct standard exactly. This state requires the prosecution to prove Barnabas's sanity beyond a reasonable doubt. A directed verdict is available when the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the non-moving party, is insufficient for a reasonable jury to find the requisite fact. Because the prosecution holds the burden on sanity, the defense can argue that the prosecution's case was too thin to sustain a rational jury finding. The judge is not personally resolving the factual dispute; she is asking whether the prosecution's evidence, taken at its best, clears the rational-jury bar. If it does not, a directed verdict for the defense is appropriate. Choice C captures this standard precisely.

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A** (originally Orig B — `backwards`, EAR_FALSITY)

The choice invites the judge to weigh the credibility of the *defense's* congregation witnesses. That move feels intuitive: if the defense witnesses are unbelievable, it seems like the defendant loses. But this exactly reverses the burden structure. The question is not whether Barnabas proved insanity; it is whether the *prosecution* proved sanity. The prosecution's burden cannot be satisfied or defeated by measuring the persuasiveness of the opposing witnesses. Even if every congregation member testified unconvincingly, the judge's only question is whether the prosecution's own evidence was sufficient for a reasonable jury to find sanity beyond a reasonable doubt.

**Gold Key — GK-CRIMINAL-SANITY-BURDEN-01** kills this trap: directed verdict analysis measures the burden-holder's evidence, not the other side's witness credibility. **Silver Key — SK-CRIMINAL-BURDEN-FOCUS-01** surfaces the tell: a choice that pivots to the defense witnesses' believability is tracking the wrong party.

---

**Choice B** (originally Orig D — `flat_misstatement`, EAR_FALSITY) — DOMINANT TRAP

The choice reads naturally: the prosecution had an expert, the judge found the expert persuasive, so why not direct a verdict for the prosecution? The pull is real — the psychiatrist's testimony is the prosecution's strongest evidence, and "personally persuaded" sounds like the judge is doing exactly what judges do. But this is a flat misstatement of directed verdict law.

**Gold Key — GK-CRIMINAL-DV-CRIM-01:** In a criminal trial, a judge can never direct a verdict for the prosecution on a factual element the jury must decide — even if the judge personally believes the prosecution proved it. Directed verdicts on the merits in criminal cases run only in one direction: they remove the case from the jury when the prosecution's case is too thin, never when it is too strong.

The Sixth Amendment jury trial right guarantees that the jury, not the judge, resolves disputed factual elements in a criminal case. Once the prosecution has produced enough evidence for a rational jury to find the element, the element goes to the jury — full stop. The judge's personal conviction is constitutionally irrelevant to a directed verdict motion by the prosecution.

---

**Choice D** (originally Orig C — `fabricated_rule`, EAR_OVERCLAIM)

The choice invents a legal distinction: the defense cannot obtain a directed verdict when the prosecution used an expert and the defense only used lay witnesses. No such rule exists. Directed verdict analysis turns on whether the burden-holder's evidence was sufficient for a reasonable jury — it does not turn on the comparative credential level of the witnesses. Lay witnesses are competent to testify about sanity observations under the MPC framework. If the prosecution's evidence, however credentialed, was insufficient for a rational jury to find sanity, the judge may direct a verdict for the defense regardless of who testified for the other side.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Tested Rule:** MPC § 4.01 establishes the substantial capacity test for insanity. When a state adopts this test AND places the burden of proving sanity on the prosecution beyond a reasonable doubt, the directed verdict inquiry for defense is governed by the sufficiency-of-evidence standard: the judge asks whether, taking the prosecution's evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, a rational trier of fact could find the essential fact (sanity) beyond a reasonable doubt.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer):**
- Model Penal Code § 4.01 (ALI) — substantial capacity test
- Mullaney v. Wilbur, 421 U.S. 684 (1975) — due process limits on placing insanity burden on defendant
- Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197 (1977) — states may place burden of affirmative defenses on defendant, but when state places burden on prosecution, due process requires the prosecution to carry it
- Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307 (1979) — the federal sufficiency standard: whether any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements; also frames why a court cannot direct verdict for prosecution in a criminal trial
- U.S. Const. amend. VI — jury trial right prohibits directed verdict for prosecution on a contested factual element

**Credited outcome unchanged:** Directed verdict for defense permissible when prosecution's evidence insufficient; directed verdict for prosecution on a jury-fact element impermissible.

**Key disagreement:** None. The original official key (original A, now C) is supported by the governing rule and authority. No NEEDS_HUMAN flag warranted.

#### question_data

**Stem:** A state uses the Model Penal Code substantial capacity test for insanity and requires the prosecution to prove sanity, when it is placed in issue, beyond a reasonable doubt. Barnabas, a worship leader at a local congregation, is on trial for aggravated assault stemming from an altercation during a Sunday morning service. He pleads insanity. The prosecution calls a forensic psychiatrist who examined Barnabas and opines that Barnabas had the substantial capacity to appreciate the wrongfulness of his conduct. Barnabas's attorney offers no expert psychiatric testimony; instead, he presents several congregation members who testify that, in their view, Barnabas lacked the capacity to understand what he was doing at the time of the incident. At the close of evidence, each side moves for a directed verdict on the issue of sanity.

**Call:** Which of the following best describes the judge's options?

**Choices:**
- A. She may grant a directed verdict for the prosecution if she finds that the congregation witnesses' testimony about Barnabas's state of mind is not credible.
- B. She may grant a directed verdict for the prosecution if she is personally persuaded by the psychiatrist's testimony that Barnabas was sane beyond a reasonable doubt.
- C. She may grant a directed verdict for the defense if she concludes that no reasonable jury could find that the prosecution proved Barnabas's sanity beyond a reasonable doubt.
- D. She may not grant a directed verdict for the defense, because the prosecution presented expert testimony while the defense relied only on lay witnesses.

#### distilled_core_question

In an MPC state where the prosecution must prove sanity beyond a reasonable doubt, after both sides rest (prosecution: expert says sane; defense: lay witnesses say insane), can the judge grant a directed verdict for either side — and on what standard?

The directed verdict analysis must center on whether the prosecution's evidence was sufficient for a reasonable jury. The judge cannot resolve the factual dispute herself on a criminal element. Directed verdict for prosecution in a criminal trial is constitutionally barred.

#### choice_by_choice_walkthrough

---

**Choice A — "directed verdict for prosecution if congregation witnesses not credible"**

**(1) C3 signal:** The stem tells you the prosecution bears the burden of proving sanity. A directed verdict tests whether the *burden-holder* produced sufficient evidence. Choice A measures the credibility of the *defense* witnesses — the non-burden-holder's evidence. That is tracking the wrong party. This is a structural error visible from the burden allocation in the stem alone.

**(2) Student label:** Wrong party — backwards burden tracking.

**(3) What a true and responsive version would look like:** "She may grant a directed verdict for the prosecution if she finds the prosecution's own evidence of sanity was so overwhelming that no reasonable jury could reject it." [Note: This would still be legally incorrect for other reasons — but the fix makes clear the issue is measuring the wrong side.]

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** A directed verdict tests the sufficiency of the burden-holder's evidence, not the persuasiveness of the opposing witnesses. Mullaney v. Wilbur, 421 U.S. 684 (1975); Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197 (1977).

---

**Choice B — "directed verdict for prosecution if judge personally persuaded by psychiatrist"** *(DOMINANT TRAP)*

**(1) C3 signal:** The choice asks whether the judge was *personally* persuaded. In a criminal trial, directed verdicts on disputed factual elements run only against the prosecution — never for it. The judge's personal conviction is irrelevant to the directed verdict motion by the prosecution.

**Gold Key — GK-CRIMINAL-DV-CRIM-01:** In a criminal trial, a judge can never direct a verdict for the prosecution on a factual element the jury must decide — even if the judge personally believes the prosecution proved it. Directed verdicts on the merits in criminal cases run only in one direction: they remove the case from the jury when the prosecution's case is too thin, never when it is too strong.

**(2) Student label:** Flat misstatement — directed verdict for prosecution on jury-fact element is constitutionally barred.

**(3) What a true and responsive version would look like:** "She may not grant a directed verdict for the prosecution because the sanity question is a factual element for the jury, even if the judge personally credits the psychiatrist's testimony."

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** The Sixth Amendment jury trial right prohibits a judge from directing a verdict for the prosecution on a disputed factual element in a criminal case. Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307 (1979). The judge's personal conviction about the weight of the expert testimony is constitutionally immaterial to a directed verdict motion by the prosecution.

---

**Choice C — "directed verdict for defense if no reasonable jury could find prosecution proved sanity BRD"** *(CORRECT)*

**(1) C3 signal:** After cutting A (wrong party) and D (fabricated rule), B and C survive. The clash axis is: what is the correct directed verdict standard — judge's personal conviction or reasonable-jury sufficiency? The stem's burden allocation and the directed verdict framework resolve this in favor of C. C states the sufficiency standard exactly.

**Silver Key — SK-CRIMINAL-BURDEN-FOCUS-01:** When all four choices describe different directed verdict standards, anchor on who bears the burden, then ask which choice correctly measures the burden-holder — any choice that measures the other side's witnesses or asks whether the judge is personally convinced is tracking the wrong party or the wrong standard.

**(2) Student label:** CORRECT — sufficiency standard for directed verdict for defense.

**(3) What a true and responsive version looks like:** This choice already is one. A directed verdict for the defense is proper when no rational jury could find the prosecution's evidence sufficient.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** MPC § 4.01; Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307 (1979) (rational jury sufficiency standard); Mullaney v. Wilbur, 421 U.S. 684 (1975); Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197 (1977). When the prosecution bears the burden of proving sanity beyond a reasonable doubt, the directed verdict for defense is available when the prosecution's evidence fails to clear the rational-jury bar.

---

**Choice D — "may not grant directed verdict for defense because prosecution had expert, defense only lay witnesses"**

**(1) C3 signal:** Nothing in the stem or any taught rule creates an expert-vs-lay witness distinction that governs directed verdict availability. The choice invents a threshold: "prosecution had expert → no directed verdict for defense." No such rule exists. This is a fabricated requirement that the stem gives no basis for.

**(2) Student label:** Fabricated rule — expert-vs-lay distinction doesn't govern directed verdicts.

**(3) What a true and responsive version would look like:** "She may grant a directed verdict for the defense regardless of whether the prosecution used an expert, if the prosecution's evidence was insufficient for a reasonable jury to find sanity."

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** No rule of directed verdict doctrine or evidence law bars a defense-verdict motion because the prosecution presented an expert. Lay witnesses are competent to testify to sanity observations, and the sufficiency standard does not weight evidence by witness credential type.
