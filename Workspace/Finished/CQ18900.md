Boaz and Ruth run "Boaz & Ruth's Wedding Garden," a Christian-owned wedding venue on a converted farm outside a small Texas town. Esther, a self-employed wedding photographer, was working a Saturday reception in the venue's main barn. While Esther was setting up a second camera near the open bar, an empty cocktail glass slipped out of a guest's hand and shattered on the wood floor, leaving a wet, slick patch. ### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The court should **allow** Esther's lost-booking claim if the missed bookings were caused by the ankle injury. Lost wages (and the self-employed equivalent of lost contract income) tied to a physical injury are recoverable economic consequences of the tortfeasor's negligence; they are not standalone pure economic loss barred by the no-economic-loss-without-physical-injury rule that the venue is trying to invoke. The anchor is straightforward: the venue owed a duty of reasonable care on its premises; the employee created a wet patch by mopping without warning cones; Esther slipped, broke an ankle, and the broken ankle is what kept her from shooting the booked weddings. The chain of causation runs from the venue's negligence to the physical injury to the lost income, so the lost income is parasitic on (and recoverable alongside) the personal-injury damages.

**Gold Key:** economic damages that flow from a tort-caused physical injury are recoverable; they are not pure economic loss. The classic case is *McDougald v. Perry*, 146 So. 2d 408 (Fla. Dist. Ct. App. 1962) (and the broader Prosser & Keeton treatment): a tort victim who misses work because of the injury can recover the lost wages as part of the compensatory damages, even though the wages themselves are an economic interest. **Silver Key:** watch the venue's argument structure — the "pure economic loss" rule is the rule for plaintiffs who suffer ONLY financial harm with no physical impact; the rule does not bar recovery of financial harm that is a CONSEQUENCE of a physical injury.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. Bar it, because missed-booking income in a negligence case is always pure economic loss.**

This is the dominant trap. It feels right because "pure economic loss" is a famous first-year rule and a wedding photographer's missed bookings look like nothing but money. The breaker is the Gold Key: the venue is invoking the standalone-pure-economic-loss rule, but that rule applies only when the plaintiff has suffered no physical injury. Esther broke an ankle. Financial harm that flows from a tort-caused physical injury is recoverable as compensatory damages; the "pure economic loss" bar is not engaged. **Silver Key:** when the defendant's argument uses the phrase "pure economic loss," check whether the plaintiff also has a physical injury — if yes, the bar does not apply and the financial harm rides in on the personal-injury claim.

**C. Allow it only as nominal damages, because the receipts are contingent on future events.**

This trap banks on the contingency wrinkle. Future-event income can indeed be harder to prove, but that is a damages-quantification problem (mitigation, evidentiary sufficiency, certainty), not a recoverability problem. Once causation and certainty are established, lost future bookings are recoverable as compensatory damages — not capped at a nominal sum. Nominal damages are reserved for cases with no compensable injury at all; here, Esther has a broken ankle, a physical-injury claim, and provable lost bookings during her recovery. This choice fails the True & Responsive test on the remedy: it undercuts the correct measure of compensatory damages.

**D. Bar it unless Esther also proves a separate contract with the venue for the lost income.**

This is the contract-vs.-tort frame trap. The temptation is to think "lost contract income" requires a contract theory of recovery. The breaker is that Esther is suing in tort for a premises-injury negligence claim, and the lost income is recoverable as a parasitic economic consequence of that tort — it is not a stand-alone contract claim against the venue for the missed bookings. The relevant contracts are the photography contracts Esther had with the BRIDE-and-GROOM clients; those contracts establish the measure of her lost income, but they are not the cause of action against the venue.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18900, a Torts item on negligence damages: a store's negligence causes a physical injury, the plaintiff misses work, and the defendant argues the lost wages are unrecoverable pure economic loss. The official key is C — the lost wages are recoverable when they flow from the tort-caused physical injury.

The black-letter rule sits in the damages phase of a negligence action. A plaintiff who establishes duty, breach, causation, and harm may recover all damages legally caused by the defendant's tort, including both the personal-injury damages and the economic consequences that flow from those physical injuries. Lost wages (and, for a self-employed plaintiff, the equivalent lost contract income) are a textbook parasitic economic damage, recoverable without an independent contract theory and without a separate "physical injury plus something else" rule. See Restatement (Second) of Torts § 924 (general measure of damages for personal-injury torts) and the treatment in Prosser & Keeton, *The Law of Torts* § 54 (lost earnings and lost earning capacity recoverable when caused by the physical injury). The classic case on the parasitic nature of lost wages flowing from a physical injury is *McDougald v. Perry*, 146 So. 2d 408 (Fla. Dist. Ct. App. 1962) (wife's lost wages recoverable where they were caused by husband's tortious injury).

The "pure economic loss" rule the venue invokes is a different doctrine. Under the rule articulated in cases like *Robins Dry Dock & Repair Co. v. Flint*, 275 U.S. 303 (1927), and the broader Prosser & Keeton treatment, a plaintiff who suffers only financial harm with no accompanying physical injury to person or property cannot recover in negligence. The rule polices the boundary between tort and contract; it does not bar financial harm that is a CONSEQUENCE of a physical injury caused by the defendant's negligence. The transformed outcome is identical to the original: Esther's missed-booking income is recoverable parasitic damages flowing from the venue's tort-caused broken ankle, and the venue's "pure economic loss" argument fails because Esther has a physical injury.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. Allow it if the missed bookings were caused by the ankle injury.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** True and responsive. The broken ankle is the physical injury that opens the door to parasitic economic damages; the missed bookings flow from that injury.
2. **Student label:** True and responsive (parasitic damages).
3. **True/responsive version:** "This choice already states it."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Lost earnings (and self-employed lost contract income) caused by a tort-caused physical injury are recoverable compensatory damages under Restatement (Second) of Torts § 924. The standalone pure economic loss bar does not apply because the plaintiff has a physical injury.

### B. Bar it, because missed-booking income in a negligence case is always pure economic loss.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Tiered-absolute overclaim. The "always" framing is the tell. The standalone pure economic loss bar is the rule for plaintiffs with NO physical injury; it is not the rule for financial harm that flows from a tort-caused physical injury. **Gold Key:** economic damages that flow from a tort-caused physical injury are recoverable; they are not pure economic loss.
2. **Student label:** Tiered absolute — the standalone-pure-economic-loss overclaim.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Missed-booking income is recoverable as parasitic damages when the plaintiff also has a physical injury caused by the defendant's negligence."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** *Robins Dry Dock & Repair Co. v. Flint*, 275 U.S. 303 (1927), and the broader Prosser & Keeton treatment establish the standalone-pure-economic-loss bar. The bar applies only to plaintiffs whose only harm is financial. Once the plaintiff has a physical injury caused by the defendant's negligence, financial harm that flows from that injury is recoverable.

### C. Allow it only as nominal damages, because the receipts are contingent on future events.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Wrong remedy. Contingent future bookings are a damages-quantification issue (certainty, mitigation), not a remedy-category issue. Nominal damages are reserved for cases with no compensable injury.
2. **Student label:** Wrong remedy — nominal caps compensatory damages.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Once causation and a reasonable degree of certainty are established, lost future earnings are recoverable as compensatory damages; they are not capped at a nominal sum."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 924; Prosser & Keeton § 54. The contingency of future bookings goes to proof, not to the remedy category.

### D. Bar it unless Esther also proves a separate contract with the venue for the lost income.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Wrong frame. The suit is in tort (premises-injury negligence), not on a contract for the missed bookings. The relevant contracts are the photography contracts Esther had with the bride-and-groom clients; those contracts measure the loss, but they are not the cause of action against the venue. **Silver Key:** watch the call — "negligence" frames the cause of action.
2. **Student label:** Wrong frame — contract theory on a tort claim.
3. **True/responsive version:** "The lost income is recoverable as a parasitic economic consequence of the tort claim, not as a stand-alone contract claim against the venue."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Imposing a contract-theory requirement would convert every tort lost-wage case into a contract case, which is not the law. The lost income rides the personal-injury claim.
