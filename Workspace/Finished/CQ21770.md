7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The initial aggressor rule is not absolute. When the victim escalates a nondeadly altercation into an apparent deadly-force attack, the initial aggressor may use proportionate force to defend against the new deadly threat. Here Jordan's knife lunge converted the shove into a deadly attack; Abigail's single swing aimed at disarming or stopping the lethal threat and was proportionate, supporting self-defense under the escalation exception.

8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A (dominant trap): True that an initial aggressor usually forfeits self-defense, but the rule allows an exception: if the victim escalates to deadly force, the aggressor may defend proportionately.

B: Verbal withdrawal can restore self-defense, but it is not the only route. Immediate escalation to deadly force can remove the initial-aggressor bar when retreat or withdrawal is impracticable.

D: The correct inquiry is whether Abigail reasonably believed she faced imminent death or serious bodily harm, not whether Jordan subjectively intended to kill her. Requiring actual intent is a misstatement.

9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

Authority floor: Model Penal Code §3.04 (Use of Force in Defense of a Person) and established common-law doctrine: an initial aggressor may regain the right to self-defense when the other party escalates to the use of deadly force and the response is proportionate. Applying that rule here, Jordan's knife lunge created an apparent deadly threat and Abigail's proportionate defensive swing supports the self-defense claim.
