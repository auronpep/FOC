### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**B is correct.** At common law, arson is the malicious burning of the dwelling of another. Any actual burning — including charring of any part of the structure — is sufficient. Here, the defendant poured lamp oil on Paul's home and lit it. The door was charred. Charring satisfies the burning element. The home is a dwelling belonging to another (Paul). ### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (incorrect):** This choice is attractive because the defendant deliberately burned a building — it looks and feels like arson. The breaker is that common law arson requires the burning of a *dwelling* of another. A warehouse used for business is not a dwelling, no matter how intentionally it was burned. The arson charge cannot stand. **Silver Key:** The "dwelling" gate is the first question to ask on any arson call — if the structure isn't a home, common law arson is off the table.

**C (incorrect):** This choice is attractive because the defendant clearly asked someone to commit arson and offered money — it looks like textbook solicitation. The breaker is that at common law, legal impossibility is a defense to solicitation. The defendant solicited the burning of his *own* workshop. At common law, burning your own property is not arson. Because the completed act would not be a crime, the solicitation charge fails.

**D (incorrect):** This choice is attractive because the defendant clearly defrauded his insurance company by burning his own property and collecting money — it looks like theft by deception. The breaker is that larceny by trick requires the victim to part with *temporary possession only*, not title. When an insurance company pays a claim, it transfers title to the money. The defendant received title, not temporary possession, so larceny by trick is the wrong charge. (This would more likely be false pretenses, but that is not the crime charged.)

### 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Common Law Arson:** At common law, arson is the malicious burning of the dwelling of another. Elements: (1) malice (intentional or willful act), (2) burning (any charring of any part of the structure suffices — scorching or smoke damage alone does not), (3) of a dwelling (a structure where people live), (4) of another (the defendant does not own the dwelling). *See* 3 William Blackstone, *Commentaries on the Laws of England* 220–226 (1768); *see also* Cornell Law Institute, Wex Legal Dictionary, "Arson" (https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/arson).

**Larceny by Trick:** Common law larceny by trick occurs when the defendant obtains possession (but not title) of another's property by fraud or deception, with intent to permanently deprive. The critical distinction from false pretenses is that the victim intends to transfer only temporary possession, not title. When a victim intends to transfer title — as in paying an insurance claim — the crime is false pretenses, not larceny by trick. *See* Cornell Law Institute, Wex Legal Dictionary, "Larceny by Trick" (https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/larceny_by_trick).

**Solicitation:** At common law, solicitation is complete when the defendant requests or encourages another to commit a crime, with intent that the crime be committed. Factual impossibility is not a defense. Legal impossibility — where the solicited act, even if completed, would not be a crime — is a valid defense. *See* Cornell Law Institute, Wex Legal Dictionary, "Impossibility" (https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/impossibility).
