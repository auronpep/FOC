## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Under the terms of a written contract, a piano teacher agreed to provide 40 weekly one-on-one lessons to a student's child over the course of 10 months for a total fee of $4,000, payable at the end of the 10-month program. The contract said nothing about mid-program payments. After teaching 10 lessons — exactly one-quarter of the program — the teacher told the student she needed $1,000 as a progress payment to continue. **A.** Only the student is in breach and liable for the teacher's damages, if any.

**D.** Both parties are in breach, and each is entitled to damages, if any, from the other.

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The piano teacher's performance was to take place over 10 months — far longer than the student's single lump-sum payment at the end. Under the doctrine of constructive conditions of exchange, when one party's performance requires a longer period than the other's, that party must complete (or at least substantially complete) performance before the other party's duty to pay becomes due, unless the contract language or circumstances indicate otherwise. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 234(2). Here, the contract said nothing about progress payments. The student's duty to pay $4,000 was not yet due. The teacher's demand for $1,000 mid-stream had no contractual basis, and her refusal to continue — abandoning the remaining 30 lessons — was a wrongful repudiation of her contractual duty. The student was fully within his rights to refuse the progress payment, and the teacher's abandonment gives the student an action for breach.

**Gold Key (GK-CONTRACTS-TUTOR-01):** When performances are not simultaneous, the party whose performance takes longer goes first — the other party's payment duty is constructively conditioned on completion. No completion, no payment duty. This is the constructive condition of exchange.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect** because it reverses who owes the performed-first duty. This choice sells the attractive idea that the student "got lessons" and should have paid something for them — a fairness impulse. But the breaker is that the contract did not call for progress payments, and the constructive condition of exchange placed the teacher's long-duration performance first. The student's payment duty had not yet become due, so his refusal was not a breach. **Gold Key GK-CONTRACTS-TUTOR-01** — the long-duration performer goes first; the payor's duty is not yet due.

**B is incorrect** because it treats the teacher's abandonment as a reasonable position. The "both reasonable" framing is the moral-common-sense bait: the teacher did do some work, so asking for partial payment feels fair. But contract law does not operate on fairness when the constructive condition is clear. The teacher had no contractual right to mid-program payment, and walking away from 30 remaining lessons is a clear repudiation. Only one party breached. **Silver Key SK-CONTRACTS-TUTOR-01** — when one performer quits mid-stream on a long-duration contract with no progress-payment clause, the "both reasonable" answer is always wrong; the quitter breached.

**D is incorrect** because it treats the student's refusal to pay as a breach. This is the dominant trap. The cognitive move it sells: "The teacher did 10 lessons, so refusing to pay anything is itself a breach — both sides broke promises." But the student never promised to pay mid-program. His only payment promise was at the end of 10 months. Because the teacher had not completed performance, the student's payment duty was not yet due. Refusing to do something you are not yet obligated to do is not a breach. **Gold Key GK-CONTRACTS-TUTOR-01** — the payor's duty is constructively conditioned on the long-duration performer's completion; no completion, no duty.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule — Constructive Conditions of Exchange (Order of Performance):**

Under the Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 234, when the performances of the parties can be rendered simultaneously, they are due simultaneously unless the contract provides otherwise. But when one party's performance requires a longer period of time than the other's, that party's complete (or substantial) performance is a constructive condition precedent to the other party's duty to render performance. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 234(2); § 238 (performance is due in the order the contract requires or, absent such provision, in the order the nature of the transaction requires).

**Authority:**
- Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 234 (Order of Performance): Where the performances can be rendered simultaneously, they are due simultaneously; where one requires a longer period, that party's performance is due first.
- Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 225 (Effects of Non-Occurrence of a Condition): Performance of a duty subject to a condition cannot become due unless the condition occurs or its non-occurrence is excused.
- *Kingston v. Preston* (1773) — foundational case on dependency of covenants and order of performance.
- *Nichols v. Raynbred* (1615) — early case on constructive conditions.

**Application:** The teacher's performance (40 weekly lessons over 10 months) necessarily took far longer than the student's single payment. Under § 234(2), the teacher's completion was a constructive condition to the student's payment duty. The contract specified no progress payments. The student's $4,000 payment duty was not yet due when the teacher had delivered only 10 of 40 lessons. The teacher's abandonment was a wrongful repudiation — a material breach. The student's refusal to pay was not a breach because his duty had not yet become due.

**Verification:** The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original.
