### 1. Final Question

Peter operates a small chamomile farm on the outskirts of a farming town. He sells dried chamomile to local bakeries and tea shops. The chamomile gives off a faintly sweet pollen drift during harvest season. Daniel, who lives next door to Peter's field, has had to repaint the exterior of his home three times in two years because the pollen discolors and adheres to his siding. Daniel sues Peter in private nuisance, alleging that Peter was negligent in failing to control the pollen. Peter denies that his conduct was unreasonable. ### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Correct answer: C (originally D).**

Under the Restatement (Second) of Torts § 822, a private nuisance exists when a defendant's conduct causes an invasion of the plaintiff's interest in the private use and enjoyment of land, and the invasion is either (a) intentional and unreasonable, or (b) unintentional but actionable under negligence principles.

Here, Daniel's claim is based on negligence — that Peter failed to control the pollen. Negligence requires unreasonable conduct: did the burden of preventing the harm outweigh the risk of the harm caused? Under the Restatement (Second) of Torts § 826, an invasion is unreasonable if the gravity of the harm outweighs the utility of the actor's conduct, or if the harm is serious but the financial burden of compensating for it (and similar harm) would make continuation of the activity not feasible.

Peter's chamomile farm serves local bakeries and tea shops — a socially useful enterprise at modest scale. The harm is repeated exterior paint discoloration. If the only way to eliminate the pollen requires equipment so costly it would exceed Peter's annual revenue and end the business, the burden on Peter is disproportionate to the harm suffered by Daniel. A court could conclude Peter's conduct was not unreasonable and therefore not negligent.

This is the *only* answer choice that directly addresses the unreasonableness balancing test that governs negligent nuisance liability. It need not guarantee victory — it is the strongest among the options because it speaks directly to the legal standard.

> **Gold Key (Nuisance-Negligence Balancing):** When a private nuisance claim is based on negligence, the defendant's failure to prevent the invasion is not unreasonable if the burden of eliminating the risk vastly exceeds the gravity of the harm, especially when the defendant's activity has social utility. Conduct that would be unreasonable in isolation may be reasonable when the cost of prevention is disproportionately high relative to the harm caused. *See* Restatement (Second) of Torts § 826.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. "The pollen landed only on Daniel's siding and never entered his home or attached garage."**

*What makes this attractive:* This choice plays on the intuition that a nuisance must involve a physical invasion of the land's interior or structures — that pollen merely sitting on exterior siding is too trivial or too "surface-level" to count. Students who fixate on the idea that nuisance requires a tangible, physical intrusion into the home itself may select this.

*The breaker:* A private nuisance requires an invasion of the plaintiff's **interest in the use and enjoyment of land**, not physical penetration of the land or structures. Pollen that discolors siding and forces repeated repainting directly impairs the use and enjoyment of realty. No physical invasion of the home's interior is required.

> **Gold Key:** This choice is defeated by the Gold Key above — the standard is invasion of the plaintiff's interest in the use and enjoyment of land, not physical penetration of realty.

**B. (Not present — see shuffle: original B is now A)**

*The original B mechanic answer is now at new letter A:*

**A. "The pollen has the same discoloring effect on every homeowner within a quarter mile, in roughly equal measure."**

*What makes this attractive:* This is a classic bar-exam bait choice. The "special harm" requirement — that a plaintiff suing for *public* nuisance must show harm substantially different from that suffered by the general public — is a heavily tested distinction. Students who know this rule exist but confuse its application may think: "if everyone is affected the same, then Daniel can't sue for private nuisance."

*The breaker:* The special-harm requirement applies to **public** nuisance, not **private** nuisance. A private nuisance claim requires only that the invasion be substantial and unreasonable with respect to the individual plaintiff's use and enjoyment of land. It is entirely irrelevant whether others are similarly affected. Daniel's claim stands on his own interference, regardless of how many neighbors share it.

> **Silver Key (Doctrine-Check Silver Key):** Always ask: "Does this rule apply to private nuisance or public nuisance?" Many students lose nuisance questions not by misunderstanding the balancing test, but by applying the wrong nuisance category's framework. When the stem says "private nuisance," the public-nuisance special-harm rule is a structural misfit — cut it on category alone.

**D. "Peter did not know the pollen would damage exterior paint when he planted the chamomile."**

*What makes this attractive:* Students who conflate the intent requirement for intentional nuisance with the standard for negligent nuisance are drawn to this choice. If the claim were that Peter *intentionally* caused the nuisance, proof that he lacked knowledge of the damage might matter (since intent requires knowledge that the interference was substantially certain). But this claim is based on negligence.

*The breaker:* When a private nuisance claim is grounded in negligence, the standard is **foreseeability**, not actual knowledge. It is enough that a reasonable person in Peter's position should have foreseen that the pollen could damage neighboring property. Peter's subjective lack of knowledge does not defeat a negligence-based nuisance claim — the question is whether the harm was objectively foreseeable.

> **Gold Key:** This choice fails because negligence uses the reasonable-person foreseeability standard, not the plaintiff's or defendant's actual knowledge. The Gold Key on nuisance-negligence balancing governs: if the harm was foreseeable (even if Peter didn't actually know), the unreasonableness analysis applies.

### 9. Black-Letter Legal Verification

**Governing Authority: Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 822, 826 (1979).**

**Section 822 (General Rule):**
One is subject to liability for a private nuisance if, but only if, his conduct is a legal cause of an invasion of another's interest in the private use and enjoyment of land, and the invasion is either:
1. intentional and unreasonable; or
2. unintentional and otherwise actionable under the rules controlling liability for negligent or reckless conduct, or for abnormally dangerous conditions or activities.

Daniel's claim falls under clause (2): the invasion is unintentional and alleged to be negligent. Thus, the liability question is whether Peter's conduct was negligent — i.e., whether it was unreasonable under the circumstances.

**Section 826 (Unreasonableness of Intentional Invasion):**
An intentional invasion of another's interest in the use and enjoyment of land is unreasonable if:
(a) the gravity of the harm outweighs the utility of the actor's conduct; or
(b) the harm caused by the conduct is serious and the financial burden of compensating for this and similar harm to others would not make the continuation of the conduct not feasible.

While § 826 is textually addressed to *intentional* invasions, courts and scholars extend its balancing framework to negligent invocations through § 822(2)'s incorporation of negligence principles. Under the general negligence framework, unreasonableness is assessed by weighing the risk (gravity of harm) against the burden of prevention (including financial cost) and the social utility of the conduct. *See also* the Learned Hand formula articulation in *United States v. Carroll Towing Co.*, 159 F.2d 169 (2d Cir. 1947), as applied in nuisance contexts.

In the transformed question, Peter's chamomile farming is a socially useful activity (utility), the harm is exterior paint damage (relatively modest gravity), and the cost of prevention would exceed his revenue and end his business (enormous burden). Under this balancing, Peter's conduct may well be found not unreasonable, defeating the negligence element of Daniel's nuisance claim.

**Verification of unchanged elements from original:**
- Tested rule: Private nuisance, negligence-based, unreasonableness balancing.
- Legal issue: What constitutes the strongest defense against a negligent private nuisance claim.
- Credited answer's legal substance: The burden of eliminating the risk (cost of prevention) outweighs the harm (damage to property), showing conduct may not be unreasonable.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "Pollen affects every homeowner within a quarter mile equally."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The stem says *private* nuisance. This choice invokes a rule about *public* nuisance (special harm). Check the category — if it doesn't match, cut it.
2. **Student label:** "Others were affected the same way, so no private nuisance."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice that addresses whether Peter's conduct was unreasonable — e.g., the cost of prevention vs. the harm caused.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The special-harm requirement (plaintiff's harm must differ from the general public's) applies exclusively to *public* nuisance under Restatement (Second) of Torts § 821B. Private nuisance under § 822 has no such requirement. This choice is a category misfit.

**Choice B: "Pollen landed only on siding, never entered the home."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Private nuisance protects the *use and enjoyment of land*, not just the interior of buildings. Exterior damage that impairs use and enjoyment counts. This choice fabricates a requirement.
2. **Student label:** "The pollen didn't get inside, so it's not a nuisance."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice acknowledging that exterior impairment of realty (siding discoloration requiring repainting) constitutes an invasion of the interest in use and enjoyment of land.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** No physical invasion of structures is required. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 822 cmt. c confirms that interference with the use and enjoyment of land — including exterior conditions — suffices.

**Choice C: "Cost of elimination equipment would exceed annual revenue and end the business."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the only choice that directly addresses the unreasonableness balancing test. The call asks for the strongest defense to a negligence claim — this choice shows the burden of prevention vastly exceeds the harm.
2. **Student label:** "Correct — the cost of stopping the pollen is disproportionate to the paint damage."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true-and-responsive answer. It directly engages the negligence unreasonableness standard.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Under Restatement (Second) of Torts § 826, an invasion is unreasonable if the gravity of the harm does not outweigh the utility of the conduct. When the cost of elimination would destroy the business, the burden on the actor far exceeds the harm (exterior paint discoloration), supporting a finding of reasonableness.

**Choice D: "Peter did not know the pollen would damage paint."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Negligence uses *foreseeability*, not actual knowledge. A defendant's subjective ignorance doesn't defeat a negligence claim if the harm was objectively foreseeable.
2. **Student label:** "Peter didn't know, so he can't be negligent."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice addressing whether a reasonable person in Peter's position should have foreseen the harm — not whether Peter actually knew.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Negligence requires only that the harm be reasonably foreseeable. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 281. Actual knowledge is not required; the standard is objective.
