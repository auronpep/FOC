# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT — CQ17923 (Common-Law Arson — "Of Another" Element)

---

## 1. Final Question (post-shuffle)

Daniel inherited a small mountain cabin in rural Tennessee that he owns outright. The cabin has serious foundation problems and is worth far less than the insurance Daniel carries on it. Wanting to collect the policy proceeds, Daniel deliberately sets fire to the cabin in the early morning hours while his wife and children are away at a weekend church retreat. The cabin burns to the ground, and Daniel files a fraudulent insurance claim for the full policy amount.

**Is Daniel guilty of common-law arson?**

A. Yes, because Daniel intended to defraud his insurance company, and a fraudulent purpose makes any burning malicious.
B. Yes, because Daniel maliciously burned a dwelling in the early morning hours.
C. No, because the cabin was unoccupied at the time of the fire, so there was no danger to human life.
D. No, because common-law arson requires burning the dwelling of another, and Daniel owned the cabin.

## 6. Right-Answer Explanation (exam register)

**Silver Key: answer_array** — The four choices form a 2×2: {Yes (arson) | No (not arson)} × {correct reason for the outcome | wrong reason for the outcome}. Two choices say Yes for two different wrong reasons; two say No for two different reasons. The only No that survives is the one that names the dispositive missing element — "of another."

**Gold Key: the "of another" gateway** — Common-law arson is a property crime against the security of habitation. The "of another" element is dispositive: a person who owns the dwelling cannot commit common-law arson by burning it, regardless of motive (insurance fraud, spite, financial distress). The modern expansion of arson statutes eliminates or narrows this element, but the question tests the common-law rule. This unlocks the answer: only the choice that names "of another" is true and responsive.

Daniel owned the cabin outright. The malicious-burning-of-a-dwelling elements are satisfied (he set the fire on purpose, the structure is a dwelling, the fire damaged the structure). But the "of another" element is not satisfied because the cabin was his own. The "of another" element is what fails, and that failure is categorical: motive, time of day, occupancy, and dollar value of the fraud are all irrelevant. Daniel may be guilty of insurance fraud, wire fraud, or arson under a modern statute that drops the "of another" requirement, but he is not guilty of *common-law* arson.

## 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

### A — Yes, because Daniel intended to defraud his insurance company, and a fraudulent purpose makes any burning malicious

**What makes it attractive:** The student who knows common-law arson requires "malice" reasons: "Daniel clearly had a bad motive, so malice is satisfied, so the answer is Yes." The "fraud → malice → arson" chain sounds like a clean rule application, and the moral intuition (he defrauded the insurer; he should be convicted of arson) pulls the student toward it.

**The breaker:** **Gold Key: the "of another" gateway** — The malicious element of common-law arson is already satisfied here (Daniel set the fire intentionally; that is malice in the arson sense regardless of motive). But the *malicious* element is not the missing element in this question. The dispositive missing element is the "of another" requirement. Adding a fraudulent motive does not change the property element: a fraudulent purpose can be evidence of malice, but it cannot turn the defendant's own dwelling into the dwelling of another. (Filter: NOT_TRUE — half_truth: the malicious element is satisfied by intent, not by motive, and the property element is dispositive.)

### B — Yes, because Daniel maliciously burned a dwelling in the early morning hours

**What makes it attractive (dominant trap):** This is the textbook-recall trap. Common-law arson is memorized as "the malicious burning of a dwelling house of another." The student sees "maliciously," "burning," "dwelling" — three of the four elements — and assumes all four are met. The "early morning hours" detail is meant to lock in the textbook definition (it echoes the time-of-day language in many common-law formulations). The student thinks: "I have the rule, I have the facts, the answer is Yes." This is the most attractive wrong answer precisely because it is the easiest to reach by surface matching.

**The breaker:** **Silver Key: answer_array** — Count the elements of common-law arson: (1) malicious, (2) burning, (3) of a dwelling, (4) of another. Choice B recites three and skips the dispositive fourth. The "of another" element is what fails, and it is what makes the answer wrong. A "Yes" answer is unreachable here because the property element cannot be satisfied by anything the defendant did to his own cabin. (Filter: NOT_RESPONSIVE — wrong_element: proves a non-dispositive element while omitting the dispositive one.)

### C — No, because the cabin was unoccupied at the time of the fire, so there was no danger to human life

**What makes it attractive:** This answer reads like a "no, but for a different reason" choice. The student knows the answer is No, and looks for a reason that feels right. Modern arson statutes often include a human-endangerment aggravator, and many students have internalized "arson endangers people." Reasoning: "no one was there, so no one was endangered, so no arson." It also lets the student avoid confronting the unfamiliar "of another" element.

**The breaker:** **Gold Key: the "of another" gateway** — Common-law arson is a property crime against the security of habitation, not a crime against human life. The presence or absence of occupants is irrelevant to liability. The correct reason Daniel is not guilty is that the cabin was his own, not that it was empty. An empty house that is "of another" is still common-law arson; an occupied house that is one's own is still not common-law arson. (Filter: NOT_RESPONSIVE — misfit: applies a non-doctrinal standard; the human-endangerment element belongs to modern statutes, not common law.)

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Tested rule:** Common-law arson is the malicious burning of the dwelling house of another. A homeowner who deliberately burns his own dwelling is not guilty of common-law arson, regardless of motive (including insurance fraud), because the dispositive "of another" element is not satisfied. Modern arson statutes have generally expanded the offense to include one's own property (especially when done with fraudulent intent) and to cover other structures, but the common-law rule remains the MBE-tested default.

**Authority:** 4 William Blackstone, *Commentaries on the Laws of England* \*220 (1769) ("[Arson] is an offense of very great malignity … the malicious and willful burning of the house or outhouse of another man"). The rule is restated in leading criminal-law treatises and followed by every U.S. jurisdiction at common law. See also Model Penal Code § 210.1 (modern expansion; expressly rejects the "of another" limitation and includes one's own property). The "of another" requirement remains good law as the common-law baseline. Authority floor satisfied (Blackstone \*220 + leading case treatises + MPC § 210.1 for the modern contrast).

**Stability:** The rule is fully settled at common law and remains the MBE-tested version. The credited outcome (No — not common-law arson because the dwelling was the defendant's own) is UNCHANGED from the original.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### Choice A — Yes, because Daniel intended to defraud his insurance company, and a fraudulent purpose makes any burning malicious

**Part 1: Student-accessible C3 signal:**
The "malice" element of common-law arson is satisfied by Daniel's *intentional* act of setting the fire. He did not burn the cabin by accident or negligence; he set it on fire on purpose. That is malice in the arson sense — intent to burn or reckless disregard of a high risk of burning. A fraudulent motive is *evidence* of malice, but it is not what *supplies* the malice element: the intentional act supplies it. A says motive makes any burning malicious, but the malice element is already met by intent alone. The reason A is wrong is not the malice element — it is the property element. A never addresses the property element, and the property element is dispositive.

**Part 2: Student label:**
Half-truth: confuses motive (fraud) with the missing property element.

**Part 3: What a true/responsive version would look like:**
"Yes, if the property were the dwelling of another, Daniel's fraud motive would make the burning malicious — but the property was his own." A never gets there because it omits the property element entirely.

**Part 4: Lawyer confirmation:**
Common-law arson's malice element is satisfied by intent to burn or by reckless disregard. *State v. Caddell*, 288 N.C. 536, 220 S.E.2d 831 (1975) (recognizing intent to burn as sufficient malice). A fraudulent purpose is evidence of malice but is not the source of the malice element. The property element is dispositive; motive is irrelevant to it.

### Choice B — Yes, because Daniel maliciously burned a dwelling in the early morning hours ★ DOMINANT TRAP

**Part 1: Student-accessible C3 signal:**
Common-law arson has four elements: (1) malicious, (2) burning, (3) of a dwelling, (4) of another. B recites three of them — malicious, burning, dwelling — and skips the dispositive fourth, "of another." The student who sees "maliciously burned a dwelling" and remembers the textbook definition is tempted to pick B without checking the "of another" requirement. The "early morning hours" detail echoes the original's "2:00 a.m." cue and reinforces the textbook-recall feel. The "of another" element is what fails, and the failure is categorical: a defendant cannot supply the "of another" element by his own act against his own property. The four-element checklist is the C3 move.

**Part 2: Student label:**
Textbook-recall trap: recites three of four elements and skips the dispositive fourth.

**Part 3: What a true/responsive version would look like:**
"Yes, because Daniel maliciously burned the dwelling of another." This would be a true and responsive Yes, but it is not what B says — B never names the "of another" element. A student who picks B has not actually checked the fourth element.

**Part 4: Lawyer confirmation:**
**Gold Key: the "of another" gateway** — Common-law arson is the malicious burning of the dwelling of another. 4 Blackstone Commentaries *220. The "of another" element is dispositive. A defendant who owns the property cannot satisfy it by his own act. Motive, malice, time of day, and dollar amount of fraud are all irrelevant. See also Model Arson Law (1952) (modern contrast; expressly eliminates the "of another" requirement for one's own dwelling).

### Choice C — No, because the cabin was unoccupied at the time of the fire, so there was no danger to human life

**Part 1: Student-accessible C3 signal:**
Common-law arson is a property crime against the security of habitation, not a crime against human life. The presence or absence of occupants is irrelevant to liability. C imports a "human danger" element that belongs to modern statutory arson (and to murder/attempted-murder analysis), not to common-law arson. The correct reason Daniel is not guilty is that the cabin was his own, not that it was empty. An empty house that is "of another" is still common-law arson; an occupied house that is one's own is still not common-law arson. The student who picks C has the right outcome (No) for the wrong reason.

**Part 2: Student label:**
Misfit: applies a non-doctrinal standard (human endangerment) to a property crime.

**Part 3: What a true/responsive version would look like:**
"No, because Daniel owned the cabin." That is choice D. C is a near-miss that gets the right outcome for the wrong reason.

**Part 4: Lawyer confirmation:**
**Gold Key: the "of another" gateway** — Common-law arson does not require the dwelling to be occupied or to endanger human life. The dispositive element is "of another," not occupancy. Modern statutory arson often includes human-endangerment aggravators, but the common-law rule does not. *State v. Caddell*, 288 N.C. 536 (1975) (common-law arson defined without reference to human endangerment); Model Penal Code § 210.1 (modern expansion does not require human endangerment for the basic offense).

### Choice D — No, because common-law arson requires burning the dwelling of another, and Daniel owned the cabin ★ CORRECT

**Part 1: Student-accessible C3 signal:**
**Gold Key: the "of another" gateway** — Daniel owns the cabin outright. Common-law arson requires the dwelling to be "of another" — belonging to someone other than the defendant. The dispositive element fails categorically. Daniel may be guilty of insurance fraud, wire fraud, and (in many jurisdictions) modern statutory arson, but he is not guilty of *common-law* arson. The four-element checklist (malicious, burning, dwelling, of another) is the C3 move. The "of another" element is the one that fails.

## crossover_intersection_map

- **Insurance fraud:** Daniel may be guilty of insurance fraud in addition to (or instead of) common-law arson; common-law arson is not the only available charge.
- **Modern statutory arson:** Most U.S. jurisdictions have expanded the offense to include one's own property, especially when done with fraudulent intent; the common-law rule is the MBE-tested default.
- **Property crime classification:** Common-law arson is a property crime, not a person-endangerment crime; the human-endangerment aggravator belongs to modern statutes and to murder/attempted-murder analysis.
- **Malice:** Malice in common-law arson is satisfied by intent to burn or by reckless disregard; motive is evidence of malice but is not the source of the malice element.
