# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

Executing a valid warrant, police raided a rented civic auditorium during a Holy Week outreach event, lawfully arresting Stephen, the director of a small Christian theater troupe, and lawfully seizing a video recording of the Passion Play performance that he had staged. Stephen was subsequently charged with “conducting an obscene dramatic performance” in violation of a state law. At trial, the prosecution called a professor to the stand. The professor testified that she was a professor of theater arts and the author of several books on the art of biblical drama. She said that although she believed biblical drama to be a valid art form, she found the performance at issue to be devoid of any literary or artistic merit. She stated further that in her opinion it was obscene. In objecting to this testimony, which of the following would be Stephen's most effective argument?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**D is correct.** Although FRE 704(a) abolished the old common-law prohibition on expert opinions that embrace an ultimate issue, an expert still may not state an opinion using terminology that has a special legal meaning or that draws the precise legal conclusion the trier of fact must reach. "Obscene" is such a term; it carries a specific legal definition under state law (prurient interest, patently offensive, lacks serious literary/artistic value). The professor crossed the line by telling the jury the performance "was obscene" rather than describing its content, themes, or production values and leaving the legal application to the jury.

**Gold Key:** Experts may embrace an ultimate issue, but may not use a term that has a special legal meaning or state the legal conclusion the jury is required to draw.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A (The professor does not qualify as an expert.)** Attractive because every evidence question involving experts begins with a qualification check, and students are trained to verify "knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education." The breaker is that the professor is a theater-arts professor and author of multiple books on biblical drama; that background supplies specialized knowledge that would assist the jury. The testimony is objectionable for a different reason.

**B (Since the matter at issue is a subjective one, expert testimony is inadmissible.)** Attractive because obscenity and artistic merit feel like matters of personal taste, so a student may conclude "no expert can help here." The breaker is that FRE 702 abandoned any strict-necessity requirement; expert testimony is admissible whenever specialized knowledge will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or determine a fact in issue. Subjectivity does not create a categorical bar.

**C (The professor's opinion relates to an ultimate issue in the prosecution.)** Attractive (dominant trap) because students remember the old ultimate-issue rule or believe experts are never allowed to "usurp the jury's function" on the bottom-line question. The breaker is FRE 704(a): an opinion is not objectionable merely because it embraces an ultimate issue. The prohibition that survives is narrower—the use of legal terminology or the drawing of legal conclusions.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is unchanged from the original: under the modern formulation of FRE 704(a), expert opinion testimony is not automatically objectionable simply because it embraces an ultimate issue to be decided by the trier of fact. However, experts may not testify in the form of a legal conclusion or use terms that have a special legal meaning (e.g., "obscene," "negligent," "insane," or "guilty"). The jury must apply the legal standard to the facts; the expert may describe the facts and give non-legal opinions that help the jury do so.

Governing authority: Fed. R. Evid. 704(a) ("An opinion is not objectionable just because it embraces an ultimate issue."); see also the Advisory Committee Notes abolishing the common-law ultimate-issue prohibition while preserving limits on legal conclusions. The "obscene" label is a legal conclusion because state obscenity statutes incorporate the Miller v. California, 413 U.S. 15 (1973) three-part test. Stating "it was obscene" therefore tells the jury the legal result rather than supplying factual or technical assistance.

The credited outcome (B / now D) is identical to the original. The legal issue (admissibility of an expert's use of a legal term on an ultimate issue in a criminal obscenity prosecution) is identical. No transform change altered the rule, the key, or the correct result.
