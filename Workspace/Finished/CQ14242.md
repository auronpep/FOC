## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

A is correct. Lydia is challenging an ordinary economic regulation: who may operate mobile commercial stands on city walkways. The ordinance does not classify by race, national origin, sex, legitimacy, alienage, or another suspect or quasi-suspect class, and it does not burden a fundamental right. That means the court uses rational-basis review. Under that deferential test, a city may address congestion gradually and may protect settled reliance interests through a grandfather line, so long as some plausible legitimate reason supports the classification.

Gold Key: ordinary economic and social-welfare classifications receive rational-basis review unless the law uses a suspect classification or burdens a fundamental right. Silver Key: first classify the line the law draws; do not let a correct result paired with strict scrutiny beat a rational-basis answer.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

B sells the absolute-rule shortcut: grandfather clauses feel like favoritism, so the answer makes them automatically unconstitutional. The breaker is the word "per se." Economic grandfather lines are not automatically invalid under Equal Protection; they are tested deferentially if no suspect class or fundamental right is involved.

C sells the most attractive wrong move: it reaches constitutionality and makes congestion sound important enough for strict scrutiny. The breaker is the scrutiny trigger. Gold Key: strict scrutiny is not chosen because the government interest sounds serious; it is chosen because the law burdens a fundamental right or uses a suspect classification. Silver Key: a correct bottom-line result with the wrong test is still not the responsive answer.

D sells the underinclusion objection: if the city is worried about congestion, why let Peter continue operating? The breaker is the review level. Underinclusiveness may matter in heightened scrutiny, but ordinary economic classifications survive rational basis if the line is plausibly related to a legitimate objective.

## 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested legal issue and credited outcome are unchanged from source question 14242: an ordinary municipal economic regulation with a grandfather exemption does not burden a fundamental right or use a suspect classification, so Equal Protection review is rational basis and the ordinance is constitutional if plausibly related to a legitimate purpose.

The Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause supplies the governing constitutional rule. For ordinary economic and social-welfare legislation, courts apply highly deferential rational-basis review. Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma, Inc., 348 U.S. 483 (1955), upheld economic regulation under rational basis and emphasized that legislatures may address perceived problems step by step. New Orleans v. Dukes, 427 U.S. 297 (1976), is the closest governing authority: the Court upheld a city ordinance that banned pushcart food vendors in the French Quarter while grandfathering vendors who had operated for eight or more years. The Court held that, absent a suspect classification or fundamental right, the ordinance was valid if rationally related to a legitimate state interest.
