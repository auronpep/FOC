## 6. Right-Answer Explanation

**Gold Key:** Under Massiah, once formal adversarial proceedings begin, the government may not deliberately elicit incriminating statements from the defendant through any government agent — including a directed informant, co-defendant, or undercover officer — in the absence of counsel. The informant's status as a "private citizen" is irrelevant if acting at government direction.

The correct answer is **A**. Once Martha was indicted, formal adversarial proceedings had commenced, and her Sixth Amendment right to counsel had attached. The government directed her friend to steer the conversation toward the conspiracy, which constitutes deliberate elicitation. Because Martha had no knowledge that her friend was a government agent, she could not have knowingly waived her right to counsel. The statements must be suppressed.

## 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B** — This choice sells the Miranda frame. It names a true rule — Miranda warnings are required only during custodial interrogation — but applies it to the wrong constitutional moment. The stem explicitly says Martha was "indicted" and "awaiting trial." That language locks the frame to post-charge Sixth Amendment rights under Massiah, not pre-charge Fifth Amendment Miranda rights. **Silver Key:** When the stem says "indicted" or "after indictment," immediately lock the frame to post-charge Sixth Amendment rights — not Miranda. Any answer that talks about custody, warnings, or interrogation room tactics is answering the wrong question.

**C** — This choice sells an absolute overclaim. It appeals to the moral intuition that government deception is always unconstitutional, but it overstates the law. Not all deception-obtained statements are inadmissible. Pre-indictment undercover operations, for example, do not violate the Sixth Amendment. The correct basis is the narrower Massiah rule: deliberate elicitation after formal proceedings without counsel.

**D** — This choice sells the "private citizen" myth. It sounds lawyerly — distinguishing sworn officers from private citizens — but it is flatly wrong under Massiah. The Supreme Court expressly held that the government cannot circumvent the Sixth Amendment by using an informant acting under government direction rather than a uniformed officer. **Gold Key:** Under Massiah, once formal adversarial proceedings begin, the government may not deliberately elicit incriminating statements from the defendant through any government agent — including a directed informant — in the absence of counsel.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing authority:** Massiah v. United States, 377 U.S. 201 (1964); United States v. Henry, 447 U.S. 264 (1980); Maine v. Moulton, 474 U.S. 159 (1985).

Under the Sixth Amendment, once formal adversarial proceedings have commenced — by indictment, information, or arraignment — the government may not deliberately elicit incriminating statements from the defendant in the absence of counsel. *Massiah v. United States*, 377 U.S. 201 (1964). The right attaches at the initiation of adversarial proceedings and protects the integrity of the attorney-client relationship during the pretrial phase.

In *United States v. Henry*, 447 U.S. 264 (1980), the Supreme Court held that placing an informant in close proximity to an indicted defendant to elicit incriminating statements violates the Sixth Amendment. The Court emphasized that the government cannot bypass the right to counsel by using an informant instead of a police officer. The informant's status as a "private citizen" is irrelevant when the government directs the elicitation.

The tested rule and legal issue are **unchanged** from the original: post-indictment deliberate elicitation by a government-directed agent without counsel violates the Sixth Amendment. The credited outcome (suppression) is also unchanged.
