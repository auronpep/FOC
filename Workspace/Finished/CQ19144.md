## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Correct Answer D)

Common-law battery is satisfied by the intentional, unlawful application of force to the person of another, where the contact is either harmful or offensive to a person of ordinary sensibilities. Physical injury is **not** an element. Nathan deliberately directed water onto Pastor Daniel without consent, in front of the congregation. A reasonable person would find having liquid deliberately poured over one's head offensive — this is not a socially permitted contact like a handshake or incidental crowd jostling. All elements are met: (1) unlawful contact (no consent, not privileged), (2) application of force (water set in motion by Nathan's volitional act), (3) offensive to ordinary sensibilities. Daniel needed no bruise for the offense to be complete.

**Silver Key (SK-CRIM-BATT-01):** *Offensive contact is its own wrong.* When you see a battery fact pattern where the contact causes clear humiliation or indignity but no physical harm, the "no injury" distractor is almost always the trap. Battery is complete the moment the offensive contact occurs — you never need to look for a bruise.

## 8. Black-Letter Legal Verification

**Governing rule:** Common-law battery = unlawful + application of force + to the person of another + harmful **or** offensive to a person of ordinary sensibilities.

**Authority stack:**

1. **Restatement (Second) of Torts § 13 (1965):** An actor is subject to liability to another for battery if "(a) he acts intending to cause a harmful or offensive contact with the person of the other . . . and (b) a harmful contact with the person of the other directly or indirectly results." Comment c: "An act which, directly or indirectly, is the cause of a contact with another's person . . . subjects the actor to liability." The Restatement explicitly treats indirect contact (via substance or object) as sufficient.

2. **Restatement (Second) of Torts § 18(1):** Offensive contact alone satisfies battery: "An actor is subject to liability to another for battery if . . . the contact is offensive, i.e., offends a reasonable sense of personal dignity."

3. **Leading case — *Wallace v. Rosen*, 765 N.E.2d 192 (Ind. Ct. App. 2002):** Confirmed that physical injury is not required; offensive contact suffices. Battery is complete upon contact that offends a reasonable sense of personal dignity.

4. **Liquid/contact case — *Alcorn v. Mitchell*, 63 Ill. 553 (1872) (classic):** Spitting in someone's face held to be battery — offensive contact via substance, no injury required. This case is frequently cited for the principle that any substance or matter placed in motion by the defendant that contacts the victim constitutes "force" sufficient for battery.

**Doctrinal summary:** The "harmful OR offensive" disjunction is well-established. Both tort-law and criminal-law formulations of battery recognize the same alternative-satisfaction structure. Physical injury is never required. Force can be applied through any medium — liquid, gas, thrown object, or indirect mechanism. The intent element is satisfied by the intent to make the volitional contact; specific motive or purpose is irrelevant to the prima facie case.

**Bank validation:** The credited outcome (battery is satisfied here) is legally sound. `PASS`.
