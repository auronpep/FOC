## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

In a written contract, Stephen, a hymn arranger, agreed to create the vocal arrangements for and supervise the studio recording of a children's Scripture-memory album for Hannah's private homeschool co-op. In return, Hannah agreed to pay Stephen a fee of $12,400, payable when the final album masters were delivered.

After Stephen delivered the masters, Hannah claimed erroneously but in good faith that the harmonies made several tracks unusable for children. Hannah orally offered to pay Stephen $9,100 in full settlement of Stephen's fee claim. Stephen orally accepted that offer, although the reasonable value of his services was in fact $12,400. Hannah paid Stephen $9,100 pursuant to their agreement.

Stephen later sued Hannah for the remaining $3,300. In a preliminary finding, the trier of fact found that there were no defects in Stephen's arrangements or recording supervision. Will Stephen be likely to prevail in his action against Hannah for $3,300?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A service provider completed a fixed-fee contract, the client made a good-faith but mistaken quality dispute, both orally settled the fee for a lesser amount, and the client paid that amount. Can the provider later recover the unpaid balance?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. Yes, because the oral agreement reducing the written fee contract is not enforceable.

B. No, because Stephen's promise to accept $9,100 became binding when Hannah made the payment.

C. No, because Stephen's acceptance of partial payment constituted a novation.

D. Yes, because payment of $9,100 cannot furnish consideration for Stephen's promise to surrender his fee claim.

### 5. Correct answer

**B**. Original key: **C**.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Hannah's good-faith dispute over Stephen's performance gave the later settlement legal bite. Even though the trier of fact later found Stephen's work was not defective, Hannah honestly believed there was a defect when she offered $9,100 in full settlement. Stephen accepted that settlement, and Hannah paid the agreed amount. That completed the accord and satisfaction.

**Gold Key:** A lesser payment can support an accord when the claim is honestly disputed, even if the dispute later turns out to be wrong.

**Silver Key:** Do not stop at "lesser payment." Ask whether the stem gives a good-faith dispute plus actual payment. If both are present, the settlement is not just an unsupported discount promise.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because the original fee agreement was written and the later reduction was oral. The breaker is performance. Hannah paid the settlement amount and Stephen accepted it, so the question is not asking whether an executory oral modification of the written contract can be enforced. The controlling move is accord and satisfaction after payment, not a writing-formality objection.

**C.** This choice reaches the correct "No" result with the wrong discharge label. Novation requires substitution of a new party or a new obligation accepted in place of the old one. The stem keeps the same two parties, Stephen and Hannah, and gives no third-party substitution. The settlement was an accord and satisfaction, not a novation.

**D.** This is the dominant trap. It sounds like the preexisting-duty rule: paying part of an already owed debt usually is not enough consideration for forgiving the rest. **Gold Key:** that rule changes when the claim is subject to a good-faith dispute. Hannah's mistaken but honest belief that the album work was defective supplied the settlement consideration, and her payment completed the satisfaction.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is unchanged: an accord is a contract under which the obligee agrees to accept a stated performance in satisfaction of the obligor's existing duty; performance of the accord discharges the original duty. Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 281 states that structure. Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 74 supplies the consideration rule for settlement of claims: surrendering or settling a claim that later proves invalid can still be consideration when the claim is doubtful or the party asserting it believes in good faith that it may be valid.

Here, Hannah's claim about defective album work was wrong, but it was made in good faith. That good-faith dispute made Stephen's promise to accept the lesser payment enforceable as an accord. Hannah then paid the $9,100, which was the satisfaction. Stephen therefore should not recover the remaining $3,300.

Research did not reveal any doctrinal-currency hotlist issue or key conflict. The authority floor is satisfied by Restatement (Second) of Contracts Sections 74 and 281. Supporting public references reviewed include Cornell Wex's accord-and-satisfaction overview and public discussions of Restatement Section 74's settlement-of-claims rule. The legal issue, rule, trap structure, and credited outcome remain the same as the source item.
