### 5. Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** A business owner owes invitees a duty of reasonable care, but that duty does not extend to protecting against criminal acts that are not reasonably foreseeable. Here, the store had eight years with no violent incidents, the area was quiet, and the stranger's attack was sudden and without warning. No prior similar incidents, no location risk factors, and no specific warning put the owner on notice. Without foreseeability, the duty to protect against this third-party criminal act was not triggered. (*Gold Key: A business is not an insurer against every random act of third-party violence; foreseeability requires specific notice facts — prior similar incidents, dangerous location, or specific threats.*)

---

### 6. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original B)** is attractive because some students equate "any crime on premises" with automatic foreseeability — if it happened there, it was foreseeable. That is exactly the trap: the statement overgeneralizes. Foreseeability in the third-party-criminal-act context is not automatic from geography alone; it depends on specific notice factors such as prior similar incidents, the character of the neighborhood, or specific threats. The store was in a quiet area with zero prior violence. *Gold Key: Mere occurrence on premises does not establish foreseeability — specific notice facts are required.*

**B (original A)** is attractive because it appeals to the powerful intuition that businesses must keep customers safe. But the legal standard is reasonable care, not strict liability or insurance. A business is not an absolute guarantor against every injury a customer suffers. This choice inflates the duty beyond what the law imposes. *The breaker: the standard is reasonable care under the circumstances, not strict liability.*

**C (original D)** is attractive because it reaches the right outcome (no liability) and students may reason that third-party criminal conduct is the intervening cause. But the reasoning is wrong: businesses do owe invitees a duty of reasonable care, including against foreseeable criminal acts. The claim fails here because foreseeability is absent, not because the duty doesn't exist. A student who picks this answer misunderstands the duty element entirely.

---

### 7. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Research

**Rule:** A property owner or business owes invitees a duty of reasonable care, including a duty to protect against foreseeable criminal acts of third parties. However, the duty to protect against third-party criminal conduct generally requires that the criminal act be *reasonably foreseeable* — typically established through prior similar incidents, the character of the location (high-crime area), specific threats, or other notice facts. A business is not an insurer against all random criminal acts. *See* Restatement (Second) of Torts § 344 (liability of possessors of land to third persons); *see also* *Estate of Smith v. Mahoney's Silver Nugget, Inc.*, 127 Nev. 855 (2011) (foreseeability is the duty threshold for third-party criminal acts); *Goldberg v. Housing Auth. of Newark*, 38 N.J. 578, 186 A.2d 291 (1962) (duty depends on foreseeability of criminal conduct); *Foster v. Winston-Salem Joint Venture*, 303 N.C. 636, 281 S.E.2d 36 (1981) (no liability for unforeseeable criminal attack absent prior similar incidents), cited in *Tort Law — Merchant's Duty to Protect Invitees from Third-Party Criminal Acts*, 4 Campbell L. Rev. 411 (1982).

**Application:** Here, the store was in a quiet area with eight years and zero violent incidents. No prior similar acts, no dangerous location, no threats. The attack was sudden and unforeseeable. The duty was not triggered. The credited answer (No) and the tested rule are unchanged from the original.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 344; *Goldberg v. Housing Auth. of Newark*, 38 N.J. 578 (1962); *Estate of Smith v. Mahoney's Silver Nugget, Inc.*, 127 Nev. 855 (2011). Authority class: leading case + Restatement section.
