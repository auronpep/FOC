### 1. Final Question

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A worker may collect business payments and put them in the owner's cashbox, but then secretly pockets some of that money intending to keep it. What common-law theft offense is it?

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

D is correct. Peter committed common-law larceny. Gold Key: for larceny versus embezzlement, ask whether the worker had possession or only custody. Peter was allowed to receive payments and put them in Lydia's cashbox, but he did not have independent possession of the business's money. Lydia's business retained possession, and Peter's secret pocketing of the cash was a trespassory taking with intent to keep it. Silver Key: employee status is only a signal to slow down; it does not decide the offense by itself.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is the dominant trap because employee theft sounds like embezzlement. The breaker is the word "all"; worker theft is embezzlement only when the worker has lawful possession, not mere custody. Gold Key: the custody/possession split kills the automatic embezzlement move.

B is attractive because the visitors voluntarily handed money to Peter. The breaker is that they intended to pay Lydia's business, not to transfer title to Peter by deception. The voluntary-payment fact does not make this false pretenses.

C is attractive because Peter had permission to handle the money. The breaker is that permission to touch or carry cash for the owner is not permission to appropriate it; custody can become larceny when the worker pockets the owner's money.

### 9. Full Black-Letter Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. At common law, larceny is a trespassory taking and carrying away of another's personal property with intent to permanently deprive. The custody-versus-possession distinction separates larceny from embezzlement: embezzlement requires lawful possession before conversion, while a worker with only custody can commit larceny by taking the owner's property. Cage v. State, 355 S.W.2d 757 (Tex. Crim. App. 1958), states the distinction directly: when property remains in the owner's actual or constructive possession, the offense is larceny rather than embezzlement. Loker v. State, 230 A.2d 814 (Md. Ct. Spec. App. 1967), similarly treats servant theft from the master's possession as larceny while distinguishing embezzlement where possession has not reached the master.

Peter had authority to collect admission payments and place them into Lydia's cashbox. That role gave him custody for a limited business purpose, not independent possession. When he removed cash from the box and pocketed it intending to keep it, he trespassorily took property from Lydia's business possession. That is larceny.

Authority links: https://law.justia.com/cases/texas/court-of-criminal-appeals/1958/29751-3.html ; https://law.justia.com/cases/maryland/court-of-special-appeals/1967/169-initial-term-1967-0.html
