## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Daniel burst into a chapel office during a weekday Bible-study signup, pointed a pistol at the treasurer, and grabbed the locked offering box. As Daniel ran toward the door, a volunteer security guard drew his own gun and fired at Daniel. The guard missed Daniel and killed a visiting choir director standing in the hallway. The jurisdiction expressly follows the proximate-cause theory of felony murder. Daniel was charged with felony murder for the choir director's death.

Is Daniel guilty of felony murder?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

In a proximate-cause felony-murder jurisdiction, is an armed robber guilty when his felony foreseeably causes a security guard to fire defensively and kill an innocent bystander?

### 3. Final answer choices

### 5. Correct answer

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is correct. Gold Key: when the question expressly supplies the proximate-cause theory, do not switch to the agency-theory rule that limits felony murder to shots fired by the felon or a co-felon. Daniel's armed robbery predictably triggered defensive gunfire, and that responsive shot killed the choir director. Silver Key: lock the jurisdiction sentence before reading the answer choices. ### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A. The guard-shooter answer is the dominant trap because it states the agency-theory rule a student may remember from the opposite jurisdiction. The breaker is the supplied jurisdiction rule. Gold Key: proximate-cause felony murder can attach when the felony foreseeably causes a resisting non-felon's fatal defensive act.

B. The personal-firing answer sounds cautious because Daniel had a pistol, but it invents a requirement the supplied theory rejects. Liability turns on proximate causation from the armed robbery, not on Daniel personally firing the fatal shot.

C. The intended-victim answer borrows intent logic from a different homicide frame. Felony murder does not require the dead person to be the robber's intended victim; the question asks whether the death was proximately caused by the felony.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is unchanged: in a proximate-cause felony-murder jurisdiction, liability may attach when the defendant's felony is a sufficiently direct and foreseeable cause of death, even if the immediate shooter is a resisting non-felon. In People v. Hernandez, 82 N.Y.2d 309 (1993), the New York Court of Appeals sustained felony-murder convictions where the victim officer was shot by another officer during a gun battle following an attempted robbery, reasoning that causation turns on whether the felons' acts were a sufficiently direct, foreseeable cause of death. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/nyctap/082_0309.htm.

Daniel committed an armed robbery. A defensive shot by a volunteer security guard during that robbery was a foreseeable response to Daniel's armed felony. The shot killed an innocent bystander. Because the jurisdiction expressly follows proximate-cause theory, the fact that the guard rather than Daniel fired the fatal shot does not defeat felony-murder liability.
