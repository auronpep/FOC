## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**B is correct.** Under FRE 411, evidence that a person was or was not insured against liability is not admissible upon the issue of whether the person acted negligently or otherwise wrongfully. Whether Stephen carried watercraft insurance has no bearing on whether he was negligent in operating the canoe. The statement is therefore not relevant to any material issue in the litigation. Under FRE 401 and 402, irrelevant evidence is inadmissible. The objection should be sustained.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (No — excited utterance):** This choice is attractive because Stephen made the statement while visibly shaken and upset immediately after a startling event, which mirrors the surface pattern of FRE 803(2). The breaker: an excited utterance must be a statement *relating to* the startling event or condition. Stephen's comment about his insurance coverage does not describe, explain, or relate to the canoe accident or Barnabas's injury. It is a tangential reassurance, not a statement about the event itself. Even if it were an excited utterance, it would still fail the relevance requirement — hearsay exceptions do not override the threshold relevance bar.

**C (No — admission of a party):** This is the dominant trap. The attraction is real: under FRE 801(d)(2), a statement offered against a party that is the party's own words is not hearsay. Stephen's statement is his own words, offered against him by the plaintiff. The breaker: the party-admission rule removes the hearsay bar, but it does not remove the relevance requirement. Like all evidence, an admissible admission must still be relevant to a material issue. Whether Stephen had insurance is not material to whether he was negligent. FRE 411 specifically bars insurance evidence on the issue of fault. The admission doctrine gets the student to "No, it's not hearsay," but relevance kills it.

**D (Yes — compromise offer):** This choice is attractive because the statement was made post-accident in an emotionally charged context, which can resemble settlement behavior. The breaker: a compromise offer under FRE 408 requires an offer to pay or promise to pay *in exchange for* a promise to discontinue or not assert a claim. Stephen offered nothing in return for any concession — he merely reassured Barnabas about insurance coverage. There was no quid pro quo, so FRE 408 is not implicated.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**FRE 411 — Liability Insurance:** "Evidence that a person was or was not insured against liability is not admissible upon the issue whether the person acted negligently or otherwise wrongfully. This rule does not require the exclusion of evidence of insurance against liability when offered for another purpose, such as proof of agency, ownership, or control, or bias or prejudice of a witness."

**FRE 401 — Test for Relevant Evidence:** "Evidence is relevant if: (a) it has any tendency to make a fact more or less probable than it would be without the evidence; and (b) the fact is of consequence in determining the action."

**FRE 402 — General Admissibility of Relevant Evidence:** "Relevant evidence is admissible unless any of the following provides otherwise. Irrelevant evidence is not admissible."

## Wrong-Answer Recovery Paths

**If student picked A (excited utterance):**
- Missed move: Checked "startling event + stress" but forgot the "relating to" requirement
- Recovery: Ask — does the statement describe or explain the event? "My watercraft policy has plenty of coverage" says nothing about the accident. It's a tangential reassurance. FRE 803(2) requires the statement to relate to the startling event.

**If student picked C (party admission) [dominant trap]:**
- Missed move: Correctly identified party admission but stopped at "not hearsay = admissible"
- Recovery: Ask — is the statement relevant to a material issue? Party admissions remove the hearsay bar, but ALL evidence must still be relevant. Insurance status doesn't make negligence more or less probable. FRE 402 is the floor.

**If student picked D (compromise offer):**
- Missed move: Saw post-accident emotional statement and assumed settlement behavior
- Recovery: Ask — did Stephen offer anything in exchange for dropping the claim? A compromise requires a quid pro quo. Mere reassurance about insurance is not an offer to settle.
