### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Mandamus is not available on these facts. The choice is **D**. A Rule 12(b)(6) denial is an ordinary interlocutory ruling that the defendant can challenge on appeal after final judgment under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. The fact that trial will be expensive and that the district judge "probably" made a legal mistake does not satisfy the demanding mandamus standard. **Gold Key:** mandamus requires a clear and indisputable right to the relief sought AND the absence of any adequate alternative means of relief. The collateral-order doctrine and the eventual final-judgment appeal are both adequate alternative means, so mandamus is unavailable. **Silver Key:** when the call asks whether mandamus is available for an ordinary interlocutory ruling, run the "clear and indisputable right" check first; ordinary legal error in denying a routine motion does not clear the bar.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Yes, because litigation expense alone creates mandamus jurisdiction.**

This distractor reframes the mandamus standard as if the cost of litigation is itself enough. The breaker is doctrinal: ordinary litigation expense is part of the cost of having a federal case and does not, on its own, supply the clear-and-indisputable-right element. Cut on a misframed-element test — the choice supplies the consequence (expense) but skips the antecedent right-and-adequate-alternative question.

**B. No, because federal courts abolished mandamus in civil cases.**

This is a flat misstatement of federal procedure. Mandamus is codified at 28 U.S.C. § 1651(a) (the All Writs Act) and remains available in civil cases, but only in extraordinary circumstances. Cut on a misstated-rule test — the choice invents a categorical abolition that the doctrine does not contain.

**C. Yes, because mandamus is available whenever a party thinks an interlocutory ruling is wrong.**

This is the dominant trap. The "mandamus is available whenever" claim is a tiered absolute: it asserts an always/only rule that has a doctrinal exception. The breaker is the **Gold Key** — the clear-and-indisputable-right standard, paired with the no-adequate-alternative-means requirement. A party who merely thinks an interlocutory ruling is wrong does not satisfy the demanding standard. The trap is engineered to look like the safe, all-purpose default ("of course the writ lies"), but the doctrine narrows the writ to extraordinary circumstances.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18182, a Civil Procedure item involving a denied Rule 12(b)(6) motion, a defendant's attempt to seek mandamus, and the standard for the writ. The official key is C: mandamus requires a clear and indisputable right and no adequate alternative remedy.

The black-letter rule sits at the intersection of the All Writs Act and the case law defining the standard for the writ. The All Writs Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1651(a), authorizes the federal courts to issue "all writs necessary or appropriate in aid of their respective jurisdictions and agreeable to the usages and principles of law." The Supreme Court has long held that the writ is "drastic and extraordinary" and is "reserved for really extraordinary causes." Roche v. Evaporated Milk Ass'n, 319 U.S. 21, 26 (1943). The standard has three classic prongs: (1) the petitioner has a clear and indisputable right to the relief sought; (2) the petitioner has no other adequate means of relief; and (3) the issuing court is satisfied that the writ is appropriate under the circumstances. The Court has also held that mandamus is not a substitute for an appeal. Bankers Life & Casualty Co. v. Holland, 346 U.S. 379 (1953). A denial of a motion to dismiss is an interlocutory ruling; the defendant can ordinarily raise the legal error in an appeal after final judgment, so the no-adequate-alternative-means prong is not satisfied.

A federal district court denies a defendant's Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss in a private civil suit, and the defendant seeks a writ of mandamus from the court of appeals on the ground that trial will be expensive and the judge probably made a legal mistake. Is mandamus available?

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. Yes, because litigation expense alone creates mandamus jurisdiction.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice supplies the consequence (litigation expense) but skips the antecedent clear-and-indisputable-right question. The mandamus standard is two-prong; expense alone does not clear either prong.
2. **Student label:** Expense-alone misframing.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Yes, litigation expense alone creates mandamus jurisdiction MIGHT be true if the writ's purpose were to spare litigants the cost of going to trial. It is not; the writ is reserved for extraordinary causes."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Ordinary litigation expense is part of the cost of having a federal case and does not, on its own, supply the clear-and-indisputable-right element. The writ is reserved for extraordinary causes; expense is not enough.

### B. No, because federal courts abolished mandamus in civil cases.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Mandamus is codified at 28 U.S.C. § 1651(a) (All Writs Act) and remains available in civil cases, but only in extraordinary circumstances. Visible from the stem: "Daniel then seeks a writ of mandamus from the court of appeals."
2. **Student label:** Federal courts abolished mandamus.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Federal courts abolished mandamus in civil cases MIGHT be true if the All Writs Act had been repealed or narrowed to exclude civil cases. It has not."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The All Writs Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1651(a), still authorizes the writ in civil cases. The "abolished" claim is a categorical exclusion the doctrine does not contain.

### C. Yes, because mandamus is available whenever a party thinks an interlocutory ruling is wrong.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the dominant trap. The "mandamus is available whenever" claim is a tiered absolute: it asserts an always/only rule that has a doctrinal exception. The Gold Key handles the clear-and-indisputable-right carve-out.
2. **Student label:** Tiered absolute (dominant trap).
3. **True/responsive version:** "Mandamus is available whenever a party thinks an interlocutory ruling is wrong MIGHT be true if the writ were a general error-correction device. It is not; the writ is reserved for extraordinary causes."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The writ is reserved for extraordinary causes. A party who merely thinks an interlocutory ruling is wrong does not satisfy the clear-and-indisputable-right element; the eventual final-judgment appeal is an adequate alternative means. Roche v. Evaporated Milk Ass'n, 319 U.S. 21, 26 (1943); Bankers Life & Casualty Co. v. Holland, 346 U.S. 379 (1953).

### D. No, because mandamus requires a clear and indisputable right and no adequate alternative remedy.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the residual answer. The clear-and-indisputable-right prong is not satisfied (a likely error is not a clear and indisputable right); the no-adequate-alternative-means prong is not satisfied (the defendant can appeal after final judgment).
2. **Student label:** Residual answer (clear and indisputable right + no adequate alternative).
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Mandamus requires a clear and indisputable right AND no adequate alternative means of relief. A denial of a Rule 12(b)(6) motion is an ordinary interlocutory ruling; the defendant can challenge the legal error in an appeal after final judgment under 28 U.S.C. § 1291, so the no-adequate-alternative prong is not satisfied.

## residual_answer

The residual is **D**. A denial of a Rule 12(b)(6) motion is an ordinary interlocutory ruling. The defendant can challenge the legal error in an appeal after final judgment under 28 U.S.C. § 1291; the no-adequate-alternative prong is not satisfied. The writ is not available.
