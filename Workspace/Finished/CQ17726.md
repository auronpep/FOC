## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** Common-law burglary is a *specific-intent* crime: the defendant must intend to commit a felony **at the moment of** breaking and entering. Intent formed after entry does not satisfy this element — no matter how clear the theft that follows.

Peter broke into Timothy's shop at night, which satisfies the breaking, entering, dwelling-of-another, and nighttime elements. But Peter entered intending only to retrieve his own table saw — a claim-of-right purpose that is not felonious. The intent to steal the drill kit was formed spontaneously *after* Peter was already inside and saw the drill on the workbench. Because felonious intent was not present at the time of entry, the specific-intent element of burglary is missing.

Peter is guilty of larceny (the drill kit) and potentially criminal trespass, but **not** burglary.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Answer A (original B):** This choice is attractive because Peter genuinely believed the table saw was his, and a good-faith claim of right does negate the intent to steal *that item*. A student who locks onto the claim-of-right fact may conclude Peter is "not guilty of any crime." But this overgeneralizes: Peter's claim of right extends only to the table saw, not to the drill kit, which he stole with no claim of right whatsoever. Peter faces larceny liability for the drill and potentially other charges. The choice overstates the scope of the claim-of-right defense.

**Answer B (original A) — DOMINANT TRAP:** This is the most tempting wrong answer. Peter *did* break in at night and *did* steal the drill kit inside. A student who focuses on the completed theft may think the burglary box is checked. But this choice commits a critical timing error: it treats the *occurrence* of a felony inside as satisfying the intent element, when the law requires that the intent to commit the felony exist **at the moment of entry**. **Gold Key:** Burglary's intent element is a specific-intent requirement locked to the time of breaking and entering. Peter's intent to steal the drill formed after he was already inside. The felony-inside-follows-entry fact pattern is not enough.

**Answer C (original D):** This choice has a surface appeal — breaking in at night *feels* inherently criminal. But common-law burglary has five distinct elements, and the intent-to-commit-a-felony-therein element is independently required. The act of breaking and entering at night is not itself the felony that the intent element refers to; the defendant must intend an *additional* felony to be committed inside. This choice collapses the intent element into the actus reus, which is a classic overclaim.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** At common law, burglary is the breaking and entering of the dwelling house of another in the nighttime with the intent to commit a felony therein. *See* Wex, Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School (https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/burglary); *see also* 3 W. Blackstone, Commentaries *224 (defining burglary as breaking and entering another's dwelling at night with intent to commit a felony).

**Authority — timing of intent:** The intent to commit a felony must exist contemporaneously with the breaking and entering. As confirmed by legal analysis: "The intent to commit a crime must exist at the moment of entry. If someone walks into an unlocked office building out of curiosity and only then decides to steal a laptop, that's not burglary — even though a theft occurred." *See* "Is Burglary a Specific Intent Crime?" (https://legalclarity.org/is-burglary-a-specific-intent-crime/). The Model Penal Code similarly defines burglary as entering a building "with purpose to commit a crime therein." MPC § 221.1.

**Claim of right:** A good-faith belief that property belongs to the defendant negates the specific intent to steal that property. *See* Florida Bar Journal, "Claims of Right in Theft and Robbery Prosecutions," Vol. 73, No. 10 (Nov. 1999). Peter's honest belief the saw was his own negates felonious intent as to the saw.

**Application:** Peter entered Timothy's shop (a dwelling-adjacent structure used for work, regularly connected to the home) at 10:00 p.m. (nighttime) by prying open a window (breaking) and climbing inside (entering). At the moment of entry, Peter intended only to retrieve his own table saw — a non-felonious, claim-of-right purpose. The intent to steal the drill kit arose after entry. Because felonious intent was not present at the time of breaking and entering, the specific-intent element of common-law burglary is not satisfied.
