### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**A is correct.** Martha sold the parcel and barn “as is,” Daniel accepted that term, there are no applicable statutes, and the facts do not show fraud, misrepresentation, concealment, or a builder-vendor implied warranty. In that posture, caveat emptor places the risk of post-closing physical defects on the buyer. ### 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Right-Answer Legal Reasoning

The source row identifies Q14929 as a Real Property / Real Property Contracts / Land-Sale Contracts item. The tested rule is the effect of an “as is” clause on post-closing physical defects in a land sale, especially when the buyer later invokes caveat emptor or a warranty deed.

The black-letter rule is unchanged. In the common-law land-sale setting, a seller generally is not liable for physical defects discovered after closing unless the seller made a misrepresentation, fraudulently concealed a defect, owes an implied warranty, or a statute imposes a disclosure duty. An “as is” clause is strong evidence that the buyer accepted the property with its physical risks. Caveat emptor points in the buyer’s direction, not the seller’s. A warranty deed protects title interests; it does not transform the sale into a warranty of physical condition.

Authority check: **Prudential Ins. Co. of America v. Jefferson Associates, Ltd.**, 896 S.W.2d 156, 160 (Tex. 1995), is a leading “as is” real-property case holding that a buyer who freely agrees to purchase commercial real estate “as is” cannot recover for later-discovered physical condition problems absent the recognized exceptions. Cornell LII’s Wex entry on caveat emptor states that the doctrine places the burden on buyers to reasonably examine property before purchase and that concealment can be an exception. Cornell LII’s Wex entries on deeds and warranty deeds confirm that warranty deeds concern title covenants and clear-title assurances. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 161 also supports the limited nature of nondisclosure liability: nondisclosure is equivalent to an assertion only in specified circumstances, such as correcting a prior misrepresentation or acting in bad faith when disclosure is required.
