## 1. Final question

Stephen attended a Saturday livestock auction at a rural sale barn. Wanting a prize calf, he slipped behind the clerk's desk and forged the owner's signature on a brand-transfer card to claim title to the animal — forgery is a felony in the jurisdiction. ## 7. Right-answer explanation

Felony murder substitutes the malice element of murder by importing the danger created by a specific, qualifying predicate felony. The doctrine therefore has two structural gates that the prosecution must clear before it ever reaches the phase/flight inquiry: **(i)** the predicate must be an inherently dangerous or enumerated felony (BARRK — burglary, arson, rape, robbery, kidnapping — at common law; states add others by statute), and **(ii)** the death must be caused by a danger created by that predicate. Forgery of a brand-transfer card is a paper fraud; it is not inherently dangerous to human life, and most jurisdictions do not enumerate it. The elderly buyer's cardiac event was an independent medical occurrence — Stephen did not touch him, displace him, frighten him, or generate any commotion. Both structural gates fail, and there is no qualifying predicate from which to import malice. **Gold Key:** *Felony murder runs on a qualifying predicate plus a danger-caused death; the "felony" label and physical proximity to a death are never enough.*

## 8. Wrong-answer explanations

**B.** Pulls the student who treats the word "felony" as the entire ignition switch for felony murder, so the brand-card forgery plus a death "at the same site" feels like it should at least clear the threshold. The breaker is that felony murder is a category test, not a coincidence test — only inherently dangerous or enumerated predicates qualify, and a paper-fraud felony does not.

**C.** Pulls the student who flips the malice substitution and reads felony murder as a heightened-mens-rea offense — if it's "murder," the doctrine must demand the murder mental state. The breaker is exactly the opposite: the felony-murder rule exists precisely so the prosecution does *not* have to prove intent to kill; the qualifying felony supplies the malice. A choice that demands intent to kill kills the rule.

**D.** *Dominant trap.* Pulls the student who has correctly memorized "during the commission of or in flight from a felony" as part of the felony-murder vocabulary and treats the flight/res gestae phase as a self-contained sufficient condition; the word "any" then sweeps in every felony. The breaker is the predicate gate — the flight/phase inquiry only matters *after* a qualifying inherently dangerous or enumerated felony is on the table; with forgery as the predicate, the phase analysis never starts. **Gold Key:** *Felony murder runs on a qualifying predicate plus a danger-caused death; the "felony" label and physical proximity to a death are never enough.* **Silver Key:** *When a choice uses absolute language ("any death," "during flight") to collapse felony murder into a one-element timing test, treat it as an overclaim and check the predicate gate first.*

## 9. Black-letter verification & legal reasoning for the credited answer

The credited answer (post-shuffle A) is correct under settled common-law felony-murder doctrine. Two limitations of the rule independently defeat liability here:

1. **Inherently-dangerous / enumerated-predicate limitation.** The felony-murder rule is universally narrowed to felonies that are either statutorily enumerated as predicates (first-degree felony murder typically reaches burglary, arson, rape, robbery, and kidnapping — "BARRK") or, in the second-degree zone, judicially classified as inherently dangerous to human life. Courts apply either an "abstract" test (felony dangerous in the abstract) or an "as-committed" test, but both filter out paper-fraud offenses. *See* Model Penal Code § 210.2 cmt. (criticizing felony murder and limiting its operation); Wayne R. LaFave, *Substantive Criminal Law* § 14.5 (inherently-dangerous-felony limitation). Leading articulations of the inherently-dangerous limitation appear in *People v. Phillips*, 64 Cal. 2d 574 (1966), and *People v. Howard*, 34 Cal. 4th 1129 (2005). Forgery is not enumerated and is not inherently dangerous to human life.
2. **Felony-danger causation limitation.** Felony murder requires that the death result from a danger created by the predicate felony — i.e., the felony's foreseeable risks. *See* LaFave § 14.5(c) (causation in felony murder); Restatement-style common-law treatment in standard homicide casebooks. The elderly buyer's sudden cardiac event was not produced by any risk inherent to forging a brand-transfer card.

#### review_truth
Felony murder requires a qualifying inherently dangerous or enumerated predicate felony and a death caused by a danger of that felony; a felony label and a coincident bystander death are not enough.
