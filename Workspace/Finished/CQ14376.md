### 6. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. Martha and Paul are not trying to use the oral agreement to add a new performance term after the written contract already became binding. They are using it to show that the writing was not supposed to take effect unless Hannah approved the melody plan. That is a condition to effectiveness. Gold Key: parol evidence may be admitted to prove a prior oral condition that had to occur before the written agreement became binding. Silver Key: separate "this writing has an extra term" from "this writing never became binding unless the condition happened."

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. No, because the parol evidence rule excludes a prior oral agreement even when that agreement is consistent with a partially integrated writing.**

This answer sounds protective of the writing and uses the right doctrine label. The breaker is the overclaim. A partially integrated writing can be supplemented by consistent terms, and the stronger point here is the Gold Key: the oral agreement is offered to prove a condition to the writing's effectiveness, not to contradict a binding term.

**B. No, because the oral agreement contradicted the writing by making the parties' duties conditional.**

This is the dominant trap. It sees the detailed signed writing and treats the oral approval term as an attack on the written duties. The breaker is the condition-versus-contradiction split: the oral evidence says the writing would not bind either side until approval occurred. Gold Key: a prior oral condition to effectiveness is admissible even though the signed writing contains performance duties.

**D. Yes, because the oral agreement required approval by someone who was not a party to the writing.**

This answer reaches the right yes/no result, and the stem does show nonparty approval. The breaker is responsiveness. The evidence is not admissible merely because Hannah was a nonparty; it is admissible because the approval was the event that had to occur before the writing took effect.
