### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

A contract was formed. The mailbox rule (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 63) makes an acceptance sent by mail effective **on dispatch** — that is, when the offeree deposits the acceptance in the mail — provided the acceptance is **properly dispatched** (correct address and correct postage). The post office's subsequent loss of the letter is irrelevant; the risk of postal loss falls on the offeror, not the offeree. Here, the stem states that Mary's acceptance was "properly addressed to Paul's bakery and bore the correct amount of postage," so the mailbox rule applies and the acceptance was effective the moment Mary deposited it in the mail.

**Gold Key:** the mailbox rule turns on **proper dispatch** (correct address + correct postage), not merely on the act of mailing. A mailed-but-misaddressed or unstamped acceptance is NOT effective on dispatch. The testable element of the rule is the "properly dispatched" requirement, not the "mailed" verb. **Silver Key:** the post office losing a properly dispatched acceptance does not undo the contract; the offeror bears the risk of postal loss.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17521, a Contracts item on the mailbox rule. The official key is C, and the official explanation is that "since the facts state that the writer put the correct address and proper postage on the acceptance, the mailbox rule applies."

The black-letter rule is the **mailbox rule** (or "deposited acceptance rule"): an acceptance sent by mail is effective on dispatch, that is, when the offeree deposits the acceptance in the mail, provided the acceptance is **properly dispatched** (correct address and correct postage). Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 63 ("Acceptance by Mail or Telegram") provides: "Acceptance by mail or telegram is effective as soon as the offeree deposits the acceptance in the mail or tenders it to the telegraph company." The "properly dispatched" element is a sub-rule: the acceptance must be properly addressed and bear the correct postage to be effective on dispatch; a mailed-but-misaddressed or unstamped acceptance is not effective on dispatch. The leading historical case for the mailbox rule is Adams v. Lindsell, 9 B. & C. 681 (K.B. 1818) (English case adopting the rule that acceptance is effective on dispatch); modern authority consistently follows § 63 and the "proper dispatch" sub-element.

The post office's loss of the letter is irrelevant under the mailbox rule. The risk of postal loss or delay is allocated to the offeror, not the offeree. The transformed outcome is identical to the original: a contract was formed when Mary deposited her properly dispatched acceptance in the mail, regardless of whether the letter was lost in transit.
