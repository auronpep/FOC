### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Ruth’s claim depends on Lydia’s promised performance under the Barnabas-Lydia contract. Barnabas’s promise to buy all required bulbs and battery packs from Lydia was part of the exchange supporting Lydia’s promise to send booth-display work to Ruth. When Barnabas bought substantial required supplies from another supplier in the first month, that failed the exchange on which Lydia’s related duty rested.

**Gold Key:** A third-party beneficiary’s claim rises no higher than the underlying contract. If the promisee’s uncured material breach or failed condition prevents the promisor’s related duty from coming due, the beneficiary cannot enforce that duty.

**Silver Key:** Do not stop at the beneficiary label. When one choice names third-party status and another choice names the contracting party’s failed performance, use the status-vs-defense axis and let the earlier performance fact decide.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This answer sells the familiar consideration instinct: Ruth herself did not bargain directly with Lydia. The breaker is that the consideration for Lydia’s promise came from Barnabas’s side of the broader exchange, so Ruth’s personal lack of consideration is not the deciding issue.

**C.** This is the dominant trap. It sells the most visible fact: Ruth was named as the person who would receive Lydia’s performance, and she relied by turning down another account. ### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule and credited outcome are preserved. The original item tests whether a third-party beneficiary can recover when the promisee materially breached the exchange that supported the promisor’s promised performance to the beneficiary. The official credited answer was C; after shuffling, the identical legal answer is B. 

UCC § 2-306 treats a requirements term as the buyer’s actual requirements in good faith, and exclusive dealing imposes best-efforts-type obligations in the goods relationship. Barnabas’s substantial purchases from another supplier defeat the requirements/exclusive-supply exchange. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 237 states the condition principle: each party’s remaining duties are conditioned on no uncured material failure by the other party to perform earlier-due exchange duties. ([Matthew Miner][2]) Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 309(2) then carries that defense into the beneficiary layer: if the contract ceases to bind in whole or in part because of nonoccurrence of a condition or present/prospective failure of performance, the beneficiary’s right is discharged or modified to that extent. ([Matthew Miner][3]) Cornell’s Wex also states the core third-party-beneficiary frame: the promisor and promisee are the contracting parties, while the third party may enforce only as an intended beneficiary whose rights have vested. ([Legal Information Institute][4])
