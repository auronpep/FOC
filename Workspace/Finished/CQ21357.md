### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: the item asks whether lawful initial entrustment followed by fraudulent conversion is larceny, embezzlement, false pretenses, or larceny by trick. The output uses verified outline code `73030500` for Crimes against Property > Embezzlement because the decisive rule is embezzlement, even though the source row listed `73030100` for larceny.

The governing black-letter distinction is grounded in the common-law theft categories. The Department of Justice Criminal Resource Manual section 1005, quoting *Moore v. United States*, 160 U.S. 268, 269 (1895), defines embezzlement as fraudulent appropriation by a person to whom property has been entrusted or into whose hands it lawfully came, and distinguishes it from larceny because larceny requires the felonious intent at the original taking. DOJ section 1006 separately states larceny elements as wrongful taking and carrying away, absence of consent, and intent to deprive, citing *United States v. Barlow*, 480 F.2d 1245, 1251 (D.C. Cir. 1972). Cornell Wex's larceny-by-trick entry confirms the adjacent distinction: larceny by trick involves deception that gets possession without title. Sources: https://www.justice.gov/archives/jm/criminal-resource-manual-1005-embezzlement; https://www.justice.gov/archives/jm/criminal-resource-manual-1006-larceny; https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/larceny_by_trick.

Applied here, Daniel's possession started lawfully through employment entrustment. His later decision to keep two microphones is the fraudulent conversion. That is embezzlement. The official key is confirmed.
