## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

During negotiations to purchase a used boat, a buyer asked a seller whether the boat had ever been in a serious accident. The seller replied: "It is a fine boat and has been thoroughly inspected and comes with a certificate of assured quality. Feel free to have the boat inspected by your own mechanic." In actuality, the boat had been in a major accident, and the seller had repaired and repainted the hull, successfully concealing evidence of the accident. The buyer declined to have the boat inspected by his own mechanic, explaining that he would rely on the seller's certificate of assured quality. At no time did the seller disclose that the boat had previously been in an accident. The parties then signed a contract of sale. After the boat was delivered and paid for, the buyer learned that the boat had been in a major accident. If the buyer sues the seller to rescind the transaction, is the buyer likely to succeed?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A buyer asked a seller directly whether a used boat had ever been in a serious accident. The seller gave a misleading answer emphasizing the boat's quality and offering inspection, while actively concealing evidence of a major prior accident. The buyer relied on the seller's statement and declined independent inspection. After closing, the buyer discovered the truth. Can the buyer rescind the contract?

### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** When a seller induces a buyer into consenting to a contract by means of a material misrepresentation — including active concealment of material facts and misleading half-truths in response to a direct question — the resulting contract is voidable at the election of the buyer. Here, the buyer asked a direct question about whether the boat had ever been in a serious accident. The seller's response, taken in context, was a misleading half-truth: by emphasizing the boat's quality and offering an inspection, the seller created the impression that the boat had no serious accident history, while actively concealing evidence of the major prior accident. The accident history of the boat would be material to a reasonable buyer's decision. The seller's active steps to conceal the damage and repair make the seller's statement the legal equivalent of an assertion that the boat had not been in an accident. The buyer is entitled to rely on the truth of material representations made by the seller and need not conduct independent tests to discover the seller's deceit. The contract is voidable, and the buyer is likely to succeed in rescinding the transaction.

**Gold Key:** Active concealment of a material defect + a misleading half-truth in response to a direct question = fraudulent misrepresentation. The seller cannot escape responsibility by pointing to the buyer's opportunity to inspect when the seller actively worked to hide the defect.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect.** This answer correctly concludes that the buyer may rescind the contract, but it misstates the reason why. Unconscionability arises when there are unfair terms coupled with an unfair bargaining process. Not every case of misrepresentation or concealment constitutes unconscionability. Here, apart from the seller's misleading statement and active concealment, there is insufficient evidence that the contract itself was unconscionable. The correct basis for rescission is fraudulent misrepresentation, not unconscionability. **Gold Key:** The governing doctrine is fraudulent misrepresentation (active concealment + misleading half-truth), not unconscionability — the two doctrines have different elements and are triggered by different facts.

**B is incorrect.** This is the dominant trap. A buyer is generally entitled to rely on the truth of material representations made by a seller and need not conduct independent tests to discover whether the seller is lying — especially when the seller has actively concealed the defect. The opportunity to inspect is not a categorical defense to a claim of fraudulent misrepresentation when the seller's own conduct (active concealment + misleading half-truth) induced the buyer's reliance. **Gold Key:** When a seller actively conceals a material defect and gives a misleading answer to a direct question, the buyer's failure to inspect does not bar rescission — the seller cannot profit from the concealment it engineered.

**D is incorrect.** A seller does not need to make an express affirmative statement ("the boat has never been in an accident") to be liable for fraudulent misrepresentation. When a buyer asks a direct question and the seller gives a misleading half-truth — emphasizing the boat's quality and offering inspection while actively concealing a known major accident — the seller's statement, taken in context and in light of the active concealment, is the legal equivalent of a false assertion. The seller cannot escape responsibility merely by pointing to the absence of a direct denial. **Gold Key:** Active concealment + misleading half-truth in response to a direct question = actionable misrepresentation, even without an express false statement.

### 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Tested rule:** Fraudulent misrepresentation in contract — active concealment of material facts + misleading half-truths in response to a direct question renders a contract voidable at the aggrieved party's election.

**Legal grounding:**

- **Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 164(1):** A party's manifestation of intention is induced by either a fraudulent or material misrepresentation by the other party upon which the recipient is justified in relying, the contract is voidable by the recipient.
- **Restatement (Second) of Torts § 551:** A party to a business transaction is under a duty to exercise reasonable care to disclose to the other party before the transaction is consummated any material fact known to him that the other party is not likely to discover, where the other party is relying on the party's superior knowledge, where the fact is basic to the transaction, and where the party knows the other party is acting under a mistaken belief as to the fact. Active concealment of a known material defect satisfies this duty.
- **Elements of fraudulent misrepresentation:** (1) false representation of a material fact, (2) scienter (knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard), (3) intent to induce reliance, (4) justifiable reliance, (5) damages. Active concealment satisfies the "false representation" element because concealing a material fact while making misleading statements is the equivalent of an assertion. *See* Restatement (Second) of Torts § 550 (fraudulent concealment).
- **Justifiable reliance despite opportunity to inspect:** A buyer is entitled to rely on a seller's material representations and is not required to conduct independent investigations to verify them, particularly when the seller has actively concealed the truth. *See, e.g.,* the principle that a party who actively conceals a defect cannot invoke the buyer's failure to inspect as a defense.
