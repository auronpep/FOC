### 6. Full right-answer explanation

D is correct. Paul promised in the lease that he would not assign or sublet without Esther's written permission. He then sublet to Ruth without getting that permission. That gives Esther a claim against Paul for breach of covenant.

Gold Key: A tenant's breach of a no-assignment/no-sublease covenant gives the landlord a breach claim, but it does not automatically void the transfer and does not let the landlord terminate the lease or evict the transferee unless the lease expressly reserves a right of re-entry or forfeiture. Esther's lease required written permission, but it did not reserve a forfeiture remedy. Silver Key: in a remedy matrix, test each remedy separately; one true half plus one false half makes the "both" answer fail.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because Esther's categorical refusal feels unfair. A student may turn that unfairness into a total excuse for Paul and conclude that Esther gets no remedy at all. The breaker is that Paul still sublet without the written permission required by the lease. Gold Key: on the generic bar-exam rule, a silent consent clause still leaves an unauthorized sublease as a breach; the arbitrary-refusal fact does not erase the covenant claim.

B is the dominant trap. It rewards the student who correctly sees Paul's breach and then adds every landlord remedy that sounds natural. The breach half is true, but the eviction half is not. Gold Key: no reserved right of re-entry means no termination or eviction. Silver Key: break the compound answer into pieces; if the eviction piece fails, the whole "both" answer fails.

C is attractive because a broken lease promise sounds like it should let the landlord remove the person now occupying the premises. The breaker is that a sublease made in breach of a transfer covenant is not automatically void, and the lease did not reserve a termination or re-entry remedy. Esther may sue Paul for breach, but she may not elect to terminate the lease and evict Ruth on these facts.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested issue remains Real Property, Nature and Ownership of Land, Landlord and Tenant, Assignments and subletting. The verified outline code is `82010404`.

The governing bar rule is that a tenant who violates a covenant against assignment or subletting is liable to the landlord for breach of covenant. Because courts favor the alienability of leasehold estates, the unauthorized transfer is not ordinarily void. A forfeiture or eviction remedy requires a lease provision or statute giving the landlord that remedy; a mere covenant requiring consent is not enough.

Authority floor, lawyer-confirmation layer only: Restatement (Second) of Property: Landlord and Tenant Section 15.2 addresses restraints on alienation of leasehold interests. Tage II Corp. v. Ducas (US) Realty Corp., 17 Mass. App. Ct. 664, 461 N.E.2d 1222 (1984), quotes Restatement Section 15.2 comment g for the proposition that a transfer made in violation of a consent restraint can remain a violation even if the transferring party argues the landlord lacked a reasonable basis to object. Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/massachusetts/court-of-appeals/1984/17-mass-app-ct-664-1.html. The California Department of Real Estate reference chapter likewise states that the law favors transferability of leasehold rights and that assignment/sublease prohibitions are construed strictly against the landlord. Source: https://www.dre.ca.gov/files/pdf/refbook/ref09.pdf. Current research also shows a modern reasonableness split under Restatement Section 15.2(2), but the generic source item tests the traditional bar-exam remedy rule: breach claim yes, termination/eviction no absent an express reserved remedy. No authority found contradicts the supplied official key under that generic MBE framing.

Credited outcome unchanged: Esther may successfully assert a breach-of-covenant claim against Paul, but she may not terminate the lease and evict Ruth because the lease did not expressly reserve that remedy.

## wrong_answer_recovery_paths

A recovery path: Do not let landlord unfairness erase the visible tenant breach. Ask whether the tenant got written permission. If no, preserve the breach claim.

B recovery path: Split the "both" answer. Keep the breach claim and cut the eviction remedy.

C recovery path: Ask where the right to evict comes from. If the lease does not reserve re-entry or forfeiture, the answer overclaims.

## outline_mastery_map

Placement: Real Property > Ownership > Landlord and Tenant > Assignments and subletting (`82010404`).

This item teaches: unauthorized leasehold transfers can create damages liability without making the transfer void or creating automatic forfeiture.

Fills: remedy scope for anti-transfer covenants; strict separation of breach from forfeiture.

Adjacent to master: Duties and remedies (`82010402`); Termination, including surrender and mitigation (`82010405`); Restraints on alienation (`82010504`).

## crossover_intersection_map

Contracts crossover: breach of covenant does not always create the remedy a party wants.

Civil Procedure crossover: eviction requires a proper remedy path, not just moral entitlement.

## review_truth

A tenant who sublets without required written permission breaches the covenant, but the landlord cannot terminate the lease or evict the subtenant unless the lease expressly reserves that forfeiture remedy.
