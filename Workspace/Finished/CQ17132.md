# CQ17132 — The Unlit Piling at Barnabas's River Landing

## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question (revised stem + call)

Barnabas owned a private boat landing on a stretch of river known for repeated nighttime thefts of cargo from boats moored or disabled near the landing. Barnabas had recently driven a row of new mooring pilings into the channel as part of a repair to the dock. One night, Barnabas's crew neglected to switch on the hazard lamps that marked the newly driven pilings. Naomi's cargo launch, traveling the channel after dark, struck an unlit piling and broke its propeller shaft, leaving the launch dead in the water near the landing. While Naomi went ashore to find a telephone and summon a tow, thieves boarded the disabled launch and stole $280,000 worth of goods. Naomi sued Barnabas to recover for the $280,000 loss of the goods and for the damage to her launch. Barnabas has stipulated that he was negligent in failing to light the pilings and admits liability for the damage to the launch, but he denies liability for the loss of the goods. On cross-motions for summary judgment on the claim for the goods, how should the court rule?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A defendant negligently creates a hazard that disables the plaintiff's vehicle in a spot with a known history of cargo theft; while the plaintiff seeks help, thieves steal the cargo. On cross-motions for summary judgment, how should the court rule on whether the theft was a superseding cause?

### 3. Final answer choices (A–D, post-shuffle)

**A.** Grant Naomi's motion, because but for Barnabas's failure to light the pilings, the goods would not have been stolen.

**B.** Grant Barnabas's motion, because the criminal acts of the thieves were a superseding cause of the loss of the goods.

**C.** Deny both motions, because there is evidence to support a finding that Barnabas should have realized that his negligence could create an opportunity for a third party to commit a crime.

**D.** Grant Barnabas's motion, because no one could have foreseen that the failure to light the pilings could result in the loss of a launch's cargo of valuable goods.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**C is correct.** A defendant remains liable for the criminal act of a third party when that act was a reasonably foreseeable result of the defendant's negligence at the time the defendant acted. Barnabas knew his landing sat on a stretch of river with a known history of repeated cargo thefts from disabled and moored boats. A reasonable jury could therefore find that Barnabas should have realized that leaving the pilings unlit — disabling boats near a theft-prone landing — created an opportunity for exactly this kind of crime. Because foreseeability is genuinely disputed on these facts, neither side is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. **Silver Key:** on cross-motions for summary judgment, when one material fact (foreseeability) is genuinely disputed, the correct answer denies *both* motions and sends the issue to the jury. **Gold Key:** a third party's crime is a superseding cause *unless* the defendant should have realized its negligence created the opportunity for that very crime — and a known local history of the same crime puts foreseeability in the jury's hands.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A (grant Naomi — but-for).** This choice rewards the satisfying observation that the chain is unbroken: had Barnabas lit the pilings, the launch would not have been disabled, and the cargo would still be aboard. But but-for cause only gets a negligence claim through the door; it never decides liability by itself, because proximate cause is the contested element. The choice proves actual cause — a non-dispositive element — and never reaches the foreseeability question the call turns on. **Silver Key:** a choice that grants judgment "because but for" answers actual cause, not the proximate-cause issue actually in dispute.

**B (grant Barnabas — superseding cause). DOMINANT TRAP.** This choice feels like mastery: it names the precise doctrine — the criminal act of a third person is a superseding cause — and that *is* the default rule, which makes it read as the sophisticated answer. The breaker is the exception the choice silently drops: a criminal act is **not** superseding when the defendant should have foreseen it, and the known history of thefts at this landing makes foreseeability a jury question, not a matter of law. **Gold Key:** a foreseeable third-party crime does not supersede — so the superseding-cause label cannot be applied on summary judgment here.

**D (grant Barnabas — no one could have foreseen).** This choice trades on intuition: a missing hazard lamp seems a world away from a cargo theft, so the loss feels freakish and unforeseeable. But "no one could have foreseen" overclaims unforeseeability as a matter of law. Given the repeated thefts at this landing, the result is not so far-reaching that no reasonable jury could find it foreseeable — so the foreseeability question belongs to the jury, defeating a defense judgment.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested rule (unchanged from the original):** Negligence requires duty, breach, actual cause, proximate (legal) cause, and damages. Proximate cause is limited by foreseeability. The intervening criminal act of a third party is generally a **superseding cause** that cuts off the original negligent actor's liability — **but not** where the actor realized or should have realized that the negligence created the opportunity for, and the likelihood of, that crime.

**Affirmative reasoning for C:** Barnabas stipulated breach (failure to light) and admitted liability for the direct vessel damage; those concessions do not resolve the goods claim, which turns solely on proximate cause. The dispositive sub-issue is whether the intervening theft was foreseeable enough to avoid being a superseding cause. A known, repeated history of cargo theft at the same landing is precisely the kind of evidence from which a reasonable jury could find the theft foreseeable. Where a reasonable jury could go either way on a material fact, neither cross-motion for summary judgment can be granted (no genuine dispute + entitlement as a matter of law is the summary-judgment standard, and the first half fails). The court must deny both motions.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):**
- **Restatement (Second) of Torts § 448** — an intentional tort or crime by a third person is a superseding cause of harm afforded by the actor's negligence *unless* the actor at the time of the negligent conduct should have realized the likelihood that the situation might be created and that a third person might avail himself of the opportunity to commit the crime.
- **Restatement (Second) of Torts § 449** ("the very risk" / foreseeable-hazard rule) — if the likelihood that a third person may act criminally is one of the hazards that makes the actor's conduct negligent, that act does not relieve the actor of liability.
- **Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 440, 442** — definition of superseding cause and the multi-factor framework, subsidiary to foreseeability.
- **Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical & Emotional Harm § 34** — abandons "superseding" terminology but reaches the same result: a defendant is liable for harms within the scope of the risks that made the conduct tortious.

#### review_truth
A third party's criminal act is a superseding cause only if it was unforeseeable; a known history of the same crime in that place makes foreseeability a jury question, so summary judgment is improper for either side.

## Gold Key(s)
- **GK-TORTS-SUPERSEDING-FORESEEABLE-CRIME-01** — *A third party's criminal act is a superseding cause that cuts off the negligent defendant's liability UNLESS the defendant should have realized its negligence created the opportunity for, and the likelihood of, that very crime; a known history of the same crime in the same place makes that foreseeability a jury question.* Type: exception. Unlocks dominant trap B. Trigger: negligence leaves property vulnerable where that crime has happened before. Authority (lawyer-confirmation only): Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 448-449.
