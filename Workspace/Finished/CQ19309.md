### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**D is correct.** The common law draws a bright-line rule: deadly force is never justified solely to protect property. A property owner may use reasonable, non-deadly force to prevent theft or trespass — blocking a door, physically restraining a shoplifter, or pulling a bag away. But the moment the force rises to the level of deadly force (a firearm, regardless of where it is aimed), the justification requires an independent threat to persons, not merely a threat to property. Here, Lydia was a non-violent shoplifter attempting to leave with merchandise. She posed no threat to Peter or anyone else. Peter had non-deadly options available (he was a large man who could have blocked the exit or restrained her). The shooting was unjustified, and Peter is liable for the resulting injury.

**Gold Key:** *Deadly force requires a threat to persons. No amount of property value and no crime-in-progress converts a property defense into a deadly-force justification. The line is categorical, not proportional.*

### 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Rule:** A person may use reasonable, non-deadly force to protect property from theft or trespass, but may never use deadly force to protect property alone. Deadly force is justified only when there is an independent threat to the safety of persons (self-defense or defense of others).

**Authority:**
- Common law rule, universally applied across U.S. jurisdictions. *See* Restatement (Second) of Torts § 79 (1979) (use of deadly force to protect property is not privileged).
- *Katko v. Briney*, 183 N.W.2d 657 (Iowa 1971) (property owner liable for injuries from spring gun set to protect unoccupied dwelling; deadly force not justified to protect property alone).
- *People v. Ceballos*, 12 Cal.3d 470 (1974) (spring gun to protect unoccupied home; deadly force not justified for property defense).
- Model Penal Code § 3.06(3)(d) (deadly force not justified solely to prevent property crime; only non-deadly force permitted unless threat to persons exists).
- SLCC Criminal Law, § 5.3 ("deadly force is never justifiable to protect real or personal property from harm").

**Application:** Peter used a firearm (deadly force) to stop a non-violent shoplifter. Lydia posed no threat to Peter's or anyone's physical safety. The property at stake ($600 Bible) does not change the analysis. Peter had non-deadly alternatives available. Under the categorical common-law rule, his use of deadly force was unjustified.

**Verification:** The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original. The official key (C → D post-shuffle) is correct. No doctrinal-currency concerns — this is a foundational common-law rule with no recent superseding authority.
