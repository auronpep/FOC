## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation
Silas's sentence is unconstitutional because it violates the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. Under the `Gold Key: eighth_amendment_proportionality`, a criminal sentence is unconstitutional if it is "grossly disproportionate" to the crime committed. In *Solem v. Helm*, the Supreme Court held that a life sentence without the possibility of parole is grossly disproportionate when triggered by a series of relatively minor, nonviolent property crimes. Although the state has a legitimate interest in punishing recidivists more severely, that interest does not override the constitutional requirement that the punishment must fit the gravity of the offenses.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations
(A) This is the **Dominant Trap**. It is attractive because recidivism is a valid basis for enhanced sentencing. However, even a recidivist sentence must satisfy the proportionality requirement of the Eighth Amendment. A life sentence for seven minor, nonviolent offenses crosses the line into gross disproportionality.

(C) This choice is attractive because it suggests the sentence is not truly "final" or "without hope." However, the possibility of executive clemency (commutation by a prefect or governor) is a matter of grace and does not satisfy the requirement for a judicial or legal possibility of parole. It does not cure an otherwise unconstitutional sentence.

(D) This choice is attractive because it highlights the minor nature of the crime, but it misidentifies the legal test. The constitutionality of a recidivist sentence is not determined solely by the penalty of the *primary* (most recent) crime in isolation, but by whether the total enhanced sentence is grossly disproportionate to the defendant's entire criminal history as triggered by that crime.

## 8. Black-Letter Verification
The Eighth Amendment prohibits "cruel and unusual punishments." This includes a "proportionality principle" that prevents sentences that are grossly disproportionate to the severity of the crime. For non-capital sentences, this is a narrow principle. *Solem v. Helm* (1983) established that life without parole for a 7th nonviolent felony (writing bad checks) is unconstitutional. *Harmelin v. Michigan* (1991) and *Ewing v. California* (2003) refined this, but *Solem* remains the primary authority for life-without-parole sentences for minor nonviolent property crimes. The tested rule (proportionality), legal issue (life without parole for minor recidivism), and credited outcome (unconstitutional) are unchanged from original QID 22303.
