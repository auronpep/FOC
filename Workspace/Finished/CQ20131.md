### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Castle doctrine can remove a duty to retreat in the home, but it does not create a license to use deadly force after the danger to persons has ended. Gold Key: castle doctrine changes the retreat question; it does not erase necessity, imminence, or proportionality. Silver Key: freeze the moment of force, not the moment of entry. When Peter fired, the intruder had dropped the tablet, left the home, and was running away empty-handed, so no imminent person-safety threat remained.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A. The serious-felony answer is attractive because burglary is the kind of intrusion that makes castle doctrine feel powerful. The breaker is timing: by the moment of the shot, the intruder was outside, empty-handed, and fleeing, so the force was not necessary to stop an imminent threat to persons.

C. The never-deadly-force answer is attractive because the facts make Peter's shot unjustified. The breaker is the word "never"; deadly force can be justified in a dwelling when the actor reasonably faces an imminent threat of death, serious bodily harm, or a qualifying violent intrusion.

D. The no-duty-to-retreat answer is the dominant trap because it states the castle-doctrine phrase students remember. Gold Key: no duty to retreat is not the same as permission to shoot a fleeing person after the threat has ended.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. Castle doctrine is an exception to a retreat requirement in the home; it does not eliminate the ordinary deadly-force requirements of necessity, imminence, and proportionality. Model Penal Code section 3.04(2)(b) permits deadly defensive force only when the actor believes it is necessary to protect against death, serious bodily harm, kidnapping, or forcible sexual intercourse, while section 3.04(2)(b)(ii)(1) states that an actor is not obliged to retreat from his dwelling. Model Penal Code section 3.06(3)(d) also limits deadly force in protection-of-property or dwelling settings to attempts to dispossess the actor of the dwelling or to commit or consummate serious property felonies under circumstances involving threatened deadly force or substantial danger of serious bodily harm. People v. Aiken, 4 N.Y.3d 324 (2005), traces the home no-retreat doctrine while emphasizing the balance between protecting the home and protecting life. Here, when Peter fired, the intruder had left the home, abandoned the property, and was running away. The castle doctrine did not justify deadly force because the imminent person-safety threat and necessity had ended.
