## 1. Final question

At a Christmas choir fundraiser held in a rented community hall, Officer Ruth lawfully arrested Daniel for stealing a portable card reader from the choir's payment table. Daniel did not resist. Ruth handcuffed Daniel and walked him to the side exit. While Daniel stood quietly with his hands cuffed behind him, Ruth became angry and repeatedly struck Daniel in the ribs with a metal flashlight. ## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Daniel was handcuffed and quiet when Officer Ruth repeatedly struck him with a metal flashlight. His response was one shoulder shove, and he stopped after that. **Gold Key:** a suspect generally may not use force to resist an arrest, but reasonable force may be used to defend against an officer's excessive force. **Silver Key:** separate the lawful arrest from the force used after the arrest. Probable cause answers the arrest question; excessive force after restraint answers the battery-defense question.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice feels legal because Officer Ruth had probable cause and the arrest itself was lawful. The breaker is that probable cause does not answer whether Ruth later used excessive force after Daniel was handcuffed.

**C.** This choice grabs the right defense word and tries to spread it across the whole episode. The breaker is scope: excessive force may justify Daniel's responsive shoulder shove, but it does not erase the separate theft charge.

**D.** This is the dominant trap. It sells the common myth that no person being arrested may ever use force against an officer. **Gold Key:** the no-resistance rule has a narrow excessive-force exception; Daniel's single shove was aimed at stopping repeated blows after he was restrained.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The source row is BarMatrix Q17186, a Criminal Law / Defenses item keyed to self-defense because the suspect was handcuffed and nonresistant, the officer repeatedly struck him, and the suspect used a single limited act of force to stop the blows. The verified outline code is **72060201**, "Defenses > Justification Defenses > Self-defense."

The tested rule is stable for bar purposes: a person generally may not use force merely to resist a known officer's arrest, even if the arrest is unlawful or even if the arrest is supported only by probable cause. But reasonable force may be justified when the officer uses greater force than necessary and the person reasonably believes immediate force is needed to protect against that excessive force. Texas Penal Code § 9.31(b)(2), (c) states that force to resist an arrest or search is generally not justified but is justified if, before resistance, the officer uses or attempts greater force than necessary and the actor reasonably believes force is immediately necessary to protect himself. Tennessee Code § 39-11-611(e)(3) states the same exception structure for an arrest, search, stop and frisk, or halt. citeturn379746search1turn379746search0
