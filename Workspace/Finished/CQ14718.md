### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is the best answer. Stephen did not merely cause a tiny fire by accident and then stand helplessly by. Once he saw the fire, he could safely extinguish it, and he chose not to because he wanted the building destroyed. Gold Key: arson is a malice crime, not a specific-intent crime; malice can be satisfied by consciously disregarding an obvious fire risk. Silver Key: do not freeze the call at the first spark; track the later choice that lets the burning become destructive.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells a real-looking concurrence objection: the match fell by reflex before Stephen formed the final decision to let the shop burn. The breaker is that the call is arson, and the destructive burning continued after Stephen consciously chose not to stop a known fire risk. Gold Key: arson malice does not require a specific intent that exists at the instant of the first spark.

B sells the low-level fault label because Stephen carelessly used a match around dry packing material. The breaker is that negligence alone is not the mens rea for arson; the legally important fact is his later conscious decision to let the fire spread.

C sells the voluntary-act objection because the match dropped when Stephen jerked his burned thumb. The breaker is the same later fact: Stephen was able to put out the fire and voluntarily walked away so the building would burn. Silver Key: when an answer locks onto the first physical movement, check whether the stem gives a later voluntary choice that answers the call.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested issue and credited outcome are unchanged: whether a defendant who accidentally starts a small fire but then knowingly lets it spread because he wants the building destroyed has the malice required for arson.

Common-law arson was the malicious burning of the dwelling of another, and modern arson statutes commonly broaden the protected property. Cornell Wex summarizes the common-law core and notes the modern expansion of arson definitions: https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/arson. The arson-malice floor is also supported by DeBettencourt v. State, 428 A.2d 479 (Md. Ct. Spec. App. 1981), which held that malice for arson includes a dangerous act done with reckless and wanton disregard of consequences: https://law.justia.com/cases/maryland/court-of-special-appeals/1981/1112-september-term-1980-0.html. Model Penal Code section 2.02(2)(c) defines recklessness as conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk, and MPC section 220.1 treats arson and related fire offenses as turning on purposeful fire-setting, insurance-motivated destruction, recklessness, and failure to control dangerous fires: https://www.criminallawweb.net/web1/MPC/PART1/snippets/220_1.htm.

Under that rule, Stephen is guilty. The accidental drop does not save him because his later decision to let the fire spread supplies the malicious burning. Negligence alone would not be enough, but the facts show conscious disregard plus a desire for destruction.
