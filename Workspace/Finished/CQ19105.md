### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Ruth's yes happened while the illegal detention was still ongoing, just two minutes after the stop, and no intervening event broke the causal chain. Gold Key: consent after an unlawful seizure must be both voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from the illegality; a bare yes during the same illegal detention does not automatically cure the violation. Silver Key: when the stem gives "illegal stop," "still detained," "two minutes later," and "no intervening event," read the question as a taint/attenuation question before trusting the word consent.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells a public-place privacy shortcut. That is attractive because Ruth is in a public plaza. The breaker is that the searched object is a personal tote, and the problem is not a lack of bag privacy; it is consent tainted by an illegal detention.

C sells a formal-paperwork rule. That is attractive because written consent sounds cleaner and easier to prove. The breaker is that the Constitution does not require written consent; the defect here is the unattenuated illegal seizure.

D sells the simple consent cure. That is the dominant trap because "she said yes" looks like the whole answer. The breaker is the Gold Key: consent obtained during an ongoing illegal detention remains tainted unless the government can show attenuation.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. Consent is a recognized basis for a warrantless search, but consent obtained during an unlawful seizure must be voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from the illegal detention. When the illegal stop is ongoing, the consent follows immediately, and no intervening event breaks the causal chain, the consent does not validate the search.

The authority floor is satisfied by the Fourth Amendment and Supreme Court attenuation cases. Brown v. Illinois, 422 U.S. 590 (1975), identifies temporal proximity, intervening circumstances, and the purpose and flagrancy of police misconduct as central attenuation considerations. Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), held that consent to search luggage was tainted where the defendant was illegally detained when consent was obtained. Utah v. Strieff, 579 U.S. 232 (2016), confirms the Brown attenuation framework; unlike Strieff, this item gives no intervening warrant or comparable break in causation.
