Police believed that Caleb, a student at a city public arts academy, was selling controlled pills during after-school rehearsals. A police officer called the academy and asked the assistant principal to summon Caleb from choir practice and search his violin case. ### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. The officer did not make an independent school-discipline request; he asked the assistant principal to summon Caleb and search the case for evidence of a crime. Gold Key: when police use a school official to do the search for them, the search is not saved merely by the school official's job title. The officer still needed a valid Fourth Amendment basis. The stem gives only a belief that Caleb was dealing pills, with no facts establishing probable cause and no voluntary consent. Silver Key: do not stop at "principal" or "police"; ask who initiated the search and what fact justifies opening the container.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because silence can feel like practical submission, especially after a warning that the search is about to happen. The breaker is that submission to a demand is not voluntary consent. Caleb did not say yes, hand over the case, or otherwise make an affirmative consent move.

B lands on the suppression side and notices the police-direction fact. The trap is that the officer's use of the assistant principal explains why Fourth Amendment scrutiny applies, but that fact alone does not prove suppression. If the officer had probable cause or a valid exception, the search could still be lawful.

D is the dominant trap because students remember that school officials often search students under a lower school-reasonableness standard. The breaker is the initiating fact: this was a police-requested evidence search, not an independent school-discipline search. Gold Key: the school-search shortcut does not erase the police-direction problem.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are preserved. The item is a criminal-procedure Fourth Amendment search-and-suppression question, not a criminal-law justification-defense question; the source outline code `72060200` appears to be a source classification defect. The verified outline code used here is `75070201` for Fourth Amendment searches and the warrant/probable-cause requirement.

The Fourth Amendment governs searches by public officials and by persons acting as instruments or agents of the government. New Jersey v. T.L.O., 469 U.S. 325 (1985), confirms that the Fourth Amendment applies to public-school searches and that ordinary school searches may use a reasonableness standard rather than probable cause. Skinner v. Railway Labor Executives' Ass'n, 489 U.S. 602 (1989), drawing on United States v. Jacobsen and Coolidge v. New Hampshire, confirms that the Fourth Amendment reaches private or non-police actors when they act as instruments or agents of the government. The police-requested search here is a law-enforcement search through the assistant principal, and the stem supplies no probable cause facts. Consent does not rescue the search because voluntary consent must be shown from the circumstances; mere silence after a threatened search is not enough. Sources checked: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/469/325/ and https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/489/602.
