## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

The load-bearing fact is the timing: the data was overwritten **before any collision or claim was on the horizon** — that is, before any duty to preserve attached. A party's duty to preserve evidence is triggered only when litigation is reasonably anticipated; until then, an automated retention cycle that routinely overwrites data in good faith is exactly the ordinary operation the rules protect. Because the loss happened in the routine, good-faith operation of the system and outside any preservation duty, the court has no basis to sanction.

**Gold Key:** No preservation duty exists until litigation is reasonably anticipated; routine, good-faith automated deletion *before* that moment is not sanctionable spoliation. **Silver Key:** On a "best argument against sanctions" call, first cut any choice that argues *for* sanctions — it answers the opposite of what was asked.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

- **A — "Sanctions are required whenever relevant ESI is missing."** This sells the gut intuition that lost relevant evidence must trigger consequences, so a careless reader grabs it as the obvious response. **Silver Key:** the call asks for the best argument *against* sanctions — A argues *for* them, so it cannot be responsive; and sanctions are never automatic, so it is also an overclaim. The breaker: "required... whenever" states an absolute the law does not contain.
- **C — "Protected only if he shows the lost data would have damaged his own defense."** This sounds lawyerly by invoking a burden, attracting the student who half-remembers that lost evidence is weighed for prejudice. The breaker reverses the real direction: the routine good-faith principle protects the party *without* forcing it to prove the missing content would have hurt itself — that condition is fabricated and backwards.
- **D — "ESI can never be the basis for discovery sanctions."** *(Dominant trap.)* This is the most seductive because it borrows the correct answer's own logic — the safe-harbor idea — and inflates it into a blanket immunity, so the student who grasped the right concept overshoots. **Gold Key:** the protection is narrow (routine good-faith loss before a duty arises); ESI lost *after* a duty attaches and without reasonable preservation steps *can* support sanctions. The breaker: "never" claims an absolute exemption the rules flatly reject.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification + right-answer legal reasoning

**Tested rule (unchanged):** the duty to preserve evidence (including ESI) attaches when litigation is reasonably anticipated; data lost before that, through the routine and good-faith operation of an information system, does not support discovery sanctions.

**Affirmative reasoning for B:** Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 37(e) governs failure to preserve ESI. As amended December 1, 2015, it applies only to ESI "that should have been preserved in the anticipation or conduct of litigation." Here the data was overwritten *before* any litigation was reasonably anticipated, so no preservation duty had attached and Rule 37(e) is never triggered — the routine, good-faith retention cycle controls and no sanction lies.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):**
- FRCP 37(e) (current, as amended Dec. 1, 2015) — sanctions for lost ESI require that the information "should have been preserved in the anticipation or conduct of litigation" and that the party "failed to take reasonable steps to preserve it."
- Former FRCP 37(e) (added 2006, originally 37(f)) — the original "routine, good-faith operation of an electronic information system" safe harbor, the source of the rule statement supplied with the item.
- *Zubulake v. UBS Warburg LLC*, 220 F.R.D. 212 (S.D.N.Y. 2003) — the duty to preserve attaches when a party reasonably anticipates litigation.
- 2015 Advisory Committee Note to Rule 37(e).

**Doctrinal-currency note (attorney review flagged):** the rule statement supplied with the source row uses the *superseded* 2006 phrasing ("absent exceptional circumstances... routine, good-faith operation of an electronic information system"). The 2015 amendment replaced that passive safe harbor with an affirmative "reasonable steps / duty to preserve" framework and reserves the harshest sanctions for intentional spoliation. The **credited outcome is unchanged** under either framework: because deletion preceded any preservation duty, no sanction lies. Superseded framing, same outcome → noted here and flagged for attorney review; not a key disagreement, so PASS stands.
