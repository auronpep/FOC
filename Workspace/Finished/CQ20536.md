## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Lydia owns a private Christian retreat-supply shop. She and Barnabas, a commercial printer, orally agree that Barnabas will sell Lydia 1,200 ordinary boxed Scripture journals at $9 each for delivery before a youth conference.

Later that day, Barnabas signs a memo stating, "Confirmed: sale of 450 boxed Scripture journals to Lydia for delivery next Friday." Lydia sues Barnabas to enforce the alleged agreement for 1,200 journals.

Assuming no exception applies, how many journals is the alleged goods contract enforceable for under the signed memo?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A signed goods memo states a smaller quantity than the alleged oral deal. With no exception, how much is enforceable?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. All 1,200 journals, because the oral agreement controls once the signed memo shows a deal existed.

B. None, because the memo misstated the quantity agreed to orally.

C. All 1,200 journals, but only if Lydia proves the oral quantity by clear and convincing evidence.

D. Only 450 journals, because the alleged contract is not enforceable beyond the quantity shown in the signed writing.

### 5. Correct answer

**D** - original key letter: **C**.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**D is correct.** This is a sale of goods, and the signed memo shows only 450 journals. **Gold Key:** under UCC Section 2-201, a signed goods memo is not destroyed merely because a term is omitted or misstated, but enforcement is capped at the quantity shown in the writing. **Silver Key:** when the answer array offers none, all, and the memo number, lock the call to enforceable quantity. The memo number is the ceiling.

Lydia can enforce the alleged contract only for 450 journals.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This is the dominant trap because it starts with a real idea: the signed memo can indicate that a deal existed. The breaker is the quantity ceiling. **Gold Key:** the memo's quantity controls the enforceable amount, so the oral 1,200-journal figure cannot expand the contract beyond 450 journals.

**B.** This choice sells the formalist instinct that a wrong quantity ruins the whole writing. The breaker is that UCC Section 2-201 is not all-or-nothing on an incorrect term. The writing can still work, but only up to the quantity it shows.

**C.** This choice sounds lawyerly because proof standards often matter when oral facts are disputed. The breaker is that the UCC quantity cap is not a burden-of-proof problem. No clear-and-convincing showing lets Lydia use the oral number to exceed the signed writing.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is unchanged: a UCC sale-of-goods statute-of-frauds writing must indicate that a contract for sale was made and be signed by the party to be charged. The writing does not fail merely because it omits or incorrectly states a term, but the contract is enforceable only up to the quantity of goods shown in the writing. Here, Barnabas signed a memo showing 450 journals. No exception is supplied. The alleged 1,200-journal oral quantity therefore cannot be enforced beyond 450 journals.

The source row supplied outline code `55010101`, but the outline map's deeper direct fit for the tested rule is `54030400` - Defenses to Formation or Enforcement > Statute of Frauds. That verified code is used everywhere in this file.

Authority floor: UCC Section 2-201(1), as published by Cornell LII, confirms both parts of the rule: an incorrect or omitted term does not automatically defeat the writing, and enforcement is limited by the quantity shown in the writing. Source checked: https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-201.

No researched authority contradicts the supplied key. The legal issue, tested rule, credited outcome, and trap mechanics remain unchanged.
