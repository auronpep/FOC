## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Only that the excavation made Ruth's cottage less valuable, because nuisance replaces support rules.**  
This sells a familiar neighbor-land frame: excavation can feel like interference with property use, so nuisance sounds close enough to fit. The breaker is the call's support-rights proof question. A nuisance label does not replace the lateral-support rule that decides whether strict liability or negligence governs the building damage.

**B. Only that Barnabas excavated and the cottage was damaged, because support liability is always strict.**  
This is the dominant trap. It grabs the true strict-liability rule for natural land and stretches it into an always rule for buildings. **Gold Key:** strict liability protects land in its natural condition; when the building's added weight contributes and the natural land would not have subsided, the building-damage claim generally needs negligence.

**D. That Ruth held an express easement for support in Barnabas's parcel.**  
This sounds property-formal: if one parcel depends on another, maybe the claimant needs an express property right. The breaker is that lateral support for natural land is a common-law support right, not an express-easement requirement. For this cottage damage, the missing proof is negligence, not an express easement.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested black-letter rule:** An adjoining landowner has a right to lateral support for land in its natural condition. If excavation withdraws naturally necessary lateral support and causes natural land to subside, strict liability generally applies. But when the land would not have subsided in its natural condition and the damage is attributable to the added weight of a building or other improvement, the plaintiff generally must prove negligence to recover for the improvement damage.

**Governing authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):** Restatement (Second) of Torts section 817 states the strict support rule for withdrawal of naturally necessary lateral support. Restatement (Second) of Torts section 819 addresses negligent withdrawal of lateral support for land or artificial additions. In *Noone v. Price*, the Supreme Court of Appeals of West Virginia summarized the majority rule: strict liability is limited to land in its natural state, and where the land would have remained supported but slips because of the additional weight of a building or structure, there is no liability absent negligence. See https://law.justia.com/cases/west-virginia/supreme-court/1982/15426-4.html. A federal decision applying the Restatement also quotes section 817 on withdrawal of naturally necessary support and section 819 on negligent withdrawal causing harm to artificial additions. See https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-ared-4_11-cv-00748/pdf/USCOURTS-ared-4_11-cv-00748-0.pdf.

The source row's official answer is consistent with that rule. The original credited answer required negligence; the transformed credited answer still requires negligence.
