# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

Lydia operates a private craft workshop that sells devotional event supplies. In a signed writing, Lydia contracted with Daniel, who runs a private Bible-memory retreat, for the sale to Daniel of 40 identical Psalm 23 shadow-lantern kits. The writing called for 20 kits to be delivered on October 10 and the remaining 20 kits on October 24. The writing did not state the place of delivery or the time or place for payment. On October 10, Lydia tendered 19 kits and said, “One kit was crushed in the courier’s truck on its way from my supplier to me, but I will deliver a replacement within four days.”

Which statement is correct?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

Seller owes 20 goods now and 20 later; seller tenders 19 now and promises a replacement in four days. Must buyer accept the current lot, and may buyer cancel the future lot?

## 3. Final answer choices

A. Daniel is entitled to accept any number of the 19 kits and reject the rest, but is not entitled to cancel the contract as to any rejected kits or the lot due on October 24.

B. Daniel must accept the 19 kits and is not entitled to cancel the rest of the contract.

C. Daniel is entitled to accept any number of the 19 kits, reject the rest, and cancel the contract both as to any rejected kits and the lot due on October 24.

D. Daniel must accept the 19 kits but is entitled to cancel the rest of the contract.

## 5. Correct answer

**B**. Original key: **D**.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** The writing calls for goods in separate dated lots, so the answer must be checked on two axes: the current lot and the remaining contract. **Silver Key:** read the choices as a 2×2 matrix: current lot accept/reject × future contract cancel/no cancel. **Gold Key:** an installment-contract cure problem does not use ordinary perfect tender. Because Lydia promised a prompt replacement for the missing kit, Daniel must accept the 19 kits tendered and cannot cancel the remaining lot.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This is the dominant trap because it sells a familiar UCC move: accept some goods and reject the rest. The breaker is the context. **Gold Key:** separate delivery lots plus prompt cure assurance move the problem out of ordinary perfect tender and into the installment-contract rule.

**C.** This choice is attractive because it gives the buyer every ordinary perfect-tender remedy: partial acceptance, rejection, and cancellation. The breaker is the wrong frame. The contract is built around separate lots, and the promised quick cure blocks both partial rejection of the current lot and cancellation of the later lot.

**D.** This choice gets the first axis right: Daniel must accept the current tender. The breaker is the second axis. A curable shortfall in one lot does not justify canceling the rest of the contract unless the nonconformity substantially impairs the value of the whole contract.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The source row supplies QID 14492, the Contracts topic, the focus-group selection percentages, the original stem, the four choices, the original credited answer, and the official explanation identifying the item as an installment-contract problem.  The selected outline code is **56050402**, listed under Contracts → Performance, Breach, and Excuse → Breach → Perfect Tender Rule. 

UCC § 2-612(1) defines an installment contract as one requiring or authorizing delivery of goods in separate lots to be separately accepted. UCC § 2-612(2) permits rejection of a nonconforming installment only when the nonconformity substantially impairs that installment and cannot be cured, and states that if the seller gives adequate assurance of cure the buyer must accept the installment. UCC § 2-612(3) treats the whole contract as breached only when the nonconformity or default substantially impairs the value of the whole contract. ([Legal Information Institute][1])

The transform preserves the tested rule, issue, trap structure, and credited outcome. The governing legal issue remains: whether a buyer under a goods installment contract may reject the current tender or cancel the remaining contract after a small, curable shortfall with prompt cure assurance. The answer remains no.
