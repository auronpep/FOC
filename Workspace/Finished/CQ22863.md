### 1. Final Question

Caleb, a worship leader and a citizen of State A, was a close spiritual protégé of Margaret, an elderly church patron who owned property in several states. When Margaret died, Caleb filed a probate claim in state court challenging Margaret's last will and testament, which left her entire estate to her adult son, David, a citizen of State B. Caleb claimed that Margaret had promised to leave him money to provide for his financial security. At the same time, Caleb filed a tortious interference claim against David in federal court, seeking $5 million in damages, alleging that David had purposely kept Margaret away from Caleb during the last year of Margaret's life to prevent any change to the will. After a trial on the merits, the federal court ruled in favor of Caleb on the tortious interference claim. Thereafter, the state court found the will to be valid. David appealed the federal court's decision, arguing that the federal court lacked subject matter jurisdiction over Caleb's claim because the claim involved probate matters.

Does the federal court have subject matter jurisdiction over the tortious interference claim?

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct because the federal court has diversity jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1332. Caleb is a citizen of State A and David is a citizen of State B, so there is complete diversity. The amount in controversy is $5 million, well above the $75,000 threshold. Gold Key: the probate exception is narrow — it bars federal courts only from (1) probating or annulling a will, (2) administering a decedent's estate, or (3) disposing of property already in the custody of a state probate court. Caleb's tortious interference claim seeks an in personam judgment for damages against David individually; it does not ask the federal court to probate the will, administer the estate, or take control of estate property. Therefore, the probate exception does not apply, and diversity jurisdiction is sufficient.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is the dominant trap because it states a correct legal principle — the probate exception does reserve core probate functions to state courts — but overstates its reach. The breaker is that the probate exception is narrow under *Marshall v. Marshall*, 547 U.S. 293 (2006). It does not bar every federal claim that merely relates to a probate matter. Caleb's tort claim for damages against David personally is not a request to probate the will or administer the estate.

B attracts students who conflate the state court's ruling on the merits of the will with the federal court's jurisdictional authority. The breaker is that the validity of the will is a merits question in the state probate proceeding; it has no bearing on whether the federal court has subject matter jurisdiction over a separate tort claim.

D sounds plausible because multi-state property might seem to create a federal interest. The breaker is that the location of estate property is not a basis for federal subject matter jurisdiction. Diversity jurisdiction requires complete diversity of citizenship and the requisite amount in controversy — not geographic dispersion of assets.

### 9. Black-Letter Verification And Legal Reasoning

The probate exception to federal diversity jurisdiction is narrow. Under *Marshall v. Marshall*, 547 U.S. 293 (2006), federal courts are barred from: (1) probating or annulling a will, (2) administering a decedent's estate, or (3) disposing of property that is in the custody of a state probate court. *Markham v. Allen*, 326 U.S. 490 (1946), established that federal courts may entertain suits that touch probate matters so long as they do not interfere with core state probate functions. A claim for tortious interference with an expected inheritance seeks an in personam judgment for damages against an individual defendant and does not require the federal court to probate a will or administer an estate. Diversity jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1332 requires complete diversity of citizenship and an amount in controversy exceeding $75,000 — both requirements are met here. The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: the probate exception does not bar this claim, and diversity jurisdiction is proper.
