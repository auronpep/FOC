## 1. Final Question (chosen variation)

Stephen, a church music coordinator, learned that a vintage keyboard donated to Faithful Church could be removed through a side door left ajar by volunteers. He approached longtime choir member Martha and explained how she could enter the church after hours and carry the keyboard out. He did not tell her he was a church employee. Martha agreed to help him steal the keyboard and sell it. A church deacon overheard their conversation and reported it to police. Police arrested Stephen and Martha. Both were charged with conspiracy to commit theft. Before trial, Stephen claimed charitable immunity as a church officer and obtained dismissal of the charge against him. Martha then moved to dismiss the conspiracy charge against her. The jurisdiction has adopted the Model Penal Code version of conspiracy. Should the court grant Martha's motion to dismiss the conspiracy charge against her?

## 6. Right-Answer Explanation

**C is correct.** Under the Model Penal Code's unilateral approach to conspiracy, a defendant may be convicted of conspiracy even if every other alleged co-conspirator is acquitted, has a personal defense, or cannot be convicted for any reason. Stephen's charitable immunity is personal to him — it means he cannot be convicted, but it says nothing about whether Martha agreed to commit theft. She did. That agreement is the conspiracy. Her motion to dismiss was properly denied.

## 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is wrong.** This is the bilateral misconception re-skinned: the trap that "conspiracy requires two guilty participants." Under the MPC's unilateral approach, only the defendant's own guilt matters. Martha agreed to commit theft. That is enough. The fact that Stephen obtained dismissal based on a personal defense does not negate Martha's agreement or make her any less guilty of conspiracy.

**B is wrong.** The but-for causation argument reverses the analytical frame. Martha is not entitled to dismissal because Stephen's conduct caused the conspiracy — she independently agreed to commit theft. Martha's own agreement is what makes her guilty, not Stephen's role. Stephen's dismissal is irrelevant to Martha's personal liability.

**D is wrong.** Martha's awareness of Stephen's status as a church employee is legally irrelevant under the MPC. Conspiracy liability under the MPC is unilateral: what matters is whether Martha herself agreed to commit theft, not what she knew about Stephen's circumstances or defenses. Martha agreed to help steal the keyboard. That is the conspiracy.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Governing authority:** Model Penal Code § 5.01(1) (conspiracy defined as agreement with another to commit a crime); see also Wayne R. LaFave, Substantive Criminal Law §§ 9.1–9.3 (describing unilateral vs. bilateral approaches).

Under the **MPC's unilateral approach**, adopted by the jurisdiction here, a person who agrees to commit a crime is guilty of conspiracy even if the other parties cannot be convicted. This is the majority view among MPC jurisdictions. The key distinction is that the personal defense of one co-conspirator — immunity, incapacity, statutory exemption — is specific to that person and does not infect the agreement itself or the other conspirator's guilt.

The **common law bilateral rule** (minority) required that both parties be capable of conviction for a conspiracy to exist. But the MPC abandoned this — precisely because the personal characteristics or defenses of one party should not shield another from liability for the agreement they independently made.

In this item: Stephen obtained dismissal based on charitable immunity (a personal defense). Martha's liability turns on her own agreement, which is undisputed. The agreement — not Stephen's conviction — is what constitutes the conspiracy. The answer is **A** (post-shuffle **C**).
