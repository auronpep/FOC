**Final question**

An affidavit states that nine months ago an informant saw stolen laptop computers in a church fellowship hall storage closet. The affidavit gives no facts suggesting the laptops remained there, no ongoing theft operation, and no recent corroboration. A magistrate issues a warrant, and officers find laptops in the storage closet.

What is the best challenge to the warrant?

**Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)**

D is correct. Probable cause must support the search when the warrant issues. Information supporting a warrant can become stale when too much time passes without facts showing ongoing criminal activity or continued presence of evidence. Here, the affidavit describes a single nine-month-old observation of stolen laptops — highly movable items — with no facts suggesting the laptops remained in the storage closet, no ongoing theft operation, and no recent corroboration. The fair probability that evidence remains at the location has not been established as of the time the warrant issued. The staleness challenge is the strongest available.

Silver Key: When evaluating a warrant challenge, check the timeline. If the items are movable and the affidavit lacks facts showing ongoing criminal activity or recent corroboration, staleness is the strongest challenge — independent of informant credibility or magistrate deference.

**Full wrong-answer explanations**

A (EAR_OVERCLAIM tiered_absolute — "probable cause, once established, lasts forever" — DOMINANT TRAP): This is the most attractive wrong answer because it sounds like a clean rule: if probable cause was established, it's established. The breaker is the overclaim. Probable cause must be current when the warrant issues; it can become stale when too much time passes without facts showing ongoing criminal activity or continued presence of evidence. The "lasts forever" framing sells the idea that time doesn't matter — but for movable items like laptops, time matters a great deal. (Silver Key: check the timeline — how old is the information?)

B (EAR_DISTORTION half_truth — "warrant is valid because a magistrate signed it"): This choice is attractive because magistrate deference is a real and important principle. A magistrate's decision that probable cause exists is given great weight. The breaker is that magistrate deference does not cure staleness. A signed warrant must still be supported by nonstale probable cause. The magistrate's signature does not create missing facts. (Silver Key: staleness is a standalone challenge — it doesn't depend on what the magistrate knew or decided.)

C (ISSUE_SENSE bait_doctrine — "invalid only if the informant was lying"): This choice is attractive because informant credibility is a real and well-known warrant-challenge doctrine (Franks v. Delaware: a warrant can be invalid if the affidavit contains knowing or reckless falsehoods). The breaker is that this is the neighboring doctrine, not the right one. The issue here is staleness, not credibility. The affidavit may be entirely truthful — the informant really did see laptops nine months ago — and the warrant can still be invalid because the information is too old to establish current probable cause. (Silver Key: staleness is independent of credibility.)

**Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer**

Tested rule: Under the Fourth Amendment, a warrant must be supported by probable cause at the time it issues. Probable cause means a fair probability that evidence of a crime will be found in a particular place. Information supporting probable cause can become stale when too much time passes between the observation described in the affidavit and the warrant's issuance, particularly when (1) the items sought are movable, (2) the affidavit contains no facts showing ongoing criminal activity, and (3) there is no recent corroboration. When staleness is established, the warrant is invalid even if a magistrate signed it, and even if the underlying information was truthful.
