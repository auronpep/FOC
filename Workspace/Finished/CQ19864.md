### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation
Murder at common law is defined by "malice aforethought." Malice does not only mean a specific intent to kill (express malice). It can also be established through three other "malice routes": (1) intent to inflict serious bodily injury (grievous bodily harm); (2) depraved-heart conduct (extreme indifference to human life); or (3) felony murder. Here, Silas's repeated blows with a heavy staff and his statement about ensuring Malchus "never walks upright" demonstrate a clear intent to inflict serious bodily injury. Because Malchus died as a result of those intended injuries, Silas is liable for murder, regardless of his disavowal of a specific intent to kill.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations
- **A:** This choice is attractive because Silas was clearly enraged by the destruction of his property. However, for a murder to be reduced to voluntary manslaughter, the provocation must be legally adequate. Common law and most modern jurisdictions hold that property damage or trespassing is not adequate provocation to justify a lethal response or a response intended to cause serious bodily harm.
- **C:** This is the dominant trap. It appeals to students who believe that murder *requires* a specific intent to kill. Silas's statement ("I do not seek your life") seems to negate express malice. However, involuntary manslaughter is reserved for deaths caused by criminal negligence or during the commission of a misdemeanor. Because Silas possessed the malice of "intent to inflict serious bodily injury," the crime is murder, not manslaughter.
- **D:** This choice relies on the false idea that a defendant is only liable for the exact level of harm they intended. In homicide law, if a defendant acts with malice (such as intent to inflict SBI) and that act causes death, they are liable for the death even if it was not their primary objective.

### 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification
**Authority:** *Commonwealth v. Dorazio*, 365 Pa. 291 (1950); 4 Blackstone, Commentaries *198-201.
Common-law murder is established by showing any one of the four types of malice. Intent to inflict grievous bodily harm is the second malice route. A defendant who intends to seriously injure a victim and causes their death is guilty of murder. Property damage is not legally adequate provocation to reduce murder to voluntary manslaughter. The transform preserves the rule (SBI intent = murder), the issue (disclaimer of intent to kill), and the credited outcome.
