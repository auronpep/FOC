Martha asks Peter, an undercover officer, to break into a private publishing house after closing and steal a framed Psalms manuscript she believes is locked in the archive room. ### 7. Full right-answer explanation
Martha is guilty because solicitation is complete when she intentionally asks another person to commit a crime she believes will be criminal. The manuscript's removal and Peter's undercover status do not erase the request.

Gold Key: The solicitor's belief controls. If the requested conduct would be criminal under the facts as Martha believed them to be, the solicitation is complete even if the real facts make success impossible.

Silver Key: Cut the answer that focuses on the missing object first. The question is about Martha's intentional request, not whether the theft could actually have been carried out.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
**A.** This choice is attractive because it follows the missing-object fact and turns it into a defense. The breaker is that solicitation does not depend on the target's actual location or on the target's willingness; the request itself is enough when Martha believed she was arranging a burglary. Gold Key defeats the trap.

**B.** This choice is attractive because it treats the wrong room location as if it defeats liability. The breaker is that factual impossibility does not undo solicitation when the defendant intentionally asks for conduct she believes would be criminal. Silver Key cuts this first.

**D.** This choice is attractive because it assumes burglary must be completed before solicitation exists. The breaker is that solicitation is complete upon the intentional request; Peter never has to enter the archive room for Martha to be guilty.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning
Texas Penal Code § 15.03(a) states the solicitation rule in belief-based terms: a defendant commits solicitation when, with the required intent, she requests or attempts to induce another to engage in conduct that, under the circumstances as the defendant believes them to be, would constitute the felony. Bien v. State, 530 S.W.3d 177 (Tex. Crim. App. 2018), applies that belief-based framing and explains that solicitation turns on what the defendant believes the circumstances are, not on whether the real-world circumstances match that belief.

That fits this item. Martha intentionally asked Peter to break in and steal what she believed was a manuscript in the archive room. The fact that the manuscript had already been moved to a police evidence locker means the requested burglary was factually impossible, but that does not defeat completed solicitation. Peter's undercover status and hidden lack of criminal intent also do not matter. The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source question.

Sources: [Texas Penal Code § 15.03](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/GetStatute.aspx?Code=PE&Value=15.03) · [Bien v. Texas](https://law.justia.com/cases/texas/court-of-criminal-appeals/2018/pd-0365-16.html)
