# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

A youth pastor at a large church organized a choir tour for the high-school youth group to a regional Christian music festival. He told the attorney he had retained that during the return leg the tour bus had been involved in a serious rollover accident caused by the bus company's negligent driver. He claimed he suffered a broken back, was hospitalized for weeks, spent months in painful rehabilitation, and as a result missed several months of salary and was unable to continue leading the youth ministry. The attorney, who had a good-faith belief that the youth pastor was telling the truth, filed a $3.5 million complaint against the tour bus company for negligence without obtaining the police report, the trip coordinator's incident log, any medical records, or statements from other participants on the tour.

During discovery the bus company produced the official police and insurance reports. They showed that the youth pastor's assistant director (who was also his brother-in-law) had been driving the bus on that leg of the trip and had suffered only minor bruises. The youth pastor had not been on the bus at all—he had stayed behind at the church to handle a family emergency. The records further showed that the youth pastor had never been hospitalized, had continued preaching and leading the youth group without interruption, and had received his full salary during the period he claimed to be disabled. The bus company ultimately prevailed at trial but incurred substantial attorney's fees defending the action.

Can the bus company recover its defense attorney's fees from the youth pastor or his attorney?

A: No, because the bus company won the case on the merits.

B: Yes, because the youth pastor and his attorney acted together to present a knowingly false claim.

C: No, because the fees the bus company paid its lawyers are governed by its retainer agreement with them.

D: Yes, because the attorney filed the complaint without conducting a reasonable inquiry into whether the youth pastor had actually been injured.

## 6. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The credited answer is A.

Under Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(b)(3), by presenting a pleading to the court an attorney certifies that to the best of the person's knowledge, information, and belief, formed after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances, the factual contentions have evidentiary support (or will likely have after further investigation). The certification is objective; a good-faith subjective belief in the client's story is not enough if no reasonable inquiry was performed. Here the attorney filed a $3.5 million complaint alleging the youth pastor personally suffered a broken back, hospitalization, and months of disability without obtaining the police report, medical records, trip logs, or any participant statements. Discovery revealed the youth pastor was not even on the bus, had no injuries, and had continued full ministry duties and salary. 

**Gold Key:** Rule 11 requires an objective reasonable inquiry into the facts before filing; good-faith reliance on the client's word alone does not satisfy the certification when the facts are readily verifiable by public records or simple investigation.

Because the certification was violated, the bus company (as prevailing party) may move for sanctions under Rule 11(c). After the 21-day safe-harbor period, the court may award reasonable expenses and attorney's fees directly resulting from the violation. The fact that the bus company ultimately won on the merits does not immunize the violation or bar the fee award. Sanctions may run against the attorney, the party, or both.

Silver Key (navigation): When the call asks whether fees are recoverable from client or attorney after the opponent "won the case," do not stop at the outcome of the underlying claim; locate the certification duty and whether a reasonable inquiry occurred.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

A (dominant trap): This choice sells the attractive but incorrect idea that "if we win on the merits, the other side cannot recover anything from us, including fees caused by our pleading." The breaker is that Rule 11 sanctions are independent of the merits outcome. A violation of the certification duty can support an award of fees to the prevailing party even when that party defeats the claim at trial. (Gold Key: reasonable inquiry is required regardless of who ultimately prevails.)

B: This choice sells the misconception that sanctions require proof of a knowing conspiracy or bad-faith fraud between lawyer and client. The breaker is that the stem expressly states the attorney had a good-faith belief in the client's account. Rule 11 imposes an objective reasonable-inquiry standard, not a subjective fraud or conspiracy test. No evidence of bad faith or conspiracy is needed (or present) for sanctions when the inquiry duty was violated.

C: This choice sells the idea that the only relevant fee relationship is the contract between the bus company and the lawyers it hired to defend the case. The breaker is that Rule 11(c) expressly authorizes the court to order the violator (attorney, party, or both) to pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney's fees, incurred by the movant because of the violation. The company's own retainer agreement with its defense counsel is irrelevant to whether the plaintiff side violated its certification duty.

## 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested black-letter rule is Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(b)(3) and 11(c): an attorney presenting a pleading certifies that after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances the factual contentions have evidentiary support, and violation of that certification may result in sanctions, including an order directing payment to the movant of part or all of the reasonable attorney's fees and other expenses directly resulting from the violation. The rule applies to the attorney, the represented party, or both. The standard is objective reasonableness under the circumstances at the time of filing, not the signer's subjective good faith. The 21-day safe-harbor provision and the limitation that monetary sanctions on the court's initiative have additional procedural prerequisites do not change the core certification duty or the availability of a fee award on a party's motion.

The legal issue is whether sanctions (specifically defense fees) are available against the signing attorney or the client when the pleading contained factual contentions that lacked evidentiary support because no reasonable inquiry was made, even though the defendant ultimately prevailed on the merits and even though the attorney subjectively believed the client.

The credited outcome is unchanged from the original: the company can recover the fees because the attorney violated the Rule 11 certification by filing without any corroborating investigation. Research grounding (Cornell LII FRCP 11 text and advisory committee notes): the 1993 amendment emphasized the affirmative duty of pre-filing inquiry and made explicit that sanctions may include fees to the movant. No subsequent amendment has altered the certification language or the availability of fees on motion for a violation of (b)(3). Good-faith belief is repeatedly distinguished from the required reasonable inquiry. Prevailing on the merits does not erase the violation. Authority floor satisfied: primary rule text (Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(b)(3), (c)(1), (c)(2), (c)(4)).

No key disagreement. The original credited answer D is preserved in substance under the new letter A.
