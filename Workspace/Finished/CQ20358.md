## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** Article III extends the federal judicial power to cases arising under the Constitution, laws, and treaties of the United States. In all cases outside the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction, the Court has appellate jurisdiction "with such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the Congress shall make." The Camp Vendor Receipts Act is a federal law, so a state-court decision interpreting that Act presents a federal-law case. Congress may regulate the route by which the Supreme Court exercises appellate jurisdiction over that kind of case.

**Gold Key:** Article III supplies the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction in federal-law cases, and Congress controls the exceptions and regulations for exercising it.

**Silver Key:** When the call challenges an appeal route, do not answer with the power that made the underlying statute valid. Match the choice to the jurisdictional route being attacked.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap):** This choice is attractive because it sounds like a careful Article III limit, especially for students who remember that current state-court review usually runs through the highest state court in which a decision could be had. The breaker is that the call asks whether Congress may create a direct appellate route for federal-law cases; Article III gives appellate jurisdiction subject to congressional regulations, and it does not make state-court review categorically unconstitutional. **Gold Key:** Article III plus congressional regulation is the unlock that defeats the "Article III bars it" trap.

**B:** This choice sells a state-sovereignty instinct: state courts normally control their own appellate structure, so a direct federal appeal feels intrusive. The breaker is that the case involves interpretation of a federal statute, and the Supreme Court's appellate review of federal-law questions from state courts is part of the federal judicial power. State sovereignty does not defeat that federal appellate route.

**C:** This choice grabs a true but nonresponsive power. ## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** Article III, Section 2 extends the judicial power to cases arising under the laws of the United States and gives the Supreme Court appellate jurisdiction in all non-original-jurisdiction cases, "with such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the Congress shall make." Constitution Annotated explains that the Exceptions and Regulations language gives Congress significant control over the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction and proceedings. It also confirms that the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction includes review of state-court decisions on federal-law questions. Martin v. Hunter's Lessee, 14 U.S. (1 Wheat.) 304 (1816), upheld Supreme Court appellate review of state-court decisions involving federal law.

**Application:** The Act creates a federal-law claim. A lower state-court decision interpreting the Act is a decision on federal law. The provision being tested regulates the route for Supreme Court appellate review; it does not expand the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction. Because Congress may prescribe regulations for the exercise of the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction in federal-law cases, the direct-appeal provision is constitutional.

**Current-law note:** Current 28 U.S.C. § 1257 generally routes Supreme Court review of state-court judgments through "the highest court of a State in which a decision could be had," and Constitution Annotated notes that this can include a lower state court when its judgment is final and not reviewable by another state appellate court. That current statutory route does not contradict the supplied key; the tested point is Congress's constitutional power to prescribe the appellate route for federal-law cases.
