### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Lydia's statement sounds alarming, but it is not a clear refusal to perform. She says she will not buy unless the contract requires her to do it, which leaves open performance if the contract is binding. The closing date has not arrived either, so there is no present nonperformance.

**Gold Key:** Anticipatory repudiation requires a clear statement or act showing that the party will commit a total breach when performance is due. Doubt, regret, market anxiety, or conditional reluctance is not enough.

**Silver Key:** Separate "I am worried she may not perform" from "she has breached now." Insecurity can point to an assurance path; it does not automatically create an immediate breach lawsuit.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because Lydia's words would make Timothy reasonably nervous about the closing. The breaker is the remedy jump: insecurity alone does not equal breach, and the stem gives no demand for assurance followed by a failure to provide it.

**C.** This choice reaches the right winner, so it feels safe. The breaker is the categorical reason. A seller is not barred from suing before closing merely because the land is one parcel; if Lydia had clearly repudiated, Timothy could treat that repudiation as a breach before the closing date.

**D.** This is the dominant trap. It grabs the strong buyer-reluctance language and calls it a total breach. **Gold Key:** the statement must be clear enough to show future nonperformance; Lydia's "unless the contract requires me" language is conditional, not a definite refusal.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is unchanged. The source row tests whether a pre-performance statement is sufficiently definite to be anticipatory repudiation. The credited answer is that the seller loses because the buyer's statement is neither repudiation nor present breach.

Restatement (Second) of Contracts sec. 250 defines repudiation as a statement or voluntary act indicating that the obligor will commit a breach that would itself support a claim for total breach. The key point is definiteness: the statement must communicate future nonperformance, not merely uneasiness. Restatement (Second) of Contracts sec. 251 separately addresses insecurity. Reasonable grounds to believe an obligor may breach can support a demand for adequate assurance; only a failure to provide adequate assurance within a reasonable time can then be treated as repudiation. [1] [2]

That authority supports the supplied key. Lydia did not say, "I will not close," or make performance impossible. She said she would not buy unless the contract required it. Timothy may have grounds for concern, but he has not alleged a missed closing date, an unequivocal refusal, or a failed assurance demand. The July 20 suit is premature.
