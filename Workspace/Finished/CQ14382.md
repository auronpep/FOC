## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

Peter’s Pilgrim Supply, a private regional distributor that sells Christian craft goods to bookstores and camp stores, signed a written agreement with Lydia Lantern Works. Under the agreement, Peter’s would distribute Lydia’s “Bethlehem Star” devotional lantern kits for four years throughout an eleven-town river district. The agreement required Peter’s to “promote in good faith the sale of Bethlehem Star lantern kits” in that district, but it said nothing about whether Peter’s could distribute other devotional lantern kits.

Nine months later, Peter’s agreed with Barnabas Bright Crafts to distribute Barnabas’s battery-free devotional lantern kits in the same eleven-town district.

If Lydia Lantern Works sues Peter’s Pilgrim Supply for breach of their distribution contract, which of the following facts, if established, would most strengthen Lydia’s case?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A written goods-distribution agreement requires good-faith promotion but is silent on competing lines; which fact best strengthens the maker’s breach claim when the distributor later carries a competing line?

## 3. Final answer choices

A. Since Peter’s began distributing Barnabas lantern kits, sales of Bethlehem Star lantern kits have fallen 4% in the eleven-town district.

B. For many years in the private Christian craft-kit industry, it has been a uniform practice for regional distributors to handle only one brand of devotional lantern kit.

C. Barnabas Bright Crafts’ national advertising campaign disparages Bethlehem Star lantern kits by saying, “Faith does not need batteries, and neither should your lantern.”

D. Before the written agreement was signed, a representative of Peter’s told Lydia Lantern Works that the arrangement would be “exclusive.”

## 5. Correct answer

**B** — original key **D**.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** The agreement is silent about whether Peter’s may carry competing devotional lantern kits. The strongest fact is an objective industry practice showing that distributors in this trade uniformly handle only one brand of devotional lantern kit. **Gold Key:** under the UCC, trade usage can explain, supplement, or qualify a written goods agreement when it can reasonably fit with the writing. **Silver Key:** when the finalist choices try to fill a silent term, sort by source. Objective trade practice is the responsive source; a prior spoken label is the trap source.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** The sales-drop choice sells a business-effect theory: once Peter’s carried Barnabas kits, Bethlehem Star sales went down. That fact may show market effect, but the call is breach of the distribution agreement, and the missing link is the contract restriction or recognized source for reading the silence. A 4% drop does not supply that missing term.

**C.** The advertisement choice sells a wrong-actor theory: Barnabas is attacking Lydia’s product. The problem is that Barnabas’s slogan is Barnabas’s conduct, not Peter’s promise. Unless Peter’s adopted or made the disparagement, the ad does not show that Peter’s breached its own good-faith promotion duty or that the contract barred competing lines.

**D.** The oral-exclusivity choice is the dominant trap because it uses the exact word the plaintiff wants: “exclusive.” **Gold Key:** a prior oral exclusivity statement is not the same kind of source as trade usage. If the written agreement is the final expression, a prior or contemporaneous oral agreement cannot contradict it; by contrast, usage of trade is a UCC-recognized way to explain or supplement a written goods agreement.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are preserved from Q14382: a written goods-distribution agreement is silent on exclusivity, the plaintiff needs the strongest fact supporting a breach theory, and the credited answer is the trade-usage fact rather than the prior oral “exclusive” statement. The source row’s official explanation states that trade usage regularly observed in the relevant trade may be introduced to interpret the agreement when reasonably consistent with the express terms. 

Lawyer-confirmation authority: UCC § 1-303(c) defines usage of trade as a practice with such regularity of observance in a trade as to justify an expectation that it will be observed; § 1-303(d) makes usage of trade relevant to ascertaining, supplementing, or qualifying the agreement; § 1-303(e) instructs that express terms and usage should be construed as consistent whenever reasonable. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) UCC § 2-202 provides that a final written expression may not be contradicted by prior or contemporaneous oral agreements, but may be explained or supplemented by course of dealing, usage of trade, or course of performance. ([Legal Information Institute][2]) The selected outline code is verified as `52040300`, “Gap-Fillers, Interpretation, and the Parol Evidence Rule > Parol Evidence Rule.”
