### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**Silver Key: Name the two independent crimes.** When a fact pattern shows (1) an agreement between two or more people to commit a crime, and (2) one of them then carries it out with the other's intentional help, the analysis runs on two separate tracks — conspiracy and the substantive crime. They do not cancel each other out.

Ruth agreed to the robbery plan with Naomi — that agreement, plus the specific intent to carry it out, formed the crime of conspiracy the moment they had a meeting of minds on the plan. Conspiracy is a separate offense from the crime the conspirators agreed to commit; it does not merge into the completed crime. *See* Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640 (1946) (conspirator liable for substantive offenses of co-conspirators); MBE rule — conspiracy does not merge with the completed offense.

Separately, Ruth intentionally aided Naomi's robbery by serving as the getaway driver. Under accomplice liability, one who intentionally facilitates the commission of a crime with the intent that the principal commit it is guilty as an accessory. Robbery is larceny by force or threat of force; Naomi used a knife to take the cash register contents from the cashier. Ruth's role as driver satisfies the actus reus of aiding, and her agreement to the plan satisfies the dual intent required — she intended to assist Naomi and intended that the robbery succeed. The fact that she panicked and drove away before Naomi reached the car does not undo her liability: the robbery was already complete the moment Naomi took the property by threat of force, and her assistance had already been rendered.

**Gold Key: Conspiracy does not merge with the completed offense.** Unlike attempt and solicitation, which merge into the completed crime, conspiracy stands independently. A defendant can be convicted of both the conspiracy and the substantive crime without violating double jeopardy. The moment the agreement is formed with specific intent, conspiracy is complete; the later commission of the target crime is a second, separate wrong.

Ruth is therefore guilty of both conspiracy and robbery. **Answer B is correct.**

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. conspiracy only.**

*Why it's attractive:* Ruth never walked into the market. She never pointed a knife at anyone. She never touched the money. It feels natural to say she "only" agreed to something — that the agreement is the only crime she committed.

*The breaker:* This choice ignores accomplice liability. Ruth didn't merely agree and do nothing — she intentionally facilitated the robbery by driving Naomi to the scene and waiting as the getaway driver. Under the doctrine of aiding and abetting, one who intentionally assists the principal with the intent that the crime succeed is guilty of the substantive offense as an accessory. Conspiracy and the completed crime are separate offenses; being guilty of one does not erase liability for the other.

**C. robbery only.**

*Why it's attractive:* Ruth's most visible act was driving the car — the physical assistance during the robbery itself. A student may reason that the "real" crime is the robbery and the agreement is just backstory.

*The breaker:* This choice erases the independent crime of conspiracy. The agreement between Naomi and Ruth to commit the robbery, made with specific intent, was itself a completed offense the moment they reached it. Conspiracy does not merge into the completed crime. The agreement and the commission are two distinct wrongs.

**D. either conspiracy or robbery, but not both.**

*Why it's attractive (DOMINANT TRAP):* This is the most seductive answer on the page. It appeals to a powerful intuition — the idea that punishing someone for both the "plan" and the "act" is double-counting, that conspiracy and the completed crime are somehow "the same thing." Many students sense a fairness principle that says you can't be convicted twice for a single course of conduct.

*The breaker:* The law explicitly rejects this. Conspiracy and the substantive offense are separate crimes with distinct elements. Conspiracy punishes the agreement; the completed crime punishes the execution. Double jeopardy does not bar conviction of both. This is the exact merger rule the MBE tests: unlike attempt and solicitation, conspiracy does NOT merge with the completed offense. **Gold Key: Conspiracy does not merge with the completed offense.** The either/or framing is a trap — the law says "both."

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Conspiracy:** A conspiracy is an agreement between two or more persons to commit a crime, accompanied by the specific intent that the crime be committed. *See* Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640 (1946); Model Penal Code § 5.03. The crime of conspiracy is complete upon the agreement with the requisite specific intent — no overt act is required at common law (though many statutes require one). Critically, conspiracy does **not** merge with the completed offense. A defendant may be convicted of both the conspiracy and the substantive crime without violating double jeopardy. *See* United States v. Felix, 503 U.S. 378 (1992); MBE Criminal Law — "conspiracy does not merge with the completed offense."

**Accomplice Liability (Aiding and Abetting):** One who intentionally aids, abets, or facilitates the commission of a crime with the intent that the principal commit the crime is guilty as an accessory and is punishable as a principal. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 2 (federal aiding and abetting); Model Penal Code § 2.06. The accomplice must have dual intent: (1) intent to assist the principal, and (2) intent that the substantive offense be committed. Here, Ruth drove Naomi to the market, waited with the engine running, and agreed to the plan — satisfying both the actus reus and mens rea of accomplice liability.

**Robbery:** Robbery is larceny plus the use of force or threat of force to take property from a person. *See* common law definition; Model Penal Code § 222.1. Naomi used a knife (threat of force) to compel the cashier to surrender the register contents — this is robbery.
