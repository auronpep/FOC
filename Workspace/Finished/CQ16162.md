### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** Timothy should be convicted of both arson and murder. Common-law arson is the malicious burning of the dwelling of another. The converted building contained Mary's upstairs apartment, so it was a dwelling, and it belonged to Mary, not Timothy. Timothy did not need to want the building to burn. **Gold Key:** for common-law arson, malice is satisfied when the defendant acts with reckless disregard of an obvious risk that another's dwelling will burn. Deliberately shearing the valve off a propane tank beside a building supplied by kitchen pilot flames is enough for that arson malice.

The murder charge then follows through felony murder. Arson is a dangerous predicate felony, and Mary died during and as a direct result of the arson. **Gold Key:** if a death is caused during the commission of arson, the killing is murder even without an intent to kill. **Silver Key:** read the answer array as an offense stack. Once arson stays in the stack, the death does not stop at manslaughter.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

The breaker is felony murder: when the reckless burning is common-law arson and the resident dies from that arson, the homicide is murder. **Gold Key:** arson is a felony-murder predicate; no separate intent to kill is required.

**B. acquitted of both offenses.**  
This choice is attractive only if the student treats the explosion as bad luck after property damage. **D. convicted of involuntary manslaughter and acquitted of arson.**  
This choice sells the common misconception that arson requires a purpose to burn the building. The breaker is the arson malice rule. **Gold Key:** common-law arson can rest on reckless disregard of an obvious risk that another's dwelling will burn, so Timothy's lack of a desire to burn the shop/apartment does not acquit him of arson.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, trap structure, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. The original asks whether a defendant who intentionally damages a gasoline pump, causing an explosion that burns a building containing another's dwelling and kills the resident, is guilty of both common-law arson and felony murder. This variation asks the same legal question with a propane regulator at a bookshop/tea room that contains Mary's upstairs apartment.

Common-law arson is the malicious burning of the dwelling of another. Primary-law support: **In re Bramble, 31 Cal. 2d 43, 48 (1947)** describes common-law arson as the willful and malicious burning of another's dwelling house. Cornell Wex states the same common-law formulation. **In re Stonewall F., 208 Cal. App. 3d 1054, 1062 n.7 (1989)** discusses the common-law and treatise view that arson malice can include conduct done under circumstances showing an obvious, strong likelihood that another's dwelling will burn. See https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/re-bramble-23861, https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/208/1054.html, and https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/arson.

Felony murder applies when a killing occurs during the commission of an inherently dangerous felony. Arson is a classic felony-murder predicate. **Lawson v. State, 64 S.W.3d 396, 397-98 (Tex. Crim. App. 2001) (Cochran, J., concurring)** gives the common-law statement that an arsonist may be guilty of murder when a person dies in the burning building even without an intent to injure. Cornell Wex summarizes felony murder as allowing murder liability when a felony unintentionally results in a killing and notes the inherently dangerous felony limitation. Justia likewise lists arson among the typical inherently dangerous felonies for felony murder. See https://law.justia.com/cases/texas/court-of-criminal-appeals/2001/1767-00-4.html, https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/felony_murder_doctrine, and https://www.justia.com/criminal/offenses/homicide/felony-murder/.

Applied here, Timothy maliciously burned another's dwelling because he deliberately damaged a propane valve in circumstances carrying an obvious fire/explosion risk, the building burned, and the building contained Mary's apartment. Mary died as the direct result of that arson. The correct legal outcome is conviction for both arson and murder.
