### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (C)

**Gold Key:** Under UCC § 2-306(1), a requirements contract is valid only if the buyer is bound to purchase its actual requirements from the seller. A promise to buy "as many as I decide to order" gives the buyer unfettered discretion — the buyer can order zero, order from a competitor, or order a million. Because the buyer never committed to any quantity, the buyer's promise is illusory. Without mutuality of consideration, there is no enforceable contract. Daniel's obligation to supply goats was real; Peter's obligation to buy was not. The agreement fails for lack of consideration, and Daniel cannot breach a contract that never existed.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (In favor of Peter, because this is a valid requirements contract) — DOMINANT TRAP**

This is attractive because the agreement looks like a requirements contract on its face: a seller agrees to supply goods at a set price, and a buyer's quantity is measured by what the buyer "decides to order." Students who recognize the UCC requirements-contract pattern without scrutinizing the buyer's commitment will jump to this answer. **The Gold Key kills this trap:** a valid requirements contract under UCC § 2-306(1) requires the buyer to be bound to purchase its actual requirements. Here, Peter only promised to buy "as many as he decides to decide to order" — language that gives him total discretion to buy nothing at all. Because Peter's promise is illusory, there is no consideration, and the agreement is unenforceable. The requirements-contract framework never gets off the ground.

**B (In favor of Peter, because of his detrimental reliance)**

This is attractive because Peter took a concrete step — he advertised the dinner to the congregation — that looks like reliance. Students who see a party changing position in response to an agreement may default to promissory reliance. The breaker: Peter only said he "already advertised" the dinner. The facts do not show that Peter decided to host the advertisement *because of* the agreement with Daniel, or that he incurred costs he wouldn't have otherwise incurred. The reliance is speculative — the dinner was already being planned, and Peter's statement that he "could only pull it off with premium goats" is forward-looking, not evidence of a past change of position. Detrimental reliance requires actual, foreseeable reliance, not mere planning.

**D (In favor of Daniel, under the doctrine of impossibility)**

This is attractive because the triggering event — an illness outbreak among Daniel's breeding herd — sounds like an unforeseeable event that made performance difficult. Students who see a dramatic external event may reflexively reach for impossibility. The breaker: impossibility requires that performance be truly impossible, not merely more expensive. Daniel could still supply premium goats; it would just cost him $13 per pound instead of $8. A sharp increase in cost, even a dramatic one, does not rise to the level of impossibility. The doctrine of impossibility applies when the subject matter is destroyed or the means of performance have ceased to exist — not when the seller's expenses have gone up.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** A promise is illusory when the promisor retains unfettered discretion whether to perform. An illusory promise does not constitute valid consideration, and an agreement lacking mutuality of consideration is unenforceable. *See* LII Wex, "Illusory Promise" (Cornell Law School) ("An illusory promise is a promise that is unenforceable due to indefiniteness or lack of mutuality, where only one side is bound to perform.").

**UCC § 2-306(1):** A term measuring quantity by "the requirements of the buyer" means "such actual output or requirements as may occur in good faith." The Official Comments and case law confirm that a requirements contract is enforceable only when the buyer has bound itself to deal exclusively or to purchase its actual needs. Where the buyer's promise is so indefinite that the buyer has no obligation at all, the agreement is not a requirements contract — it is an illusory promise. *See* UCC § 2-306(1); *Simcala, Inc. v. American Coal Trade*, 1987.

**Application:** The agreement states Daniel will provide "as many premium roaking goats at $8 per pound as Peter decides to order." Peter's promise is entirely discretionary — he can order zero goats or order from someone else. This is the classic illusory-promise formulation: "I will buy as much as I feel like buying." Because Peter gave no binding obligation, there is no consideration for Daniel's promise. The agreement is unenforceable, and Daniel cannot breach a non-existent contract.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-306(1); LII Wex, "Illusory Promise," Cornell Law School, https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/illusory_promise.
