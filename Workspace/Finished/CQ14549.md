# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question

Ruth owned a private event barn where she hosted hymn nights and small Christian retreats. Stephen, a specialist in lifting and setting heavy bell frames on high rooftops, contracted in a signed writing to lift and set a custom bronze bell frame atop Ruth's barn. An exculpatory clause in the contract provided that Stephen would not be liable for any physical damage to Ruth's barn occurring during installation of the bell frame. The contract also contained a per-day damages clause if Stephen did not complete performance by a specified date, and it stated that time was of the essence. Another clause provided that any later agreement for extra work under the contract had to be in writing and signed by both parties.

Well before Stephen needed to begin work, and with ample time remaining to complete performance, Stephen notified Ruth that he was selling his rigging business to Timothy, who was equally skilled in lifting and setting heavy bell frames on high rooftops. Timothy had agreed to take over the Stephen-Ruth contract. Assume that Ruth orally agreed with Stephen to accept Timothy's services, and that Timothy performed on time but negligently installed the wrong bell frame.

Will Ruth succeed in an action against Stephen for damages for breach of contract?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

If an obligee accepts an equally qualified delegate's performance but never releases the original promisor, can the obligee recover from the original promisor when the delegate breaches?

## 3. Final Answer Choices

A. No, because by accepting Timothy's substitution for Stephen, Ruth effected a novation that discharged Stephen's duties under the Stephen-Ruth contract.

B. Yes, because Ruth did not agree to release Stephen from liability under the Stephen-Ruth contract.

C. No, because the per-day damages clause in the Stephen-Ruth contract provided only for damages caused by delay in performance.

D. Yes, because Ruth received no consideration for accepting Timothy's services in Stephen's place.

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. Ruth agreed to accept Timothy's services, but the stem never says she agreed to release Stephen. A delegation can move the work to a delegate; it does not, by itself, erase the original promisor's liability. Gold Key: acceptance of a substitute performer is not the same thing as a release of the original promisor. Silver Key: in a two-Yes/two-No delegation array, first ask whether the original promisor was released; if no release appears, the answer stays in the Yes lane.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the most tempting move: Ruth accepted Timothy, and Timothy was equally skilled, so the student wants to call the substitution complete. The breaker is that novation requires more than acceptance of performance; it requires Ruth's assent to discharge Stephen, and that release fact is missing. Gold Key: no release means no novation.

C pulls attention to a real contract clause and a real fact: Timothy was not late. The clause only sets a per-day measure for delay; it does not say Ruth has no remedy for a different breach, such as installing the wrong bell frame.

D sounds like a formation answer because consideration often matters in contract changes. The breaker is that the tested event is a delegation accepted by the obligee, not a new substituted contract releasing Stephen; fresh consideration to Ruth is not required to keep the delegation effective.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged. The item tests delegation of duties and the difference between accepting a delegate's performance and releasing the delegating obligor by novation.

Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 318(3), delegation of performance and a delegate's assumption of the duty do not discharge the delegating obligor's duty or liability unless the obligee agrees otherwise. The same no-release principle is mirrored in UCC section 2-210(1), which states that delegation does not relieve the delegating party of duty or liability for breach. The common-law novation frame is confirmed by Restatement (Second) of Contracts sections 279 and 280: a substituted contract discharges the original duty, and a novation is a substituted contract adding a new party. Eagle Industries, Inc. v. Thompson quotes and applies those Restatement sections in that way. Research sources: Restatement section 318 summary at https://matthewminer.name/law/outlines/1L/2nd%2BSemester/LAW%2B506-002%2B%E2%80%93%2BContracts%2BII/R2C%2B%C2%A7%2B318; UCC section 2-210 at https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-210; Restatement sections 279 and 280 discussion in Eagle Industries at https://law.justia.com/cases/oregon/supreme-court/1995/321-or-398.html.

Applied here, Ruth's oral agreement to accept Timothy's services made Timothy an accepted delegate for performance. It did not say that Ruth released Stephen, accepted Timothy's promise in satisfaction of Stephen's existing duty, or substituted Timothy as the only obligor. Timothy's timely but negligent installation of the wrong bell frame is a breach of the delegated performance, and Stephen remains liable on the original contract. The per-day delay clause addresses delay damages only and does not preclude damages for the wrong installation. The extra-work writing clause is not triggered by accepting a delegate's services. No doctrinal-currency hotlist issue is implicated.
