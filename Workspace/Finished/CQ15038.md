B is attractive because the missing language in Martha's deed feels like the cleanest chain-of-title defect. The breaker is the Gold Key: an easement appurtenant is part of the benefited land package and passes with the dominant estate unless excluded, so the later deed did not have to restate it.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification AND Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer
The tested issue remains Real Property, Rights in Land, Nature and Type, and the verified outline code is `81020101`. An easement appurtenant is a servitude benefit tied to ownership of a dominant estate, while an easement in gross is personal or tied to a holder rather than to benefited land. Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes sections 1.2, 1.5, and 5.6 support the servitude/appurtenant framework and the nonseverability of appurtenant benefits. Courts state the MBE rule directly: once an easement appurtenant is established, it attaches to the dominant estate and passes with conveyances of that estate even when the later conveyance does not mention the easement. See `Miller v. Romanauski`, 2014-Ohio-1517, https://www.supremecourt.ohio.gov/rod/docs/pdf/8/2014/2014-ohio-1517.pdf; `Myers v. Spencer`, 318 Mich. 155 (1947), https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914cb24add7b04934800251. The in-gross distinction is also reflected in `Springob v. Farrar`, https://www.sccourts.org/media/opinions/HTMLFiles/COA/2949.htm, which contrasts a personal, nontransferable easement in gross with an appurtenant easement that passes with the dominant estate. Research did not contradict the supplied official key. The credited outcome is unchanged: Martha receives the easement because she received the benefited tract.
