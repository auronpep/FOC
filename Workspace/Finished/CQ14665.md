### 6. Full right-answer explanation

D is correct because Peter caused a death while driving recklessly, and neither asserted defense gets him out.

**Gold Key:** Voluntary intoxication does not erase recklessness. A defendant who would have appreciated the risk when sober is treated as reckless even if his drunkenness kept him from actually appreciating it.

**Gold Key:** In this trap, do not ask whether danger actually existed. Ask whether the defendant’s apprehension of danger was reasonable enough to support self-defense against a recklessness-based manslaughter charge.

**Silver Key:** C and D both convict Peter because he acted recklessly. The C/D fight is not “convict or acquit.” It is **actual danger** versus **reasonable apprehension**. D names the legally decisive axis.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A is wrong.** It gives Peter too much credit for an honest fear. Honest fear alone is not enough on this answer set. The missing word is **reasonable**. The answer is a half-truth.

**B is wrong.** Gold Key: Voluntary intoxication does not make recklessness disappear. This answer runs the intoxication rule in the wrong direction.

**C is wrong.** Silver Key: C picks the wrong fact inside the correct conviction result. The problem is not that Peter was “in fact” safe. A person can sometimes defend based on a reasonable mistaken belief. The defect here is that Peter’s apprehension was not reasonable.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The original official explanation identifies D as correct because manslaughter can be based on criminal negligence or recklessness, voluntary intoxication is not a defense to recklessness, and unreasonable self-defense does not defeat this manslaughter charge. 

The MPC grounding matches that explanation. MPC Article 210 defines criminal homicide to include reckless causation of another human being’s death, and MPC § 210.3 classifies reckless criminal homicide as manslaughter. ([Open Casebook][1]) MPC § 2.08 provides the intoxication rule used here: when recklessness is an offense element, self-induced intoxication does not excuse lack of risk-awareness if the actor would have perceived the risk when sober. ([Open Casebook][2]) MPC § 3.04 frames self-protection in terms of a belief that force is immediately necessary, subject to § 3.09 limits. ([Criminal Law Web][3]) Secondary MPC teaching materials state the § 3.09 point directly: if the defendant is reckless or negligent about the facts supporting a defense, and that culpability suffices for the charged offense, the justification is unavailable. ([Criminal Law][4])

**GK-CRIM-RECKLESS-INTOXICATION-01**
Voluntary intoxication does not erase recklessness. If the defendant would have perceived the risk when sober, the law treats him as reckless even if drunkenness kept him from seeing it.

**GK-CRIM-SELFDEFENSE-REASONABLE-APPREHENSION-01**
In a self-defense manslaughter trap, do not ask only whether the defendant honestly feared danger or whether danger actually existed. Ask whether the apprehension was reasonable enough to excuse the reckless killing.

## Silver Key(s)

**SK-CRIM-ACTUAL-DANGER-VS-REASONABLE-APPREHENSION-01**
When two answers both convict because the defendant acted recklessly, stop comparing outcomes. The fight is actual danger versus reasonable apprehension.
