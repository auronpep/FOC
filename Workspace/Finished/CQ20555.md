## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

A is correct. At common law, voluntary manslaughter requires adequate provocation — provocation that would cause a reasonable person to lose self-control. The traditional and still-controlling MBE rule is that words alone, no matter how shocking, inflammatory, or emotionally devastating, cannot satisfy the adequate-provocation element. Lydia's disclosure was entirely verbal. Peter's outrage, while understandable, does not change the legal analysis. Because no adequate provocation exists, Peter's killing of Lydia is murder.

> **Gold Key — The Words-Alone Wall:** At common law, words alone — no matter how inflammatory, insulting, or emotionally devastating — are categorically insufficient to constitute adequate provocation. Physical conduct (or its equivalent) is required.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B (new position B) — Voluntary manslaughter, because the news would inflame anyone's passions:**
This is the most attractive wrong answer and the dominant trap. It appeals to the gut-level intuition that hearing a trusted pastor has a secret gambling addiction would make any reasonable person erupt. The cognitive move is: "This would enrage anyone, so it must be adequate provocation." The breaker: the common-law rule is not about how much passion was felt or how justified the outrage was. The rule is about the *form* of the provocation: was it conduct, or was it words? Lydia spoke. Words alone do not qualify. Emotional devastation, no matter how intense, cannot convert verbal provocation into legally adequate provocation at common law.

**C (new position C) — Murder, because Peter premeditated by choosing to confront in person:**
This answer reaches the correct verdict (murder) but for the wrong reason. The cognitive move is: "He chose to go there; he must have planned something." The breaker: traveling to confront someone about a dispute — even an angry confrontation — is not evidence of premeditation toward killing. The facts say Peter had no weapon and no plan to harm her. This distractor tests whether you can identify *why* murder is the right answer. It is murder because of the words-alone rule, not because of premeditation.

**D (new position D) — Voluntary manslaughter, because immediate killing means no cooling off:**
This distractor isolates one element of the voluntary-manslaughter test (no cooling-off period) and treats it as sufficient. The cognitive move is: "He killed her right away, so it must be voluntary manslaughter." The breaker: voluntary manslaughter requires BOTH adequate provocation AND no cooling off. Peter had no cooling-off period, but he also had no legally adequate provocation, because words alone do not qualify. Element one fails, so the absence of element two cannot save the defense.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Governing Rule:** At common law, adequate provocation must be such that it would cause a reasonable person to lose self-control to the point of killing. The traditional rule — still controlling on MBE questions — is that words alone cannot constitute adequate provocation, regardless of emotional content.

**Authority:**
- **Cornell LII Wex — Provocation:** "Almost universally, the courts have rejected words alone as providing adequate provocation for a homicide unless accompanied by conduct indicating a present intention and ability to cause bodily harm." ([https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/provocation](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/provocation))
- **Model Penal Code contrast:** MPC § 210.3(1)(b) uses "extreme mental or emotional disturbance" and does permit words alone to support a manslaughter reduction. But the MBE tests the common-law rule, not the MPC. (*See* Matthew Miner Criminal Law Outline; [https://matthewminer.name/law/outlines/2L/1st%2BSemester/LAW%2B535-001%2B%E2%80%93%2BCriminal%2BLaw/Voluntary%2BManslaughter](https://matthewminer.name/law/outlines/2L/1st%2BSemester/LAW%2B535-001%2B%E2%80%93%2BCriminal%2BLaw/Voluntary%2BManslaughter))
- **Key cases:** *People v. Chevalier*, 544 N.E.2d 942 (Ill. 1989) (verbal admission of infidelity is not adequate provocation); *State v. Shane*, 590 N.E.2d 272 (Ohio 1993) (same).

Words-alone disclosures are notably absent from the adequate-provocation list. ([https://jdadvising.com/criminal-law-and-procedure-highly-tested-mbe-topics-charts-and-a-checklist](https://jdadvising.com/criminal-law-and-procedure-highly-tested-mbe-topics-charts-and-a-checklist))
