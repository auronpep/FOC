### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B** is the answer. The motion is for a new trial after conviction. The reason that matters is not the grand-jury omission, the prosecutor’s motive, or the bare fact that live cross-examination was lost. The reason is materiality: timely access to the perjury conviction would have created a reasonable probability of a different verdict.

**Gold Key:** Brady/Giglio materiality asks whether timely disclosure of favorable impeachment evidence creates a reasonable probability of a different trial result; prosecutorial bad faith is not required.

**Silver Key:** When the call asks why a new trial is granted after conviction, cut answers aimed at charging-stage fairness, prosecutor motive, or a lost impeachment tool unless they state the outcome-prejudice threshold.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** The grand-jury answer feels serious because it points to the first place the prosecutor withheld the conviction. The breaker is stage. The new-trial motion is about trial fairness after conviction, not whether the grand jury heard defense-favorable impeachment material. **Gold Key:** the grand-jury disclosure trap fails because the trial-disclosure rule protects the defendant’s ability to use material favorable evidence at trial.

**C.** The strategic-delay answer sounds like the strongest condemnation of the prosecutor. The breaker is the required reason for relief. The new-trial standard turns on materiality — a reasonable probability of a different result — even if the prosecutor’s intent looks bad.

**D.** The lost-cross-examination answer captures the most concrete harm and is the dominant trap. The breaker is that it stops one step too early. The governing reason is not merely that Daniel lost a live impeachment method; it is that timely disclosure would probably have mattered to the verdict.

### 8. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are preserved. The original and the variation both test the prosecutor’s delayed disclosure of impeachment evidence and the materiality threshold for a due-process new-trial claim.

Under **Brady v. Maryland**, suppression of favorable, material evidence violates due process regardless of prosecutorial good faith or bad faith. ([Justia Law][1]) **Giglio v. United States** applies that principle to impeachment evidence affecting the credibility of a government witness. ([Justia Law][2]) **United States v. Bagley** states the operative materiality test: evidence is material if there is a reasonable probability that, had it been disclosed, the result would have been different. ([Justia Law][3]) **United States v. Williams** confirms the grand-jury trap: a federal prosecutor is not constitutionally required to present defense-favorable evidence to the grand jury. ([Justia Law][4])
