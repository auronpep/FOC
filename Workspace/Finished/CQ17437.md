# CQ17437 — Barnabas's Bouldering Wall

## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question (revised stem + call)

Barnabas built a bouldering wall — a low climbing wall — on the back of his
property, which, as he knows, is commonly climbed by neighbor children as well
as his own. The wall is in good condition, is not defective in any way, and is
normally surrounded by thick foam crash mats to cushion a climber who lets go or
slips off. Before leaving with his family for the day, Barnabas dragged the
crash mats into the garage and stacked them against the wall there. While
Barnabas and his family were away, Timothy, aged 11, a new boy in the
neighborhood, wandered into Barnabas's yard and saw the bouldering wall. Timothy
had not previously been aware that it was there, but, having frequently climbed
bouldering walls before, he decided to climb it and started up. Near the top he
lost his grip on one move and took a hard fall onto the bare ground, suffering a
serious injury that would have been prevented by the crash mats. An action has
been brought against Barnabas on Timothy's behalf to recover damages for the
injuries Timothy sustained in the fall. In this jurisdiction, the traditional
common-law rules pertaining to contributory negligence have been replaced by a
pure comparative negligence rule.

In his action against Barnabas, will Timothy prevail?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A landowner stores the safety padding from a backyard apparatus that neighbor
kids use; a new, experienced 11-year-old trespasses in, uses it without the
padding, and is hurt on bare ground. In a pure-comparative-negligence
jurisdiction, will the child prevail — and which "No" states the correct reason?

### 3. Final answer choices (A–D, post-shuffle)

**A.** No, because Timothy entered Barnabas's yard and climbed the wall without
Barnabas's permission.

**B.** No, because children likely to be attracted by the bouldering wall would
normally realize the risk of climbing it without crash mats.

**C.** No, because Timothy did not know about the bouldering wall before entering
the yard and thus was not "lured" onto the premises.

**D.** No, because Timothy failed to exercise reasonable care commensurate with
his age, intelligence, and experience.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

The action sounds in attractive nuisance — the only theory that reaches a child
who trespasses and is hurt by an artificial condition. The doctrine asks five
things, and the one that decides this item is whether the children attracted to
the condition would, because of their youth, fail to realize the risk. Here they
would not. A bouldering wall is climbed for the thrill of height; the danger of
falling without padding is the obvious, visible point of the thing. Timothy is
11 and an experienced climber, squarely old enough and skilled enough to
appreciate that letting go above bare ground will hurt. **Gold Key:** attractive
nuisance protects children against dangers they *cannot* appreciate, not against
obvious risks an attracted, age-appropriate child plainly grasps — when the
child would realize the risk, element (iii) fails and the landowner owes no
attractive-nuisance duty. That is the only choice that states a legally valid
reason Timothy loses. **Silver Key:** every choice begins with "No," so the
outcome is conceded and the question is purely *which reason is correct* — keep
the one whose rationale survives the law and cut the three that misstate it.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**D — No, because Timothy failed to exercise reasonable care commensurate with
his age, intelligence, and experience. (DOMINANT TRAP)**
This reads like the right answer because the child *was* careless — climbing
above bare ground is exactly the kind of thing a reasonable child of his age and
experience would not do, so "he was negligent" feels like a clean defense. The
breaker is the jurisdiction line you were handed: pure comparative negligence
replaced contributory negligence here. **Gold Key:** under comparative
negligence a plaintiff's own fault *reduces* recovery proportionally but never
*bars* it, so "the plaintiff was careless" can never, standing alone, support
"the plaintiff will not prevail." A reason that merely shrinks the award answers
*how much*, not *whether* — it is non-responsive to a call about prevailing.

**A — No, because Timothy entered Barnabas's yard and climbed the wall without
Barnabas's permission.**
This pulls because trespass feels like it should forfeit any claim — you broke
in, you lose. The breaker is that attractive nuisance is a *trespasser* doctrine:
lack of permission is its precondition, not its defeater. **Gold Key:** the whole
point of attractive nuisance is to protect children who are, by definition,
trespassing without permission — so the absence of permission is what triggers
the analysis, never what ends it. The choice runs the doctrine backwards.

**C — No, because Timothy did not know about the bouldering wall before entering
the yard and thus was not "lured" onto the premises.**
This is attractive because the doctrine's name suggests the child must have been
"attracted" — lured — onto the land by the condition itself, and Timothy wasn't.
The breaker is that the modern majority rule (Restatement § 339) abandoned the
allurement requirement. **Gold Key:** it is enough that a child already on the
land for any reason encounters the artificial condition; the condition need not
have drawn the child in. The choice states a minority/abandoned rule as if it
controlled.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested rule (unchanged from original):** attractive nuisance — a possessor's
liability for physical harm to trespassing children caused by an artificial
condition on the land.

**Governing authority (lawyer_confirmation):** Restatement (Second) of Torts
§ 339. A possessor of land is subject to liability for physical harm to
trespassing children caused by an artificial condition if: (a) the place is one
where the possessor knows or has reason to know children are likely to trespass;
(b) the condition is one the possessor knows/should know involves an unreasonable
risk of death or serious bodily harm to such children; (c) **the children,
because of their youth, do not discover the condition or realize the risk**
involved in intermeddling with it; (d) the utility of maintaining the condition
and the burden of eliminating the danger are slight compared with the risk; and
(e) the possessor fails to exercise reasonable care to eliminate the danger.
Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/attractive_nuisance_doctrine

**Affirmative reasoning for B:** Element (c) is the failure point. An 11-year-old
experienced climber attracted to a climbing wall realizes the risk of climbing
without crash mats; the danger is obvious to a child of his age and experience.
With element (c) unmet, the attractive-nuisance duty does not attach and Timothy
cannot recover on that theory — so the correct reason he will not prevail is that
the attracted children would realize the risk. The comparative-negligence
overlay does not change this: it would only matter if Barnabas owed and breached
a duty, and here element (c) defeats the duty before apportionment is reached.

**Comparative-negligence point (lawyer_confirmation):** pure comparative
negligence reduces a plaintiff's recovery by his percentage of fault but never
bars it. See Li v. Yellow Cab Co., 13 Cal. 3d 804 (1975) (adopting pure
comparative negligence); Restatement (Third) of Torts: Apportionment of Liability
§ 7. The MBE instructs applicants to apply pure comparative negligence unless
told otherwise, and the stem confirms it here.

**Issue / outcome confirmation:** the tested rule (attractive nuisance, element
(iii)), the legal issue (does a landowner owe a duty to an experienced child
trespasser injured by an obvious-risk artificial condition), and the credited
outcome (plaintiff does not prevail, because the attracted children would realize
the risk) are all UNCHANGED from the original. Web research confirms element (c)
and does not contradict the official key → verdict PASS, not NEEDS_HUMAN.
