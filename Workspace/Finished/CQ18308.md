## 7. Right-Answer Explanation (Choice B)

Brady requires three elements: the evidence must be (1) favorable to the defendant, (2) suppressed by the prosecution, and (3) material (i.e., creating a reasonable probability of a different outcome). This question turns on the suppression element. Suppression means the prosecution withheld material the defense did not have and could not have obtained through the exercise of reasonable diligence. Lydia's civil attorney obtained the same management letter through a civil subpoena, reviewed it in full, and cited portions of it in a separate insurance proceeding—all months before the criminal trial. There was nothing left to suppress. The prosecution cannot withhold from the defense what the defense already has.

**Gold Key:** Brady's "suppression" element requires the prosecution to have withheld something the defense lacked. When defense counsel independently possessed the material—especially when counsel actually obtained and used it in separate litigation before trial—the prosecution's failure to separately hand it over is not suppression. You cannot suppress what the defense already had.

**Silver Key:** The stem tells you exactly what defense counsel did with this letter: obtained it, reviewed it, cited it in another proceeding before trial. That is your answer. When the stem shows the defense independently had and used the disputed document, scan for any choice telling you to ignore that fact or calling the Brady duty "absolute." ## 9. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Tested rule:** Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83 (1963) requires the prosecution to disclose evidence favorable to the accused. Brady violations require three elements: (1) the evidence is favorable (exculpatory or impeachment); (2) the prosecution suppressed the evidence, willfully or inadvertently; and (3) the suppression resulted in prejudice (materiality — reasonable probability of a different outcome). Strickler v. Greene, 527 U.S. 263 (1999).

**Suppression element and independent access:** Suppression requires that the prosecution withheld material that the defense did not have and could not obtain through due diligence. Courts have uniformly held that no Brady suppression occurs when the defendant or defense counsel independently possessed or had access to the evidence. The constitutional guarantee is satisfied, not violated, when the defense has the information it needs; Brady does not compel the prosecution to hand the defense a document the defense already holds. Where, as here, defense counsel not only obtained the same document through a civil subpoena but actually used portions of it in a separate proceeding months before trial, the suppression element cannot be satisfied.

**Credited outcome unchanged:** Q18308's credited answer (C, now shuffled to B) is that there is no Brady violation because defense counsel had independent access. The tested rule (Brady suppression / independent-access limitation), legal issue, and outcome are identical in the transform.

**Doctrinal-currency check:** Brady v. Maryland and Strickler v. Greene remain controlling. No Supreme Court decision since 1999 has altered the three-element test or the independent-access limitation. No currency flag required.

**Attorney review:** Not required. The credited answer is consistent with well-established Supreme Court precedent.
