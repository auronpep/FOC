## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** Official-act immunity for civil damages turns on the *nature of the act*, not the actor's motive or current job status. The former Governor's order to de-prioritize a contractor's bids was an act within the outer perimeter of gubernatorial responsibility, regardless of whether the motive was policy disagreement or personal retaliation. Because the act was official, the former Governor is immune from civil damages. The plaintiff's remedy — if any — lies in the political process, not in a private damages suit.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A (original C) — DOMINANT TRAP:**
*What makes it attractive:* Students instinctively believe that a corrupt or retaliatory motive should strip an official of immunity. It feels unjust to shield a leader who acted out of personal spite. This is the most natural gut reaction.
*The breaker:* Official-act immunity for civil damages does not turn on the actor's subjective motive. Courts classify the *act itself* — was it within the outer perimeter of official responsibility? — not the President's or Governor's alleged reason for doing it. **Gold Key:** The immunity analysis asks what the actor *did*, not *why* they did it. A politically motivated but officially classified act is still immune.

**Choice B (original D) — FABRICATED REQUIREMENT:**
*What makes it attractive:* Students who know that executive power is limited by statute may reason that immunity should only attach when the legislature specifically authorized the action. It sounds like a plausible separation-of-powers check.
*The breaker:* Immunity for civil damages asks whether the act was *official*, not whether the act would survive a separate merits challenge. The question is one of *act classification*, not statutory authority. **Gold Key:** Immunity turns on the act's official character, not on whether Congress (or the legislature) specifically green-lit that particular decision.

**Choice D (original A) — STATUS CONFUSION:**
*What makes it attractive:* Students may reason that immunity is a privilege of office, so once the official leaves, the shield drops. This feels intuitive — you can't be "President" if you're not President.
*The breaker:* Immunity for official acts protects conduct *taken while in office* even after the official leaves office. The relevant question is whether the act was official when performed, not whether the actor currently holds the office. **Gold Key:** Official-act immunity is attached to the *conduct*, not the *current title*.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** Under Nixon v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 731 (1982), a President is entitled to absolute immunity from damages liability predicated on official acts. The Court held that this immunity extends to all acts within the "outer perimeter" of the President's duties of office. Id. at 756. The immunity is a "functionally mandated incident of [the] unique office, rooted in the constitutional tradition of the separation of powers." Id. at 749.

**Application to this question:** Directing enforcement priorities (or bid prioritization) of an executive agency is an official act within the outer perimeter of executive responsibility. The former executive's immunity does not depend on: (a) whether the executive is currently in office — immunity attaches to the official act itself; (b) the executive's subjective motive — courts classify the act, not the intent; or (c) whether the legislature specifically authorized the action — immunity turns on the act's official character, not on a separate merits analysis.

**Trump v. United States, 603 U.S. 593 (2024)** addressed *criminal* immunity and distinguished between absolute immunity for core constitutional powers and presumptive immunity for other official acts in the criminal context. It did not overrule Nixon v. Fitzgerald's holding on *civil damages* immunity, which remains absolute for official acts within the outer perimeter.

**Authority:** Nixon v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 731 (1982) (leading case); Clinton v. Jones, 520 U.S. 681 (1997) (no immunity for unofficial acts or pre-presidential conduct); Butz v. Economou, 438 U.S. 478 (1978) (absolute immunity for certain executive officials in civil damages).

**Verification:** The tested rule (absolute civil damages immunity for official acts within the outer perimeter), the legal issue (immunity analysis for a former executive's official act), and the credited outcome (immune) are all unchanged from the original. No doctrinal drift detected.
