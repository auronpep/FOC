## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** The Fourth Amendment's prohibition on unreasonable searches and seizures applies only to government actors — police, other government officials, or private persons acting at the government's direction. Private school employees are not state actors. New Jersey v. T.L.O., 469 U.S. 325 (1985), explicitly addressed public school officials as representatives of the State; the Court's reasoning does not extend to employees of private or parochial schools, who are not agents of the government. See In re Devon T., 85 Md. App. 674, 701 n.7 (1991).

Because Hannah is a headmaster at a privately operated school and there is no indication she was working in conjunction with any government official, the Fourth Amendment's protections are entirely inapplicable to her search. The motion to suppress fails at the threshold — there was no state action. This is the most effective argument because it resolves the issue automatically, without needing to analyze the reasonableness of the search, the quality of the evidence, or any implied-consent doctrine.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. The special relationship between a high school headmaster and a student implies the student's consent to a search by the headmaster.**

*Why attractive:* Students do have a reduced expectation of privacy at school, and the in loco parentis concept has real doctrinal support. A student might reasonably be thought to have consented to certain administrative searches by enrolling.

*What breaks it:* Even in public-school cases where the Fourth Amendment applies, implied consent from the student-school relationship has limits. The Supreme Court in New Jersey v. T.L.O. required reasonable suspicion, not mere implied consent. A body search — reaching into a student's pocket — based solely on an anonymous double-hearsay note would strain any implied-consent rationale well past its breaking point. More fundamentally, this argument concedes that the Fourth Amendment applies and then tries to satisfy it with implied consent, when the better answer is that the Amendment doesn't apply at all. **Gold Key:** The Fourth Amendment restricts only government actors; a private school headmaster is not a state agent.

**B. Special concern for the well-being of young people justifies a warrantless search of a student suspected of selling drugs to other students.**

*Why attractive:* This echoes the actual reasoning in Vernonia School District 47J v. Acton, 515 U.S. 646 (1995), where the Supreme Court upheld random drug testing of student athletes in a public school, citing the school's custodial responsibility for children. The "special concern for youth" language feels authoritative and on-point.

*What breaks it:* Vernonia and its progeny apply to public school officials who are state actors. The Fourth Amendment analysis only begins once state action is established. Here, the school is privately operated — Hannah is not a government agent — so the Fourth Amendment framework (including its exceptions) never comes into play. Invoking Vernonia's "special concern" rationale presupposes that the Fourth Amendment applies, which is the very question at issue. Additionally, even under Vernonia, a body search based solely on an anonymous hearsay tip would face serious reasonableness problems. **Gold Key:** The Fourth Amendment restricts only government actors; a private school headmaster is not a state agent.

**D. Hannah had probable cause to believe that Daniel was in possession of dangerous drugs.**

*Why attractive:* Probable cause is the familiar standard for lawful searches, and the anonymous note did allege drug possession. A student might think the note provided enough to establish probable cause.

*What breaks it:* This answer fails for three independent reasons. First, an anonymous note that itself reports hearsay ("heard through the grapevine") does not ordinarily establish probable cause — it lacks the indicia of reliability that courts require. Second, even if Hannah had probable cause, that alone does not justify a warrantless search by a government actor; the Fourth Amendment requires both probable cause and a warrant (or a recognized exception). Third, and most fundamentally, the entire probable-cause framework presupposes that the Fourth Amendment applies — but Hannah was not a government actor, so the Amendment's requirements are irrelevant. **Gold Key:** The Fourth Amendment restricts only government actors; a private school headmaster is not a state agent.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution protects against unreasonable searches and seizures by the government. It applies to searches conducted by police, other government officials, and private persons acting as government agents. It does not apply to searches conducted by private individuals or entities who are not acting on behalf of the government.

**Authority:**
- U.S. Const. amend. IV.
- New Jersey v. T.L.O., 469 U.S. 325, 333–337 (1985) — holding that the Fourth Amendment applies to public school officials because they act as representatives of the State, not merely as surrogates for parents; the Court's reasoning is explicitly limited to public school settings.
- In re Devon T., 85 Md. App. 674, 701 n.7 (1991) — noting that the Fourth Amendment would not apply to employees of private or parochial schools because they are not government agents.
- LegalClarity.org, "Does the 4th Amendment Apply to Schools? Public vs. Private" (2026) — confirming that private school employees are not state actors and the Fourth Amendment does not apply to their searches.

**Application:** Hannah is the headmaster of Grace Fellowship Academy, a privately operated school. There is no indication she was acting at the direction of, or in conjunction with, any government official. The anonymous note came from a fellow student, not from police. Hannah's search of Daniel's pocket was undertaken in her capacity as a private school administrator. Because she was not a government actor, the Fourth Amendment's protections do not apply, and Daniel's motion to suppress fails at the threshold.
