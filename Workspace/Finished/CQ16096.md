## 7. Full right-answer explanation

A is the best answer. Lydia is guilty of felony murder **only if** the killing was a foreseeable consequence of the robbery she helped facilitate. **Gold Key:** A person who, before a crime, agrees in advance to aid the principal is an *accomplice* (sometimes called an "accessory before the fact") and is liable as a principal for the foreseeable consequences of the crime aided. **Silver Key:** Don't reach for the conspiracy rule when the fact pattern describes an accomplice. The "in furtherance" language is for co-conspirators; the accomplice test is *foreseeability*.

Lydia's pre-robbery agreement to stash the books made her an accomplice. As an accomplice to the robbery, she is liable for the death that occurred in the course of the robbery — but only if a shooting was a foreseeable consequence of that robbery. Robbery is an inherently dangerous felony, so a shooting during the escape is generally foreseeable. The answer is A.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. guilty, because an accomplice is responsible for all crimes committed in furtherance of the crime to which he or she is an accomplice.** (dominant trap)

This sells the *conspiracy* rule on an *accomplice* fact pattern. The "in furtherance" language belongs to conspiracy liability (a co-conspirator is responsible for crimes committed in furtherance of the conspiracy); the accomplice rule is **foreseeability**, not "in furtherance." **Gold Key:** For an accomplice, the reach is *foreseeable* consequences, not every consequence of every later offense. **Silver Key:** When you see "in furtherance" in an answer, check the doctrinal frame — it is the conspiracy rule, not the accomplice rule. The state of mind in a felony-murder-accomplice case is judged by foreseeability, not by membership in a conspiracy.

**C. not guilty, because she was an accessory after the fact.**

This sells the categorization trap. "Accessory after the fact" describes a person who helps a felon *after* the felony is completed, in order to hinder apprehension, trial, or punishment — and that is a separate, lesser offense that does not extend to the principal's earlier crimes. **Silver Key:** The *timing of the agreement* controls the category. An agreement made **before** the crime is accomplice (a.k.a. accessory before the fact); help rendered **after** the crime is accessory after the fact. Lydia agreed **before** the robbery. She is an accomplice, not an accessory after the fact, and the accessory-after-the-fact label is wrong on these facts.

**D. not guilty, because she did not know that Cornelius was going to use deadly force to accomplish the robbery.**

This sells the mental-state trap. The accomplice test is *objective foreseeability*, not subjective knowledge. **Gold Key:** What matters for accomplice liability for the foreseeable-consequence crime is not whether the accomplice actually foresaw the additional harm, but whether a reasonable person in the accomplice's position would have foreseen it. The "I didn't know" defense does not defeat liability for a foreseeable consequence of a robbery — robbery being an inherently dangerous felony in which shootings are foreseeably possible.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

**Pass-1 Step 1 — tested black-letter rule.** A person who, with intent to aid the commission of a crime, actually aids, counsels, or agrees to aid the principal before the crime is an *accomplice* (also called an "accessory before the fact") and is liable as a principal for the crime aided and for any *foreseeable* additional crimes committed in the course of it. A person who helps the principal *after* the crime is completed, in order to hinder apprehension, trial, or punishment, is an *accessory after the fact* and is liable only for that separate, lesser obstruction-type offense; the after-the-fact label does not extend to the principal's earlier crimes. The natural-and-probable-consequences doctrine (the common-law majority rule and the modern bar-exam default) holds the accomplice responsible only for consequences that were *foreseeable* at the time of the aid — not for every consequence of every subsequent offense, and not for crimes "in furtherance" of a *conspiracy* (a distinct doctrine that requires an agreement plus an overt act).

**Authority (lawyer confirmation only).** The common-law / modern-bar rule is reflected in *People v. Prettyman*, 14 Cal. 4th 248 (1996) (foreseeability, not actual knowledge, controls accomplice scope under the natural-and-probable-consequences doctrine) and *State v. Ulvinen*, 313 N.W.2d 425 (Minn. 1981) (post-offense cleanup help can be charged as an accessory-after-the-fact-type offense but does not extend the helper's liability to the principal's earlier crimes). The Model Penal Code § 2.06 (complicity) generally requires a *purpose* to promote or facilitate the underlying crime and rejects the open-ended natural-and-probable-consequences rule; the source row's "foreseeable-consequence" framing is the common-law / bar-exam default, which the variation preserves.
