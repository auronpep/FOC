## 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B — "The interior of a vehicle is always within the immediate control of an arrestee, so police may always search it incident to arrest."**
This choice's attraction is its confident, rule-sounding statement — it sounds like a bright-line police power. The breaker: it states the exact opposite of the governing rule. Under *Gant*, a vehicle search incident to arrest is *presumptively unreasonable* unless the arrestee can still reach the passenger compartment or the offense could produce vehicle evidence. This choice would, if true, justify *every* vehicle search incident to arrest, which is precisely the overbroad rule *Gant* rejected. **Silver Key:** *SK-CRIM-CALL-FOCUS-NN* — ask yourself "does this give a *reason* the court would rule for Timothy?" This choice actually states a rule that would justify the search against him.

**C — "The search of the food truck was not the result of a valid inventory search."**
Attractive because "inventory search" sounds like a plausible warrant exception — the student knows that police sometimes inventory impounded vehicles. The breaker: the call asks *why* the court rules the search unlawful. Whether this could have been a valid inventory search is irrelevant — the police weren't claiming an inventory exception. The search was conducted as a search-incident-to-arrest, and it fails on those terms. This answer is not responsive to the call.

**D — "Searches conducted without prior approval by a judge or magistrate are per se unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment."**
This is the dominant trap. It is attractive because the Fourth Amendment *does* generally require warrants, and many students memorize this as a bedrock principle. The breaker: the rule is stated too broadly — it omits the well-established exceptions (search incident to arrest, consent, plain view, automobile exception, inventory search, exigent circumstances, etc.). While warrantless searches are presumptively unreasonable, they are not *per se* unreasonable. The court's ruling in Timothy's favor is based on the specific *Gant* analysis, not on a blanket warrant requirement. **Gold Key:** *GK-CRIM-GANT-NN* — the *Gant* rule shows that the vehicle search exception *could* apply if conditions were met; the court's ruling is about the failure of those conditions, not about warrants generally.

## 8. Full Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule:** Under the Fourth Amendment, a warrantless search is presumptively unreasonable. However, one well-established exception is search incident to a lawful arrest (*Chimel v. California*, 395 U.S. 752, 1969). For vehicle searches incident to arrest, the Supreme Court held in *Arizona v. Gant*, 556 U.S. 332 (2009), that police may search a vehicle incident to a recent occupant's arrest **only if** the arrestee is within reaching distance of the passenger compartment at the time of the search **or** it is reasonable to believe the vehicle contains evidence of the offense of arrest.

**Application:** Timothy (1) was handcuffed and locked in the patrol car — not within reaching distance of the food truck's cargo compartment — and (2) the offense (operating without a permit) is not one that would produce evidence stored in a vehicle. Neither *Gant* prong is satisfied. Therefore the search was unreasonable.

**Authority:** *Arizona v. Gant*, 556 U.S. 332 (2009); *Chimel v. California*, 395 U.S. 752 (1969).

## distilled_core_question

Police arrest Timothy for operating a food truck without a permit, handcuff him, place him in a patrol car, then search the truck's cargo compartment and find stolen goods. If the court rules the search unlawful, it will be because: (A) Timothy was secured and couldn't reach the truck; (B) police can always search a vehicle incident to arrest; (C) it wasn't a valid inventory search; (D) warrantless searches are per se unreasonable.

## call_and_prediction

This is a **reverse-reasoning** call. The student must identify the *specific reason* the search fails, not a general rule about the Fourth Amendment.

**Prediction:** Most students pick D (per se unreasonable — 32% predicted, dominant trap) or A (correct — 35% predicted). The CLASH will be between A and C, resolved by responsiveness to the call.

## governing_c3_lane

**Lane:** Fourth Amendment — Vehicle Search Incident to Arrest

**Governing rule (student-facing):** *Arizona v. Gant* says police can search a vehicle incident to arrest only if (1) the arrestee could reach the passenger compartment when the search happened, or (2) police reasonably think the vehicle has evidence of the crime they arrested for.

**Why this lane:** The facts state an arrest, a vehicle, a post-arrest search, and a challenge to that search. The call asks for the legal reason the search fails. Every wrong answer is either an overgeneralized Fourth Amendment principle or a true statement about a different doctrine.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### Choice A — Correct

**Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice ties the outcome to the specific fact that Timothy was handcuffed and couldn't reach the truck. It matches the common-sense idea: "How could he access the truck if he's locked in a police car?"

**Student label:** Correct — matches the *Gant* rule's first prong (reaching distance).

**What a true/responsive version would look like:** This is already the correct answer.

**Lawyer confirmation:** Under *Arizona v. Gant*, 556 U.S. 332 (2009), the safety-of-officers justification for a vehicle search incident to arrest vanishes when the arrestee is secured away from the vehicle. The first *Gant* prong is unsatisfied. The second prong also fails because operating without a permit is not an offense that would produce vehicle evidence.

---

### Choice B — NOT_TRUE (backwards)

**Student-accessible C3 signal:** "Always" is an absolute qualifier. No Fourth Amendment rule says police can *always* search a vehicle incident to arrest. The presence of "always" signals an overclaim.

**Student label:** States the opposite of the actual rule — the *Gant* rule limits vehicle searches, it doesn't authorize them unconditionally.

**What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Police may search a vehicle incident to arrest only when the arrestee could reach the passenger compartment or the vehicle might contain evidence of the arrest offense."

**Lawyer confirmation:** *Gant* explicitly rejected the broader rule from *New York v. Belton* that some courts had interpreted to permit vehicle searches whenever an occupant was arrested. The correct rule is the two-prong *Gant* test.

---

### Choice C — NOT_RESPONSIVE (misfit)

**Student-accessible C3 signal:** The statement is true (this search wasn't an inventory search), but it doesn't answer the call. The call asks *why* the search was unlawful. Answering "it wasn't a valid inventory search" is like answering "what color is the car?" with "it's not a boat" — true but irrelevant.

**Student label:** True but doesn't respond to the call. It points to a different doctrine.

**What a true/responsive version would look like:** This would need to explain why the search-incident-to-arrest exception failed, not why a different exception wasn't invoked.

**Lawyer confirmation:** The inventory search exception (*South Dakota v. Opperman*, 428 U.S. 364, 1976) allows police to inventory impounded vehicles for safety and evidentiary reasons. But here, police weren't impounding the food truck — they were searching it incident to Timothy's arrest. The inventory-search doctrine is not the basis of the search, so its absence is not the reason the search was unlawful.

---

### Choice D — Dominant Trap, NOT_TRUE (overclaim/tiered_absolute)

**Student-accessible C3 signal:** "Per se" is lawyer-speak for "automatically / by itself." The choice says any warrantless search is automatically unreasonable. Even without knowing Fourth Amendment exceptions, a student can spot the problem: if this were true, police could never frisk a suspect for weapons, never accept consent to search, never seize evidence in plain view. The absolute language is the tell.

**Student label:** Overbroad rule — it states the warrant requirement as absolute, ignoring all exceptions.

**What a true/responsive version would look like:** "The search was unreasonable because no warrant exception applied, specifically because Timothy was secured and couldn't access the vehicle, and the permit offense wouldn't produce vehicle evidence."

**Lawyer confirmation:** The Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement is subject to numerous well-established exceptions. The *Gant* vehicle-search exception is one of them. The court's ruling is not that searches are per se unreasonable without warrants — it's that the particular search failed the *Gant* test.

## Gold Key(s)

**GK-CRIM-GANT-NN — The Gant Rule**
Police may search a vehicle incident to a recent occupant's arrest only if (1) the arrestee is within reaching distance of the passenger compartment at the time of search, or (2) it is reasonable to believe the vehicle contains evidence of the offense of arrest.
- **Type:** rule
- **Unlocks:** The deciding test for vehicle search-incident-to-arrest.
- **Trigger:** Arrestee + vehicle + post-arrest search.
- **Last-minute review:** Yes.

**GK-CRIM-GANT-TWO-NN — Secured Arrestee**
If the arrestee is handcuffed and secured in a patrol car, the "reaching distance" prong of Gant is automatically unsatisfied.
- **Type:** distinction
- **Unlocks:** Eliminates any argument that the arrestee could still access the vehicle.

## wrong_answer_recovery_paths

**If a student picks D (dominant trap — per se unreasonable):**
The student knows warrants are the general rule but has memorized it as an absolute. Recovery: ask yourself "Are there *any* exceptions to the warrant requirement?" If you can name one (consent, plain view, search incident, etc.), then "per se" is too strong.

**If a student picks C (inventory search misfit):**
The student is looking for a procedural defect. Recovery: re-read the call. It asks *why* the court rules the search unlawful. The fact that the search wasn't a valid inventory search may be true, but it doesn't explain what made the search-incident-to-arrest fail.

**If a student picks B (backwards):**
The student is confusing the *Belton* rule (overruled by *Gant*) with current law. Recovery: "always" is a universal qualifier — no Fourth Amendment rule uses "always" for searches; the Amendment is built around "reasonableness," not absolutes.
