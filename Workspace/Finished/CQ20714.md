### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**D is correct.** Lydia is not relying on a general taxpayer grievance. She is challenging a congressional spending provision and claiming that the spending violates a specific constitutional limit on that spending power.

**Gold Key:** Federal taxpayers generally cannot sue just because tax money is being spent, but the Flast exception allows standing for a challenge to congressional taxing-and-spending action when the claim invokes a specific constitutional limit on that spending power. The Establishment Clause is the classic trigger.

**Silver Key:** Lock the call as **standing**, not merits. Do not ask whether the robotics kits are actually unconstitutional; ask whether this taxpayer is allowed into court to make that claim.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** The choice sounds technical because “nexus” is a real standing word and it gestures at a required connection. The breaker is that this fact pattern supplies the spending-power connection the taxpayer needs: a congressional spending provision challenged under a specific religious-establishment limit.

**B.** The choice sells the easy myth: taxpayer + federal spending = standing. The breaker is the word **any**. Federal taxpayer standing is not available for all spending challenges; the exception is narrow.

**C.** The choice sells a merits instinct: maybe the program is not really supporting religion unless the saved money is later used for religious activity. The breaker is the call. The question asks whether Lydia may bring the challenge, not whether she has already proved the downstream religious use theory.

---

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

The tested rule is unchanged from the original row: federal taxpayer standing is generally barred, but **Flast v. Cohen** recognizes a narrow exception for a taxpayer challenge to a congressional taxing-and-spending enactment alleged to violate a specific constitutional limit on that spending power, including the Establishment Clause. Justia’s Supreme Court summary states that taxpayers generally lack standing but have standing when federal revenue is allegedly used to violate the Establishment Clause, and the Flast syllabus states the two nexuses: a logical link to the legislative spending enactment and a nexus to the precise constitutional infringement. ([Justia Law][1])

The variation keeps the same legal issue and outcome. Congress enacted a spending provision. Lydia is a federal taxpayer. Her claim alleges unconstitutional support for religious entities under the Establishment Clause. The merits fact that the kits contain no religious content does not defeat standing. Later cases keep Flast narrow, but they do not defeat this item because the challenge here is to congressional spending, not merely executive-branch discretionary spending or a tax credit. ([Legal Information Institute][2])
