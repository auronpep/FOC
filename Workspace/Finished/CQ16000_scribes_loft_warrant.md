# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

A county prosecutor received information that illegal wagering pools were being run out of a Christian-themed coffeehouse called the Scribes' Loft on Olive Street. She applied for a search warrant. Her affidavit said that a confidential informant had personally seen the wagering sheets while inside the coffeehouse, that the informant had often supplied information to law enforcement before, and that every prior tip from him had proved accurate. The affidavit did not name the informant because it said his identity needed to remain secret for his safety and continued usefulness. The warrant that issued authorized a search of “the premises known as the Scribes' Loft and located at 212 Cedar Avenue” and of “all persons on those premises at the time of execution who are found to possess wagering records.” There was a Bible-supply shop called the Scribes' Loft at 212 Cedar Avenue, but the officers assigned to execute the warrant went instead to the Scribes' Loft coffeehouse on Olive Street. Behind the counter, they found wagering records in Daniel's cash drawer. Daniel, the coffeehouse owner, was charged under the state gambling statute. He moved to suppress the wagering records. His most effective argument is that:

A. the information contained in the affidavit and warrant did not properly identify Daniel's premises.
B. the affidavit submitted in support of the warrant was based entirely on hearsay.
C. the information contained in the affidavit submitted in support of the warrant was not corroborated by any independent police observation.
D. the affidavit submitted in support of the warrant did not disclose the informant's name.

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A warrant affidavit names reliable confidential-informant facts, but the warrant describes one address while officers search a different business. What is the strongest suppression argument?

## 6. Full right-answer explanation

A is the best argument. **Gold Key:** a warrant must particularly describe the place to be searched. Here, the warrant pointed to the Scribes' Loft at 212 Cedar Avenue, but the officers searched the Scribes' Loft coffeehouse on Olive Street. **Silver Key:** when three choices attack the informant and one tracks the place searched, follow the concrete place mismatch.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B.** This choice sells a familiar trial-evidence instinct: hearsay sounds like something a court should reject. The breaker is that warrant affidavits may rely on reliable hearsay; the better attack is that the warrant did not properly identify the place officers searched.

**C.** This choice sells the instinct that an informant tip must always be independently corroborated. The breaker is that corroboration is not an absolute requirement where the affidavit supplies reliability and firsthand observation.

**D.** This choice sells distrust of anonymous informants. The breaker is that anonymity alone is not fatal when the affidavit explains reliability, basis of knowledge, and the reason for nondisclosure.

## 8. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The Fourth Amendment requires warrants to particularly describe the place to be searched. Informant tips are evaluated under a totality-of-the-circumstances probable-cause approach; reliability and basis of knowledge remain important practical considerations. The variation preserves the same tested rule, issue, trap structure, and credited outcome as the source row: the strongest suppression argument is the warrant's failure to identify the defendant's premises, not the hearsay/informant defects.

Authority floor: U.S. Const. amend. IV; Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 (1983); Maryland v. Garrison, 480 U.S. 79 (1987).
