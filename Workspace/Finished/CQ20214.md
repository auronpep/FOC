### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Transporting large quantities of propane in a tank truck is an abnormally dangerous activity. Under the Restatement (Third) of Torts § 20, one who carries on an abnormally dangerous activity is subject to strict liability for physical harm resulting from that activity. The activity must (a) create a foreseeable and highly significant risk of physical harm even when reasonable care is exercised, and (b) not be a matter of common usage. Transporting large quantities of propane by truck satisfies both elements: the risk of explosion exists regardless of the care taken, and it is not an activity commonly undertaken by members of the community. Because strict liability applies, Martha can recover without proving negligence — the fact that the propane exploded and caused her injury is sufficient.

**Gold Key:** Abnormally dangerous activity = strict liability; no need to prove negligence. The activity must create a foreseeable risk of serious harm even with reasonable care, and must not be common usage. Transporting large quantities of flammable/explosive substances (gasoline, propane) by truck qualifies.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is wrong** — and this is the dominant trap. It is true that the jurisdiction has a statute prohibiting parking vehicles longer than 30 feet, and Peter's truck was 38 feet. But negligence per se only applies when the statute violated was *designed to protect against the type of harm that occurred*. The length-parking statute is a traffic-flow regulation; it was not designed to protect against the risk of a propane explosion. The choice is attractive because it correctly identifies a statutory violation and gives it a plausible-sounding label ("traffic safety statute"), leading students to assume the statute's purpose matches the harm. The label is an overclaim: the statute regulates vehicle length on city streets, not hazardous-material safety.

**B is wrong** — This choice invokes the reasonable-person standard, which is the general breach element of a negligence claim. But the call asks why the claim will *most likely* succeed. The reasonable-person standard is a negligence theory, and it requires proving breach, causation, and damages. Strict liability under the abnormally-dangerous-activity doctrine is a stronger, cleaner path to recovery because it eliminates the need to prove any negligence at all. The choice is attractive because violating a no-parking sign *does* look like unreasonable conduct — but it answers the wrong question. The call is about the *theory of liability*, not whether a reasonable person would have parked there.

**D is wrong** — Peter's awareness that a statute prohibited parking in a fire lane is irrelevant to the negligence per se analysis. Negligence per se turns on whether the statute was designed to protect against the type of harm that occurred, not on the defendant's subjective knowledge of the statute. The fire-lane statute was designed to keep access clear for emergency vehicles, not to protect against propane explosions. The choice is attractive because it highlights a real statutory violation and the defendant's awareness of it, which feels like it should matter — but awareness of a statute does not make its violation the basis for liability when the statute's purpose does not match the harm.

### 9. Black-Letter Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Negligence per se** (Restatement (Third) of Torts § 14): An actor is negligent if the actor violates a statute that is designed to protect against the type of accident or harm caused by the actor's conduct, and the plaintiff is a member of the class of persons the statute is designed to protect. The statute must be (a) designed to protect against the *type of risk* that materialized, and (b) aimed at the *class of persons* to which the plaintiff belongs. Here, neither the vehicle-length statute nor the fire-lane statute was designed to protect against explosions of transported propane. Negligence per se does not apply.

**Strict liability for abnormally dangerous activities** (Restatement (Third) of Torts § 20): One who carries on an abnormally dangerous activity is subject to strict liability for physical harm resulting from the activity. Section 20(b): An activity is abnormally dangerous if it (1) creates a foreseeable and highly significant risk of physical harm even when reasonable care is exercised by all actors, and (2) is not a matter of common usage. Transporting large quantities of propane by tank truck meets both criteria: the risk of explosion persists despite reasonable care, and it is not a common activity. Peter is strictly liable for Martha's injuries.

**Authority:** Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical and Emotional Harm §§ 14, 20 (Am. Law Inst. 2010); see also Wex, Cornell LII, "negligence per se" (reviewed Aug. 2023), https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/negligence_per_se.
