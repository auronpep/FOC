## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Daniel owned a small private studio that made scented Scripture-memory candles for home Bible-study groups. A fire began somewhere inside Daniel's studio and spread into Ruth's neighboring bookbinding shop, damaging several shelves of handbound devotionals. Daniel did not intentionally start the fire, and Ruth has no evidence showing how the fire began. Daniel had, however, failed to install an automatic fire-suppression unit required by a criminal safety statute for candle-making studios. Ruth can produce evidence that, if the unit had been installed, it could have put out the fire before it reached her shop.

In Ruth's action against Daniel to recover for the fire damage, is it possible for Ruth to prevail?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A defendant did not cause a fire to start, but violated a fire-safety statute that required equipment that could have stopped the fire before it damaged the plaintiff's property. Can the plaintiff possibly prevail in negligence?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. No, because Ruth has no evidence that Daniel negligently caused the fire to begin.

B. Yes, because Ruth was harmed by Daniel's violation of a statute designed to prevent this kind of fire damage.

C. No, because the statute imposes only criminal penalties.

D. Yes, because a landowner is strictly liable under the Rylands v. Fletcher doctrine whenever fire spreads from the landowner's premises.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Ruth can possibly prevail because the statute required the very kind of fire-suppression equipment that could have stopped the fire before it damaged her shop. Gold Key: A criminal safety statute can supply the civil negligence standard when it is designed to protect this kind of plaintiff from this kind of accident. Silver Key: Split the event into two moments: starting the fire and failing to stop its spread. Ruth does not need proof that Daniel negligently started the fire if the statutory violation caused the spread damage.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because the stem loudly says Ruth cannot prove how the fire began. The breaker is the Silver Key: the claim can rest on failure to install required safety equipment that would have stopped the spread, not on negligent ignition.

C is attractive because the statute is described as criminal, so the answer tries to keep it out of civil negligence. The breaker is the Gold Key: a criminal safety statute can still define the civil standard of care when it targets the class of persons, kind of harm, and hazard involved.

D is attractive because it reaches the plaintiff-friendly result and invokes a famous land-based strict-liability doctrine. The breaker is that Rylands-style strict liability is not a general rule for every fire that spreads from land; modern strict liability is limited to abnormally dangerous activities, and ordinary candle production with missing safety equipment is a negligence-per-se lane.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. The governing negligence-per-se rule is that a court may use a statute as the standard of care when the statute was designed to protect a class including the plaintiff against the kind of harm and hazard that occurred. Restatement (Second) of Torts section 286 states that legislative requirements may define the reasonable-person standard when those purpose conditions are met, and section 288B treats an unexcused violation of an adopted statutory standard as negligence itself. Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical and Emotional Harm section 14 states the same exam rule: an actor is negligent if, without excuse, the actor violates a statute designed to protect against the type of accident caused and the victim is in the protected class.

Authority layer: Restatement (Second) of Torts sections 286 and 288B; Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical and Emotional Harm section 14; Cornell Wex, negligence per se, https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/negligence_per_se; CALI, Negligence Per Se and Res Ipsa Loquitur, https://saidtorts.lawbooks.cali.org/chapter/negligence-per-se-and-res-ipsa-loquitur/.

The Rylands distractor does not control because American strict liability in this lane is tied to abnormally dangerous activities, not ordinary landownership or every spread of fire. Restatement (Second) of Torts sections 519 and 520 define that abnormally dangerous activity framework. Cornell Wex, abnormally dangerous activity, https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/abnormally_dangerous_activity.

Daniel violated a criminal safety statute requiring the equipment meant to prevent fires in candle-making studios from spreading. Ruth's neighboring property damage is the kind of harm the statute targets, and Ruth can offer evidence that the missing unit could have extinguished the fire before it reached her shop. Even without proof that Daniel negligently started the fire, the statutory violation can establish breach, and the missing fire-suppression unit can be a cause in fact of the spread damage. Ruth can possibly prevail.
