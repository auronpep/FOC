Peter sued a county sheriff's deputy for false arrest. ## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** All three items are admissible because none is being offered for the truth of the matter asserted—that is, none is offered to prove that the robbery suspect actually matched the description. Instead, all three are offered to show the effect on the listener: what description the deputy heard, which is directly relevant to whether the deputy reasonably believed Peter was the robber.

The deputy's testimony about what he heard is classic non-hearsay—it is offered to show what description reached the deputy's ears, not whether the description was accurate. The dispatcher's testimony about what he read over the radio is likewise offered to show what was transmitted to the deputy, not for its truth. The written note is also admissible: although it is a writing, the best evidence rule (FRE 1002) applies only when a party seeks to prove the *terms* of a writing. Here, the note is offered to show what the deputy heard over the radio, regardless of whether the description in the note was accurate. The best evidence rule is therefore inapplicable.

**Gold Key (Effect-on-Listener Rule):** When an out-of-court statement is offered not to prove the truth of what was said but to show its effect on the person who heard it—such as to establish knowledge, notice, motive, or state of mind—the statement is not hearsay under FRE 801(c) and is admissible for that limited purpose. The statement's relevance lies in the fact that it was spoken and heard, not in whether it was true.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Hearsay definition — FRE 801(c):** "Hearsay" is a statement, other than one made by the declarant while testifying at the current trial or hearing, offered in evidence to prove the truth of the matter asserted. Fed. R. Evid. 801(c).

**Non-hearsay purpose — effect on the listener:** A statement that would otherwise be hearsay is *not* hearsay when it is offered for a purpose other than proving the truth of the matter asserted. The classic non-hearsay purpose is to show the statement's effect on the listener—for example, to establish the listener's knowledge, notice, motive, or state of mind. *See* Fed. R. Evid. 801(c) Advisory Committee Notes; 2 McCormick on Evidence § 249 (8th ed.). The statement is relevant because it was spoken and heard, not because it is true.

**Application:** Here, the deputy's defense is that he reasonably believed Peter was the robber based on the description he heard. The issue is *what description the deputy heard*, not whether the description was accurate. All three items—the deputy's testimony, the dispatcher's testimony, and the note—are offered to show what was communicated to the deputy, i.e., the effect on the listener. None is offered to prove that the suspect actually matched the description. Therefore, none is hearsay under FRE 801(c).

**Best evidence rule — FRE 1002:** "An original writing, recording, or photograph is required in order to prove its content unless these rules or a federal statute provides otherwise." Fed. R. Evid. 1002. The rule applies only when the proponent seeks to prove the *terms* or *content* of a writing. *See* Fed. R. Evid. 1002 Advisory Committee Notes ("Application of the rule requires a resolution of the question whether contents are sought to be proved.").

**Application to the note:** The note is not being offered to prove that the description it contains is accurate (i.e., to prove the truth of its terms). It is offered to show what the dispatcher read over the radio and therefore what the deputy heard. Because the proponent is not seeking to prove the content of the writing, the best evidence rule does not apply.

**Authority:** Fed. R. Evid. 801(c); Fed. R. Evid. 1002; 2 McCormick on Evidence § 249 (8th ed.); *see also* Fed. R. Evid. 1002 Advisory Committee Notes (rule applies only when contents are sought to be proved).

**Verification:** The tested rule (non-hearsay purpose / effect on listener), the legal issue (admissibility of evidence offered to show what the officer heard), and the credited outcome (all three items admissible) are unchanged from the original.
