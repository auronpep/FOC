## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** An initial aggressor — the person who first uses or threatens deadly force — cannot claim self-defense unless he clearly withdraws and communicates that withdrawal, and the other party persists. Peter's shout ("I'll blow you away") was a threat of deadly force that provoked the thief to stop and arm himself. Peter was the initial aggressor. He never withdrew. His self-defense claim fails.

Peter shouted a threat of deadly force at a man who was already leaving. That threat turned a fleeing thief into an armed confrontation. Under the initial-aggressor rule, the person who provokes a deadly encounter cannot then claim self-defense for his own response — unless he withdraws and the other party continues. Peter never withdrew. He escalated, and the thief responded to the escalation. The provocation defeats the claim.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A (original C):** This choice is attractive because the thief was literally driving away when Peter first stepped outside — a student may think "he was leaving, so no self-defense needed." But the real reason the defense fails is not withdrawal; it is provocation. The thief stopped *because* Peter threatened him. Withdrawal doctrine would help Peter only if he had created no provocation and the thief was truly fleeing. Here, Peter's own words reignited the confrontation. **Silver Key:** When two choices both sound plausible, ask which one the *facts* resolve. The facts scream provocation — Peter shouted first. That makes provocation the axis, not withdrawal.

**Choice B (original A):** This choice targets the imminence element of self-defense. A student may reason: "The thief was driving away — the danger was over." But imminence is judged at the moment of the shot, not the moment Peter stepped outside. When the thief stopped, got out, and grabbed a tire iron, the threat of deadly harm was immediate. The law does not require a person to wait until the last possible second. The danger was reasonably imminent when Peter fired. **Gold Key:** An initial aggressor cannot claim self-defense. Peter provoked the encounter. Even if the threat was imminent, the initial-aggressor bar kills the defense before imminence is even reached.

**Choice D (original B):** This choice sounds like a force-proportionality argument: "Was a shotgun too much against a tire iron?" But both the tire iron (a heavy blunt weapon capable of killing) and the shotgun are deadly force. When the threat is deadly, the response may be deadly. The proportionality element is satisfied here. The real problem is not how much force Peter used — it is that he started the fight. **Gold Key:** Initial aggressor status defeats self-defense regardless of proportionality. The question is not whether the force was proportional; it is whether Peter had the right to use force at all.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Tested rule:** Self-defense — the initial-aggressor doctrine. A person who provokes or initiates a confrontation with the purpose of causing death or serious bodily harm, or who first threatens deadly force, is the "initial aggressor" and cannot claim self-defense unless he clearly withdraws and communicates withdrawal, and the other party persists. MPC § 3.04(2)(b)(i) (no duty to retreat if actor was the initial aggressor); *People v. McManus*, 67 N.Y.2d 541 (1986); N.Y. Pattern Jury Instruction, Justification — Use of Deadly Physical Force.
