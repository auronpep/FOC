## 1. Final Question

Peter is a well-known Christian painter. After his death, his will left a herd of 30 goats to his niece, Lydia, who raises and sells livestock. Each goat in the herd is identified by a numbered ear tag, 1 through 30. Lydia had a printed catalog made with a photo and description of each goat. On September 1, she mailed a copy of the catalog to Martha, a fellow livestock dealer, with the following cover letter:

"I know you've always admired my uncle's herd, so I'm giving you first chance to buy any of these goats before I offer them to other buyers. The price is $1,200 per goat, however many you want. Send me your order by September 15, or I'll put them on the open market." (signed)

On September 3, Martha sent Lydia a letter that said: "I accept your offer to sell goat Number 12 for $1,200. I will come to your farm on September 6 to pick up the goat and will pay cash at that time."

On September 4, after receiving Martha's letter, Lydia called Martha and said that because several of the goats had recently won awards at the county fair, she would not sell goat Number 12 for less than $2,200. Martha agreed on the phone to pay $2,200 for goat Number 12.

Between September 1 and September 4, the fair market value of goat Number 12 increased by $1,000.

On September 7, Martha sent and Lydia received a letter that said: "I accept your offer to sell goats 1 through 11 and 13 through 30 for $1,200 each. I will pick them up tomorrow and pay at that time."

On September 8, Martha arrived at Lydia's farm and tendered payment of $1,200 each for all 30 goats. Lydia refused to sell any goats except Number 12, for which she insisted the price was $2,200. Martha left without buying any goats, saying Lydia would be hearing from her lawyer.

If Lydia asserts a claim against Martha for breach of a contract to purchase goat Number 12 for $2,200, the court should find for

A: Martha, because the fair market value of goat Number 12 increased by $1,000 between September 1 and September 4.

B: Lydia, because she had not received payment from Martha prior to their conversation on September 4.

C: Lydia, because she relied on Martha's promise to pay $2,200 for goat Number 12.

D: Martha, because Lydia's promise to sell goat Number 12 for $2,200 was not in writing.

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** Under UCC § 2-209(3), if a contract as modified falls within the Statute of Frauds (UCC § 2-201 — sale of goods for $500 or more), the modification itself must be satisfied by a writing. An oral modification of such a contract is unenforceable even though no consideration is needed for the modification under § 2-209(1).

Lydia's September 1 letter was an offer to sell each goat for $1,200. Martha's September 3 letter was an acceptance of the offer as to goat Number 12. A valid contract for the sale of goat Number 12 at $1,200 was formed. On September 4, Lydia and Martha orally agreed to raise the price of goat Number 12 to $2,200. This was a modification of an existing contract for the sale of goods. As modified, the contract calls for the sale of a good at a price well in excess of $500, so UCC § 2-201 applies. Under § 2-209(3), the Statute of Frauds requirements must be satisfied. The oral modification was not in writing, so it is unenforceable. Lydia cannot enforce the $2,200 price. The original $1,200 contract remains in effect, but Lydia is not suing on that — she is suing on the unenforceable modification. Judgment for Martha.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (wrong):** This choice tempts the student to think that an increase in market value somehow excuses performance or invalidates a contract modification. But the fair market value of the goods has nothing to do with whether a modification satisfies the Statute of Frauds. The Statute of Frauds is a writing requirement, not a fairness or equity test. Even if the value had not changed at all, the oral modification would still fail for lack of a writing. The FMV increase is a red herring.

**B (wrong):** This choice plays on the misconception that payment is required for a contract to be formed. Lydia's offer called for acceptance by sending an order — Martha's September 3 letter was a valid acceptance even though payment had not yet been made. A contract was formed on September 3 when Martha dispatched her acceptance. The fact that payment hadn't been received before the September 4 phone call is irrelevant to whether the subsequent oral modification is enforceable.

**C (wrong) — Gold Key applies:** This is the dominant trap. Students who spot that the modification might lack consideration may reach for promissory estoppel as a saving doctrine. But two things kill this choice. First, under UCC § 2-209(1), a modification needs no consideration to be binding — so the consideration gap this answer implicitly targets doesn't exist. Second, and independently, UCC § 2-209(3) requires the Statute of Frauds to be satisfied for modifications of contracts involving goods priced at $500 or more. The oral modification here fails the Statute of Frauds. Promissory estoppel does not override the Statute of Frauds writing requirement in this context. Additionally, the facts do not show that Lydia changed her position to her detriment in reliance on Martha's oral promise — she simply demanded a higher price. **Gold Key:** The Statute of Frauds under § 2-209(3) bars enforcement of the oral modification regardless of reliance.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** UCC § 2-209(3) — "The requirements of the statute of frauds section of this Article (Section 2-201) must be satisfied if the contract as modified is within its provisions."

**UCC § 2-201(1):** A contract for the sale of goods for the price of $500 or more is not enforceable unless there is some writing sufficient to indicate that a contract for sale has been made between the parties and signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought.

**Application:** The original contract (Lydia's offer, Martha's acceptance) was formed through writings — the catalog/cover letter and the acceptance letter — and is enforceable. The September 4 oral agreement modified the price term from $1,200 to $2,200. As modified, the contract is for the sale of a good at a price ($2,200) well above the $500 Statute of Frauds threshold. Under § 2-209(3), the Statute of Frauds must be satisfied for the modified contract. The oral modification was not in writing. Therefore, the modification is unenforceable.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-209(3) (Modifications); UCC § 2-201 (Statute of Frauds for Sale of Goods). See also *Green Construction Co. v. First Indemnity of America Insurance Co.*, 735 F. Supp. 1254, 1261 (D.N.J. 1990) (applying § 2-209(3) to bar enforcement of oral modification of contract for goods over $500); *Trad Industries v. Brogan*, 805 P.2d 54, 59 (Mont. 1991) (same). Cornell LII: https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-209
