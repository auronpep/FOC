## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Nine members of a private rare-hymnal theft ring, including its leader Paul, were indicted for the murder of Daniel, a tenth member who had become an informant. Paul pleaded guilty.

At the trial of the other eight members, the state's evidence showed the following: Paul announced a lakeside hymn-sing dinner to welcome Daniel back after Daniel's release from jail. The dinner was a setup. Paul had learned that Daniel had gained his release by informing investigators about the ring's thefts. Paul decided to use the dinner to show the others what happened to informants. He told no one about his plan. At the dinner, after everyone had consumed large quantities of homemade wine, Paul announced that Daniel was an informant and struck him in the chest with a heavy iron lantern in front of the others. The eight other members watched and did nothing while Daniel slowly died.

The jury found the eight other members guilty of murder, and they appealed. Should the appellate court uphold the convictions?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

Eight people watch their leader kill a member after no warning, aid, encouragement, or legal duty; can their murder convictions stand?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. No, because murder requires a specific intent to kill and there is insufficient evidence that the eight members intended Daniel's death.

B. Yes, because voluntary intoxication does not excuse criminal responsibility.

C. No, because merely being present when another person commits a crime does not make a person an accomplice.

D. Yes, because the eight members did not try to get help after Daniel was struck.

### 5. Correct answer

C (original key A).

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. The eight members were present and silent, but the evidence does not show that they helped, encouraged, planned, agreed to help, or had a legal duty to prevent Paul's killing. Gold Key: presence plus knowledge is not accomplice liability unless it becomes aid, encouragement, agreement to aid, attempted aid, or a legal-duty omission. Silver Key: when the call asks whether convictions should stand, separate the result from the reason; the right result needs the accomplice-liability reason.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because it reaches the same "No" result and points to missing intent. The breaker is the reason: murder is not limited to a specific intent to kill, so the choice sells a false mental-state rule instead of the missing accomplice act. Gold Key: the deciding defect is not only intent; it is the absence of aid, encouragement, agreement to aid, attempted aid, or legal duty.

B is attractive because the stem includes heavy drinking and voluntary intoxication is a familiar criminal-law phrase. The breaker is responsiveness: the eight members are not liable because they did not act as accomplices, regardless of whether voluntary intoxication would excuse someone else.

D is attractive because watching a dying person without helping feels blameworthy. The breaker is the invented duty: without a legal duty to act or other aid or encouragement, failure to rescue does not turn bystanders into murder accomplices.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited answer is correct under accomplice-liability doctrine. Model Penal Code sec. 2.06(3)(a) requires, for ordinary accomplice liability, that the defendant act with the purpose of promoting or facilitating the offense by soliciting, aiding, agreeing to aid, attempting to aid, or failing to act despite a legal duty to prevent the offense. Hicks v. United States, 150 U.S. 442 (1893), confirms the classic boundary: mere presence at a killing is not enough unless the person aids, abets, advises, or encourages the crime. The transformed facts preserve the original rule, issue, and credited outcome. Paul alone formed the plan and struck Daniel; the eight others merely watched and had no stated legal duty to intervene. Their murder convictions should not be upheld.
