# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

Sarah was furious. The head chef at the community-kitchen ministry where Sarah worked — Hannah — had publicly corrected her in front of the volunteer team after a near-miss on the serving line, and Sarah felt humiliated. Sarah stewed for a week and decided Hannah should pay for the embarrassment, but not with her life.

The ministry used an industrial-strength oven and grill degreaser (the same brand the kitchen had always used to strip carbonized grease from the commercial ovens) to deep-clean the line at the end of each shift. Sarah went online and downloaded the manufacturer's Safety Data Sheet for the degreaser. The SDS stated that the product "is not expected to be lethal by ingestion or skin contact" but warned that "prolonged skin contact causes severe chemical burns, that eye contact causes permanent eye damage, and that vapor inhalation causes respiratory damage typically requiring hospitalization."

Two days later, while Hannah was on break, Sarah poured roughly a cup of the degreaser into the inner pocket of Hannah's work apron, which Hannah kept hanging on her station hook. Sarah then reseated the apron so the spill would not be visible. Hannah returned, took her apron home, and the next morning loaned it to her friend Ruth, a church member who sometimes helped with catering jobs, to wear for a private luncheon Ruth was hosting. Ruth wore the apron against her skin for several hours while plating food. The degreaser soaked through, caused severe chemical burns over Ruth's torso, and Ruth died the following day. Hannah, who never put the apron on, suffered only minor skin irritation on one hand.

If Sarah is charged with the murder of Ruth, she should be found:

- **A.** not guilty, because Sarah did not know that Ruth would be exposed to the degreaser.
- **B.** guilty, because Ruth's death resulted from an act that Sarah performed with the intent to cause great bodily harm to a human being.
- **C.** not guilty, because Sarah did not intend to cause the death of any person.
- **D.** guilty, because the use of an industrial-strength chemical degreaser on another person is an inherently dangerous activity.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Murder is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought. Malice aforethought is satisfied by any of four mental states: (1) intent to kill, (2) intent to cause great bodily harm, (3) depraved-heart recklessness, or (4) the felony-murder rule. Sarah never wanted anyone to die, but she deliberately used a substance that the manufacturer's own Safety Data Sheet said would cause severe chemical burns, permanent eye damage, and respiratory injury typically requiring hospitalization. That is the textbook profile of an intent to cause great bodily harm. **Gold Key:** Malice has four faces, and an intent to inflict serious bodily injury is one of them — the killer's "I didn't mean to kill" is not a defense. Under the transferred-intent doctrine, Sarah's GBH-aimed act toward Hannah is treated as the malice for any death the act causes, including Ruth's; the killer is liable for murder of the actual victim, not just the intended one. Ruth died from severe chemical burns that flowed directly from the degreaser Sarah put in the apron pocket. Sarah is guilty of Ruth's murder.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A — not guilty, because Sarah did not know that Ruth would be exposed to the degreaser.** This choice sells a transferred-intent bait: it identifies a real doctrine and then misapplies it. The transferred-intent rule answers the question, "If Sarah meant to harm Hannah, can the law treat her malice as if it pointed at Ruth?" The answer is yes. The wrong-direction move is to flip that doctrine into a "no knowledge of the bystander → no liability" rule, which is not the law. A defendant who directs a harmful act at one person is liable for the harm that act actually causes to a bystander, even if the bystander's existence was unexpected. **Silver Key:** Transfer moves the *malice*, not the *knowledge* — Sarah did not need to know Ruth would be there; she only needed to know the act would cause serious harm. Spotting that A asks the right question ("who did she know was exposed?") and gives the wrong-direction answer ("therefore no liability") is the kill.

**C — not guilty, because Sarah did not intend to cause the death of any person.** The dominant trap. The cognitive move is the intuitively fair-sounding one: "She didn't want anyone to die; she only wanted to hurt Hannah; murder is about killing, not hurting; therefore not murder." That move silently collapses the four flavors of malice into one. The breaker is that malice is not synonymous with intent to kill. Intent to cause great bodily harm is an independent, sufficient form of malice, and the law treats an unintended death flowing from a GBH-aimed act as murder. Sarah's own research — the SDS — put the GBH profile in her hands before she poured. **Gold Key:** Malice has four faces; "I didn't mean to kill" answers the wrong one.

**D — guilty, because the use of an industrial-strength chemical degreaser on another person is an inherently dangerous activity.** This choice sells a category swap: it pulls a civil-law / tort concept (inherently / ultra-hazardous activity as a basis for strict liability) into a criminal malice analysis. Dangerous activity, standing alone, is not malice aforethought. Malice is a mens rea requirement; a "dangerous means" theory does not satisfy it. The breaker is the "one mold, one filter" rule visible in the answer text: the choice is true (a degreaser is dangerous) but not responsive to the actual call (does Sarah have the mental state for murder?). It confuses the actus reus lens with the mens rea lens.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested rule (unchanged from source):** Murder = unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought; malice aforethought includes intent to kill, intent to cause great bodily harm, depraved-heart recklessness, and felony murder; an intent to cause GBH aimed at one person transfers to an unintended victim who dies from the act.

**Authority floor (real, primary):**

- **18 U.S.C. § 1111** — federal murder statute, defining murder as "the unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought." (Authority: rule number.)
- **Model Penal Code § 210.2** — murder is homicide committed "purposely or knowingly" or "recklessly under circumstances manifesting extreme indifference to the value of human life." **MPC § 210.0(3)** defines serious bodily injury as "bodily injury which creates a substantial risk of death or which causes serious, permanent disfigurement, or protracted loss or impairment of the function of any bodily member or organ" — exactly the harm profile on the degreaser SDS. (Authority: rule number.)
- **People v. Bland, 28 Cal. 4th 313 (2002)** — the California Supreme Court's leading articulation of transferred intent: "In its classic form, the doctrine of transferred intent applies when the defendant intends to kill one person but mistakenly kills another. The intent to kill the intended target is deemed to transfer to the unintended victim so that the defendant is guilty of murder." (Authority: leading case, https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/28/313.html.)
- **LII Wex, "malice aforethought"** — express malice includes "killings where a person intended to cause death or grievous bodily harm to another." (Authority: encyclopedic restatement of common law, https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/malice_aforethought.)

**Application to the right answer.** Sarah researched the degreaser and read the SDS, which warned of severe chemical burns, permanent eye damage, and respiratory damage typically requiring hospitalization. The MPC § 210.0(3) definition of "serious bodily injury" tracks those harms almost verbatim (substantial risk of death / serious permanent disfigurement / protracted impairment of organ function). Sarah therefore acted with the intent to cause great bodily harm to a human being. That is express malice. Under People v. Bland, the GBH-malice Sarah directed at Hannah transfers to Ruth, the actual victim of the act that flowed from the degreaser-soaked apron. Sarah is guilty of Ruth's murder. The fact that Hannah survived and that Sarah did not know Ruth would wear the apron is irrelevant: transferred intent does not require knowledge of the bystander's existence; it requires that the killing flowed from the very act aimed at the intended target.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A — "not guilty, because Sarah did not know that Ruth would be exposed to the degreaser."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal.** The transferred-intent doctrine is the right area of law, and the choice is built on a real transferred-intent idea. The defect is that the choice applies the doctrine backwards. Transferred intent says: malice directed at one person is malice as to the person actually harmed. A inverts that into: no knowledge of the bystander → no malice as to the bystander. The bait is real doctrine used in the wrong direction. C3-read: the answer's own premise (Sarah did not know Ruth was there) is exactly the situation transferred intent exists to handle.
2. **Student label.** `transferred-intent-bait`
3. **What a true-and-responsive version would look like.** "Guilty, because the malice Sarah directed at Hannah transferred to Ruth under the doctrine of transferred intent, even though Sarah did not know Ruth would be exposed." That phrasing is the *true* transferred-intent rule; A's phrasing is its mirror.
4. **Lawyer confirmation.** People v. Bland, 28 Cal. 4th 313 (2002): "In its classic form, the doctrine of transferred intent applies when the defendant intends to kill one person but mistakenly kills another. The intent to kill the intended target is deemed to transfer to the unintended victim so that the defendant is guilty of murder." The killer's lack of awareness of the actual victim is not an exception to transferred intent; it is the textbook case for it.

### B — "guilty, because Ruth's death resulted from an act that Sarah performed with the intent to cause great bodily harm to a human being."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal.** This is the residual. The SDS gave Sarah the GBH profile; the four-faces-of-malice anchor names intent to cause GBH as a sufficient form of malice; transferred intent routes that malice to Ruth, the actual victim of the degreaser-soaked apron. B is the only choice that is both true on the facts and responsive to the precise call.
2. **Student label.** `GBH-intent` (correct answer).
3. **What a true-and-responsive version would look like.** This is the true-and-responsive version.
4. **Lawyer confirmation.** MPC § 210.0(3) defines serious bodily injury as "bodily injury which creates a substantial risk of death or which causes serious, permanent disfigurement, or protracted loss or impairment of the function of any bodily member or organ." The SDS warns of severe chemical burns (substantial risk of death / protracted impairment of organ function), permanent eye damage (serious permanent disfigurement / protracted loss of organ function), and respiratory damage typically requiring hospitalization (substantial risk of death). MPC § 210.2 makes homicide committed purposely or knowingly into murder. 18 U.S.C. § 1111 codifies malice aforethought for federal law. People v. Bland supplies transferred intent. Sarah is guilty of Ruth's murder.

### C — "not guilty, because Sarah did not intend to cause the death of any person." *(dominant trap)*

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal.** True that Sarah did not intend to kill; false that murder requires intent to kill. The four-faces-of-malice anchor names intent to cause GBH as an independent form of malice. The trap silently collapses the four flavors of malice into one. This is the most common collapse on this item type.
2. **Student label.** `no-intent-to-kill`
3. **What a true-and-responsive version would look like.** A wrong-direction version of the four-faces-of-malice anchor. The true anchor is: malice has four independent faces, and "I didn't intend to kill" is not a defense when the killer acted with any of the other three. C's phrasing reduces malice to intent to kill only.
4. **Lawyer confirmation.** LII Wex on malice aforethought: "Express malice murders included killings where a person intended to cause death or grievous bodily harm to another." The historical common-law rule is well settled; the MPC modernizes the language but reaches the same place (MPC § 210.2(a) — purposely or knowingly — covers both intent to kill and intent to cause serious bodily injury).

### D — "guilty, because the use of an industrial-strength chemical degreaser on another person is an inherently dangerous activity."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal.** This is a category-swap: it pulls a civil-law / tort concept (inherently / ultra-hazardous activity as a basis for strict liability) into a criminal malice analysis. The defect is visible from the answer text alone: it states a category ("inherently dangerous activity") and treats that category as if it were the rule on a *mens rea* call. The civil doctrine is about the *means*; the criminal call here is about the *mental state*.
2. **Student label.** `dangerous-activity`
3. **What a true-and-responsive version would look like.** "Guilty, because Sarah acted with malice aforethought (specifically, the intent to cause great bodily harm to Hannah, which transferred to Ruth)." D's phrasing would have to import the mental state to become true and responsive.
4. **Lawyer confirmation.** Civil-law ultra-hazardous-activity doctrines (e.g., Restatement (Second) of Torts § 519) impose strict liability for harm caused by abnormally dangerous activities. They do not supply the mens rea for criminal murder, which is a separate question. The MPC and common-law rules on murder both require a culpable mental state; "dangerous means" alone does not satisfy it.
