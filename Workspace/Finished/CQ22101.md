## 1. Final question (revised stem + call)

A city ordinance placed every lot in the Cedar Gate neighborhood in a residential district and allowed retail or service businesses only in the city's market district. The ordinance also stated that no business could be operated on land zoned residential. When Cedar Gate was created, its recorded neighborhood plan said residents could run quiet home craft businesses if the business did not disturb other residents.

Lydia owned a cottage in Cedar Gate and operated a Scripture-card lettering service from a small studio in the cottage. Ruth entered into a contract to buy both the cottage and the lettering service. After signing the contract, Ruth learned about the city ordinance barring businesses in the residential district. She could not have discovered the violation by reasonable inquiry before signing. Ruth immediately told Lydia that she would not complete the purchase because of the zoning violation.

If Lydia sues Ruth for breach of contract, should the court find in Lydia's favor?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**D is correct.** Every land-sale contract carries an implied promise that the seller will deliver marketable title at closing. Marketable title is not merely a paper-chain concept; it also means the buyer will not be forced to take property in its present condition with a serious legal cloud that exposes the buyer to litigation or enforcement. **Gold Key:** the mere existence of zoning restrictions usually does not make title unmarketable, but an existing violation of zoning restrictions does. Lydia is operating the very business Ruth contracted to buy in a residential district where businesses are barred. Ruth does not have to close and inherit that fight. **Silver Key:** in a two-"No" pair, pick the answer that names the legal reason the buyer can walk away, not the answer that merely repeats a helpful fact from the stem.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A - No, because Ruth could not have discovered the zoning violation by reasonable inquiry before entering the contract.** This answer feels fair because Ruth was not careless, and it lands on the right result. But the operative reason is not the buyer's investigation ability. The breaker is the seller's inability to deliver marketable title while the property is being used in violation of zoning law. **Silver Key:** right result plus the wrong reason is still a trap; lock onto the title defect, not the sympathy fact.

**B - Yes, because Lydia's lettering service was allowed by the recorded Cedar Gate neighborhood plan.** The private plan is attractive because it is a property document and it specifically blesses quiet home craft businesses. But private permission does not cancel a public zoning prohibition. The city ordinance still makes Lydia's current use unlawful, so the buyer would be taking property exposed to enforcement.

**C - Yes, because the city ordinance prohibiting Lydia's business existed before the contract was formed.** This answer sells the idea that Ruth bought subject to old public law and therefore assumed the risk. The missing distinction is the whole item: the existence of zoning restrictions is different from a present violation of those restrictions. **Gold Key:** zoning restrictions alone usually do not defeat marketability; existing zoning violations can.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule:** In a land-sale contract, the seller is expected to deliver marketable title unless the contract validly provides otherwise. Marketable title is title free from reasonable doubt and from defects that expose the buyer to substantial litigation risk. The mere existence of zoning laws normally does not make title unmarketable, but a current violation of zoning or land-use restrictions can make title unmarketable because the buyer would take property subject to enforcement or litigation.

**Authority:** `Lohmeyer v. Bower`, 170 Kan. 442, 227 P.2d 102 (1951), states the exam rule cleanly: present violations of zoning/building restrictions, not the mere existence of those restrictions, made the title doubtful and unmarketable because they exposed the buyer to litigation. Cornell Wex's marketable-title entry also reflects the modern black-letter description that marketable title is title free from ownership disputes or litigation threats and lists zoning-law violations as a kind of encumbrance that can make title unmarketable.

**Confirmation:** The credited outcome is unchanged from the source: the buyer should not be liable for refusing to close. The legal issue is unchanged: whether an existing zoning violation in the property/business being sold excuses the buyer from completing the land-sale contract. The public-law/private-plan trap is unchanged: a private neighborhood plan permitting the use cannot override a city zoning prohibition. Not on the doctrinal-currency hotlist. Authority floor satisfied by a real leading case.
