# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

Daniel lived in State A, where he ran a pottery and olive-oil trade. Lydia, a merchant who lived in State B, sued Daniel in a State B state court to collect $9,000 she claimed he owed her on an unpaid wholesale order. Daniel traveled to State B to attend a regional market fair and to visit his brother Timothy. While Daniel was at the fair, a process server personally handed him the summons and complaint. Daniel entered a special appearance and moved to quash service, arguing that he had insufficient contacts with State B to support personal jurisdiction. Does the State B court have personal jurisdiction over Daniel?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A non-resident defendant, briefly in the forum state for unrelated reasons, is personally handed the summons and complaint while physically present there and contests personal jurisdiction for lack of contacts. Does the forum have personal jurisdiction over him?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**D is correct.** Personal service on a defendant who is physically present in the forum state is, by itself, a constitutionally sufficient basis for personal jurisdiction — "transient" or "tag" jurisdiction. It does not matter that Daniel lives elsewhere, that his visit was brief, or that the market fair and his brother have nothing to do with Lydia's claim; once the process server handed him the papers inside State B, the court had power over him. **Gold Key:** in-state personal service on a physically present defendant independently confers general personal jurisdiction — no minimum-contacts analysis, no consent, no long-arm required, even for a transitory visit. **Silver Key:** the stem hands you Daniel's "insufficient contacts" argument; that priming is bait — in-state service is a separate traditional basis that bypasses the contacts test entirely, so don't run a contacts analysis the facts make irrelevant.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A — "No, because Daniel was in State B only to attend the market fair and to visit his brother."** This is the trained reflex: after *International Shoe*, "Does the defendant have enough contacts?" feels like the whole of personal jurisdiction, and a brief, unrelated visit looks like far too little — the stem even frames it as Daniel's own argument. The breaker is that minimum-contacts is the test for a defendant who is *absent* from the forum; when the defendant is personally served *inside* the forum, in-state service is an independent traditional basis that needs no contacts at all. **Gold Key:** tag jurisdiction bypasses the contacts inquiry.

**B — "No, because nothing indicates that Daniel consented to jurisdiction."** Consent is a real, valid basis for personal jurisdiction, so its absence feels like a missing ingredient. The breaker is that consent is *one* path to jurisdiction, not a prerequisite — its absence cannot defeat jurisdiction when another basis (in-state service) is independently satisfied.

**C — "Yes, because Lydia filed her lawsuit in a State B court."** It reaches the right outcome and points to a true, concrete fact — the suit really is pending in State B — which makes it feel sufficient. The breaker is that where a plaintiff chooses to file says nothing about the court's power over the defendant; if filing alone conferred jurisdiction, the personal-jurisdiction requirement would be meaningless.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested rule (unchanged from the original):** transient / "tag" personal jurisdiction — personal service of process on a defendant physically present in the forum state confers personal jurisdiction consistent with the Due Process Clause, regardless of the defendant's contacts with the state, the brevity of the visit, or the relationship of the suit to the visit.

- **Controlling authority:** *Burnham v. Superior Court*, 495 U.S. 604 (1990) — the Supreme Court unanimously upheld jurisdiction over a non-resident personally served while temporarily in the forum, holding the minimum-contacts test of *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), is **not required** when the defendant is served in-state; *International Shoe* and *Shaffer v. Heitner*, 433 U.S. 186 (1977), govern *absent* defendants. Grounded in the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause and a long historical pedigree (the *Pennoyer v. Neff* presence tradition).
- **Why A fails:** minimum-contacts analysis applies to absent defendants; it is the wrong framework for an in-state-served defendant.
- **Why B fails:** consent (and domicile, and in-state service) are alternative traditional bases; any one suffices, so the absence of consent is not dispositive.
- **Why C fails:** the forum of filing is not a basis of personal jurisdiction over the defendant.

Credited outcome **D = original B ("Yes, because personally served in State B")** is correct and **unchanged** from the source item. Tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are identical; only the surface story moved. `agrees_with_official_key: true`.

**Doctrinal-currency check (PJ is on the hotlist):** the recent PJ development is *Mallory v. Norfolk Southern Ry.*, 600 U.S. 122 (2023), but it concerns **consent-by-registration** for corporations — a different traditional basis — and does not disturb *Burnham*'s tag-jurisdiction rule. Framing is current; outcome unchanged. Not `NEEDS_HUMAN`.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):** *Burnham v. Superior Court*, 495 U.S. 604 (1990); *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945); U.S. Const. amend. XIV (Due Process). Authority floor met (leading case + constitutional provision).
