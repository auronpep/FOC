## 6. Full right-answer explanation

**A is correct.** Paul was physically pinned and not free to leave. A deputy then made a direct threat and asked what Paul had done with Ruth. That is the confession layer, not the search layer.

**Gold Key:** Custody + police words likely to get an incriminating answer = Miranda danger zone. Without warnings and a valid waiver, the prosecution cannot use the custodial statement in its case-in-chief.

**Silver Key:** When the call asks whether an **admission** is suppressed, sort the answers by layer first. Search, hot pursuit, and emergency-search answers are adjacent-call bait unless they explain why this statement could be used without a valid waiver.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B is wrong.** It is the dominant trap and survives from the original geometry. It answers a neighboring question: whether the farm entry/search was lawful and whether that violation would suppress evidence. The call asks whether Paul’s **admission** should be suppressed. **Silver Key:** stay on the statement layer. Do not let a search fact pull you into a search-remedy answer.

**C is wrong.** “Hot pursuit” belongs to the arrest/search-entry lane. The deputy’s pursuit may explain how the officer caught Paul, but it does not answer whether Paul validly waived his right to silence before answering a direct accusatory question.

**D is wrong.** Public safety can be a real Miranda exception, which makes this a better trap than the original vague emergency-search wording. But the question asked “what did you do with Ruth,” after Paul was pinned. That is a past-crime confession question, not a narrow question to neutralize an immediate public danger. **Gold Key:** the public-safety exception is narrow. It does not erase Miranda whenever police are in a tense investigation.

## 8. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The rule and outcome are unchanged from the original. Miranda governs statements from custodial interrogation; the Supreme Court describes the problem as admissibility of statements obtained from a person subjected to custodial police interrogation. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) Innis defines interrogation to include express questioning and police words or actions reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. ([Legal Information Institute][2]) Here, Paul was pinned and directly asked/threatened to confess. That supports suppression under the Miranda/waiver theory reflected in answer A.

The revised D tests the public-safety exception more cleanly. Quarles recognizes a public-safety exception when questions are reasonably prompted by immediate danger, such as locating a hidden gun posing a public danger. ([Legal Information Institute][3]) Paul’s statement was not elicited by a narrow immediate-safety question; it was elicited by a threat to explain what he did with Ruth. For B, the farm-search trap remains legally adjacent. Open fields generally do not receive the same Fourth Amendment protection as the home or curtilage, and fences or no-trespassing signs do not by themselves create a protected expectation of privacy in open fields. ([Justia Law][4])

The public-safety exception is narrow: it covers questions reasonably prompted by an immediate safety danger, not a threat-driven request for a past-crime confession after the suspect is secured.

## Silver Key(s)

### SK-CRIM-CONFESSION-LAYER-01

When the call asks whether an admission is suppressed, sort choices by layer first: confession/waiver beats search, pursuit, and emergency-search frames.
