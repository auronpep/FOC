### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Daniel has committed forgery. He falsely made a check payable to "cash" for $750, signed Stephen's name without authority, and intended to use the check to obtain money he was not owed. A completed check purporting to draw funds on a depositor's account has apparent legal efficacy because, if presented and honored, it would operate to transfer funds from the named account to the holder. **Gold Key:** forgery is complete when the defendant falsely makes a writing with apparent legal efficacy with intent to defraud; the victim need not actually lose money, and the forger need not successfully utter the document. The "false making" is the actus reus.

**Silver Key:** when the call asks "has the defendant committed forgery," ask whether the false making has occurred. Loss and successful uttering are not elements of forgery.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. No, because Stephen did not actually lose money yet. (Dominant trap.)**

This is the analytically most attractive wrong answer. The student reaches for "no harm, no foul" — the lay intuition that forgery requires the victim to have actually lost money. The breaker is the **Gold Key:** the victim's actual loss is not an element of forgery. The actus reus is the false making with intent to defraud; loss (if it occurs) is a downstream consequence, not a required element. On these facts, the false making occurred when Daniel wrote the check, signed Stephen's name, and intended to defraud.

**C. Yes, but only if Daniel successfully cashes the check.**

This answer is attractive to a student who reads "plans to cash" as a half-step and concludes the forgery is not yet complete until the cashing succeeds. The breaker is the **Silver Key:** forgery is complete at the false making. Successful uttering is a separate crime (uttering a forged instrument), but it is not a required element of forgery itself. Daniel completed the false making when he wrote the check, signed Stephen's name, and formed the intent to defraud.

**D. No, because checks are not writings with legal significance.**

This answer is attractive to a student who is uncertain what counts as a "writing" in the forgery sense. The breaker is the **Gold Key:** a check is precisely the type of writing the forgery rule covers because a check has apparent legal efficacy — it purports to draw funds from a depositor's account and, if presented and honored, operates to transfer those funds. Checks are the textbook example of a writing with apparent legal efficacy.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18111, a Criminal Law item involving a finder of an unused checkbook who writes a check payable to "cash," signs the owner's name without authority, and plans to cash it. The official key is C — the answer is yes, the finder committed forgery because the false making of a check with apparent legal efficacy, with intent to defraud, satisfies the elements of forgery without regard to actual loss or successful uttering.

The doctrinal authority is well-settled. At common law, forgery is "the false making or material alteration, with intent to defraud, of any writing that, if genuine, would apparently be of legal efficacy or the foundation of a legal liability." Model Penal Code § 241.1 codifies the modern rule: a person is guilty of forgery if, with purpose to defraud anyone or with knowledge that he is facilitating a fraud to be perpetrated by anyone, he "alters any writing of another without his authority" or "makes, completes, executes, authenticates, issues or transfers any writing so that it purports to be the act of another who did not authorize that act." The "apparent legal efficacy" requirement is satisfied when the writing, if genuine, would be the foundation of a legal liability — and a check payable to "cash" that purports to draw on a depositor's account fits that description exactly. The Modern Federal Forgery Statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1513 (forging United States obligations) and the general federal forgery framework, treats the false making with intent as the operative act; uttering is a separate offense, but the false making itself is complete at the moment the writing is falsely made. State forgery statutes track the same rule, requiring (i) a writing, (ii) false making, (iii) apparent legal efficacy, and (iv) intent to defraud. The transformed outcome is identical to the original: Daniel completed forgery when he wrote the check, signed Stephen's name without authority, and formed the intent to defraud — irrespective of whether Stephen ever lost money or whether Daniel ever cashed the check.
