## 6. Right-Answer Explanation

**A is correct.**

The call asks for what is NOT admissible on the issue of whether Stephen's Apiary produced this honey. A prior judgment from a different lawsuit — even one involving substantially similar facts — does not tend to make it more or less probable that Stephen's Apiary produced THIS jar. The prior case resolved a legal dispute between different parties; whatever that judgment concluded, it does not establish who made the item now at issue. It is entirely irrelevant to the specific factual question before the jury.

## 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B is wrong.** The branded wax seal on the jar naturally suggests Stephen's Apiary manufactured it — a mark is a familiar signal of origin, and on a "NOT admissible" call, a student might hesitate, wondering whether the mark could be counterfeit and therefore unreliable. But the mark's admissibility does not depend on whether it was legitimately affixed; its presence on the jar tends to make it more probable that Stephen's Apiary (or someone copying their mark) produced it, which goes directly to the fact at issue. An inscription or label purporting to have been affixed in the course of business indicating ownership, control, or origin is self-authenticating. The seal is admissible.

**C is wrong** *(DOMINANT TRAP).* Placing the disputed jar alongside a jar of concededly genuine Stephen's Apiary honey for the jury to compare is an intuitive and powerful way to assess origin — yet students are often drawn to C, believing that expert comparison is required rather than jury comparison. That requirement is invented. **Gold Key (GK-EVIDENCE-PRIOR-JUDGMENT-ORIGIN-01):** A prior judgment against a party in a different lawsuit is not admissible to prove a factual issue in the current case — which tells you A is the answer, not C. C is wrong precisely because jury authentication by comparison with authenticated specimens is expressly permitted; no expert is required.

**D is wrong.** Testimony that the honey was purchased from a vendor who regularly received stock from Stephen's Apiary traces the jar to Stephen's distribution channel, making it more probable that Stephen's produced it. While circumstantial, relevance requires only a tendency to make the contested fact more probable — not conclusive proof. D is clearly admissible.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Governing rule:** FRE 401 — evidence is relevant if it has any tendency to make a fact of consequence more or less probable. FRE 402 — relevant evidence is generally admissible.

**Why A is NOT admissible:** A prior judgment from a different case does not address who made THIS honey. It resolves a legal dispute between different parties; even in "substantially similar" cases, that judgment does not establish a fact for the new proceeding. The judgment has no tendency to make it more or less probable that Stephen's Apiary produced this specific jar.

**Why B IS admissible:** FRE 902(7) — inscriptions, signs, tags, or labels purporting to have been affixed in the course of business and indicating ownership, control, or origin are self-authenticating. The branded wax seal qualifies.

**Why C IS admissible:** FRE 901(b)(3) — authentication by comparison of the questioned item with authenticated specimens by the trier of fact is expressly permitted. No expert required.

**Why D IS admissible:** FRE 401 — testimony placing a product on a manufacturer's regular distribution path has a tendency to make that manufacturer's production of the item more probable. Admissible.

**Authority:** FRE 401, 402, 901(b)(3), 902(7). Prior judgments from separate cases are inadmissible as substantive evidence of facts in subsequent independent actions — the judgment resolves different parties' dispute and has no tendency to prove who made a specific item in the new case.
