Ruth owned a private hymn-recording studio that was being renovated for a family worship album project. On February 6, Ruth entered into a contract with Timothy, an audio technician, to calibrate and test the studio's microphone system. Does Ruth have valid legal grounds to cancel the contract with Timothy and hire a replacement?

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Timothy asked for a delay, Ruth refused, and Timothy then said he would try to start on the required date. That is not an unequivocal statement that he would not perform or could not perform. It creates possible concern, but concern is not the same thing as anticipatory repudiation. Without an unequivocal refusal or inability, Ruth does not yet have valid legal grounds to cancel and hire a replacement.

Gold Key: A party anticipatorily repudiates only by clearly showing that performance will not or cannot happen; a request for more time or uncertain language gives the other side, at most, an assurance problem, not automatic cancellation.

Silver Key: Separate the emotional signal from the legal signal. "I am behind and want more time" may create insecurity, but "I will try to start on time" is not a clean refusal.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because Timothy asked for a delay after admitting that another job had him behind schedule. That feels unfair to Ruth, but the implied covenant is not the tested lever here. The breaker is the call: Ruth needs valid grounds to cancel before performance is due, and the facts do not show a bad-faith evasion of the contract. Gold Key: the key pre-performance question is unequivocal nonperformance, not whether the request made Ruth frustrated.

C sells the right "No" result with a remedy detour. Timothy's ability to start on April 20 would prevent Ruth from cancelling for anticipatory repudiation, but that does not mean Timothy would be entitled to force Ruth to continue through specific performance. Service-contract disputes are normally handled by damages, and the cancellation question does not turn on Timothy's equitable remedy.

D is the dominant trap because it converts Ruth's insecurity into the missing legal element. Insecurity can matter, but it does not equal repudiation. Ruth may have reason to worry or seek assurance, yet Timothy did not say that he would begin late or refuse to perform. Gold Key: a delay request or uncertainty points to adequate assurance, not automatic cancellation. Silver Key: do not let the word "insecure" substitute for a clear refusal.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The verified outline code is `56050403`, listed in `OUTLINE_CODES_COMPLETE.md` as Contracts, Performance, Breach, and Excuse > Breach > Anticipatory repudiation.

The governing common-law authority is Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 250, which treats a statement or act as repudiation only when it is sufficiently positive to be reasonably understood as a refusal or inability to perform. Restatement Section 250 comment b confirms the exam rule used here: expressions of doubt or requests to modify performance are not enough by themselves. Restatement Section 251 provides the adjacent remedy when reasonable grounds for insecurity arise: the obligee may demand adequate assurance and, in proper circumstances, suspend performance while waiting for assurance. Restatement Section 359 confirms why the specific-performance distractor does not explain the result: specific performance is unavailable when damages would adequately protect the injured party.
