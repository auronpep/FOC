### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.** The patrol-car statement should not be suppressed on either asserted ground.

**Gold Key:** Miranda is not triggered by custody alone. It is triggered by custodial interrogation, which includes express questioning and its functional equivalent. Here, Martha was under arrest and not warned, but the statement came during transport without a question or equivalent prompting.

**Gold Key:** Deliberate elicitation requires a police action designed to draw out the incriminating statement. Paul hoped Martha would talk, but the stem gives no act beyond arrest, notice of the warrant, and ordinary transport.

**Silver Key:** This is a two-switch answer array. Test the first ground and second ground separately. First switch: no-warning ground is off. Second switch: deliberate-elicitation ground is off. With both switches off, the denial answer is forced.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** The attractive move is to stop at “custody + no Miranda warnings.” That gets one fact right but misses the Gold Key threshold: no interrogation or equivalent prompting occurred, so the first ground does not support suppression.

**B.** The attractive move is to treat two constitutional labels as adding up to suppression. The Silver Key blocks that move: each ground is a separate switch, and both switches are off.

**D.** The attractive move is to treat Paul’s hope as the same thing as deliberate elicitation. Gold Key: deliberate elicitation requires police action designed to draw out the statement, not a silent wish that the suspect might volunteer one.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The source row credits denial of the motion, with 51% choosing the credited answer and the highest wrong-answer pick rate on the no-warning-only ground. The transformed item keeps the same two suppression grounds and same no-suppression outcome. 

For Miranda, the governing line is custody plus interrogation, not custody alone. *Miranda v. Arizona* states that volunteered statements are not barred by the Fifth Amendment, and *Rhode Island v. Innis* defines interrogation as express questioning or its functional equivalent: police words or actions reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. *Arizona v. Mauro* is especially close to this item’s trap: officers do not interrogate a suspect merely by hoping the suspect will incriminate himself. ([Justia Law][1])

For the post-indictment Sixth Amendment layer, *Massiah* and *Fellers* apply the deliberate-elicitation standard after formal proceedings have begun, but *Kuhlmann v. Wilson* requires action beyond merely listening that is designed deliberately to elicit incriminating remarks. Paul did not question Martha, stage a conversation, use an informant, or make a prompt designed to draw out the statement. ([Justia Law][2])
