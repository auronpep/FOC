## 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is the best defense. Lydia may have created foreseeable risk to Paul, the box, or people crowded around the checkout table. Hannah, however, was three streets away and was injured only through a concealed chip whose signal was not apparent from the outside of the box. Under the majority Palsgraf approach, the duty question asks whether this plaintiff was within the reasonably apparent orbit of risk. She was not.

Gold Key: Majority Palsgraf is not "no duty anywhere." It is "no duty to a plaintiff outside the reasonably apparent risk zone."  
Silver Key: When two answers use no-duty language, ask "no duty to whom?" The answer that limits the defense to the remote plaintiff beats the answer that wipes out duty to everyone.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells the idea that the strange hidden device changes negligence into automatic liability. The breaker is that an unusual object does not create strict liability unless a separate strict-liability doctrine applies, and this call asks for Lydia's negligence defense.

B sells a clean damages shortcut by treating Hannah's claim as if she had no bodily harm. The breaker is visible in the stem: her implanted insulin pump shut down and she was sent to emergency care, so the live issue is duty to a foreseeable plaintiff, not absence of injury.

D is the dominant trap. It borrows the right "no duty" vocabulary, but it overshoots. Gold Key: Majority Palsgraf denies duty to a plaintiff outside the reasonably apparent risk zone; it does not say Lydia owed no duty to Paul or to people at the checkout table who were foreseeably exposed to the falling box.

Long Island Railroad Co., 248 N.Y. 339, 162 N.E. 99 (1928), negligence is relational: the defendant must owe a duty to the injured plaintiff, and the "risk reasonably to be perceived defines the duty to be obeyed." The New York Courts archive reproduces Cardozo's majority opinion and identifies the case as Palsgraf v Long Is. R.R. Co., 248 N.Y. 339.
