### 7. Full right-answer explanation

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**D. Yes, because the First and Fourteenth Amendments affirmatively protect the institute's right to employ only instructors who share and communicate its views.**

This reaches the right yes/no result and leans into the institute's Christian identity. ### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule remains the state action requirement for individual-rights claims against private entities. The Fourteenth Amendment's operative limitation is directed at state action, and incorporated First Amendment claims against nonfederal actors require action fairly attributable to a state. A private school's personnel decision does not become state action merely because the school is licensed, accredited, regulated, or substantially funded by the government.

Lawyer-confirmation authority: U.S. Const. amend. XIV, section 1 uses state-directed language. *Rendell-Baker v. Kohn*, 457 U.S. 830 (1982), held that a private school whose income came almost entirely from public sources and that was subject to regulation was not a state actor when it discharged employees; the discharge decisions were not compelled or influenced by state regulation, and public funding did not transform the decisions into acts of the state. Primary U.S. Reports source: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep457/usrep457830/usrep457830.pdf. *Blum v. Yaretsky*, 457 U.S. 991 (1982), confirms that a state normally is responsible for a private decision only when it exercises coercive power, provides significant encouragement, or the private party performs a function traditionally and exclusively reserved to the state. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/457/991/. The Constitution Annotated states the baseline rule that the Fourteenth Amendment limits governmental entities, not private parties. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/constitution-conan/amendment-14/state-action-doctrine.

The state accredits and partially funds it, but the state did not command, encourage, approve, or participate in the discharge. The court should grant the motion to dismiss because the challenged conduct is not state action.
