## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap — the overclaim):** This choice sells the tempting idea that any intentional contact with someone else's property is automatically actionable. It sounds like a bright-line rule, and students who memorize "trespass = intentional contact" without the qualifier will grab it. The breaker: trespass to chattels requires *substantial* interference — dispossession, impairment, or loss of use. Harmless brief contact, without more, does not cross the line. **Silver Key:** Lock the tort's threshold first. If the chattel was not dispossessed, impaired, or deprived of use, the claim fails before any contact analysis matters.

**B (the conversion bait):** This choice sounds lawyerly because it mentions "full market value" — a phrase students associate with property torts. It baits the student who confuses trespass to chattels with conversion. Conversion requires the plaintiff to show the defendant exercised such dominion and control that the plaintiff should recover the full value of the chattel. Trespass to chattels does not require proof of full market value; it requires proof of *actionable interference*. The claim here fails not because of a proof gap but because there was no actionable interference at all.

**D (the annoyance-as-harm trap):** This choice appeals to the student who reasons: "Barnabas was irritated, so he suffered harm, so he should win." It conflates emotional displeasure with the kind of property interference the tort protects. Trespass to chattels protects *possessory and use interests*, not feelings. Irritation without dispossession, impairment, or loss of use is not enough.

## 8. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 217–218.

- § 217: A person is subject to liability for trespass to chattels if he intentionally dispossesses another of a chattel, or uses or intermeddles with a chattel in the possession of another.
- § 218: One who commits a trespass to a chattel is liable to the possessor if: (a) he dispossesses the other of the chattel; (b) the chattel is impaired as to its condition, quality, or value; (c) the possessor is deprived of the use of the chattel for a substantial time; or (d) bodily harm is caused to the possessor or harm is caused to some person or thing in which the possessor has a legally protected interest.

**Application:** Lydia intentionally moved the chest. But the chest remained locked, visible, undamaged, and accessible. No dispossession (Barnabas still had access). No impairment (the chest was unharmed). No deprivation of use for a substantial time (the chest was moved briefly and remained available). No bodily harm or harm to a legally protected interest. The interference was de minimis — not substantial enough to trigger liability under § 218.

**Trespass to chattels vs. Conversion:** Conversion is the "greater wrong" — serious interference warranting recovery of the chattel's full value. Trespass to chattels covers minor interference. The key distinction is the *degree* of interference. Here, the interference was so minor it did not even reach the trespass threshold.

**Credited outcome unchanged:** The original question's credited answer (B — no liability because no dispossession, impairment, or loss of use) maps directly to the transformed credited answer (C — same reasoning, re-skinned facts). The legal substance, rule, and outcome are identical.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 217–218 (1965). See also *CompuServe Inc. v. Cyber Promotions, Inc.*, 962 F. Supp. 1235 (S.D. Ohio 1997) (applying Restatement framework to determine whether electronic intermeddling constitutes actionable trespass).

#### Wrong-Answer Recovery Paths

**If a student picked A:** They overgeneralized "intentional touching = trespass." Recovery: The word "any" is a red flag. The rule requires *substantial* interference. Go back to the stem: was the chest dispossessed, impaired, or deprived of use? No. The threshold isn't met.

**If a student picked B:** They confused trespass to chattels with conversion. Recovery: "Full market value" is a conversion concept. The call says "trespass to chattels." When the choice discusses a different tort, it's not responsive. Focus on the trespass threshold instead.

**If a student picked D:** They conflated emotional harm with property harm. Recovery: Trespass to chattels protects possessory and use interests, not feelings. Irritation without dispossession, impairment, or loss of use is not actionable.
