### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**A is correct.** Barnabas took and carried away the facsimile page from the display with the intent to steal it. That is enough for larceny.

**Gold Key:** A completed taking of another’s property is larceny even if the defendant was wrong about the item’s identity, value, or authenticity.

**Silver Key:** Read this array as a charge-bundle matrix: first remove burglary if the entry was not unlawful, then choose completed larceny over attempted larceny if the object was actually taken.

Barnabas is not guilty of burglary because the bookstore was open to customers, and the burglary definition requires unlawful entry. He is not guilty only of attempted larceny because he actually took property from the display.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

The breaker is the completed-taking fact: Barnabas physically took property of another, so the case is larceny, not merely attempted larceny. **Gold Key:** factual mistake about the item’s identity or value does not convert a completed taking into attempt.

**C.** This choice is attractive because it correctly sees larceny and adds the planned theft at entry. The breaker is the burglary entry element. The building was open to customers, and the jurisdiction’s burglary definition requires unlawful entry.

**D.** This choice is attractive because it uses both bait facts: intent at entry and the facsimile/free-card fact. The breaker is the charge bundle. The entry was not unlawful, and the taking was completed.

For burglary, the stem’s definition controls: “entering a building unlawfully with the intent to commit a crime.” The general burglary concept requires unlawful entry, and a modern public-entry rule treats entry into premises open to the public as licensed unless permission is revoked or exceeded.
