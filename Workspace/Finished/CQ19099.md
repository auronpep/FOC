## 1. program_frame

This is a Criminal Law mental-state item.

The question is not asking whether Simon was drunk. It tells you he was. The question is what legal use he can make of that fact when the charge is **attempted murder**.

The answer must stay narrow: voluntary intoxication can attack the **specific intent to kill**. It does not erase every possible offense from the bottle throw.

## 2. student_access_test

This item is **anchor-assisted**.

A student needs two tiny anchors:

1. **Attempt requires specific intent.**
2. **Voluntary intoxication may negate specific intent, but only that kind of mental-state requirement.**

The structural part is still visible. Choices B, C, and D use the loud trap words **all**, **never**, and **only**.

## 3. question_data

**Question:** After voluntarily drinking heavily, Simon throws a heavy glass bottle toward a crowd from a balcony. The bottle narrowly misses Dana. Simon is charged with attempted murder. Witnesses say Simon was laughing and yelling nonsense, not aiming at anyone in particular. How may Simon use voluntary intoxication?

**Choices:**

A. To argue he lacked the specific intent to kill required for attempted murder
B. To obtain acquittal from all charges arising from the bottle throw
C. Not at all, because voluntary intoxication is never relevant to attempt
D. Only if Simon's intoxication was caused by a mental disease

**Official key:** A

## 4. call_and_prediction

**Call:** How may Simon use voluntary intoxication?

**Prediction:** He may use it only to argue that he lacked the **specific intent to kill** required for attempted murder.

**Adjacent-call trap:** Do not answer, “Is Simon innocent of everything?” The call is about the use of intoxication against this charge.

## 6. governing_c3_lane

**Lane:** CUT.

This is a rule/anchor item. The answer array is built around the scope of voluntary intoxication.

**Gold Key:** Voluntary intoxication may negate specific intent. It does not automatically defeat all charges.

**Silver Key:** When intoxication appears, name the charged offense’s mental state first. Then limit the defense to that mental-state element.

## 7. choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. To argue he lacked the specific intent to kill required for attempted murder

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This answer is narrow. It matches the charge and the mental-state element.
2. **Student label:** Specific-intent survivor.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This is already true and responsive.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Attempt requires intent to commit the target crime, and voluntary intoxication evidence may be considered on whether the defendant formed a required specific intent. ([Legal Information Institute][1])

### B. To obtain acquittal from all charges arising from the bottle throw

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The phrase **all charges** is too big. It turns a limited mental-state argument into a total defense.
2. **Student label:** All-charges overclaim.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** “To argue he lacked the specific intent required for attempted murder.”
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Voluntary intoxication may matter for a specific-intent offense; it does not automatically defeat general-intent or reckless offenses. That is why the official explanation notes that other charges may remain.

### C. Not at all, because voluntary intoxication is never relevant to attempt

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The answer says **never**, but attempt is a specific-intent crime. That points the anchor backward.
2. **Student label:** Never-relevant reversal.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** “Voluntary intoxication may be relevant if it prevents the formation of the specific intent required for attempt.”
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The attempt anchor is specific intent plus a substantial step. A voluntary-intoxication argument can be relevant to the specific-intent part. ([Legal Information Institute][1])

### D. Only if Simon's intoxication was caused by a mental disease

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The answer adds a new condition: mental disease. The call did not require that.
2. **Student label:** Mental-disease invented condition.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** “No mental disease is required; voluntary intoxication may be used if it negates specific intent.”
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Mental disease belongs to insanity-style analysis. This row tests voluntary intoxication as evidence against specific intent.
