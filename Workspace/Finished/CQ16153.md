### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** For result crimes like murder, the concurrence requirement is satisfied if the defendant had the requisite intent at the moment of committing the act that causes the death — it does not matter that the intent had faded by the time the result actually occurred. The security guard's negligence in failing to find the device does not sever proximate cause — Daniel's act was both the but-for and proximate cause of Lydia's death. ### 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Tested rule:** First-degree murder requires premeditation and deliberation. Concurrence for result crimes requires intent at the time of the act, not at the time of the result. Transferred intent applies when a defendant intends to kill one person but kills another — the intent transfers to the actual victim. A third party's negligence in failing to prevent harm does not sever proximate cause when the negligence is a foreseeable response to the defendant's act.

**Authority:**

1. **Concurrence (intent-at-act principle):** The common-law concurrence requirement for result crimes is that the mens rea must accompany the actus reus — the act that causes the result. The intent need not persist until the moment of the result. *See* LaFave, *Criminal Law* § 6.3(b) (6th ed.) ("Where the crime is defined in terms of a result, the required concurrence is between the intent and the act, not between the intent and the result."); *People v. Bland*, 28 Cal. 4th 317, 322 (2002) (discussing concurrence and transferred intent principles).

2. **Transferred intent:** "In its classic form, the doctrine of transferred intent applies when the defendant intends to kill one person but mistakenly kills another. The intent to kill the intended target is deemed to transfer to the unintended victim so that the defendant is guilty of murder." *People v. Bland*, 28 Cal. 4th 317, 322 (2002); *Bradshaw v. Richey*, 124 Ohio St. 3d 124 (2009) (transferred intent "firmly rooted" in state precedent); Model Penal Code § 2.03 (causation provision addressing unintended victim). *See also* transferred intent doctrine, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transferred_intent.

3. **Proximate cause / intervening negligence:** A third party's negligence in failing to discover or prevent a danger created by the defendant is a foreseeable intervening cause and does not supersede the defendant's liability. The defendant's act remains the proximate cause of the harm. *See* LaFave, *Criminal Law* § 6.4(c) (intervening causes — foreseeable responses to defendant's conduct do not break proximate cause).

This item tests first-degree murder through the doctrines of concurrence, transferred intent, and proximate cause. The fact pattern is designed to trigger three common student errors: (1) believing that remorse before the result negates intent, (2) thinking that killing an unintended victim reduces the degree of murder, and (3) treating third-party negligence as a superseding cause that eliminates murder liability. The C3 path requires the student to recognize that intent is measured at the time of the act, that transferred intent applies, and that foreseeable intervening negligence does not break proximate cause.

#### Student Access Test

A smart student who has learned the C3 Gold Keys for this item should be able to eliminate all three wrong answers using only: (1) the visible fact that Daniel planted the device with premeditation, (2) the Gold Key that intent is measured at the time of the act, (3) the Gold Key that transferred intent applies when the actual victim differs from the intended victim, and (4) the structural observation that the night watchman's negligence was a foreseeable response to a hidden danger. No outside legal doctrine is required.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Daniel, a maintenance supervisor at a rural church camp, decided to kill the camp director (Pastor Paul) after being told his position was eliminated. He rigged a fire device to the camp truck's ignition on Tuesday night, then drove to a motel. At 4 a.m. Wednesday, overcome with guilt, he called the night watchman (Stephen) and warned him. Stephen left a note, looked at the truck, saw nothing, placed a "DO NOT DRIVE" sign, and went to call police. Before police arrived, the assistant director (Lydia) started the truck and was killed.

**Call:** Daniel is guilty of

**Choices:**
- (A) murder in the second degree, because he had no intention of killing the camp's assistant director.
- (B) manslaughter, because at the time the device fired, he had no intent to kill, and Lydia's death was in part the fault of the night watchman.
- (C) murder in the first degree, because, with premeditation and deliberation, he killed whoever would start the truck.
- (D) only attempted murder of Pastor Paul, because Lydia's death was the result of the night watchman's negligence.

#### Distilled Core Question

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks what crime Daniel is guilty of. The student should predict: first-degree murder, because Daniel acted with premeditation and deliberation, concurrence is satisfied at the time of the act, transferred intent applies to the unintended victim, and third-party negligence doesn't sever proximate cause.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "murder in the second degree, because he had no intention of killing the camp's assistant director."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice says Daniel didn't intend to kill Lydia, so it's only second-degree. But ask: did Daniel intend to kill *someone*? Yes — he rigged the truck to kill whoever drove it. The fact that Lydia wasn't the specific person he had in mind doesn't reduce the crime.
2. **Student label:** Ignores transferred intent.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A correct version would say: "Even though Daniel intended to kill Pastor Paul, the law transfers that intent to Lydia, so Daniel is guilty of murdering Lydia."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Transferred intent doctrine — intent to kill Paul transfers to Lydia. *People v. Bland*, 28 Cal. 4th 317 (2002).

**Choice B: "manslaughter, because at the time the device fired, he had no intent to kill, and Lydia's death was in part the fault of the night watchman."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice points to Daniel's remorse and the watchman's negligence. But ask: when does the law measure intent? At the moment Daniel planted the device — not at the moment it exploded. And was the watchman's failure to find a hidden device surprising or unforeseeable? No — it's exactly what you'd expect.
2. **Student label:** Conflates remorse with lack of intent; treats third-party negligence as superseding cause.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A correct version would say: "Daniel had intent at the time of the act, and the watchman's negligence was a foreseeable intervening cause that doesn't break proximate cause."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Concurrence: intent at time of act. Proximate cause: foreseeable intervening negligence is not superseding. LaFave, *Criminal Law* §§ 6.3(b), 6.4(c).

**Choice C: "murder in the first degree, because, with premeditation and deliberation, he killed whoever would start the truck."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice correctly identifies premeditation and deliberation. Daniel planned the killing in advance (premeditation) and carried it out with a calculated method (deliberation). The transferred intent doctrine fills the gap between intended and actual victim.
2. **Student label:** Correct.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true and responsive choice.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** First-degree murder requires premeditation and deliberation. Transferred intent applies. Concurrence satisfied at time of act.

**Choice D: "only attempted murder of Pastor Paul, because Lydia's death was the result of the night watchman's negligence."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice says it's only attempted murder because the watchman was negligent. But ask: did someone die? Yes. Did Daniel's act cause the death? Yes — he planted the device. The watchman's failure to find it doesn't erase Daniel's responsibility.
2. **Student label:** Treats third-party negligence as severing murder liability entirely.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A correct version would say: "The watchman's negligence was foreseeable and doesn't break proximate cause, so Daniel is guilty of murder, not just attempted murder."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Proximate cause: foreseeable third-party negligence is not superseding. A death occurred with intent to kill = murder.

#### Review Truth

When a defendant commits an act with premeditated intent to kill, the concurrence requirement is satisfied at the moment of the act — a later change of heart does not erase the intent, and transferred intent makes the defendant guilty of murdering the actual victim even if a different person was targeted.

**GK-CRIM-MURDER-01 — Intent-at-Act Principle**
"For result crimes like murder, concurrence requires that the defendant had the requisite intent at the moment of committing the act that causes death — the intent need not persist until the moment the result actually occurs."
