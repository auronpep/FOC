## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**B is correct.** Impeachment by prior conviction runs through two independent
doors, and each conviction is tested against its own door. The aggravated-assault
felony comes in through the felony door: a felony conviction of a non-party
witness is admissible to impeach, subject only to ordinary balancing. The
false-statement misdemeanor comes in through the dishonesty door: any crime whose
proof required a dishonest act or false statement is admissible to impeach
regardless of whether it is a felony or a misdemeanor, with no balancing
discretion to keep it out. Both convictions are recent (three years and one year,
well inside the standard ten-year window). Two convictions, two open doors, so
both are admitted.

**Gold Key:** a witness can be impeached with *any* felony AND with *any*
crime—felony or misdemeanor—whose elements required dishonesty or a false
statement; a conviction admissible through either door comes in.
**Silver Key:** the four choices are a 2×2 inclusion lattice (X-only / Y-only /
both / neither); test each conviction against its own admissibility door, count
how many pass, and pick the choice matching the count.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. aggravated assault only.** The violent felony is the conviction a student is
sure about, so it feels safe to admit that one and stop there, treating the
permit-application misdemeanor as too minor to matter. The breaker: grade is not
the test for a dishonesty crime — the false-statement misdemeanor comes in
automatically through its own door, so "only" the felony understates what is
admissible. **Gold Key:** the dishonesty door admits a qualifying crime *of any
grade*, which is exactly the conviction this choice drops.

**C. neither crime.** This choice reads the objection as if prior convictions are
generally too prejudicial to let in, flipping impeachment-by-conviction into a
rule of exclusion. The breaker: the default runs the other direction — qualifying
convictions are admitted to impeach, and here each conviction independently
qualifies, so the count is two, not zero.

**D. knowingly making false statements in the produce-vendor permit application
only.** This rides the common myth that you can impeach *only* with crimes of
dishonesty, which makes the honest-but-violent felony look irrelevant to
credibility. The breaker: a felony is admissible to impeach a non-party witness
on its own felony footing, so the assault conviction is not excluded just because
it is not a "dishonesty" crime.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are UNCHANGED from the
original: FRE 609 impeachment of a witness by evidence of a criminal conviction,
with both a felony and a dishonesty misdemeanor admissible.

- **FRE 609(a)(1)(A):** for a crime punishable by death or imprisonment for more
  than one year, the conviction "must be admitted, subject to Rule 403, in a
  civil case or in a criminal case in which the witness is not a defendant."
  Aggravated assault is a felony and the witness is not a defendant, so it is
  admissible subject to ordinary Rule 403 balancing.
- **FRE 609(a)(2):** for any crime regardless of punishment, the conviction "must
  be admitted if the court can readily determine that establishing the elements
  of the crime required proving — or the witness's admitting — a dishonest act or
  false statement." Knowingly making false statements in a permit application
  squarely qualifies, so the misdemeanor is admissible with no discretion to
  exclude it.
- **FRE 609(b):** the ten-year limit is not triggered; both convictions
  (three years and one year old) are recent.

Authority: Federal Rules of Evidence 609(a)(1)(A), 609(a)(2), 609(b). Rule
numbers only; no case citation asserted. Credited outcome (both crimes
admissible) confirmed against the rule — no key disagreement.
