### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** Daniel is guilty of first-degree murder because he intentionally and with premeditation killed Timothy. The jurisdiction defines first-degree murder as the intentional premeditated killing of another. Premeditation requires only a moment of deliberate reflection before the killing, and any appreciable time for deliberation satisfies it. Here, Daniel was threatened the day before, spent the night with the threat hanging over him, armed himself with a pistol the next morning, traveled to the stash house, and shot Timothy in the chest. That overnight interval—during which Daniel made the conscious choice to arm himself and go to the scene—establishes premeditation beyond question.

The defenses of duress and necessity are both unavailable for murder. At common law, duress (a threat of death or serious bodily harm from another person) is never a defense to the intentional killing of an innocent third party. The law does not recognize any threat so severe that it excuses the killing of a blameless human being. Necessity (choosing the lesser of two evils under natural or situational compulsion) is likewise unavailable for murder. Neither defense can reduce or excuse a murder charge.

**Gold Key:** *Duress and necessity are never defenses to murder. No threat of death to the defendant excuses the intentional killing of an innocent person.*

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Tested Rule:** First-degree murder = intentional + premeditated killing. Duress and necessity are not defenses to murder.

**Authority:**

1. **Common law rule — duress excluded from murder:** At common law, duress is not a defense to murder. The rationale is that the defendant's life is not more valuable than the innocent victim's life. This is a well-established MBE black-letter rule. *See* LaFave, *Criminal Law* § 5.3(b) ("The common law rule is that duress is not a defense to murder"); *People v. Anderson*, 637 P.2d 354 (Nev. 1981) (duress not available as defense to murder under common law).

2. **Necessity excluded from murder:** Necessity (choice of evils) is likewise unavailable for murder. The balancing-of-harms rationale that underlies necessity cannot justify the killing of an innocent person. *See* SLCC Criminal Law, § 5.4 ("choice of evils cannot justify criminal homicide"); *People v. Unger*, 362 N.E.2d 319 (Ill. 1977) (necessity/duress not a defense to murder).

3. **Premeditation standard:** Premeditation requires only that the defendant deliberated and reflected before the killing, even momentarily. An overnight interval between the decision to kill and the killing itself is more than sufficient. *See* *People v. Anderson*, 637 P.2d 354 (Nev. 1981) (premeditation may be formed in an instant; time for reflection is the key).

4. **Model Penal Code contrast (for attorney awareness only):** MPC § 2.09(1) defines duress broadly and does not categorically exclude murder, but the MBE tests the common law rule unless the question specifies MPC jurisdiction. This question does not invoke the MPC.

**Verification:** The credited answer (first-degree murder, rejecting duress) is fully supported by common law authority. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original.

#### Distilled Core Question

A defendant intentionally and with premeditation kills another person after being threatened with death if he refuses. Can duress or necessity negate first-degree murder?

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks for the correct legal outcome. The student should predict: first-degree murder, because (1) the killing was intentional, (2) premeditation is established by the overnight interval, and (3) duress/necessity are not defenses to murder.

#### Trigger Facts

| Fact | Legal Significance |
|------|-------------------|
| Daniel wanted out of the crew | Establishes the coercion context |
| Paul threatened to kill Daniel if he refused | Duress trigger (human threat of death) |
| The next morning Daniel armed himself | Premeditation: conscious choice to prepare |
| Daniel went to the stash house | Premeditation: deliberate travel to the scene |
| Daniel shot Timothy in the chest, killing him instantly | Intentional killing of an innocent person |
| First-degree = intentional + premeditated | Governing rule |
| Second-degree = any other murder | Contrast rule |

#### Governing C3 Lane

**CUT** → Eliminate choices that misstate the law (necessity for murder, duress for murder).
**CLASH** → Between first-degree and second-degree: does the overnight interval establish premeditation?
**CALL** → Premeditation is established; first-degree murder is correct.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "not guilty, because of the defense of necessity."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Necessity is a choice-of-evils defense that applies when natural circumstances force someone to commit a crime to prevent a greater harm. Here, the threat came from a person (Paul), not from nature or circumstances. Even setting that aside, necessity is never a defense to murder. The choice is structurally defective on two levels.
2. **Student label:** Necessity doesn't apply to murder.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "not guilty, because the killing was justified as the lesser evil" — but this is not the law for homicide.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Necessity is not a defense to murder at common law. *People v. Unger*, 362 N.E.2d 319 (Ill. 1977).

**Choice B: "guilty of first-degree murder."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Daniel intentionally killed Timothy. He had the entire night to reflect on his decision, armed himself in the morning, and traveled to the stash house. That is premeditation. And duress/necessity are never defenses to murder.
2. **Student label:** First-degree murder — intentional, premeditated, duress excluded.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true and responsive answer.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Common law: duress not a defense to murder. Premeditation requires only a moment of deliberation; overnight is sufficient. *People v. Anderson*, 637 P.2d 354 (Nev. 1981).

**Choice C: "guilty of second-degree murder."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice sees the killing as intentional murder but misses the premeditation. Daniel didn't act in the heat of the moment—he spent the night thinking about it, armed himself, and went to the scene. That's premeditation, which makes it first-degree.
2. **Student label:** Second-degree ignores premeditation.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "guilty of second-degree murder because the killing was intentional but not premeditated" — but the facts clearly show premeditation.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Premeditation may be formed in an instant; any appreciable time for reflection suffices. Overnight + arming = clear premeditation.

**Choice D: "not guilty, because of the defense of duress."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the dominant trap. Duress is the right *area* of defense—Paul threatened Daniel with death, which is exactly the kind of human threat that triggers duress analysis. But the bright-line rule is: duress is never a defense to murder. No matter how sympathetic the coercion, the law does not excuse the killing of an innocent person.
2. **Student label:** Duress feels right but is categorically excluded for murder.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "not guilty, because Daniel reasonably believed he had no choice but to kill Timothy to save his own life" — but this is not the law for murder.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Common law: duress is not a defense to murder. LaFave, *Criminal Law* § 5.3(b). The defendant's life is not more valuable than the innocent victim's.

#### Residual Answer

**B — guilty of first-degree murder.** This is the only choice that is both true (correctly states the law) and responsive (answers the call). It survives CUT, CLASH, and CALL.
