## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Daniel and Lydia owned **Galilee Harvest Loft** as joint tenants. The property included two separate workshop spaces. Daniel used one space for a small Christian printmaking studio and rented the other to Stephen, who restored old Bible maps.

During an argument over damaged printing plates, Daniel struck Stephen and injured him. Stephen obtained and properly recorded a judgment for $16,000 against Daniel. The jurisdiction’s statute provides:

> Any judgment properly recorded shall, for 10 years from recording, be a lien on the real property then owned or later acquired by the person against whom the judgment is entered.

Lydia lived several states away and did not learn of Stephen’s judgment until later. Before Stephen levied, executed, or otherwise took further action against Daniel’s interest, Daniel died. The common-law joint tenancy is unmodified by statute.

Lydia then brought an appropriate action against Stephen to quiet title to Galilee Harvest Loft. The court should hold that Stephen has:

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A creditor records a judgment against one joint tenant, but the debtor joint tenant dies before execution or severance. Does the creditor still have a lien on the property?

### 3. Final answer choices

**A.** No lien, because Lydia had no actual notice of Stephen’s judgment until after Daniel died.

**B.** A lien against Daniel’s undivided one-half interest in Galilee Harvest Loft, because Stephen’s judgment was recorded before Daniel died.

**C.** No lien, because Daniel’s death terminated the interest to which Stephen’s lien attached.

**D.** A lien against all of Galilee Harvest Loft, because Stephen occupied one of the workshop spaces while Daniel and Lydia held title as joint tenants.

### 5. Correct answer

**C** — original key **D**.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.** The judgment lien attached only to Daniel’s interest. Daniel held that interest as a joint tenant, which means it was subject to survivorship. Because Stephen recorded the judgment but did not execute, levy, sell, or otherwise sever Daniel’s joint-tenancy interest before Daniel died, Daniel’s interest ended at death. Lydia’s survivorship interest then left her owning the whole property free of Stephen’s lien.

**Gold Key:** A lien against one joint tenant does not itself sever the joint tenancy. The creditor must act before the debtor joint tenant dies, or the survivorship feature can wipe out the debtor’s attachable interest.

**Silver Key:** Treat the answer array as a timing-and-object pair: lien on whose interest, and did that interest still exist after death?

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells a notice story. That is attractive because Lydia was innocent and absent, so “no notice” sounds like a clean equity reason. The breaker is that notice is not the resolving axis; the resolving axis is joint tenancy survivorship. Lydia wins because Daniel’s joint-tenancy interest ended, not because she lacked actual notice.

**B.** This is the dominant trap. It sells the filing-date rule: the judgment was recorded before Daniel died, so the lien must still sit on Daniel’s half. The breaker is the **Gold Key:** recording attaches only to Daniel’s joint-tenancy interest, and that interest disappeared when Daniel died before Stephen severed or enforced against it.

**D.** This choice sells a whole-property upgrade. It is attractive because Stephen physically occupied part of the property and the property had two owners. The breaker is that a judgment against Daniel does not become a lien against Lydia’s interest or the whole property merely because Stephen rented space there.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule is unchanged from the source row: a judgment lien against one joint tenant attaches only to that joint tenant’s interest; a mere lien does not sever a common-law joint tenancy; if the debtor joint tenant dies before enforcement or severance, the debtor’s interest terminates and the surviving joint tenant takes free of the lien. The source row states the original question facts, the answer choices, the 44% correct rate, and the official key D. 

Legal authority supports the credited outcome. In **Zeigler v. Bonnell**, the court stated that the judgment lien could attach only to the debtor joint tenant’s interest, that the debtor’s interest terminated at death, and that after death there was no interest left to levy upon. ([Justia Law][1]) The same page states the operational risk for the creditor: if the creditor executes and sells during life, the joint tenancy can be severed; if the creditor waits and the debtor dies first, the lien is lost. ([Justia Law][1]) Illinois authority is consistent: commentary on **Harms v. Sprague** explains that a lien by fewer than all joint tenants does not sever joint tenancy, and if the debtor joint tenant dies, the survivor becomes sole owner free and clear of that lien. ([ATG Forum][2])
