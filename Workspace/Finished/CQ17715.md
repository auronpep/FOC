## 1. Final Question

**The Vintage Motorcycle Restoration**

Daniel sues Jesse for breach of contract on a vintage motorcycle restoration. Daniel calls Abigail as his first witness and asks, "Did you personally see Jesse sign the March 17 work order at your shop?" Jesse objects that Abigail lacks foundation. During argument, the court and both lawyers discuss on the record that Abigail would answer yes and would identify the signing. The court still excludes the testimony. Daniel makes no additional formal offer of proof. Is the substance of the excluded evidence sufficiently preserved?

Has the plaintiff preserved the substance of the excluded testimony for appeal?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**D is correct.** Under FRE 103(a)(2), a party may preserve a claim of error in the *exclusion* of evidence either (i) by an offer of proof, **or** (ii) without any offer, when the substance of the evidence "was apparent from the context within which questions were asked." Here, the substance was apparent: the question itself named the witness, the contract, the date, and the shop, and the on-record discussion expressly stated that Abigail would answer "yes" and would identify the signing. The trial court and counsel heard exactly what the testimony would have been. No further formal offer was required.

**Gold Key: An offer of proof is unnecessary when the substance of the excluded evidence is apparent from the context — including the question itself, the objection, and any on-record clarification by counsel.** (FRE 103(a)(2).) The rule is the context exception, not a presumption about excluded witnesses; the proponent still bears the burden of showing error, but the rule removes the form requirement once substance is clear from the record.

The Advisory Committee Notes confirm that the context exception — originally a criminal-only rule — was made applicable to civil as well as criminal cases by the 1998 amendment. The modern (2011 restyling) text preserves the substance.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**Governing rule:** Federal Rule of Evidence 103(a)(2). When the trial court excludes evidence, the substance of the evidence must be made known to the court by an offer of proof, **unless the substance was apparent from the context within which questions were asked.** The 2011 restyling (effective December 1, 2011) is purely stylistic; the substance is unchanged. The Advisory Committee Notes to the 1998 amendment confirm that the context exception — which had previously been a criminal-only rule in some courts — is applicable to civil as well as criminal cases. [LII / Cornell text: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_103; uscode.house.gov historical text; Crushendo and Understanding Evidence summaries of the current rule.]

**Supporting case law:** The Fifth Circuit applies the rule strictly when no offer is made: *United States v. Winkle*, 587 F.2d 705 (5th Cir. 1979) ("the Fifth Circuit will not even consider the propriety of the decision to exclude evidence if no offer of proof was made at trial"); *Espino v. City of Kingsville*, 676 F.2d 1075 (5th Cir. 1982); *United States v. Cutler*, 676 F.2d 1245 (9th Cir. 1982). These cases illustrate the *default* — make a formal offer — but they all presuppose that the substance is *not* apparent from context. When substance is apparent, no offer is required; the rule itself states the exception.

**Why D is correct under the governing rule:** the question itself named the witness, the document, the date, and the location, and the on-record discussion by court and counsel stated that the witness would answer "yes" and would identify the signing. The substance of the excluded evidence is therefore apparent from the context. No formal offer was needed. The legal substance of the credited answer is **unchanged** from the original.
