## 1. Final question

Two defendants, Paul and Stephen, were being tried together in federal court for robbing a federally insured bank courier who was carrying a ministry fundraiser deposit. The prosecutor sought to call a jail inmate who had worked beside Paul in the jail kitchen. The inmate would testify that Paul admitted, "Stephen and I took the courier's bag." Paul would not testify at trial. The prosecutor asked the court to instruct the jury that the inmate's testimony could be considered only against Paul and not against Stephen. Can the inmate's testimony be admitted in the joint trial over Stephen's objection?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

In a joint criminal trial, can the prosecution use a non-testifying defendant's confession that directly names the codefendant if the judge gives a limiting instruction?

## 3. Final answer choices

A. Yes, because Paul's statement was a declaration against penal interest.

B. No, because Paul made the statement without Miranda warnings.

C. No, because the limiting instruction cannot ensure that the jury will not consider the testimony when deciding Stephen's guilt.

D. Yes, because the limiting instruction sufficiently protects Stephen.

## 5. Correct answer

C (original key B).

## 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is the best answer. The testimony would put Paul's out-of-court confession before the jury in a joint trial, and the confession directly incriminates Stephen. Because Paul will not testify, Stephen cannot cross-examine him about the accusation. Gold Key: under Bruton, a limiting instruction is not enough when a non-testifying codefendant's confession directly incriminates the defendant in a joint trial. Silver Key: do not let the hearsay label or the judge's limiting instruction move the call away from confrontation.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A. This choice sells a real hearsay instinct: Paul's statement hurts Paul, so it sounds like a declaration against penal interest. The breaker is that a hearsay exception does not answer Stephen's confrontation problem in a joint trial.

B. This choice sells the familiar Miranda trigger. The breaker is role and source. The statement was not made during custodial interrogation by a known government agent, and Stephen is asserting his own confrontation right, not Paul's Miranda right.

D. This is the dominant trap because limiting instructions usually matter. The breaker is the special Bruton setting. Gold Key: when the confession directly names the codefendant and the confessing defendant does not testify, the constitutional risk is too high for the instruction to cure.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The legal issue and credited outcome are unchanged: a non-testifying codefendant's confession that directly incriminates another defendant cannot be admitted in their joint trial merely with a limiting instruction. Bruton v. United States, 391 U.S. 123 (1968), holds that admitting such a confession violates the codefendant's Confrontation Clause right despite an instruction limiting use to the confessing defendant. The declaration-against-interest label does not cure the Sixth Amendment problem, and Miranda is not the basis for Stephen's objection. The testimony should not be admitted over Stephen's objection in this joint trial.
