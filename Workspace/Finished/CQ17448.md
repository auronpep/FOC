## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** Promissory estoppel requires a *clear and definite promise* — the doctrine enforces promises that lack consideration, but it cannot create a promise where none exists. A cry for help, an expression of distress, or mere presence at a disaster scene is not a promise to pay. Without an express or reasonably implied promise, the doctrine never gets off the ground.

Daniel voluntarily repaired the roof. Martha stood in the parking lot watching the damage happen. She did not ask him to do anything, did not say she would pay, and did nothing from which a promise to pay could be inferred. The entire theory of promissory estoppel collapses at the first element: no promise, no estoppel.

This is the only defense listed that actually engages the promissory estoppel framework. The other three choices attack doctrines that are either inapplicable (officious intermeddler — she was present and the emergency called for action), irrelevant (speculative value — PE doesn't measure the promisor's benefit), or flatly wrong (no consideration — PE exists precisely to enforce promises without consideration).

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### (A) The value of the church's property is too speculative to measure.

**Why it's attractive:** Students who confuse promissory estoppel with quasi-contract or unjust enrichment might think the plaintiff's recovery is measured by the value of the benefit conferred on the defendant. If the benefit is "too speculative," the argument goes, the plaintiff can't recover.

**The breaker:** Promissory estoppel does not measure recovery by the value received by the promisor. Recovery is based on the *detriment sustained by the promisee* in reliance on the promise. The value of the church's property is irrelevant to Daniel's claim. This choice is a misfit — it answers a different question entirely.

### (C) There was no consideration for any promise Martha might have made.

**Why it's attractive:** This is the **dominant trap**. Every first-year law student knows that contracts require consideration. The choice weaponizes that foundational knowledge against the specific doctrine being tested. It *sounds* right — and it would be right if this were a breach-of-contract claim.

**The breaker:** **Gold Key:** Promissory estoppel exists precisely to enforce promises that *lack* consideration. The whole point of the doctrine is that when a promisee justifiably relies on a gratuitous promise to their detriment, the court will enforce it despite the absence of consideration. Arguing "no consideration" doesn't defeat a promissory estoppel claim — it describes the condition that *triggers* the doctrine. This is the most dangerous wrong answer because it is legally true in isolation but fatally wrong as applied to the call.

### (D) Daniel was an officious intermeddler.

**Why it's attractive:** The officious intermeddler doctrine bars recovery by someone who voluntarily confers a benefit without being asked. Daniel *did* pull over on his own initiative. A student who fixates on the fact that Martha never asked for help might latch onto this.

**The breaker:** The officious intermeddler doctrine applies when someone acts *without request and without the situation calling for the action*. Here, a violent storm had just torn open a roof and rain was pouring into a building. The emergency called for immediate action. Moreover, Martha was present at the scene — her presence in the midst of the emergency, watching the damage unfold, negates the "uninvited stranger" character that makes the intermeddler doctrine apply. This choice is the weakest distractor but still tests whether the student can distinguish emergency intervention from officious meddling.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule — Promissory Estoppel (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90):**

A promise which the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance on the part of the promisee and which does induce such action or forbearance is binding if injustice can be avoided only by enforcement of the promise.

**Elements:**
1. A clear and definite promise by the promisor;
2. The promisor should reasonably expect the promise to induce reliance;
3. The promisee actually and justifiably relies on the promise; and
4. Injustice can be avoided only by enforcing the promise.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90 (1981). The doctrine originated in *Hughes v. Metropolitan Railway Co.*, [1877] 2 App. Cas. 439 (HL), and was revitalized in the U.S. by the Restatement. The UCC also reflects the principle in the firm-offer rule (UCC § 2-205) and the modification-without-consideration rule (UCC § 2-209).

**Application:** The critical element here is the first one — a clear and definite promise. Martha's presence at the scene, watching her church being damaged by rain, is not a promise to pay anyone. She made no statement that could be construed as an offer or a promise of compensation. Daniel acted entirely on his own initiative. Without a promise, the doctrine cannot apply, regardless of how much Daniel spent or how beneficial his actions were.

**Verification against original:** The tested rule (promissory estoppel requires a promise), the legal issue (whether a cry for help / presence at a disaster implies a promise to pay), and the credited outcome (no promise, therefore no PE claim) are all unchanged from the original. The transform preserves the invariant layer perfectly.
