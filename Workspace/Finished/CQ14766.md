### 1. Final question

Hannah sued Paul for wrongful death after Daniel was struck by a falling stage-light frame during a private Scripture-song concert at Paul's rented event hall. At trial, Ruth, the county fire marshal who investigated the incident, authenticated a voice memo she dictated into her phone at the end of the inspection. Is the voice memo admissible as evidence?

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**C** is the answer. Ruth is on the stand, but her live memory is not enough. She made the voice memo when the inspection details were fresh, she authenticated it, and she testified that it was accurate when made. That is the recorded recollection path.

**Gold Key:** under FRE 803(5), a record qualifies when the witness once knew the matter, now cannot recall well enough to testify fully and accurately, made or adopted the record while the matter was fresh, and the record accurately reflects that knowledge. If admitted, the contents may be read or played into evidence, though the record itself is received as an exhibit only if the adverse party offers it.

**Silver Key:** track the offered item. The offered item is the earlier voice memo, not the formal incident report that Ruth later prepared from it.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Yes, under the public records exception for an investigator's factual findings.**

This answer is attractive because Ruth is a public investigator and the facts later appeared in a formal incident report. The breaker is the **Silver Key:** the offered item is the earlier voice memo, not the formal public report. The voice memo comes in, if at all, through recorded recollection, not because it is itself the public record.

**B. No, because the formal incident report is the best evidence.**

This answer is attractive because the voice memo led to a later written report, so the later document feels more official. The breaker is object discipline: the proponent is offering the voice memo to prove Ruth's remembered inspection facts, not to prove the contents of the later report. The original-document rule does not force the proponent to use a later report instead of the earlier recording.

**D. No, because it is hearsay and is an official incident report offered against the defendant in a civil death case.**

This answer is attractive because students remember special caution around police or investigative reports and hearsay. The breaker is the **Gold Key:** hearsay can still be admitted when it fits FRE 803(5), and this record has the fresh, accurate, no-memory foundation. The answer also overclaims the public-records point because this is a civil case and the offered item is not the formal incident report.

### 8. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested issue remains Evidence, Hearsay and Circumstances of Its Admissibility, with the specific outline node `33040302` for hearsay exceptions not requiring unavailability of the declarant. That code appears verbatim in `OUTLINE_CODES_COMPLETE.md` as `Hearsay > Hearsay Exceptions > Not requiring unavailability of declarant`.

The governing rule is Federal Rule of Evidence 803(5), recorded recollection. It covers a record about a matter the witness once knew but now cannot recall well enough to testify fully and accurately, if the record was made or adopted when fresh and accurately reflects the witness's knowledge. Ruth made the voice memo at the end of the inspection, authenticated it, testified it was accurate when made, and lacks present memory. Those facts satisfy the exception. The rule also limits the form of use: if admitted, the contents are read or played into evidence, and the record itself is received as an exhibit only if the adverse party offers it.

FRE 803(8), public records, explains the public-records distractor. Public-office factual findings from a legally authorized investigation can qualify in civil cases, but the answer choice points to the wrong offered item. The offered evidence is Ruth's personal voice memo used to prepare the formal report, not the formal public record itself. The source row's credited answer therefore stays the recorded recollection exception.

FRE 1002, the original-document rule, explains the best-evidence distractor. The rule requires an original writing, recording, or photograph when a party is proving that item's content. Here, the proponent is not trying to prove the terms of the later incident report. The voice memo is the earlier record of Ruth's remembered facts, so the later report is not the required "best evidence."
