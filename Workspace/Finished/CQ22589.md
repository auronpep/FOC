### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** Under the doctrine of frustration of purpose, a party's remaining contractual duties are discharged when that party's principal purpose is substantially frustrated without fault by an unforeseen event whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption of the contract. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 265. Here, MercyStream's principal purpose — promoting its platform by advertising Paul's exclusive album during his live televised concert — was completely destroyed by Paul's death. The network could still air the ad, but the entire campaign was built around the concert broadcast. Paul's death was unforeseeable and no fault of MercyStream's. The fact that the contract did not expressly mention the concert is irrelevant; the parties' shared understanding that the ad would run during the concert establishes the basic assumption. MercyStream is discharged from its obligation to pay.

**Gold Key:** Frustration of purpose ≠ impossibility. Impossibility asks "can the performance still happen?" Frustration asks "does the performance still matter to the party who bargained for it?" When the performance is possible but the reason for it is gone, frustration of purpose — not impossibility — is the correct doctrine.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**C (original A) — Dominant Trap:** This choice is attractive because it seems logical on the surface: the network can still run the ad at the agreed time, so there is no physical or legal barrier to performance. A student who focuses only on whether performance is *possible* — without asking whether the *purpose* survives — will pick this. **The breaker:** The doctrine of frustration of purpose does not require that performance be impossible. It requires only that the party's principal purpose be substantially destroyed by an unforeseen event. The ad can air, but it is worthless to MercyStream without the concert. **Gold Key:** Frustration of purpose ≠ impossibility. The performance can still happen; the reason for it cannot.

**D (original B):** This choice tempts the student who fixates on the written contract's silence about the concert. It seems to invoke the parol evidence rule — if it's not in writing, it doesn't count. **The breaker:** The frustration-of-purpose doctrine looks to the parties' shared basic assumptions, not just the four corners of the written agreement. Under Restatement § 265, the critical question is whether the non-occurrence of the event (the concert) was a basic assumption of the contract. The parties' mutual understanding satisfies this even without an express term. The parol evidence rule does not bar proof of the foundational purpose.

**A (original C):** This choice names the wrong doctrine. Impossibility applies when performance cannot occur — the music hall burned down, the subject matter was destroyed. Here, the network can still broadcast the ad; the performance is literally possible. **The breaker:** Impossibility requires that the contractual duty itself be incapable of performance. The network's duty (to air the ad) is unaffected. What is destroyed is MercyStream's *reason* for contracting, not the network's ability to perform. Naming impossibility here misidentifies the legal issue.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule — Frustration of Purpose:**

Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 265 provides:

> Where, after a contract is made, a party's principal purpose is substantially frustrated without his fault by the occurrence of an event the non-occurrence of which was a basic assumption on which the contract was made, his remaining duties to render performance are discharged, unless the language or the circumstances indicate the contrary.

**Elements:**
1. A supervening event occurs after contract formation;
2. The event substantially frustrates a principal purpose of the party seeking discharge;
3. The frustration is without that party's fault;
4. The non-occurrence of the event was a basic assumption on which the contract was made.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 265; *Krell v. Henry*, [1903] 2 K.B. 740 (coronation case — contract frustrated when coronation was cancelled even though the written agreement did not mention it); *Taylor v. Caldwell* (1863) 3 B. & S. 826 (distinguishing impossibility from frustration).

**Application to this item:** All four elements are satisfied. Paul's death was a supervening, unforeseeable event. MercyStream's principal purpose — advertising an exclusive album during a live televised concert — was completely destroyed. MercyStream was not at fault. The non-occurrence of the concert (i.e., Paul being alive to perform) was a basic assumption of the contract, as evidenced by the parties' shared understanding that the ad would run during the concert broadcast.

**Distinction from impossibility:** Impossibility (*Restatement* § 266) applies when the performance itself cannot occur (destruction of subject matter, supervening illegality). Here, the network can still air the ad. The performance is possible; only the *purpose* is gone. This is the textbook frustration-of-purpose scenario.

**Verification:** The tested rule (frustration of purpose), the legal issue (discharge of contractual duties when the principal purpose is destroyed), and the credited outcome (the streaming service is discharged) are all unchanged from the original. The official key is confirmed.

Although the written contract did not expressly state it, both parties understood the ad would run during the last 15 minutes of Paul's live concert, which the network was broadcasting. The spot was designed to promote MercyStream by featuring Paul's "surprise" album release available only on that platform. On March 14, Paul was killed in a car accident. **Call:** Should the court rule in the network's favor?

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "No, because the contract did not specifically state the advertisement would run during the concert."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice says the defense fails because the contract didn't mention the concert. But the question is whether the *purpose* was frustrated, not whether the contract was complete. The frustration-of-purpose doctrine looks to the parties' basic assumptions, not just the written words.
2. **Student label:** Parol evidence red herring — the doctrine doesn't require express terms.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A correct version would say "No, because the streaming service's principal purpose was substantially frustrated by an unforeseeable event." The reason needs to name the correct doctrine, not a contract-formality rule.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Frustration of purpose under Restatement § 265 does not require the basic assumption to be stated in the writing. *Krell v. Henry* ([1903] 2 K.B. 740) held that the coronation need not be mentioned in the contract for frustration to apply.

**Choice B: "No, under the doctrine of frustration of purpose."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The campaign was built around the concert. The concert is gone. The reason for the contract is destroyed. This is the classic frustration scenario — performance is possible but pointless for the party who bargained for it.
2. **Student label:** Correct — frustration of purpose discharges the duty.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This choice is already true and responsive. It names the correct doctrine and reaches the correct outcome.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** All four elements of Restatement § 265 are satisfied: (1) Paul's death was a supervening event; (2) MercyStream's principal purpose (advertising during the concert) was substantially frustrated; (3) MercyStream was not at fault; (4) Paul's performance was a basic assumption of the contract.

**Choice C: "Yes, because there was nothing preventing the network from running the advertisement at the agreed time."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the dominant trap. It's true that the network can still air the ad — but that's not the question. The question is whether *MercyStream's purpose* survived. The ad without the concert is like a billboard in an empty field. It can exist, but it doesn't serve the reason it was created.
2. **Student label:** Dominant trap — performance is possible, but that's not the test.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A correct "Yes" answer would need to explain why frustration of purpose does NOT apply — for example, if the purpose wasn't substantially frustrated, or if the event was foreseeable.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Frustration of purpose does not require that performance be impossible. The doctrine focuses on the destruction of the party's principal purpose, not the other party's ability to perform. *See* Restatement § 265; *Krell v. Henry*.

**Choice D: "Yes, since the understanding that the ad would run during the concert was not in the written contract."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice tries to use the contract's silence as a weapon. But frustration of purpose doesn't depend on what's in the writing — it depends on what the parties assumed when they made the deal. Both sides knew the ad was for the concert.
2. **Student label:** Wrong — the parol evidence rule doesn't bar the frustration defense.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A correct "Yes" answer would need to show that the basic assumption requirement was not met — for example, if the concert was never part of the parties' shared understanding.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The basic assumption element of § 265 is satisfied by the parties' mutual understanding, even without an express term. *Krell v. Henry* is directly on point.
