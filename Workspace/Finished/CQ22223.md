## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

1. Final question

The director of a state Good Samaritan Winter Pantry program was subpoenaed before a state grand jury investigating kickbacks in emergency-food contracts. After being sworn, Daniel invoked the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination. The prosecutor then granted Daniel use immunity, and Daniel testified that for years he had known his assistant, Timothy, and other program employees were taking kickbacks from pantry suppliers. Timothy was later charged in state court with receiving kickbacks. At Timothy's trial, Daniel was called as a witness for the defense. On direct examination, Daniel testified that he had never heard of Timothy or any other program employee taking corrupt payments. On cross-examination, the prosecutor asked Daniel, "Didn't you testify before the grand jury that you had known for years that Timothy was taking kickbacks from program vendors?" Daniel refused to answer, saying he had received immunity before the grand jury testimony. Should the court compel Daniel to answer the question?

A defense witness denied hearing of the defendant's kickbacks at trial, but he had earlier said the opposite under oath before a grand jury after receiving use immunity. Can the court force him to answer the cross-examination question, and does that prior statement come in for both impeachment and truth?

7. Full right-answer explanation

`B` is right because the court should compel Daniel to answer, and the answer serves both evidentiary jobs. `Gold Key:` a prior inconsistent statement made under oath at a grand jury proceeding falls within `FRE 801(d)(1)(A)`, so it is not limited to impeachment; it may be used for its truth as well. `Gold Key:` a witness who voluntarily testifies on direct about a subject cannot stop relevant cross-examination on that same subject by reviving the Fifth midstream. `Silver Key:` when the array offers "impeachment only," "substantive only," and "both," ask whether the stem supplies the special trigger for both. A sworn grand-jury statement does, so the matrix points to "both," not either half-answer.

8. Full wrong-answer explanations

`A` sells the student who remembers the hearsay upgrade but forgets the baseline impeachment rule. Once Daniel gave a prior inconsistent statement, the prosecutor could use it to attack his credibility; because it was also under oath at a grand jury proceeding, it is not stuck in the substantive-only lane.

`C` sells the student who overreads the immunity point and treats the old grant as a permanent shield against any later questioning. Daniel chose to testify on direct that he had never heard of kickbacks, so he opened the door to relevant cross on that same subject; the earlier immunity does not let him give the safe half of the story and block the check.

`D` sells the classic evidence half-truth: prior inconsistent statements impeach, so stop there. The breaker is the oath-plus-proceeding trigger. Because the statement was made under oath before a grand jury and Daniel is now on the stand subject to cross-examination, the same statement is admissible for truth too.

9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The credited outcome is unchanged from the source item: the proper result is "yes, and both uses." The governing evidence rules are `FRE 613` and `FRE 801(d)(1)(A)`. `FRE 613` permits impeachment with a witness's prior inconsistent statement. `FRE 801(d)(1)(A)` separately provides that when the declarant testifies and is subject to cross-examination, a prior inconsistent statement given under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, or other proceeding is not hearsay. A grand jury qualifies as an "other proceeding," so Daniel's prior statement can be used substantively as well as for impeachment. Authority: `FRE 801(d)(1)(A)` and `FRE 613` at Cornell LII (`https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_801`, `https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_613`).

On the Fifth Amendment point, `Kastigar v. United States`, `406 U.S. 441` (1972), explains that use-and-derivative-use immunity is sufficient to compel the original testimony, but it protects the witness against use of compelled testimony against that witness. Standing alone, the old immunity grant does not automatically compel fresh testimony in a later proceeding; `Pillsbury Co. v. Conboy`, `459 U.S. 248` (1983), makes that clear. But that does not change the answer here, because Daniel voluntarily testified on direct about the very subject of the cross-examination. Under `Rogers v. United States`, `340 U.S. 367` (1951), and `Brown v. United States`, `356 U.S. 148` (1958), a witness may not tell a one-sided version of the subject and then invoke the privilege to block relevant follow-up on the same subject. So the court should compel the answer, and the answer is admissible for both impeachment and truth.
