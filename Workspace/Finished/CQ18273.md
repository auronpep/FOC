### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** Voluntary intoxication can, in some jurisdictions, be used to negate a *specific-intent* mens rea element — for example, to show the defendant could not form the premeditation required for first-degree murder. But when the legislature makes an element strict liability, the prosecution does not need to prove any mental state as to that element. If no mens rea is required, there is nothing for intoxication to negate. Here, the DUI statute imposes strict liability as to the blood-alcohol threshold. Hannah's self-induced impairment cannot erase an element the law does not require her to have thought about.

**Gold Key (Voluntary Intoxication — Strict-Liability Wall):** Voluntary intoxication is a defense only when it negatives an *element* of the offense. If the element is strict liability, there is no mental state to negative — the defense hits a wall. This is true regardless of how drunk the defendant was.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (new letter A) — attractive because:** Students know that extreme intoxication can sometimes matter in criminal law, and the idea that severity should count feels intuitively right. If the defendant was *really* drunk, surely that should matter. **Breaker:** The severity of intoxication is irrelevant when the element is strict liability. The legislature has decided that no mental state is required as to the blood-alcohol threshold. Whether Hannah was mildly buzzed or blackout drunk, the strict-liability element is established the moment her BAC crosses the line. No amount of intoxication negates an element that requires no mens rea. **Gold Key:** The Voluntary Intoxication — Strict-Liability Wall applies regardless of degree.

**B (new letter B) — THE DOMINANT TRAP — attractive because:** This is the most dangerous distractor. Students correctly learn that voluntary intoxication can negate *knowledge* — a specific-intent mens rea. They then overgeneralize: if intoxication can negate knowledge of a victim's age in some statutory-rape debates, why not knowledge of one's own blood-alcohol level? The logic feels internally consistent. **Breaker:** The blood-alcohol threshold is strict liability. The prosecution does not need to prove Hannah *knew* her BAC was over the limit. Because the element requires no mental state, there is no knowledge for intoxication to negate. The half-truth (intoxication negates knowledge) is true for specific-intent elements but collapses when applied to strict-liability elements. **Gold Key:** Voluntary Intoxication — Strict-Liability Wall.

**C (new letter C) — attractive because:** Students conflate extreme intoxication with insanity. If someone is so drunk they can't perceive reality, it feels like they should qualify for some kind of incapacity defense. **Breaker:** Voluntary intoxication does not constitute legal insanity. Insanity requires a mental disease or defect. Acute self-induced intoxication, no matter how severe, is not a mental disease or defect under any recognized insanity standard (M'Naghten, MPC § 4.01, or otherwise). **Gold Key:** Intoxication ≠ Insanity — self-induced impairment is not a mental disease or defect.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Rule:** Voluntary intoxication is not a defense to a strict-liability element because no mens rea exists for intoxication to negate.

**Authority:**

1. **Model Penal Code § 2.08(1):** "Except as provided in Subsection (4) of this Section, intoxication of the actor is not a defense unless it negatives an element of the offense." When an element is strict liability, there is no element to negative. MPC § 2.08(2) further provides that when recklessness establishes an element, self-induced unawareness of risk is immaterial.

2. **Montana v. Egelhoff, 518 U.S. 37 (1996):** The Supreme Court held that a state may constitutionally bar evidence of voluntary intoxication to negate the mens rea of a criminal offense. The Court affirmed that voluntary intoxication is not a fundamental principle of justice and that states have broad discretion to exclude it as a defense.

3. **Ohio Rev. Code § 2901.21(d):** "Voluntary intoxication may not be taken into consideration in determining the existence of a mental state that is an element of a criminal offense." (Representative of the majority statutory approach.)

4. **Common-law foundation:** 1 Hale, *Pleas of the Crown* 32: "The intoxicated defendant shall have no privilege by this voluntary contracted madness, but shall have the same judgment as if he were in his right senses."

**Application:** The jurisdiction treats the blood-alcohol threshold as strict liability. No mens rea is required as to BAC level. Hannah's voluntary intoxication therefore cannot negate an element that requires no mental state. The credited answer (D) is correct.
