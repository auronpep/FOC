### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** Burglary requires entry with the intent to commit a crime inside. Timothy forced his way into Lydia's private office, but the intended inside act was only looking at the answer cards. The stem tells us that looking at those cards was not a crime. His mistaken belief that it was criminal does not create the required burglary intent.

**Gold Key:** Burglary intent points to the intended inside offense, not to the defendant's guilty feelings. If the intended inside conduct would not be a crime even if completed, the burglary intent is missing.

**Silver Key:** Split "bad entry" from "inside-object intent." The breaking and entering supplies the entry act, but the answer turns on whether the thing he entered to do was itself criminal.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because factual impossibility is often no defense when the intended crime is real but completion fails because of a fact the defendant did not know. The breaker is the frame: Timothy did not intend a real crime that failed because the cards were hidden; he intended conduct that the law did not criminalize, so the problem is legal impossibility/lack of burglary intent.

**B.** This choice sells a simple completion story: Timothy did not find or read the cards, so it feels as if the crime never got far enough. The breaker is that burglary does not require completion of the intended inside offense; if the required intent exists at entry, the burglary is complete even if the intended offense never happens.

**D.** This is the dominant trap because the stem gives a forced entry plus a defendant who thought he was doing something criminal. **Gold Key:** a mistaken belief that noncriminal conduct is criminal does not supply intent to commit an actual crime inside. The breaking and entering is not enough for burglary without that inside criminal-object intent.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. Burglary requires entry with intent to commit an additional crime inside. At common law, that was breaking and entering the dwelling house of another at night with intent to commit a felony therein; modern formulations broaden the place/time elements but preserve the required purpose to commit a crime inside. Model Penal Code section 221.1(1) uses the formulation "purpose to commit a crime therein," and modern statutes use the same core idea. For example, South Carolina Code sections 16-11-312(A) and 16-11-313(A) require entry without consent and intent to commit a crime therein. See https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t16c011.php.

The legal-impossibility principle confirms the result. In People v. Jaffe, 185 N.Y. 497 (1906), the court held that when the intended completed act would not be a crime, the actor's belief that it is criminal does not make it a criminal attempt. The same idea controls the burglary-intent problem here: Timothy's intended inside conduct was not a crime, so he lacked the burglary intent. See https://www.nycourts.gov/reporter/archives/p_jaffe.htm.

The credited answer remains acquittal. The wrong alternatives preserve the same traps: completion bait, bad-motive/breaking-and-entering bait, and factual-impossibility bait.
