## 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. The warrant listed tiny stolen pendants and authorized a search of Mary's apartment. Gold Key: the scope of a warrant search follows the object named in the warrant and the places within the described premises where that object could reasonably be found. A cloth pouch can hold tiny pendants, so opening it stayed within the warrant. Silver Key: match the object to the container; a pendant can fit in a pouch, but it cannot fit in a digital file.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because closed containers feel like a second privacy layer. The breaker is that a valid warrant for small physical items already reaches containers in the searched place where those items could reasonably be hidden.

C is attractive because it correctly starts with the valid warrant and says yes. The breaker is its overreach: a physical pendant cannot be hidden in a digital file, so the warrant's object does not justify searching every file on a laptop.

D is attractive because a personal keepsake pouch sounds private. The breaker is the categorical word "never"; privacy in the container does not defeat a valid warrant when the listed object could reasonably be inside it.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule is Fourth Amendment warrant scope. The Fourth Amendment requires warrants to particularly describe the place to be searched and the persons or things to be seized. Once officers have a valid warrant to search a described place for a described physical object, the search may extend to places and containers within that place where the object could reasonably be found. United States v. Ross, 456 U.S. 798, 824-25 (1982), states the object-and-place principle: search scope is defined by the object sought and the places where there is probable cause to believe it may be found. Although Ross involved an automobile search, the Court explained the same scope measure by comparison to a magistrate-authorized warrant. Here, tiny silver cross pendants could reasonably be hidden inside a cloth pouch in the apartment, so opening the pouch stayed within the warrant. A warrant for physical pendants would not extend to digital files because the object could not be physically located there.
