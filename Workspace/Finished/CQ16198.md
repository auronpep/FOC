## 7. Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

Lydia, the homeowner, consented to the police entering her farmhouse. A homeowner's consent to police entry is binding on a non-paying guest — even if the guest has a legitimate expectation of privacy as an overnight guest (*Minnesota v. Olson*), the owner's consent overrides that interest. The police were lawfully present in the kitchen via Lydia's invitation. Once there, they recognized Paul as matching the description of the robber, giving them probable cause to arrest. The search of Paul's person incident to that lawful arrest was valid, and the stolen items found on his person are admissible. **Silver Key:** When the police enter with the homeowner's consent, the guest's Fourth Amendment rights are not violated — even if the guest is an overnight guest with standing to challenge a nonconsensual entry.

**Gold Key:** A homeowner may consent to police entry into the home, and that consent overrides any Fourth Amendment privacy interest of a non-paying guest. The guest cannot block the owner's consent. *See Georgia v. Randolph*, 547 U.S. 103 (2006) (distinguishing between present co-occupant refusal and absent guest); *Illinois v. Rodriguez*, 497 U.S. 177 (1990) (consent by person with common authority).

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A — Grant the motion, because, as a guest, the thief has sufficient standing to contest the lack of probable cause at the time of entry.** The attraction: students may think that because the police lacked probable cause at the moment they entered, the entry was unconstitutional. The breaker: police did not need probable cause to enter because they had the homeowner's consent. Consent is an exception to both the warrant requirement and the probable cause requirement. The entry was lawful regardless of whether the police had probable cause at that moment. The lack of PC before seeing Paul is irrelevant because the police were lawfully present via consent.

**C — Grant the motion, because, as a guest, the thief has sufficient standing to contest the entry of the house without a warrant. (Dominant Trap)** The attraction: this states a correct legal principle — an overnight social guest DOES have a legitimate expectation of privacy and standing to challenge a warrantless nonconsensual entry (*Minnesota v. Olson*). Students who know this doctrine may pick C without considering the critical exception: the homeowner's consent overrides the guest's privacy interest. The breaker: while Paul as an overnight guest would have standing to challenge a nonconsensual entry, here the entry WAS consensual — Lydia invited the police in. A homeowner's consent is binding on a non-paying guest. The guest cannot override the owner's consent.

**D — Deny the motion, because the thief had no ownership or other possessory interest in the premises.** The attraction: this reaches the correct outcome (deny), and students who are unsure about the reasoning may be drawn to it because it sounds plausible — Paul was just a guest, after all. The breaker: this misstates the legal reason. An overnight social guest DOES have a legitimate expectation of privacy in the host's home (*Minnesota v. Olson*). The reason the motion should be denied is NOT that Paul lacked a privacy interest, but rather that the homeowner's consent overrode whatever privacy interest Paul had. Right answer, wrong reason.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Authority:**

(1) *Minnesota v. Olson*, 495 U.S. 91 (1990) — An overnight guest has a legitimate expectation of privacy in the host's home and standing to challenge a warrantless nonconsensual entry. The Court reasoned that staying overnight serves a valuable social function and creates a reasonable expectation of privacy.

(2) However, the homeowner's consent to police entry overrides the guest's privacy interest. *See Illinois v. Rodriguez*, 497 U.S. 177 (1990) — A person with "common authority" over premises may consent to a search, and that consent is valid against others with a privacy interest in the premises. The homeowner has superior authority to the guest.

(3) *Georgia v. Randolph*, 547 U.S. 103 (2006) — A physically present co-occupant may refuse consent, but an absent guest cannot override a present co-owner's consent. Here, Lydia (the owner) consented, and Paul (the guest) was not in a position to refuse.

(4) Once police were lawfully present in the kitchen via Lydia's consent and recognized Paul matching the description, they had probable cause to arrest. The search of Paul's person incident to that lawful arrest was valid under *Chimel v. California*, 395 U.S. 752 (1969) and *United States v. Robinson*, 414 U.S. 218 (1973).

The tested rule (homeowner consent overrides guest's privacy interest + search incident to arrest), legal issue (whether evidence found during a search incident to arrest after homeowner-consented entry should be suppressed), and credited outcome (motion denied; evidence admissible) are UNCHANGED from the original.
