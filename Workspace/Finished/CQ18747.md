### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Stephen committed **embezzlement**. The common-law distinction between the theft offenses turns on the manner in which the defendant first came into possession of the property. Larceny requires a trespassory taking — a wrongful caption at the outset. False pretenses requires a false representation of a material past or present fact that induces the victim to pass title to the property. Embezzlement, by contrast, covers the fraudulent conversion of property that the defendant lawfully came into possession of by entrustment. The facts put this squarely in embezzlement: Timothy lawfully entrusted the Bible to Stephen for restoration; Stephen lawfully accepted possession; and Stephen then fraudulently converted the Bible by selling it to a private collector and keeping the proceeds. **Gold Key:** the lawful possession is what makes the later conversion embezzlement, not larceny or false pretenses.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Larceny, because every wrongful taking of property is larceny.**

The dominant trap. The overclaim is attractive because everyday English treats "theft" as a synonym for larceny, and a student who half-remembers that larceny is "the wrongful taking of personal property" reaches for the broad form. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: larceny requires a trespassory taking — a wrongful caption at the outset — and here Stephen's possession was lawful. The lawful possession re-routes the offense into embezzlement, not larceny.

**D. False pretenses, because Stephen sold the Bible to someone else.**

The neighboring-doctrine trap. This is attractive because a sale-to-a-third-party fact pattern can look like false pretenses. The breaker is the mechanics: false pretenses requires a false representation of a material fact that induces the victim to pass title; here Stephen never made a false representation to Timothy, and Timothy did not pass title to Stephen. The offense against Timothy is the conversion of lawfully possessed entrusted property, not obtaining title by fraud.

**B. No offense, because Timothy voluntarily handed over the Bible.**

The consent-coverage overclaim. This sounds like a black-letter defense, but consent to entrust property for one purpose (restoration) does not authorize fraudulent conversion to a different purpose (sale for the bailee's own benefit). The breaker is call focus: the question asks for the best theft offense, and the entrustment-plus-conversion fact pattern is exactly what embezzlement covers.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18747, a Criminal Law item on the embezzlement / larceny / false pretenses distinction. The official key is A: embezzlement, because the shop owner lawfully possessed the camera before converting it. The supplied lb_rule_reference confirms the rule: "A bailee or agent who lawfully possesses property and later fraudulently converts it commits embezzlement rather than larceny."

The common-law distinction between these three theft offenses turns on the manner in which the defendant first came into possession of the property, and (for false pretenses) on whether the victim was induced to pass title. Larceny is the trespassory taking and carrying away of the personal property of another with intent to permanently deprive. False pretenses is the fraudulent obtaining of title to the personal property of another by means of a false representation of a material past or present fact, made with intent to defraud, on which the victim relies. Embezzlement is the fraudulent conversion of the personal property of another by one who is already in lawful possession of it. The Model Penal Code consolidates these distinctions into a single "theft" framework (§§ 223.1–223.4), but the common-law distinction remains highly tested on the bar. The Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment and standard criminal-law treatises (LaFave, Dubber, Dressler) all confirm that a bailee who lawfully comes into possession and later fraudulently converts the property commits embezzlement, not larceny. The transformed outcome is identical: Stephen lawfully received the Bible, then sold it and kept the proceeds. Embezzlement is the best theft offense.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. Larceny, because every wrongful taking of property is larceny.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Larceny requires a trespassory taking — a wrongful caption at the outset — and here Stephen's possession was lawful.
2. **Student label:** Colloquial-larceny overclaim.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Larceny might be the right answer if Stephen had taken the Bible without Timothy's consent; here Timothy consented to the entrustment, so the original caption was lawful."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Larceny is the trespassory taking and carrying away of the personal property of another with intent to permanently deprive. Where possession is lawful at the outset, the later conversion is embezzlement, not larceny. The colloquial "every wrongful taking is larceny" formulation misstates the rule. (Common-law theft-offense distinction; LaFave, Dubber, Dressler.)

### B. No offense, because Timothy voluntarily handed over the Bible.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Consent to entrust property for one purpose does not authorize fraudulent conversion to a different purpose.
2. **Student label:** Consent-coverage overclaim.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Timothy's consent to the entrustment is what made Stephen's possession lawful; it is not a defense to the later fraudulent conversion."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Embezzlement is the fraudulent conversion of property by one who is already in lawful possession of it. The lawful possession comes from the entrustment, and the offense is the later conversion, not the original taking.

### C. Embezzlement, because Stephen lawfully possessed the Bible before converting it.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Lawful possession plus later fraudulent conversion is the textbook embezzlement fact pattern.
2. **Student label:** Residual answer.
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Embezzlement is the fraudulent conversion of the personal property of another by one who is already in lawful possession of it. Stephen lawfully accepted the Bible for restoration and later sold it and kept the proceeds. (Common-law theft-offense distinction; Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment; Model Penal Code § 223.4.)

### D. False pretenses, because Stephen sold the Bible to someone else.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** False pretenses requires a false representation of a material fact that induces the victim to pass title; here Stephen never made a false representation to Timothy, and Timothy did not pass title.
2. **Student label:** False-pretenses misfit.
3. **True/responsive version:** "False pretenses might be the right answer if Stephen had obtained the Bible by lying to Timothy; here Stephen obtained the Bible by accepting an entrustment, not by lying."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** False pretenses is the fraudulent obtaining of title to the personal property of another by means of a false representation of a material past or present fact, made with intent to defraud, on which the victim relies. The sale to the collector is a downstream disposition, not a misrepresentation to Timothy.
