### 6. Full right-answer explanation

A is correct. The injury came from the ordinary, visible risk of falling from a six-foot stepladder, not from a hidden danger unique to the furnace.

**Gold Key:** A manufacturer generally does not have to warn about an open and obvious danger. Here, the danger that materialized was falling from a ladder. A warning on the furnace about ordinary ladder balance would not turn the furnace into a defective product merely because the manufacturer omitted it.

**Silver Key:** Do not let “no warnings” automatically become “defective.” First ask what risk caused the injury. If the risk is an ordinary, obvious outside risk, the warning-defect answer is the trap.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B is wrong.** This choice gives a common-sense scolding answer, not the product-defect reason. The issue is not whether Daniel made the best household decision. The issue is whether the furnace manufacturer had to warn about the risk that injured him. Hiring a professional is not the rule.

**C is wrong.** This invents an accessory rule. A furnace manufacturer is not required to attach a ladder merely because a homeowner might use a ladder to clean the furnace. The answer adds a design requirement that the problem does not support.

**D is wrong.** This is the dominant trap. It sounds like a real warning-defect answer because warnings and instructions can matter in products liability. But **Gold Key:** no warning is required for an obvious danger. **Silver Key:** the injury source is the ladder fall, not a hidden furnace-specific danger. The missing-warning fact is not enough by itself.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The black-letter rule is unchanged from the source: a manufacturer generally has no duty to warn against open and obvious dangers, and the source explanation states that nothing about the furnace added to the ordinary danger of working on a ladder.  Products liability recognizes warning defects as a category of product defect; the Restatement Third, as described by ALI, covers manufacturing, design, and inadequate-instructions-or-warnings defects. ([American Law Institute][1]) A warning-defect theory turns on whether reasonable warnings or instructions could reduce or avoid a foreseeable risk and whether their omission makes the product not reasonably safe. ([The Florida Bar][2]) But an obvious risk generally does not require a warning; Justia’s products-liability summary gives the ordinary example that a steak knife need not warn that it is sharp. ([Justia][3])

Here, the risk that materialized was the visible danger of falling from a six-foot ladder. The variation changes the names and setting only. It preserves the same tested rule, the same correct answer, and the same warning-defect trap.
