## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question - revised stem + call

Daniel was charged with capital first-degree murder after a fatal shooting outside a city-sponsored Christmas choir festival. The only available punishments were death or life in prison without parole. During jury selection, the trial judge, over Daniel's objection, granted the prosecution's for-cause challenges to five prospective jurors. Each juror said, after questioning by both sides, that he or she was personally opposed to the death penalty and was unsure whether he or she could ever vote to impose it.

The jury convicted Daniel and, after a separate sentencing hearing, sentenced him to death. On appeal, Daniel's only argument is that excusing those prospective jurors violated his federal constitutional right to a jury chosen from a fair cross section of the community.

How should the court of appeals rule on the conviction and the death sentence?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

In a capital case, may a court affirm both conviction and death sentence when jurors unsure they could impose death were excused for cause and the defendant raises only a fair-cross-section challenge?

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**C** is the answer. The court should affirm both the conviction and the death sentence. A juror may be excused for cause in a capital case if the juror's views about the death penalty would prevent or substantially impair the juror's ability to perform the duties of a juror. That rule applies to service in the guilt and penalty phases. **Gold Key:** death qualification is allowed when the juror's views would prevent or substantially impair impartial performance; it is not a fair-cross-section violation merely because the removed jurors share a view about capital punishment. **Silver Key:** match the remedy to the only argument made. The appeal attacks fair cross section, not a separate sentencing error.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This answer sells a partial-remedy instinct: keep the conviction but redo only the penalty phase because the challenged jurors cared about death. The breaker is the death-qualification rule. **Gold Key:** the Constitution permits excusing jurors whose death-penalty views would prevent or substantially impair their duties, so the sentence does not need a new jury on this record.

**B.** This answer treats the jury-selection objection as poisoning the whole case. The breaker is overreach. The fair-cross-section argument does not defeat a properly death-qualified jury, so neither phase is reversed.

**D.** This answer tries to convert the jury-selection objection into an automatic life sentence. The breaker is invented remedy. The valid death-qualification ruling leaves the death sentence in place; it does not require resentencing to life.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are preserved. The original and this variation both ask whether excusing prospective capital jurors who are unsure they could ever impose death violates the fair-cross-section right and what happens to the conviction and sentence.

Under **Wainwright v. Witt, 469 U.S. 412 (1985)**, a juror may be excluded for cause when the juror's views would prevent or substantially impair the performance of juror duties. Under **Lockhart v. McCree, 476 U.S. 162 (1986)**, using a death-qualified jury for the guilt phase of a capital trial does not violate the Sixth Amendment fair-cross-section requirement. The credited answer is therefore to affirm both the conviction and the death sentence.
