### 1. Final Question

Three years ago, Martha suffered a severe head injury during a retreat-center maintenance accident. During the next three months, she had eight sudden fainting episodes, each lasting about two minutes. Every episode happened at home. After that three-month period, Martha had no further episodes.

Last week, Martha was carefully operating a small motorized lift in a private Christian publishing warehouse when she suddenly lost consciousness because of the old head injury. The lift rolled into Stephen, permanently injuring him.

Martha is charged under a state statute defining "reckless maiming" as "causing permanent injury to another person by acting in knowing disregard of the plain and strong likelihood that death or serious personal injury will result."

Which of the following is Martha's most effective argument in defense against the charge of reckless maiming?

### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**D is correct.** The statute requires more than a bad result. It requires Martha to act in knowing disregard of a plain and strong likelihood that death or serious personal injury will result. The strongest defense is that she reasonably believed the fainting episodes were over: they happened only in the three months after the accident, only at home, and not again for nearly three years.

**Gold Key:** in blackout-operation questions, separate the blackout from the earlier choice to operate. The state can use the earlier choice only if the defendant knew the serious-risk condition was still plainly likely. **Silver Key:** time-slice the answer choices. A choice about what Martha could do after fainting does not answer whether she knowingly disregarded the risk before she fainted.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A** sells the real unconsciousness idea. It is attractive because Martha truly could not steer or stop the lift after she fainted. The breaker is timing: the charged recklessness, if any, would be the earlier decision to operate the lift while knowingly disregarding a strong likelihood of serious harm. **Gold Key:** the involuntary blackout movement does not erase an earlier culpable risk choice.

**B** sells a certainty standard. It is attractive because Martha did not know the exact injury would happen, and the accident may feel unforeseeable in hindsight. The breaker is the statutory wording: the state need not prove she knew injury would occur; it must prove knowing disregard of a plain and strong likelihood that death or serious personal injury would result.

**C** sells a clean-hands story. It is attractive because the head injury was not Martha's fault. The breaker is that the source of the medical condition is not the charged mental state. If Martha knowingly disregarded a plain and strong likelihood of serious harm when she operated the lift, the nonculpable origin of the old injury would not defeat the charge.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule.** Criminal liability ordinarily requires a voluntary act and the required mens rea. Under Model Penal Code section 2.01(2), a bodily movement during unconsciousness is not a voluntary act. But when a defendant voluntarily begins a dangerous activity while aware of a condition that makes loss of consciousness plainly likely, the culpable act may be the earlier decision to engage in the activity. For recklessness-style culpability, Model Penal Code section 2.02(2)(c) requires conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk; the source statute here uses the even more pointed phrase "knowing disregard of the plain and strong likelihood" of death or serious personal injury.

**Authority floor.** Model Penal Code sections 2.01 and 2.02(2)(c) supply the voluntary-act and conscious-disregard rules. People v. Decina, 2 N.Y.2d 133, 138 N.E.2d 799 (1956), is the leading blackout-driving illustration: the relevant culpability can lie in knowingly driving while subject to seizures, not in the involuntary movement during the seizure itself.

**Source-key confirmation.** Research does not contradict the supplied official key. The source tested whether the defendant's reasonable belief that prior unconsciousness episodes had ended negated knowing disregard of a strong likelihood of serious harm. The variation preserves the tested rule, legal issue, timing structure, mental-state requirement, dominant unconsciousness trap, and credited outcome.
