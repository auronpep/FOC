## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** Daniel's strongest defense is that Martha, the robbery victim, justifiably shot Paul while resisting the robbery. Felony murder usually treats a killing during an enumerated felony as murder even without an intent to kill. But the hard issue here is not Daniel's lack of intent. It is who legally caused the killing. Under the common agency limitation recognized in many jurisdictions, felony-murder liability does not attach when the fatal act is committed by the victim or another adversary of the felony, rather than by Daniel or one of his accomplices.

**Gold Key:** For co-felon deaths, separate "a death happened during the felony" from "a felon or accomplice did the killing." The surviving felon's best defense is the agency/adversary-killing limitation when a victim justifiably kills the co-felon.

**Silver Key:** Do not let the toy-pistol facts become the whole case. The call asks for the most effective defense, so choose the answer that attacks the legal source of the killing rather than the answer that argues the robbery was supposed to stay peaceful.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### Choice A - Dominant Trap

**Why it is attractive:** The toy pistols and Daniel's insistence on no violence make the robbery feel less deadly than a real armed robbery. A student may convert that feeling into a foreseeability defense and say no one should have expected a death.

**The breaker:** Robbery creates the normal risk that victims, bystanders, or police will resist with force. The toy-pistol detail may make Daniel feel less blameworthy, but it does not give the best felony-murder defense. **Silver Key:** the stronger move is to ask who did the killing; Martha's justified adversary act is the defense that matters.

### Choice B

**Why it is attractive:** Daniel did not want a killing, demanded no violence, and used toy pistols instead of real guns. That makes "no malice" sound morally and factually fair.

**The breaker:** Felony murder supplies malice from the intent to commit the underlying felony. Daniel's promise-conditioned participation may matter to blameworthiness, but it does not defeat felony murder by itself. **Gold Key:** the co-felon death defense is not "I lacked malice"; it is that the fatal shot came from the resisting victim.

### Choice C

**Why it is attractive:** Paul was Daniel's partner in the robbery, so calling Paul a "victim" sounds odd. The answer also notices the unusual fact that the dead person is not the store owner.

**The breaker:** Felony murder is not limited to the intended robbery victim. A death during the felony can trigger felony-murder analysis even if the person killed was not the target of the robbery. The better defense is narrower: Paul was killed by the justified act of an adversary resisting the felony.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule:** Felony murder generally treats a killing committed during an enumerated felony such as robbery as murder, with malice supplied by the intent to commit the felony. The co-felon death problem turns on causation and attribution. Under the agency theory or adversary-killing limitation recognized in many jurisdictions, a surviving felon is not guilty of felony murder for a co-felon's death when the fatal shot is fired by the victim, police, or another adversary who is resisting the felony. Some jurisdictions use broader proximate-cause reasoning, so the doctrine varies; the source question asks for the most effective listed defense, and the justified-victim-shooting argument is the only listed argument that supplies a real defense.

**Authority floor:** *People v. Washington*, 62 Cal. 2d 777, 402 P.2d 130 (1965), reversed a felony-murder conviction where a robbery victim killed the defendant's accomplice during the robbery and framed the issue as whether a robber can be convicted of murder for a killing committed by someone resisting the robbery. See https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/people-v-washington-24520. *Commonwealth v. Redline*, 391 Pa. 486, 137 A.2d 472 (1958), likewise treated a justifiable killing of a robber by a resisting police officer as unavailable to support murder liability against the surviving felon. See https://law.justia.com/cases/pennsylvania/supreme-court/1958/391-pa-486-0.html. Cornell's Wex summary also notes the jurisdictional exception where a store owner kills one robber and the exception prevents felony-murder liability for the surviving co-felon. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/felony_murder_rule.

Adjacent mastery: manslaughter causation, robbery as enumerated felony, proximate-cause felony murder jurisdictions.

## crossover_intersection_map

Criminal law crossover with self-defense by the victim, causation, accomplice liability, and robbery. No constitutional criminal procedure issue is added.

## review_truth

When a robbery victim justifiably kills a co-felon while resisting, the surviving felon's strongest felony-murder defense is that the fatal act was the adversary's justified act, not the felonious act of an accomplice.
