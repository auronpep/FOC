## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Martha owned a successful private Bible-memory tutoring company, but she had first learned woodworking by building small market booths with her father. To celebrate her nephew Timothy's completion of a year-long Scripture memory challenge, Martha decided to make him a gift with her own hands instead of buying one.

Martha entered into a written agreement with Lydia. The writing stated that Martha would personally build a cedar reading alcove in Lydia's backyard and that Lydia would pay $1,600 to Timothy on June 30 if Martha completed the work to Lydia's satisfaction. Timothy did not learn of the arrangement until June 30.

Before signing the written agreement, Lydia and Martha orally agreed that Lydia would make a reasonable effort to obtain a home-improvement loan to pay for the project, but that if Lydia could not obtain the loan by May 1, their agreement would have no effect. Lydia made reasonable efforts to obtain the loan but could not do so. On May 1, Lydia told Martha that she was calling off the project because the loan had not been obtained.

In an action for breach of contract brought against Lydia by the proper party, will Lydia be successful in asserting her inability to obtain the loan as a defense?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

Before signing a written service contract, the parties orally agreed that the writing would have no effect unless the buyer obtained a loan by a stated date; the buyer tried and failed to get the loan. Can the buyer use that failed loan condition as a defense?

### 3. Final answer choices

B. No, because Lydia is estopped from denying the validity of the written agreement.

C. No, because the loan arrangement was an oral agreement made before the writing and it contradicts the writing's terms.

D. Yes, because obtaining the loan was a condition precedent to the existence of an enforceable contract.

### 5. Correct answer

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is correct. Lydia is not trying to use the loan conversation to add a term to an already effective written contract. She is using it to show that the written agreement was not to become effective unless the loan condition happened. The condition failed after reasonable efforts, so the agreement had no effect.

Gold Key: a prior oral condition to the effectiveness of a written agreement can be proved despite the parol evidence rule because it goes to whether the contract became binding at all.

Silver Key: track the timing and purpose of the oral agreement. Before signing plus "no effect" language points to formation, not later modification.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because it reaches the same yes/no result as the credited answer and grabs a familiar idea: oral agreements can sometimes matter even around written service contracts. The breaker is timing. A modification changes an existing contract after formation; the loan agreement here was made before signing and was framed as a condition to the agreement's effectiveness. Silver Key: before signing plus "no effect" language points to formation, not modification.

B is attractive because Lydia signed a writing and then tried to avoid it, which can feel like the sort of move estoppel should block. The breaker is that the stem gives no reliance or inconsistent-position facts creating estoppel. Lydia is not accepting the written agreement while denying it when convenient; she is asserting that the parties agreed the writing would have no effect if the loan failed.

C is the dominant trap. It is attractive because students remember the parol evidence rule as a bar on prior oral agreements that contradict a writing. The breaker is the Gold Key: a prior oral condition to the effectiveness of the writing is admissible to show that no enforceable contract came into existence when the condition failed. The loan condition does not rewrite Lydia's payment promise; it explains when the whole agreement would become binding.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested issue remains Contracts, Contract Content and Meaning, Parol Evidence Rule, and the verified outline code is `52040300`. The source row's credited answer is consistent with the governing common-law rule.

The parol evidence rule generally bars prior or contemporaneous agreements offered to vary or contradict a binding integrated writing. But it does not bar evidence offered to show that the writing never became an effective contract. Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 217 states the condition-precedent rule for an integrated agreement subject to an oral condition, and Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 214 permits prior or contemporaneous evidence on threshold questions about integration and invalidating causes. The contract here is a service/construction-style project, so the common-law Restatement framing is the cleanest MBE authority layer; UCC Section 2-202 is not the controlling rule for this service project.

Lydia made reasonable efforts and failed to obtain the loan, so the condition precedent did not occur. The credited outcome remains unchanged: Lydia can assert the failed loan condition as a defense.
