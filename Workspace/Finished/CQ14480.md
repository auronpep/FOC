## 1. Final question

A Roman magistrate in Berea posted the following reward at the city gates:

> "To anyone who provides information leading to the arrest and conviction of
> whoever stole the original Isaiah scroll from the Berean synagogue — 500
> denarii."

Lydia, a purple-cloth merchant from Thyatira, knew of the reward, located the
thief — a man named Demetrius — and brought him to the magistrate, who arrested
him. The magistrate and Demetrius then agreed that, in exchange for Demetrius's
testimony against a man named Apollos, all charges against Demetrius would be
dropped. Demetrius testified and was released. The magistrate refused to pay
Lydia the reward on the ground that Demetrius was never convicted.

Would Lydia be likely to prevail in a breach of contract action against the
magistrate?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (B)

**B is correct.** A performance that is subject to an express condition cannot
become due unless the condition occurs or its non-occurrence is excused. Lydia's
entitlement to the 500-denarii was subject to two express conditions: (i) the
arrest of the thief and (ii) his conviction. Lydia satisfied the first by
delivering Demetrius to the magistrate. The second did not occur — but its
non-occurrence is excused under the **doctrine of prevention** (Restatement
(Second) of Contracts § 245): where a party's breach by non-performance
contributes materially to the non-occurrence of a condition of one of his
duties, the non-occurrence is excused. The magistrate's own act of striking the
plea deal with Demetrius — in exchange for testimony against Apollos — was
the very act that foreclosed the conviction. A party cannot rely on a
condition that its own wrongful conduct prevented. Lydia is entitled to the
reward.

> **Gold Key (GK-CONTRACTS-PREVENTION-01):** A condition to a duty is excused
> when the obligor's breach by non-performance contributes materially to the
> condition's non-occurrence. When a reward is conditioned on the offeror's
> own future conduct (e.g. the conviction of a suspect), and the offeror is
> the only party that can cause that conduct to occur, ask: did the offeror
> do something that made the condition impossible or improbable? If yes, the
> condition is excused and the reward is owed.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A is incorrect.** The choice misstates the unilateral-contract notice rule.
Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 54(2), an offeree who accepts by
rendering the requested performance is required to give the offeror notice
only when the offeree has reason to know that the offeror will not learn of
the performance with reasonable certainty and promptness in any other way.
Here the magistrate had **actual** knowledge of the performance: Lydia walked
Demetrius in. Notice was therefore unnecessary, and the magistrate's actual
knowledge defeats the argument. (legal_leak — the structural flaw is
doctrinal, not C3-visible.)

## 9. Black-letter verification + right-answer legal reasoning

**Governing rule (lawyer confirmation).** Restatement (Second) of Contracts
§ 245 (Excuse of Condition by Prevention or Hindrance):

> "Where a party's breach by non-performance contributes materially to the
> non-occurrence of a condition of one of his duties, the non-occurrence is
> excused."

**Application.** The magistrate's contractual duty to pay the reward was
subject to two express conditions: (i) the arrest of the thief, and (ii) his
conviction. Lydia delivered Demetrius to the magistrate, satisfying (i). The
magistrate's own act — striking a plea deal in exchange for testimony against
Apollos and dropping all charges — was a breach by non-performance of his
duty as the prosecuting authority, and that breach contributed **materially**
to the non-occurrence of (ii). Under § 245 the non-occurrence is excused, and
the magistrate's duty to pay the 500 denarii became absolute.

**Side rule (notice in unilateral contracts, lawyer confirmation).**
Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 54(2): an offeree who accepts by rendering
performance is not required to give notice if the offeror learns of the
performance with reasonable promptness and certainty, or if the offeror has
adequate means of doing so. Here, the magistrate had actual knowledge — Lydia
brought Demetrius to him. The notice answer fails on its face.

**Compared to the original.** The tested rule (doctrine of prevention) and
the credited outcome (the detective/offeree wins) are unchanged from Q#14480.
The same four distractor mechanics (notice misstatement / conditions literal /
public-policy misfit / prevention correct) are preserved. The dominant trap
(22% — literal-conditions answer) is preserved. The recognition test is
satisfied (see § 12): the two one-sentence stem summaries share nothing
beyond the rule itself and the prevention mechanism.

## trigger_facts

1. **Two-part express condition.** The reward offer is conditioned on
   "arrest AND conviction" of the thief. The first part is for the

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A — "No, because Lydia failed to notify the magistrate that she had accepted the reward offer."

**4-part block.**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** legal_leak. The stem does not
   establish that Lydia failed to give notice — it simply doesn't mention
   notice. The choice needs the outside rule that notice is excused when
   the offeror has actual knowledge of the performance.
2. **Student label:** "Sounds plausible; misses that notice is unnecessary
   when the offeror has actual knowledge of the performance."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** a notice-based
   answer would need a stem that establishes the offeror was *not*
   informed of the performance by the offeree's actions (e.g. the offeree
   mailed a sealed envelope, or the performance was hidden). The current
   stem has Lydia walking Demetrius to the magistrate.
4. **Lawyer confirmation.** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 54(2): an
   offeree who accepts by rendering performance is not required to notify
   the offeror if the offeror has adequate means of learning of the
   performance with reasonable promptness and certainty, or if the
   offeror actually learns of it. Here, the magistrate had actual
   knowledge: Lydia brought Demetrius in person. Notice was unnecessary.
   A misstates the rule — `flat_misstatement` (EAR_FALSITY) — and breaks
   the NOT_TRUE filter.

### B — "Yes, because the magistrate himself prevented the conviction of Demetrius." (credited)

**4-part block.**

   prevention fact. The doctrine that turns that fact into "excuse" is

   non-occurrence of a condition to the offeror's own duty.
4. **Lawyer confirmation.** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 245:

   the non-occurrence of a condition of one of his duties, the
   non-occurrence is excused." The magistrate's contractual duty to pay
   was subject to the conviction of Demetrius. The magistrate's act of
   striking a plea deal and dropping the charges was a breach by
   non-performance of his prosecuting duty; it contributed materially to
   the non-occurrence of the conviction. The non-occurrence is excused;
   the reward is owed. Lydia wins.
