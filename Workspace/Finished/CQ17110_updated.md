## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**D is correct.** Esther’s note did not ask Paul for a return promise. It asked Paul to do the requested act: print and deliver Mary’s order on credit. Paul did that before Esther died.

**Gold Key:** When a guaranty offer invites acceptance by performance, the requested extension of credit is the acceptance. Death after that performance does not undo the formed contract.

**Silver Key:** Lock the sequence before reading the answers: note → requested credit extended → death → default. The answer that preserves that order wins.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells the idea that a guaranty must be bilateral because the lender or supplier should promise something back. The breaker is the word **always** and the mismatch with **unilateral**: this offer invited performance, not a return promise.

**B.** This choice sells a real timing instinct: death can cut off an unaccepted offer, and notice sounds like the missing step. **Gold Key:** the requested credit was extended before death, so performance had already accepted the guaranty; notice was not the formation trigger.

**C.** This choice sells a moral picture: Esther is helping Mary, so the promise feels like a gift. The breaker is the word **all**; a guaranty can be supported by the requested extension of credit.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged from the original: an offer of guaranty that invites acceptance by extending credit is accepted when the creditor extends the requested credit before the guarantor’s death. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 50 states that acceptance must be made in the manner invited or required by the offer, and that acceptance by performance requires performance or tender of at least part of what the offer requests. ([Open Casebook][1]) Restatement § 54 states that when an offer invites acceptance by performance, notification is not necessary to make that acceptance effective unless the offer requests notification. ([Open Casebook][2]) Restatement § 36 lists death or incapacity as a method of terminating the power of acceptance, which matters only if death occurs before acceptance. ([Open Casebook][3]) Restatement § 45 also supports the unilateral-contract frame by protecting invited performance once performance begins, with the offeror’s duty conditional on completion of the invited performance. ([Open Casebook][4]) For guaranty doctrine, *Davis v. Wells* recognizes that notice questions arise when notice is needed to complete mutual assent, but the performance of the contemplated consideration can itself supply acceptance in the relevant formation analysis. ([Justia Law][5])
