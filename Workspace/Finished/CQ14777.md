## 1. Final Question

Peter and his friend Daniel agreed to sell a rare manuscript they knew had been stolen from a university library. Daniel later disappeared and cannot be found. At Peter's trial for conspiracy to sell stolen property, the prosecutor calls Daniel's wife, Ruth, to testify that Daniel told her: "Peter said I still owe him half the money from that manuscript sale."

**A.** inadmissible, because Daniel's statement was not in furtherance of the conspiracy.

**B.** inadmissible, because Daniel is not shown to have firsthand knowledge that the manuscript was stolen.

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** A statement against interest under FRE 804(b)(3) does not require the declarant to be a party to the litigation — a non-party declarant's statement is admissible if (i) the declarant is unavailable, (ii) the statement was against pecuniary, proprietary, or penal interest when made, and (iii) a reasonable person in the declarant's position would not have made the statement unless believing it to be true.

This is a double-hearsay problem (FRE 805). Ruth's testimony contains two out-of-court statements: (1) Peter's statement to Daniel ("I still owe him half the money from that manuscript sale"), and (2) Daniel's statement to Ruth (repeating Peter's words). Each layer must independently satisfy a hearsay exception or exemption.

**Layer 1 — Peter's statement to Daniel:** Peter is the defendant. His out-of-court statement offered against him is an admission by a party-opponent under FRE 801(d)(2)(A) — it is exempt from the hearsay rule. This layer is admissible.

**Layer 2 — Daniel's statement to Ruth:** Daniel is not a party to this trial (he fled and is not being tried here). His statement cannot come in as a party-opponent admission. It also cannot come in under the co-conspirator exemption (FRE 801(d)(2)(E)) because Daniel's casual confession to his wife was not made "in furtherance of" the conspiracy — it was idle narrative about a past event, not a statement advancing the conspiracy's objectives.

However, Daniel's statement qualifies as a **statement against interest** under FRE 804(b)(3): (i) Daniel is unavailable (he jumped bail and cannot be found, satisfying FRE 804(a)(5)); (ii) the statement was against his pecuniary and penal interest when made — admitting that he owes Peter half the money from selling a stolen manuscript exposes him to criminal liability and a financial obligation; and (iii) a reasonable person would not make such a statement unless it were true. The statement is admissible under this exception.

Both layers are admissible. The testimony comes in. **C is correct.**

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### D (Original A) — Dominant Trap — Party-Opponent Misapplication

**Why it's attractive:** The case involves a conspiracy, and students have drilled that co-conspirator statements are admissible as party-opponent admissions. The choice offers a clean, familiar path to admissibility. Students who stop at "conspiracy + statement = admissible" without checking the details will grab this answer.

**The breaker:** The party-opponent exemption (FRE 801(d)(2)) applies only when the statement is offered against the party who made it — or, for co-conspirators, when the statement was made *during and in furtherance of* the conspiracy. Daniel is not a party to this trial (only Peter is on trial), and his casual statement to his wife was not in furtherance of the conspiracy. The party-opponent exemption does not apply to Daniel's layer of the hearsay. **Gold Key:** The statement-against-interest exception exists precisely for situations like this — a non-party declarant's damaging statement that would otherwise be excluded.

### A (Original C) — True Premise, Wrong Conclusion

**Why it's attractive:** This choice correctly identifies that Daniel's statement was not in furtherance of the conspiracy. Students who spot the "in furtherance" problem will feel validated and select this answer without considering alternative exceptions.

**The breaker:** While it is true that the co-conspirator exemption does not apply, this choice concludes inadmissibility without considering that Daniel's statement independently qualifies as a statement against interest under FRE 804(b)(3). The co-conspirator exemption is not the only path to admissibility for a non-party declarant's statement. The correct analysis is: co-conspirator exemption fails → statement-against-interest exception applies → testimony is admissible.

### B (Original D) — Fabricated Firsthand-Knowledge Requirement

**Why it's attractive:** Students know that many hearsay exceptions require the declarant to have personal knowledge. This choice sounds plausible — "he didn't know it was stolen, so his statement doesn't count."

**The breaker:** The statement-against-interest exception does not require the declarant to know that the property was stolen. It requires the declarant to have personal knowledge of the facts he is asserting — here, Daniel knows he and Peter sold the manuscript and that Peter claims Daniel owes him money. That is sufficient. The statement is against Daniel's interest precisely because it implicates him in a financial obligation and potential criminal conduct, regardless of whether he knew the manuscript was stolen.

## 9. Black-Letter Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing authorities:**

1. **FRE 801(c)** — Hearsay defined: an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted.

2. **FRE 805** — Hearsay within hearsay: "Hearsay within hearsay is not excluded under the hearsay rule if each part of the combined statements conforms with an exception to the rule." Both layers must independently qualify.

3. **FRE 801(d)(2)(A)** — Party-opponent admission: A statement offered against an opposing party that was made by the party in an individual capacity is not hearsay. Peter's statement to Daniel qualifies — Peter is the defendant and his statement is offered against him.

4. **FRE 801(d)(2)(E)** — Co-conspirator statement: Requires (i) a conspiracy existed, (ii) the declarant and defendant were members, and (iii) the statement was made during and in furtherance of the conspiracy. Daniel's statement to Ruth fails the "in furtherance" requirement — casual narrative to a spouse is not advancing the conspiracy's objectives. *See Bourjaily v. United States*, 483 U.S. 171 (1987).

5. **FRE 804(b)(3)** — Statement against interest: A statement that (A) was against the declarant's pecuniary, proprietary, or penal interest when made, such that a reasonable person would not have made it unless believing it to be true; and (B) the declarant is unavailable. Daniel's statement satisfies all elements: he is unavailable (FRE 804(a)(5) — jumped bail, cannot be found), the statement was against his financial and penal interest, and he had personal knowledge of the facts.

6. **FRE 804(a)(5)** — Unavailability: A declarant is unavailable if the proponent cannot procure the declarant's attendance by process or other reasonable means.

**Confirmed:** The tested rule (double hearsay + statement against interest), legal issue (admissibility of non-party declarant's statement), and credited outcome (admissible) are unchanged from the original.

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

#### Program Frame

This item tests the student's ability to analyze double hearsay (hearsay within hearsay) under FRE 805, specifically whether each layer of a two-statement chain independently satisfies a hearsay exception or exemption. The item layers three doctrines: (1) party-opponent admissions under FRE 801(d)(2)(A), (2) the co-conspirator exemption under FRE 801(d)(2)(E) and its "in furtherance" requirement, and (3) the statement-against-interest exception under FRE 804(b)(3) and its application to non-party declarants. The item is Core difficulty and is a C3_SOLVE — the elimination path is structural once the student knows the narrow Gold Key distinction.

#### Student Access Test

The C3 path is accessible to a student who has completed the C3 lessons on hearsay exemptions and exceptions. The student must: (1) identify the two layers of hearsay, (2) recognize that Peter's statement is a party-opponent admission, (3) recognize that Daniel's statement fails the co-conspirator exemption because it was not in furtherance of the conspiracy, (4) recognize that Daniel's statement qualifies as a statement against interest. Step 4 requires a Gold Key — the student needs to know that the statement-against-interest exception applies to non-party declarants. Without this key, the student cannot confidently eliminate Choice A or select Choice C.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Peter and his friend Daniel agreed to sell a rare manuscript they knew had been stolen from a university library. Daniel later disappeared and cannot be found. At Peter's trial for conspiracy to sell stolen property, the prosecutor calls Daniel's wife, Ruth, to testify that Daniel told her: "Peter said I still owe him half the money from that manuscript sale."

**Call:** Ruth's testimony is

**Choices:**
- A. inadmissible, because Daniel's statement was not in furtherance of the conspiracy.
- B. inadmissible, because Daniel is not shown to have firsthand knowledge that the manuscript was stolen.
- C. admissible, as evidence of a statement against interest by Daniel.
- D. admissible, as evidence of a statement by a party-opponent.

#### Distilled Core Question

Two conspirators agree to sell stolen property. One flees (unavailable). The prosecutor calls the fugitive's spouse to testify that the fugitive said, "Defendant said I still owe him money from that sale." Each layer of this double hearsay must independently satisfy an exception. The fugitive's casual statement to his spouse was not "in furtherance" of the conspiracy. Is the testimony admissible?

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks whether Ruth's testimony is admissible. The student should predict: this is a double-hearsay problem. Both layers must independently qualify. Layer 1 (Peter's statement) = party-opponent admission. Layer 2 (Daniel's statement) = co-conspirator exemption fails (not in furtherance), but statement against interest applies (unavailable, against pecuniary/penal interest). Prediction: admissible, statement against interest. **Answer: C.**

#### Trigger Facts

1. **Peter and Daniel agreed to sell a stolen manuscript** — establishes conspiracy; both are co-conspirators.
2. **Daniel disappeared and cannot be found** — establishes declarant unavailability under FRE 804(a)(5).
3. **Prosecutor calls Daniel's wife Ruth** — establishes the witness and the testimony being offered.
4. **Daniel told Ruth: "Peter said I still owe him half the money from that manuscript sale"** — contains two layers of hearsay: (a) Peter's statement to Daniel, and (b) Daniel's statement to Ruth.
5. **Peter is the defendant** — his statement is offered against him as a party-opponent admission.
6. **Daniel's statement to Ruth was casual narrative** — not made to advance the conspiracy; fails the "in furtherance" requirement.
7. **Daniel's statement admits he owes Peter money from selling stolen property** — against his pecuniary and penal interest.

**Subject:** Evidence — Hearsay
**Lane:** Double Hearsay (FRE 805) → Layer-by-Layer Analysis
**Deciding Phase:** CLASH (between Choice A and Choice C — both address the co-conspirator exemption's failure, but only C considers the alternative exception)
**Method Class:** hard_structural (the elimination path is visible; the Gold Key is a narrow doctrinal unlock, not a broad anchor)

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice D: "Admissible, as evidence of a statement by a party-opponent."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice says "party-opponent." Ask: whose statement is being offered, and is that person a party? Daniel's statement is being offered — but Daniel is not a party to this trial. Only Peter is on trial. The party-opponent exemption applies to statements by the party against whom they are offered. Daniel ≠ party. This choice misidentifies the exemption.
2. **Student label:** Wrong exemption for Daniel's layer.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Admissible, as evidence of a statement by a party-opponent **and** a statement against interest" — but this would still be incomplete because it doesn't address the double-hearsay structure.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 801(d)(2)(A) applies to statements by a party to the litigation. Daniel is not a party. His statement cannot come in under this exemption. **Gold Key:** The statement-against-interest exception (FRE 804(b)(3)) exists precisely for non-party declarants' damaging statements.

**Choice B: "Inadmissible, because Daniel is not shown to have firsthand knowledge that the manuscript was stolen."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice invents a requirement. Does the statement-against-interest exception require the declarant to know the property was stolen? No — it requires personal knowledge of the facts asserted. Daniel knows he and Peter sold the manuscript and that Peter claims Daniel owes him money. That's sufficient.
2. **Student label:** Fabricated requirement.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Inadmissible, because Daniel lacked personal knowledge of the facts he was asserting" — but this would be false because Daniel does have personal knowledge.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 804(b)(3) requires personal knowledge of the facts stated. Daniel personally knows about the sale and the debt. He does not need to know the manuscript was stolen for the statement to be against his interest.

**Choice C: "Admissible, as evidence of a statement against interest by Daniel."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice identifies the correct exception for Daniel's layer. Check the elements: (a) Daniel is unavailable — yes, he disappeared. (b) The statement was against his pecuniary/penal interest — yes, admitting he owes money from selling stolen property exposes him to liability. (c) A reasonable person wouldn't say it unless true — yes. Layer 1 (Peter's statement) is a party-opponent admission. Both layers qualify. Testimony is admissible.
2. **Student label:** Correct — both layers independently satisfy exceptions.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true/responsive version.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 805 — each layer independently satisfies an exception. Layer 1: FRE 801(d)(2)(A). Layer 2: FRE 804(b)(3). Both admissible.

**Choice A: "Inadmissible, because Daniel's statement was not in furtherance of the conspiracy."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice correctly identifies that the co-conspirator exemption fails — but then concludes inadmissibility. The C3 move: when one exception fails, check for alternatives. The co-conspirator exemption is not the only exception available for a non-party declarant's statement.
2. **Student label:** True premise, wrong conclusion.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Although Daniel's statement was not in furtherance of the conspiracy, it is admissible as a statement against interest."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The co-conspirator exemption (FRE 801(d)(2)(E)) does fail because Daniel's casual statement to his wife was not in furtherance. But FRE 804(b)(3) provides an independent basis for admissibility. The choice is not responsive to the full analysis.

#### Residual Answer

After CUTting D (wrong exemption) and B (fabricated requirement), the CLASH is between A and C. Both correctly identify that the co-conspirator exemption fails. The axis: whether an alternative exception applies. The splitting fact: Daniel's statement was against his pecuniary and penal interest, he is unavailable, and a reasonable person would not make it unless true — satisfying FRE 804(b)(3). **CALL: C.**
