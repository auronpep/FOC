## 7. Right-Answer Explanation

Philip launched the javelin with a clear intent to kill Eli. That is the intent-to-kill branch of malice aforethought — the mental state that supports murder. Eli escaped; Ruth did not.

**Gold Key GK-CRIMINAL-TRANSFER-INTENT-01:** When an actor intends to kill Person X but the weapon instead kills Person Y, the intent to kill transfers from X to Y. The full murderous mental state travels with the doctrine — not a reduced recklessness or negligence.

Philip therefore possesses every element of murder with respect to Ruth's death: an unlawful killing of a human being committed with malice aforethought (supplied by transferred intent). He is guilty of murder, not any lesser offense.

**Silver Key SK-CRIMINAL-TRANSFER-INTENT-01:** When A and C both say "yes" but disagree on degree, lock onto the actor's mental state toward the intended victim. Philip meant to kill — that intent transfers as murder-level malice. Pick C, not A.

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A — "Yes, but only of involuntary manslaughter because Ruth's death was accidental."**

The cognitive move this choice sells: Ruth's presence at the point of impact was unintentional — she was not Philip's target — so the death might feel like an "accident" supporting a lesser charge. A student who accepts that logic slides from "yes, some liability" to "but only manslaughter."

The breaker: transferred intent carries the full mental state intact, not a diminished version. Philip's mental state was intent to kill, which is the mental state for murder, not involuntary manslaughter (which requires recklessness or criminal negligence). The accidental identity of the victim does not alter the degree of the offense. **Gold Key GK-CRIMINAL-TRANSFER-INTENT-01** confirms: the full murderous intent travels. **Silver Key SK-CRIMINAL-TRANSFER-INTENT-01** names the deciding axis: what mental state did Philip have? Intent to kill → murder.

**Choice B — "No, because Philip intended to kill Eli, not Ruth."**

The cognitive move: if Philip never specifically directed his lethal purpose at Ruth, there is no intent as to Ruth — and without intent, no murder. This logic is intuitive without knowing the rule.

The breaker: this is a flat misstatement of the transferred intent doctrine. The doctrine exists precisely to answer this situation. When the intended victim escapes and an unintended person is killed instead, the law treats the actor's intent toward the intended victim as equivalent to intent toward the actual victim. Victim identity does not defeat the charge. **Gold Key GK-CRIMINAL-TRANSFER-INTENT-01** kills this trap: the intent to kill Eli is legally sufficient for murder as to Ruth.

**Choice D — "No, because transferred intent applies only to battery, not homicide." (DOMINANT TRAP)**

The cognitive move: a student who studied transferred intent in torts (battery context — Restatement Second of Torts § 16) may import that memory into criminal law and confidently invoke it as a doctrine-specific limit: "that's a battery rule, not a homicide rule." This is the dominant trap because the student has a real piece of doctrine and applies it in the wrong domain.

The breaker: criminal law applies the transferred intent doctrine to all completed result crimes, including murder. No American jurisdiction restricts it to battery. The battery framing comes from the tort context and does not travel. **Gold Key GK-CRIMINAL-TRANSFER-INTENT-01** kills this trap directly: transferred intent applies to completed homicide.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Tested rule:** When an actor intends to kill Person X but the act instead kills Person Y, the intent to kill transfers from X to Y, making the actor guilty of murder for Y's death. The doctrine applies to completed homicide under common law.

**Credited outcome:** Philip is guilty of murder for Ruth's death. This is unchanged from the original.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer):**
- Common law doctrine of transferred intent: universally applied in American jurisdictions to completed homicide. LaFave, Substantive Criminal Law § 14.7 (transferred intent applies to result crimes including murder).
- Model Penal Code § 2.03(2): where a purposeful or knowing actor causes a result involving a different person than intended, the actor is not relieved of liability unless the actual result is "too remote or accidental to have a bearing on the actor's liability." An unintended victim killed by the same act aimed at the intended victim is not too remote — liability stands.
- The "battery only" claim (Choice D) reflects the tort rule in Restatement (Second) of Torts § 16 — a different legal system. Criminal law applies its own doctrine to homicide.
