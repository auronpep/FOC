## 1. Final Question

Daniel applied for a paid trainee slot with a private gospel-recording studio. The studio's selection coordinator, Lydia, had authority to choose the trainees. Eager to be selected, Daniel promised Lydia his first $700 stipend payment if she would choose him. Lydia agreed, selected Daniel, and accepted the $700 after the stipend was paid.

Daniel and Lydia were later charged under a state statute providing that "it shall be a felony for any person with responsibility for selecting paid trainees to solicit, demand, or receive anything of value from a selected trainee in exchange for selecting that trainee." They were tried jointly, Lydia as a principal and Daniel as an accessory.

What is Daniel's best argument for dismissal of the charge?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

Can a selected trainee be charged as an accessory to a selector's offense when the statute punishes selectors for receiving value from selected trainees in exchange for selection?

## 3. Final Answer Choices

A. Daniel did not aid Lydia in committing the offense.

B. The statute was intended to protect people in Daniel's position from people in Lydia's position.

C. A person cannot be tried as an accessory until the principal has first been tried and convicted.

D. No person can be charged as an accessory if the offense could not have been committed without that person's participation.

## 4. Correct Answer

B (original key D).

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is the best argument. The statute is aimed at people who control selection decisions and take value from the people they select. Daniel's strongest dismissal argument is that the law treats selected trainees as the protected class, not as the accomplices of the selector.

Gold Key: When a statute is designed to protect one participant from the other participant's abuse of position, the protected participant is not treated as an accomplice merely because the offense used that participant's payment, consent, or participation.

Silver Key: In a "best argument" dismissal call, do not stop at whether Daniel helped in the ordinary factual sense. Lock onto the statute's direction: who is the law punishing, and whom is it protecting?

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because Daniel did hand over the value and the question labels Lydia as the principal. The breaker is the visible payment fact: Daniel promised and then paid the $700, so a bare "no aid" argument does not match the stem.

C is attractive because it sounds like a procedural protection for an accessory. The breaker is that modern accessory liability does not require the principal to be tried and convicted first; that old sequencing rule is not Daniel's best dismissal argument.

D is the dominant trap because the offense cannot happen unless there is a selected trainee on the other side of the exchange. The breaker is the call: Daniel is charged as an accessory to the substantive offense, not with conspiracy, so the essential-participation idea does not by itself dismiss the accessory charge. Gold Key: the better version is not "essential participation always defeats accessory liability"; it is the narrower protected-class / legislative-purpose argument in B.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. A person may ordinarily be liable as an accomplice if, with the required purpose, the person aids or agrees to aid another in committing the offense. But accomplice liability has a protected-participant limit: a person is not an accomplice if the person is a victim of the offense, or if the person's conduct is inevitably incident to the offense and punishing that person would conflict with the statute's purpose.

Authority layer: Model Penal Code section 2.06(6)(a) excludes the victim of the offense from accomplice liability, and section 2.06(6)(b) excludes conduct inevitably incident to the offense unless the law provides otherwise. Model Penal Code section 2.06(7) also confirms that an accomplice may be convicted even if the principal has not been prosecuted or convicted. Gebardi v. United States, 287 U.S. 112 (1932), supports the same legislative-purpose principle: when the substantive statute shows a policy to leave a participant's conduct unpunished, general party-liability or conspiracy doctrine should not override that policy. Supporting URLs: https://www.unodc.org/cld/en/legislation/mhl/criminal_code_2011/part_i/2.06._liability_for_conduct_of_another_complicity./section_2.06..html and https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/287/112/

Here, the statute punishes persons with selection authority who solicit, demand, or receive value from selected trainees in exchange for selection. Daniel is the selected trainee. His best dismissal argument is that the statute was designed to protect people in his position from people in Lydia's position. That does not guarantee dismissal in every jurisdiction, but among the answer choices it is the only argument that fits the governing limitation on accomplice liability.
