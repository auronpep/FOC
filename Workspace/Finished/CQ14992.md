Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)
The deed creates a fee simple subject to an executory limitation (sometimes called a defeasible fee simple) in Rachel, with a shifting executory interest in Jonathan that cuts short Rachel's fee simple upon Jonathan's marriage. Under the common law Rule Against Perpetuities, the executory interest is valid because it must vest, if at all, within Jonathan's lifetime—a life in being at the creation. Jonathan cannot marry after death, so the interest will vest or fail within his life. Thus Rachel holds a defeasible fee simple and Jonathan holds an executory interest. Silver Key: clash — the axis is whether the condition is limited to a life in being; the fact that Jonathan's marriage must occur, if at all, during his lifetime resolves it.

The common law Rule Against Perpetuities requires that a contingent future interest must vest, if at all, no later than 21 years after the death of a life in being at the creation of the interest. See, e.g., Restatement (First) of Property § 374. Here the executory interest in Jonathan is limited to his marriage. Jonathan is a life in being at the conveyance, and he cannot marry after his death, so the interest will vest or fail within his lifetime. Thus the interest is valid. The grant to Rachel "and her heirs and assigns" creates a fee simple; the limitation "until Jonathan marries" creates a fee simple subject to an executory limitation (sometimes termed a defeasible fee simple), with a shifting executory interest in Jonathan. Therefore Rachel holds a defeasible fee simple (fee simple subject to an executory limitation) and Jonathan holds an executory interest.
