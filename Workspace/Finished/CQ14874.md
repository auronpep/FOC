## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. Dr. Naomi is not merely describing Lydia's symptoms, the words Lydia used, or the limits of hypnotic recall. She would tell the jury that Lydia was in fear for her life when she struck Stephen. Gold Key: in a criminal case, FRE 704(b) blocks an expert from stating an opinion about whether the defendant did or did not have a mental state that is an element of the charged crime or a defense. Self-defense makes Lydia's fear a jury question, so the expert's direct opinion on that fear is inadmissible. Silver Key: once two answers both say "No," do not stop at the result; choose the no answer that names the actual exclusion.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification AND Legal Reasoning For The Right Answer

The tested issue remains Evidence, Relevancy and Excluding Relevant Evidence, Expert Testimony, Opinion on ultimate issue, and the verified outline code is `32020804`. FRE 704(a) generally says an opinion is not objectionable merely because it embraces an ultimate issue, but FRE 704(b) creates a criminal-case exception: an expert witness may not state an opinion about whether the defendant did or did not have a mental state or condition that constitutes an element of the crime charged or of a defense. The Supreme Court's current reading in Diaz v. United States keeps Rule 704(b) narrow, but it confirms the decisive distinction: the rule bars direct opinions about the particular defendant's own mental state while allowing some group-based mental-state testimony that leaves the defendant's own mens rea to the jury.

Here, Lydia's asserted self-defense makes her fear at the time of the killing part of the defense. Dr. Naomi's proposed testimony would directly state that Lydia was in fear for her life when she struck Stephen. That is an opinion about Lydia herself and about a mental state that makes the defense work. The testimony is therefore inadmissible under FRE 704(b). Current expert-testimony doctrine under FRE 702, including the December 2023 preponderance/reliable-application amendment, does not change the result; even a qualified and reliable expert cannot give the direct mental-state opinion barred by Rule 704(b). The hypnosis fact may raise reliability or foundation questions, but it is not the constitutional reason for exclusion on this item. Rock v. Arkansas undercuts any broad idea that hypnotically refreshed testimony is constitutionally forbidden in all criminal cases.
