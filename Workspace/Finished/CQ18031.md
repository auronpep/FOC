### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

Peter may claim self-defense because he effectively withdrew from the encounter before Mark escalated to deadly force. The initial-aggressor rule bars self-defense only until the aggressor completely withdraws and communicates that withdrawal. Peter's raised hands, verbal statement ("I'm done — I don't want any more trouble!"), and physical retreat satisfy the withdrawal requirement. Once Peter withdrew, Mark became the new aggressor by arming himself with a heavy cast-iron object and charging. A cast-iron lawn ornament raised overhead constitutes deadly force. Peter's response — swinging a wooden chair — is proportionate to the threat and legally justified after his effective withdrawal.

**Gold Key:** An initial aggressor regains the right to self-defense by (1) completely withdrawing and (2) effectively communicating that withdrawal. Once both steps are satisfied, the other party's continued aggression makes them the new aggressor.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap):** This choice sells the overbroad heuristic every student memorizes: "initial aggressor = no self-defense, period." It's the most attractive wrong answer because the bar-exam trope "you started it, you can't claim self-defense" is deeply drilled — but it ignores the withdrawal exception entirely. The initial-aggressor rule is a gate, not a life sentence. Once Peter withdrew and communicated it, the bar lifted. **Gold Key:** The withdrawal-and-communication exception defeats the "permanent bar" reading.

**B:** This choice conflates causation with permanent forfeiture. Yes, Peter shoved Mark first — but the law does not say "whoever causes a conflict can never defend themselves." The entire point of the withdrawal doctrine is that an aggressor can reset the legal situation. This answer mistakes a factual observation (Peter started it) for a legal rule that doesn't exist in that absolute form. **Gold Key:** Withdrawal restores defensive rights; causation alone does not permanently forfeit them.

**D:** This choice sounds lawyered but misfires. It confuses two distinct concepts: withdrawal (which restores aggressor eligibility) and retreat (which is a separate duty before using deadly force under minority rules). The majority rule imposes no duty to retreat at all. More importantly, the call asks about self-defense generally — not a retreat-specific jurisdiction. Even under minority retreat rules, no retreat is required when retreat is impossible (Peter was trapped against a fence). **Silver Key:** Name the axis — this is a CLASH between "withdrawal" and "retreat." The fact that resolves it: withdrawal and retreat are different doctrines, and the call doesn't specify a retreat jurisdiction.

### 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Tested rule:** An initial aggressor forfeits the right to claim self-defense unless (1) the aggressor completely withdraws from the fight and communicates that withdrawal, after which the other party becomes the new aggressor, or (2) the victim responds to non-deadly force with deadly force.

**Authority:**
- Model Penal Code § 3.04(2)(b)(i) — use of force not justified if actor was initial aggressor, "unless the actor withdraws from the encounter and effectively communicates to the other person his intent to do so, but the latter nevertheless continues or threatens the use of unlawful physical force."
- NY Penal Law § 35.15(1)(a) — defendant who is the initial aggressor is not justified in using deadly physical force unless "he or she has withdrawn from the encounter and effectively communicated such withdrawal" but the other person persists.
- North Carolina pattern jury instruction (CJI2d Justification Person Deadly Force) — same withdrawal-and-communication exception.
- See generally Cynthia Lee, *Firearms and Initial Aggressors*, 101 N.C. L. Rev. 1 (2022).

**Verification:** The tested rule, credited answer (C), and legal substance are unchanged from the original. Research confirms the MPC and majority-state rule: withdrawal + communication restores self-defense rights. The original key (C) is correct.
