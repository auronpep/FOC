# CQ22039 - Lydia's Cedar Grove Option

## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question - revised stem and call

Lydia owned Covenant Candleworks, a private company that made Scripture-themed candles and devotional journals for Christian bookstores. After a planning team projected the company's land needs for the next 45 years, Lydia's company began looking for a quiet tract near one of its workshops.

Mary owned a 72-acre cedar grove nearby. Lydia's company offered to purchase the grove, but Mary refused, saying that she wanted to keep caring for the land until she stepped away from the business. Instead, Mary and Lydia's company signed a written option agreement. For an immediate cash payment, Mary granted the company the right to purchase the cedar grove at a fixed price 34 years from the date the agreement was executed.

Lydia's company did not record the option. The next spring, Mary suffered a serious illness and sold the cedar grove to Stephen. When Mary sold the grove, she told Stephen about Lydia's company's option.

Is Lydia's company's interest under the option agreement valid?

### 6. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**D is correct.** A standalone option to purchase land is an interest that must satisfy the Rule Against Perpetuities unless the option is attached to a lease or another property interest held by the optionee. Lydia's company has only a paid option. It does not lease the cedar grove, possess the land, or hold another real-property interest in it. The option cannot be exercised until 34 years after creation, so it is not certain to vest or fail within lives in being plus 21 years. Under common-law RAP, the option is void from the start.

**Gold Key:** A purchase option in gross is not saved just because it is a contract; if it can first be exercised beyond the perpetuities period and is not tied to the optionee's lease or other land interest, it is void. **Silver Key:** lock the call as **validity**, not priority. Recording, actual notice, and consideration may matter in other option disputes, but they do not answer whether the interest violates RAP.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

- **A (dominant trap).** This choice is attractive because the stem says Stephen was told about the option, so the recording-act instinct says he should not be able to ignore it. The breaker is the **Silver Key:** the call asks whether the option interest is valid, not whether an otherwise valid option binds a purchaser with notice. **Gold Key:** actual notice cannot validate an interest that common-law RAP voided at creation.

- **B.** This choice feels lawyerly because unrecorded real-estate interests often create priority problems against later purchasers. The breaker is the same call lock: failure to record does not by itself make the option invalid, and Stephen had actual notice anyway. The real invalidating rule is RAP, not recording.

- **C.** This choice grabs a true contract fact: Lydia's company paid money for the option, so the option was supported by consideration. The breaker is that consideration proves only a non-dispositive element. **Gold Key:** a paid option can still be void if the property interest may vest outside the common-law RAP period.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item.** The item tests whether a standalone option to purchase land at a fixed price, exercisable only after a period beyond lives in being plus 21 years, is valid under common-law RAP. The credited outcome remains **No**: the option is void under RAP.

**Authority floor.** The common-law formulation is that no property interest is valid unless it must vest, if at all, not later than 21 years after one or more lives in being at creation. Restatement (First) of Property section 374 states the permissible perpetuities period; Restatement (First) of Property section 395(a) recognizes the lease-option exception for options limited in favor of a lessee and exercisable no later than the lease term. The New York Court of Appeals in *Symphony Space, Inc. v. Pergola Properties, Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 466 (1996), confirms that options to purchase land are subject to the rule against remote vesting and that an option that may be exercised beyond the permitted period is unenforceable. Source links consulted: https://law.justia.com/cases/new-york/court-of-appeals/1996/88-n-y-2d-466-0.html and https://www.law.cornell.edu/nyctap/comments/i96_0141.htm. The Restatement lease-option exception was cross-checked through https://www.gfrlaw.com/what-we-do/insights/%E2%80%9Ccharming-head%E2%80%9D-rule-against-perpetuities.

**Application.** Lydia's company received a standalone option, not an option appendant to a lease. The option is exercisable at a fixed time 34 years after execution. Because that time is beyond 21 years and is not tied to a validating life in being, the option is not certain to vest or fail within the common-law perpetuities period. Recording, actual notice, and consideration do not cure that defect.

**Doctrinal-currency note.** RAP is not on the hotlist in the QA gate. Some modern jurisdictions use wait-and-see, USRAP, or commercial-transaction exceptions, but the MBE default in this item is common-law RAP. Those reforms are contrast points, not a reason to change the official key.
