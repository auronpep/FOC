## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

B is the best answer. Naomi is not contractually bound to pay the reward. **Gold Key:** For a unilateral reward offer, the person who performs the requested act must have known of the specific offer at the time of performance. An act done in ignorance of the offer does not manifest acceptance of that offer and creates no contract. **Silver Key:** When the stem explicitly says the performer learned of the reward only *after* completing the act, immediately cut every choice that treats the bare act of return as sufficient to bind the offeror.

Ruth returned the watch before she knew of Naomi's posted reward. Her performance therefore could not constitute acceptance of the unilateral offer. No contract was formed, and Naomi has no contractual duty to pay.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Yes, because Naomi received the benefit she requested in the notice.**  
This is the dominant trap. It sells the fairness intuition that the owner got exactly what the notice asked for (the watch returned), so payment must be due. The breaker is that benefit to the offeror is irrelevant without acceptance. The stem's "only after" timing shows Ruth's act was not induced by or in acceptance of the offer. **Gold Key:** knowledge of the offer at the time of the act is required; receipt of the requested item alone does not create a contract.

**C. Yes, because returning lost property is always consideration for any existing reward.**  
This sells the overbroad "the act itself is always enough" story. It feels morally right that returning lost things should trigger posted rewards. The breaker is that it invents a special rule for lost property that erases the knowledge requirement. Returning property can be consideration, but only when done as acceptance of a known offer. The "only after" fact defeats it.

**D. Yes, because Ruth would have relied on the reward if she had known of it.**  
This sells a promissory-estoppel or reliance fairness story. It invites the student to rewrite the facts ("she would have known and relied"). The breaker is that hypothetical reliance is not acceptance or consideration. The actual facts control: the act occurred without knowledge, so no contract formed regardless of what might have happened.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested black-letter rule (unchanged from source):** In a unilateral contract formed by a public reward offer, acceptance occurs only when the offeree performs the requested act with knowledge of the offer. Performance in ignorance of the offer does not constitute acceptance, even if the performance matches exactly what the offer requested. The offeror is not contractually bound to pay a reward to someone who did not know of the reward when the act was done.

**Authority grounding (lawyer-confirmation layer only):** This is black-letter common law. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 23 provides that an offer can be accepted only by a person to whom it is addressed and only in the manner invited; performance that is not induced by the offer is not acceptance. Classic reward cases and hornbook law uniformly require knowledge of the reward at the time of the act (see, e.g., the rule illustrated in early reward cases collected in Washington University Law Review discussions of "Acceptance—Necessity for Knowledge of Offer of Reward"). The source row's official explanation states the identical rule: "Acceptance requires knowledge of the offer. Hannah completed the act before learning of the reward, so her performance did not manifest acceptance of Samuel's offer."

The credited outcome is unchanged: no contractual obligation. The legal issue (knowledge requirement for acceptance of a unilateral reward offer) is identical. Research confirms no doctrinal drift or superseded framing on this formation point.
