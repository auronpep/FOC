## Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)
B is correct. Under Fed. R. Crim. P. 8(a), an indictment may charge two or more offenses in separate counts if the offenses are (1) of the same or similar character, (2) based on the same act or transaction, or (3) connected with or constitute parts of a common scheme or plan. Counts 1 and 2 arise from the identical April 10 armed bank robbery (same act or transaction). Count 3, although occurring on a later date, is a bank robbery committed with the identical modus operandi and the same weapon; that makes it "of the same or similar character" as the first robbery. Because at least one of the three independent statutory grounds is satisfied for every count, joinder in a single indictment is proper. Whether the court should nevertheless sever the counts for prejudice under Rule 14 is a separate question that the problem does not ask.

Silver Key: When the call asks whether joinder is "proper," check whether any one of Rule 8(a)'s three disjunctive grounds is met on the facts; do not demand that every ground be satisfied or that the offenses all arise from one single transaction.

## Full wrong-answer explanations
**A (dominant trap, original B mechanic):** Attractive because the two robberies happened on different calendar dates, so it feels as though they must be part of one continuous "transaction" or else the government must prove some extra "identity of evidence" linking them before joinder is allowed; students often import evidentiary concepts (like 404(b) or identity exceptions) into the joinder rule. Breaker: Rule 8(a) lists three independent, disjunctive bases. "Same or similar character" is a standalone ground that does not require the offenses to be part of a single transaction and does not contain any "identity-of-evidence test." The April 25 robbery shares the exact same weapon and modus operandi with the April 10 robbery; that is enough for the similar-character prong.

**C (original A mechanic):** Attractive because joinder combines charges for one trial and therefore feels like something that should require the defendant's consent or agreement, especially when the defendant is arguing prejudice. Breaker: Rule 8(a) authorizes joinder whenever one of the three listed connections exists; the rule text contains no consent requirement. Consent is irrelevant to the propriety of joinder under 8(a). (Rule 14 severance for prejudice is also decided by the court, not by defendant consent.)

**D (original D mechanic):** Attractive because only one defendant is charged, so it feels as though the government should be able to throw every accusation against that person into a single trial without further restriction. Breaker: Rule 8(a) requires a substantive connection among the offenses themselves (same/similar character, same transaction, or common scheme or plan). The identity of the defendant is a necessary background fact but is never sufficient by itself. Unrelated crimes against the same person would not satisfy the rule.

## Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The credited answer is correct and the tested black-letter rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are identical to the source question. Fed. R. Crim. P. 8(a) states: "The indictment or information may charge a defendant in separate counts with 2 or more offenses if the offenses charged—whether felonies or misdemeanors or both—are of the same or similar character, or are based on the same act or transaction, or are connected with or constitute parts of a common scheme or plan." (Current text confirmed via LII/Cornell and Justia; the 2002 amendment was purely stylistic restyling with no substantive change to the three disjunctive grounds.)

Counts 1 and 2 are indisputably "based on the same act or transaction" (the April 10 armed bank robbery plus its accompanying § 924(c)-style firearm count). Count 3 is a separate bank robbery on April 25 that used "the same modus operandi and the same weapon." That factual match satisfies the independent "same or similar character" ground. Joinder is therefore proper under the plain language of the rule. Whether the trial judge should grant a Rule 14 severance for unfair prejudice is a distinct inquiry the problem does not pose. No research contradicts the supplied official key; the outcome and reasoning are unchanged. Authority floor met by direct citation to the governing rule text (FRCP 8(a)).
