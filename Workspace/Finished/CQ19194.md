### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**D** is the best answer. **Gold Key:** A federal marriage-recognition law that denies federal benefits to same-sex spouses who are lawfully married under state law fails when its purpose and effect are to impose a separate, lesser status on those marriages. **Silver Key:** Do not let “Congress controls benefits” or “no one has a right to a benefit” answer the wrong question. The call is Equal Protection: why did the government draw this line?

The statute denies federal recognition to a marriage the state has made lawful. The answer does not need to promote sexual orientation to suspect-class status. It also does not ask whether federal benefits exist as a free-standing right. It asks whether this classification may be justified. Under the controlling Windsor/Romer line, a bare desire to demean or injure an identifiable group is not a legitimate governmental interest.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** The attraction is that strict scrutiny feels like the strongest pro-challenger answer. **Gold Key:** Windsor did not formally make sexual orientation a suspect classification; the case invalidated the federal marriage definition without that strict-scrutiny move.

**B.** The attraction is that rational basis usually lets a legislature win, and “uniformity” plus “tradition” sound like ordinary rational-basis interests. **Gold Key:** Windsor treats this federal marriage-recognition exclusion as dignity/animus-driven, so those labels do not rescue the statute.

**C.** The attraction is that Congress really does administer federal benefits, and the Constitution usually does not create an entitlement to a particular benefit. **Silver Key:** The call is not “is there a right to the benefit?”; it is “may Congress deny the benefit by drawing this classification?”

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

The governing authority confirms the official key. In **United States v. Windsor**, the Supreme Court considered a DOMA definition that excluded same-sex spouses from federal recognition and benefits; the case itself involved a surviving spouse denied a federal estate-tax exemption. The Court held that DOMA imposed disadvantage, separate status, and stigma on state-law same-sex marriages and violated Fifth Amendment due process/equal-protection principles. ([Justia Law][1]) The Constitution Annotated summary likewise explains that Windsor struck DOMA § 3, which defined “spouse” and “marriage” for federal law, and concluded that no legitimate purpose overcame the purpose and effect of disparaging and injuring state-law same-sex spouses. ([Legal Information Institute][2]) Romer supplies the loaded “bare desire to harm” principle: animus toward an identifiable group is not a legitimate governmental interest even under rational-basis review. ([Legal Information Institute][2])

The variation preserves the tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome. It changes the benefit from estate-tax exemption to federal employee spousal health coverage, but the statute still operates across federal programs and benefits, and the denial still rests on federal refusal to recognize a state-law same-sex marriage.
