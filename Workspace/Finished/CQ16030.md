### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. Martha does not need to be the person who swung the flashlight if the battery was a reasonably foreseeable act committed by her co-conspirator in furtherance of their illegal operation. Gold Key: under Pinkerton-style co-conspirator liability, a conspirator can be liable for a substantive crime committed by another conspirator when the crime is within the scope of the conspiracy, in furtherance of it, and reasonably foreseeable. Silver Key: in a 2-guilty/2-not-guilty array, do not stop at who moved the weapon; ask whether the act protected the shared criminal venture. Lydia struck the officer while protecting the counterfeit-wristband operation, and the cousins kept the flashlight there for that protective purpose. Martha is guilty of battery.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because it reaches the guilty result and turns the stored flashlight into a blameworthy fact. The breaker is that the court does not need a reckless-indifference theory here; the liability comes from the conspiracy and Lydia's foreseeable act in furtherance of it.

B is attractive because "inherently dangerous crime" sounds like a familiar criminal-law sorting test. The breaker is that this call is not asking for a felony-murder or dangerous-felony classification; the relevant question is whether the battery was foreseeable and in furtherance of the conspiracy.

D is attractive because it uses the cleanest fact in Martha's favor: she did not personally strike the officer. Gold Key: personal commission is not required when a co-conspirator commits a foreseeable substantive crime in furtherance of the conspiracy, so the non-shooter/non-striker fact does not end the analysis.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law and Procedure, Other Crimes, Battery, and the verified outline code is `73020300`. Battery is an intentional harmful or offensive contact with another person; striking the officer with a heavy flashlight is a battery. The vicarious-liability rule is the Pinkerton rule: a conspirator may be liable for substantive crimes committed by a co-conspirator when the offense is committed in furtherance of the conspiracy, is within the scope of the unlawful project, and is reasonably foreseeable as a natural consequence of the unlawful agreement. The governing authority is *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640 (1946), especially the Court's limits for offenses not in furtherance, outside the project, or not reasonably foreseeable. Research sources consulted: Cornell/LII, https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/328/640, and the DOJ Justice Manual discussion of Pinkerton liability, https://www.justice.gov/archives/jm/criminal-resource-manual-2482-pinkerton-vs-aiding-and-abetting. The credited outcome is unchanged: the co-conspirator used a weapon kept to protect the illegal sales operation, so the battery was foreseeable and in furtherance of the conspiracy.
