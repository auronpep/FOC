## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

The correct answer is **C**.

The objection should be overruled because the prosecution does not have to prove, at the admissibility gate, that the tin is certainly the same tin Timothy tossed. The prosecution must produce enough evidence to support a finding that the item is what it claims. Here, officers saw Timothy toss a blue mint tin, caught him, returned within a few minutes, and found a blue mint tin in the same place.

**Gold Key:** Authentication under FRE 901(a) asks for evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims; the judge does not make an independent preponderance finding that the object is authentic.

**Silver Key:** When one answer says "sufficient evidence to support a finding" and another answer demands a judge's preponderance finding, choose the lighter authentication gate unless the rule expressly makes the judge decide the fact.

Choice C is the only answer that uses the correct authentication threshold and matches the same-place, short-time recovery facts in the stem.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The credited answer is correct under the governing federal evidence rule. Federal Rule of Evidence 901(a) provides that authentication is satisfied when the proponent produces evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is. Federal Rule of Evidence 104(b) uses the same conditional-relevance gate: when relevance depends on whether a fact exists, proof must be introduced sufficient to support a finding that the fact exists.

Research confirms the official key. The current FRE 901(a) text uses the "sufficient to support a finding" standard for authentication, and FRE 104(b) uses the same screening threshold for conditional facts. In Huddleston v. United States, 485 U.S. 681 (1988), the Supreme Court explained that under Rule 104(b) the trial court does not weigh credibility or make its own preponderance finding of the conditional fact; it asks whether the jury could reasonably find the conditional fact.
