### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. Gold Key: larceny normally requires a trespassory taking plus intent to permanently deprive, but when the original taking is wrongful, the trespass continues while the taker keeps wrongful possession. If the intent to keep the property forms during that continuing trespass, the law connects the later intent back to the wrongful taking. Ruth did not borrow the van with permission; she wrongfully took the keys and drove away. When she later decided to keep and sell the van while still in possession, the continuing-trespass doctrine supplied the concurrence needed for larceny.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A pulls because the first plan was only a temporary joyride. The breaker is that "never" ignores the continuing-trespass rule: an initially wrongful joyride can become larceny if the permanent-deprivation intent forms while the wrongful possession continues.

B pulls because the sale to the parts garage looks like a separate property crime. The breaker is that the theft from the neighbor is larceny, not false pretenses from the owner, because Ruth did not obtain title from the neighbor by deception.

D pulls because basic larceny timing requires intent and taking to coincide. Gold Key: the continuing-trespass exception handles the timing problem when the original taking was trespassory and the later intent arises during the ongoing wrongful possession.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged. Common-law larceny requires a trespassory taking and carrying away of another's personal property with intent to permanently deprive. The continuing-trespass doctrine supplies concurrence when the original taking was wrongful but the intent to steal forms later while the wrongful possession continues. Regina v. Riley, 169 Eng. Rep. 674 (1853), is the classic common-law continuing-trespass authority; the reported principle is that a wrongful taking can become larceny when the intent to steal forms later during continuing possession. Modern bar and criminal-law summaries state the same rule: if a defendant takes property without permission intending only temporary use, then later decides to keep or sell it before returning it, larceny can attach through continuing trespass. Those authorities confirm the source key: Ruth's initial unauthorized taking of the van and later sale while still in wrongful possession make her guilty of larceny of the van.

## review_truth

When an initial taking is trespassory, a later intent to permanently deprive can satisfy larceny through continuing trespass while the wrongful possession continues.
