# CQ21730

## 1. Final Question

At a ticketed Christian music festival, Lydia boarded a paid aerial gondola that carried guests from a remote parking area to the main entrance. Before boarding, Lydia showed the operator a medical alert card explaining that she had a panic disorder and needed calm handling if she became anxious. Once the gondola car was moving over a ravine, the operator used the cabin intercom to imitate Lydia's breathing, told the waiting line that he was going to make her "melt down before the Hallelujah chorus," and deliberately passed three staffed unloading platforms despite Lydia's requests to exit. Lydia later required treatment for severe emotional distress.

Lydia sues the operator for intentional infliction of emotional distress. Is Lydia likely to prevail?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

Can targeted public humiliation by a passenger-control operator, done with knowledge of the plaintiff's panic vulnerability and followed by severe distress, support an IIED claim?

## 3. Final Answer Choices

A. No, because spoken humiliation can never be extreme and outrageous.

B. Yes, because the facts show abuse of a passenger-control position, targeted humiliation, intent or recklessness, and severe distress.

C. Yes, but only because Lydia was not allowed off the gondola at the unloading platforms.

D. No, because intentional infliction of emotional distress always requires physical impact.

## 5. Correct Answer

B (original key A).

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. The claim is not built on one ordinary insult. The operator controlled Lydia's transportation, knew from her medical alert card that panic was a specific vulnerability, used the intercom to humiliate her publicly, ignored repeated requests to leave at staffed platforms, and said he wanted to make her melt down. Gold Key: words can cross the IIED line when special facts turn them into targeted abuse of authority or known vulnerability. Silver Key: do not ask whether the words alone are rude; ask what the operator did with his control over the passenger and what he knew about the plaintiff. Those facts support extreme and outrageous conduct, intent or recklessness, causation, and severe distress.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the ordinary-insult limit. That limit matters because IIED is not a claim for every rude comment. The breaker is the word "never." The stem adds passenger-control power, repeated targeted humiliation, known panic vulnerability, deliberate refusals to unload, and treatment-level severe distress. Gold Key: the "mere words" limit gives way when the words are paired with abuse of authority or known susceptibility.

C sells the confinement shortcut. Being kept on the gondola is powerful context, but the IIED claim does not depend only on physical inability to exit. The breaker is "only." The claim rests on the whole pattern: targeted humiliation, known vulnerability, passenger-control power, intent or recklessness, and severe distress. Silver Key: keep the call on IIED, not on a separate confinement-style theory.

D sells a physical-impact gate. Physical impact can strengthen some tort claims, but IIED does not always require impact. The breaker is the fabricated requirement. Severe emotional distress can be enough when caused by extreme and outrageous conduct done intentionally or recklessly.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. Intentional infliction of emotional distress requires extreme and outrageous conduct, intent or recklessness as to causing severe emotional distress, causation, and severe distress. The ordinary-insult limit is real, but special facts can make verbal abuse outrageous, including abuse of a position of authority or control and exploitation of a known susceptibility.

Restatement (Second) of Torts section 46 supplies the core rule for liability for extreme and outrageous conduct that intentionally or recklessly causes severe emotional distress. Restatement section 46 comments d, e, and f are the governing confirmation layer for the key distinctions: ordinary insults usually do not suffice, abuse of a relationship or position may make conduct outrageous, and knowledge of a plaintiff's special vulnerability can matter. Public instruction sources also track the same elements and Restatement framing: Colorado's civil jury instructions require extreme and outrageous conduct, intent or recklessness, and severe emotional distress, and Washington's pattern instruction notes the Restatement comment e abuse-of-position principle.

Under that rule, Lydia is likely to prevail. This is not only embarrassing speech. The operator used passenger-control power, knew of a panic disorder, deliberately targeted that vulnerability over the intercom, bypassed safe unloading points, and caused severe distress requiring treatment. Those facts satisfy the IIED elements without adding a physical-impact requirement. Sources checked: https://www.coloradojudicial.gov/media/8682 and https://govt.westlaw.com/wciji/Document/I2c83f1c0e10d11dab058a118868d70a9.

Fills: outrageousness threshold, mere-insults limit, abuse of authority, known susceptibility, severe distress, physical-impact myth.

Adjacent to master: 64010103 False imprisonment; 64010101 Assault; 61020203 Negligent infliction of emotional distress.

## crossover_intersection_map

False imprisonment is adjacent because the skipped platforms add control pressure, but the tested claim is IIED.

Negligent infliction of emotional distress is adjacent because emotional harm is present, but the operator's purposeful/reckless targeting keeps the lane intentional.

Common-carrier or passenger-control relationships matter only as outrage context, not as a separate regulatory claim.

## review_truth

Targeted humiliation can become outrageous when a passenger-control actor exploits known vulnerability and causes severe distress intentionally or recklessly.
