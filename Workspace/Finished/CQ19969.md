## 1. Final Question

At a Friday-night Bible-study game night in Esther's apartment, Naomi drank two cups of "Jericho berry soda" from a punch bowl. She reasonably believed the drink was nonalcoholic, and nothing about its smell or taste suggested otherwise. Unknown to Naomi, Timothy, trying to make the party louder, had poured a large amount of high-proof liquor into the bowl.

Within minutes, Naomi became severely intoxicated and delusional. Naomi is charged with assault. She had no reason to know the punch contained alcohol. The jurisdiction applies the M'Naghten test for insanity.

Which of the following best describes Naomi's defense?

**D.** Naomi has no defense because she chose to attend the gathering and chose to drink from the punch bowl.

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** Involuntary intoxication is treated differently from voluntary intoxication. When the defendant unknowingly ingests an intoxicant, the defense may be complete if the intoxication either negates the required mental state or produces a condition that satisfies the jurisdiction's insanity test. Naomi did not know the drink contained alcohol, had no reason to suspect it, and the intoxication caused a delusion in which she believed Barnabas was not a person but a bronze statue. In a M'Naghten jurisdiction, that matters because M'Naghten asks whether, because of the mental condition, the defendant did not know the nature and quality of the act or did not know the act was wrong.

**Gold Key:** Involuntary intoxication can be a complete defense when it creates insanity-equivalent incapacity. The key is not the size of the intoxication alone; it is the combination of unknowing ingestion and M'Naghten-level loss of awareness about the act.

**Silver Key:** Sort the source of intoxication before sorting the size of the excuse. Unknown or coerced ingestion stays in the complete-defense lane; ordinary voluntary intoxication usually does not.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are preserved. The item tests the criminal-law defense of involuntary intoxication when the intoxication creates a state equivalent to legal insanity. Under the M'Naghten formulation, a defendant is excused by insanity when, because of the relevant mental condition, the defendant did not know the nature and quality of the act or did not know the act was wrong. M'Naghten's Case, 8 Eng. Rep. 718, 10 Cl. & Fin. 200 (H.L. 1843), is the leading source for that rule. See https://law.justia.com/cases/foreign/united-kingdom/8-eng-rep-718.html and the University of Minnesota Law Library summary at https://lawlibrarycollections.umn.edu/classic-cases-criminal-mnaghten-rule.

For intoxication, the relevant black-letter point is that involuntary intoxication is not treated like ordinary voluntary intoxication. Model Penal Code section 2.08(4) recognizes non-self-induced intoxication as a defense when it leaves the actor lacking substantial capacity to appreciate criminality or conform conduct to law. Cornell Wex likewise summarizes involuntary intoxication as a possible complete defense where an intoxicant was unknowingly ingested and rendered the person incapable of understanding the nature of the act. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/involuntary_intoxication. The source facts satisfy that rule: Naomi unknowingly drank the spiked beverage, had no reason to suspect alcohol, became delusional, and did not understand she was striking a person. The credited answer remains a complete-defense answer, not a partial-defense answer.
