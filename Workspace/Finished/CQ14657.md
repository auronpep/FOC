### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Paul intentionally fired multiple rifle rounds through the front window of an occupied commercial setting while police were outside. That conduct creates a grave, obvious risk to human life. **Gold Key:** Reckless-indifference murder can be common-law murder even without a purpose to kill the actual victim. When the statute ranks ordinary common-law murder above felony murder, do not stop at felony murder if the facts also show malice.

**Silver Key:** The call asks for the **most serious** homicide degree. A true lower degree is not enough. Climb the ladder and pick the highest surviving degree.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A is wrong.** First-degree murder requires knowingly killing another person after deliberation. The facts show deliberate firing, but not a knowing killing of Stephen after deliberation. The trap is converting “deliberately fired” into “deliberately killed.”

**C is wrong.** Third-degree felony murder is available because the death occurred during the robbery. But the call asks for the most serious degree. **Silver Key:** do not stop at a true lower degree when a higher surviving degree fits.

**D is wrong.** Manslaughter undercounts the facts. The absence of a planned killing does not end the murder analysis. **Gold Key:** firing rifle rounds through a storefront to hold off police can supply implied malice / reckless-indifference murder.

### 8. Black-letter verification and lawyer-confirmation reasoning

The tested rule and result are unchanged from Q#14657: a killing caused by extreme reckless indifference to human life can satisfy malice for common-law murder; under the supplied statute, that makes the homicide second-degree murder unless it is felony murder, which the statute separately ranks as third-degree. Cornell Wex states that common-law murder requires malice aforethought and that implied malice includes both felony murder and depraved-heart murder. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) In *United States v. Fleming*, the Fourth Circuit treated reckless and wanton conduct grossly deviating from reasonable care as sufficient malice for second-degree murder. ([Justia Law][2]) The supplied statute, not outside law, controls the degree ranking.

**GK-CRIMINAL-DEPRAVED-HEART-MOST-SERIOUS-01**
A death caused by extreme reckless indifference to human life can be common-law murder even without a purpose or knowledge to kill the actual victim. If the statute ranks common-law murder above felony murder, do not stop at felony murder.

### Silver Key(s)

**SK-CRIMINAL-HOMICIDE-LADDER-01**
When the call asks for the most serious degree, a true lower degree is still wrong. Climb the statutory ladder and pick the highest surviving degree.

**SK-CRIMINAL-ACT-VS-KILL-02**
Separate deliberate dangerous conduct from a knowing killing of this victim. Deliberating before firing does not automatically satisfy a statute requiring knowingly killing another person.
