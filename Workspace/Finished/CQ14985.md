## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

C is correct. Each tenant in common has the right to possess the whole, but possession does not enlarge one co-tenant's power over the other's ownership. A boundary-line agreement permanently fixes — and can shift — title to the land itself, so it reaches the *ownership* interest, not merely possession. Barnabas could agree to a line for his own undivided share, but he had no authority to bind Peter's undivided interest. Peter never signed and never ratified, so his interest is unaffected and he may quiet title along the true boundary. **Gold Key:** one tenant in common cannot bind a co-tenant's undivided title interest by a boundary agreement; only an agreement every co-tenant joins binds them all. **Silver Key:** when two choices share the credited outcome ("win"), the decoy is the one resting on an irrelevant fact — cut it and keep the one that states the actual rule.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

- **A (lose, mutually beneficial).** It feels right that a deal good for everyone should stick — fairness should reward a sensible compromise. But "mutually beneficial" is a common-sense appeal, not the governing test; a benefit to the co-tenancy does not give one co-tenant authority to bind the other's title. Even a perfectly fair agreement binds only the interest of the co-tenant who signed it.
- **B (lose, sole possession). DOMINANT TRAP.** Barnabas really was the only one living on and working the land when he signed, so it is tempting to read sole possession as the authority to speak for the property. Possession and ownership are different things: a co-tenant in sole possession still cannot alter or bind the absent co-tenant's *ownership* interest. **Gold Key:** only an agreement every co-tenant signs binds them all — sole occupancy confers no such power.
- **D (win, credit union not a party).** This one lands on the right result — Peter does win — and the missing lender feels like it ought to matter. But the mortgagee's absence is not why Peter prevails; the agreement would fail against Peter even if the credit union had no interest at all. The choice proves a non-dispositive point and answers a different question than the call asks. **Silver Key:** of the two "win" choices, this is the irrelevant-fact decoy — the rule choice (C) is the responsive one.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested rule (unchanged from the original):** A tenant in common cannot, acting alone, bind a co-tenant's undivided interest in the common property; an agreement that fixes or alters the boundary — and thus affects title — binds only the co-tenants who join it. The absent, non-signing, non-ratifying co-tenant may quiet title on the true line.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only).** The bedrock cotenancy maxim is that "a cotenant has no authority to bind another cotenant with respect to the latter's interest in common property" (general common-law rule of concurrent ownership; see also the agreed-boundary doctrine, which requires the consent of the owners whose title is affected). A lease is sometimes distinguished because it conveys only the lessor co-tenant's own possessory right and does not divest the other's interest; a boundary agreement is different in kind because it reaches title, so the no-binding rule controls. No single primary citation is pinned here with certainty for the boundary-agreement-plus-cotenancy fact pattern specifically; the rule is stated plainly and flagged for attorney review. **Verdict: agrees with the supplied official key (B → new C).** Credited outcome, tested rule, and legal issue are UNCHANGED from the original.
