### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct. Gold Key:** vehicle probable cause can reach containers found in the vehicle, but it does not automatically reach a former passenger’s tote after she has left the vehicle. **Silver Key:** lock the object searched first. The challenged evidence is the heroin in Ruth’s tote, not the pills, marijuana, or cocaine found in Daniel or the minivan.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** The warrant language is attractive because it sounds more restrained than an immediate warrantless search. The breaker is the missing first step: the officers still needed a lawful basis to take Ruth’s tote before applying for a warrant.

**B.** The city-shuttle fact is attractive because it makes Ruth look mobile. The breaker is that ordinary departure is not the same thing as an emergency that supplies a search basis.

**D.** This is the dominant trap. It uses the real vehicle-search rule, but **Silver Key:** the object searched was Ruth’s tote after she had left the minivan. A vehicle-probable-cause rationale would matter if the tote were still a container found in the minivan; it does not answer this call.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule and credited outcome are consistent with the controlling authority. *Wyoming v. Houghton* holds that police with probable cause to search a car may inspect passengers’ belongings **found in the car** if they can conceal the object of the search. ([Justia Law][1]) That is why the vehicle-search denial answer is attractive. But this variation, like the source row, removes the woman’s bag from the vehicle-search frame before the police search it.

The separate limit comes from particularized-probable-cause doctrine. *Ybarra v. Illinois* states that a person’s proximity to others suspected of crime does not, without more, create probable cause to search that person, and the warrant/search power over one place or person does not erase individualized protection for others present. ([Justia Law][2]) *United States v. Di Re* likewise states that a person does not lose immunity from a personal search merely by being present in a suspected automobile. ([Justia Law][3])

Legal conclusion: the officers had a basis to search the minivan and containers in it, but not Ruth’s carried tote after she left the minivan. The transform preserves the legal issue, trap structure, and credited result.

## Gold Key(s)

**GK-CRIMINAL-MANGER-TOTE-01 — distinction.** Vehicle probable cause can reach containers found in the vehicle, but it does not automatically reach a former passenger’s tote after she has left the vehicle. A search of the person’s carried effects needs a separate basis tied to that person or item.

Authority: *Wyoming v. Houghton*; *Ybarra v. Illinois*; *United States v. Di Re*. Houghton states the car-container rule; Ybarra and Di Re state the individual-search limit. ([Justia Law][1])
