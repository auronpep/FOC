## 1. Final question

In the state of Grace Harbor, two small family-run Christian workshops are the only manufacturers in the United States of pure beeswax sanctuary candles and frankincense-scented prayer votives. The candles are made entirely from local beeswax harvested from hives tended by church members and botanicals grown in the state's coastal fields. These candles are sold to churches, Christian retreat centers, and religious supply retailers located in every state for use in worship services, weddings, and home prayer altars. Grace Harbor's legislature is considering a statute imposing a tax solely on the manufacture of these sanctuary candles, calculated at 3% of their wholesale value. Which of the following arguments would be LEAST helpful to the state in defending the constitutionality of this proposed state tax on sanctuary candles?

## 6. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

D is correct. This is the LEAST helpful argument because it fundamentally misstates the scope of state authority. The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states powers not delegated to the federal government, but it does not give states plenary power to tax in any manner they choose without regard to other provisions of the Constitution. A state tax on local manufacture must still satisfy the requirements of the Dormant Commerce Clause (and potentially other limits such as the Privileges and Immunities Clause or Equal Protection). Citing the Tenth Amendment as an absolute shield is the weakest possible defense because it does not engage the actual constitutional constraints that apply to state taxation affecting interstate commerce.

**Silver Key (call focus):** On a "LEAST helpful" / negative call, first eliminate the choice that overclaims an absolute power the Constitution does not grant; the other three choices at least attempt to speak to the real tests (nexus/timing, discrimination, multiple burdens) even if they would not ultimately prevail.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is incorrect (attractive because it sounds like a timing/nexus defense). The argument that the economic impact will be passed on to both in-state and out-of-state purchasers, making the tax wholly nondiscriminatory, could be helpful in defending against a Dormant Commerce Clause challenge. Non-discrimination is one of the core prongs of the test for state taxes affecting interstate commerce; showing that the burden does not fall more heavily on out-of-state interests addresses a key constitutional concern.

B is incorrect (attractive because it invokes the "only one state can tax it" fairness intuition). The argument that a tax on manufacture can be imposed only by the state where manufacturing occurs and therefore is unlikely to create a multiple tax burden could be helpful. The Dormant Commerce Clause is concerned with preventing undue burdens on interstate commerce, including the risk of cumulative taxation by multiple states; arguing that the tax is confined to the place of manufacture and will not produce such duplication engages that concern directly.

C is incorrect (attractive because it sounds like a classic "pre-interstate commerce" defense). The argument that the candles have not yet entered the channels of interstate commerce at the time of manufacture and taxation could be helpful. It speaks to the scope of Congress's commerce power and the point at which state regulation of local activity may be less likely to be seen as regulating interstate commerce itself.
