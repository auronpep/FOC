### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

C is correct. An implied-in-fact contract arises when mutual assent is inferred from the parties' conduct rather than from express words. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 4 cmt. a. Here, Lydia specifically requested the service ("fix these for me"), Peter performed it, and Lydia — who understood from the posted schedule that the service was paid — watched the work and said nothing to object. Her silence in the face of known paid conduct, coupled with her prior affirmative request, supplies the conduct-based mutual assent required. The key is that Lydia **knew** it was a paid service and had the opportunity to object but did not. Peter's conduct (performing the service) and Lydia's conduct (requesting it and silently accepting completion while knowing the price) together manifest a meeting of the minds inferred from action, not words — the textbook definition of an implied-in-fact contract.

**Gold Key (GK-CONTRACTS-IMPLIED-01):** An implied-in-fact contract requires mutual assent shown through conduct, not words. The critical signal: one party performs with the other's knowledge, the recipient knows the service is not gratuitous, and the recipient has a reasonable chance to object but stays silent. That silence + prior knowledge of price = assent inferred from conduct.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A:** This choice sells the idea that someone who subjectively believed no payment was due can still be bound merely because a service was performed. But this choice actually describes a **mistake about whether fees apply to begin with**. A genuine mutual-assent problem exists: if Lydia reasonably believed the service was prepaid through her student fees, there is no meeting of the minds AND no conduct indicating assent to pay. The choice is wrong not because mistake never matters, but because a payer who acts under a good-faith belief that the service is already paid for never manifested assent — implied-in-fact or otherwise. The choice confuses students who conflate "a service was performed" with "she implicitly agreed to pay." The Gold Key reminds us: silence only counts when the recipient **knows** the service is not free.

**B:** This choice describes a family-member favor accomplished through direct spoken request ("her mother asked"). The direct verbal communication ("asked Peter to file") makes this behave like an express arrangement, not an implied-in-fact one. The question specifically asks for an implied-in-fact contract, and this pattern's communication frame — even if no price is discussed — looks more like a gratuitous family favor or express agreement than conduct-assent. Students who pick this one mistake "no discussion of payment" for "no contract," missing that the call is asking for the clearest *implied-in-fact* pattern, not just any possible contract.

**D:** The dominant trap. This choice describes a person who was physically unable to assent — the student fainted and was unconscious. An implied-in-fact contract requires conduct from *both* parties from which assent can be inferred. An unconscious person cannot request a service, cannot know what is being done, and cannot signal acceptance or rejection through conduct (or silence). No amount of performance by the other side creates mutual assent when one party is incapacitated. The trap is the emergency-services intuition: a student may think the patient should have to pay, but that would be an implied-in-law/restitution idea, not an implied-in-fact contract.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governed rule:** Implied-in-fact contract formation (Contracts — Formation — Implied-in-fact contracts, outline code 55020400).

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 4 cmt. a (1981) ("A promise may be stated in words ... or may be inferred wholly or partly from conduct."); *Baltimore & Ohio R.R. v. United States*, 261 U.S. 592 (1923) (implied-in-fact contract requires conduct from both parties evidencing mutual assent); Wex Legal Dictionary, Cornell LII, "contract implied in fact" (elements: unambiguous offer, unambiguous acceptance, mutual intent to be bound, consideration — all inferred from conduct, not words).

**Rule statement:** An implied-in-fact contract is a true contract, enforceable as any express contract, in which the parties' mutual assent is inferred from their conduct rather than expressed in words. The elements are the same as an express contract (offer, acceptance, mutual intent, consideration); the difference is the *mode* of manifestation — conduct, not language. Critically, the recipient must have a reasonable opportunity to reject the benefit and fail to do so, or must engage in conduct that a reasonable person would interpret as assent.

**Application to the credited answer (C):** Lydia's conduct — bringing the discs, requesting the service, watching the resurfacing, and saying nothing to object while knowing the service was paid — constitutes conduct from which assent is reasonably inferred. Peter's conduct (performing the requested service) is the counterpart performance. Together their actions manifest mutual intent to be bound. Consideration is the service in exchange for the implied promise to pay the posted rate. This is the canonical implied-in-fact pattern.

**Confirmation:** The legal issue, tested rule, and credited outcome are **unchanged** from the original Q14429. The original tested whether a bystander's silence while watching mistakenly-directed-but-requested-by-conduct work = implied-in-fact contract. The variant preserves the identical doctrinal core: conduct request + knowing silence → assent inferred from conduct. Bank_validation_verdict: PASS.

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

#### Program Frame

This item tests your ability to identify an **implied-in-fact contract** — a true contract where mutual assent is inferred entirely from the parties' conduct, not from any spoken or written words. You need to distinguish this pattern from (a) situations where no assent exists because of mistake, (b) express contracts communicated in words, and (c) circumstances where one party was incapable of assenting at all. The MBE loves to test the boundaries of implied-in-fact contracts by placing distractors that look superficially similar (someone watches work happen and says nothing) but differ on the knowledge axis.

#### Student Access Test

The CUT-phase choices (D and B) are hard-structural — any student who can see that an unconscious person cannot assent, and that direct verbal communication is not conduct-based assent, can eliminate them without outside doctrine. The CLASH-phase (A vs. C) requires the Gold Key anchor: did the recipient know the service was not free? If the student has learned that "silence = assent ONLY when the silent party knew the service was not gratuitous," the clash resolves cleanly.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Peter operates a disc-resurfacing booth at a Christian university tech fair under a signed service agreement with the university. Lydia, a student, brings six scratched DVDs to the booth, sets them on the counter, and says, "Here — fix these for me." Peter resurfaces all six and sets them on the counter. Lydia picks them up, sees the finished work, and walks away without a word. The fair's posted schedule listed disc resurfacing as a paid service with per-disc rates. Assume no other communication occurred between them.

**Call:** Which of the following fact patterns most clearly suggests an implied-in-fact contract?

**Choices:**
- **A:** A university advisor had mistakenly told Lydia the fair's services were covered by her student fees, and Lydia believed this when she brought her discs to Peter.
- **B:** Lydia's mother had previously asked Peter to file her tax forms, and Peter did so as a favor without any discussion of payment.
- **C:** Lydia saw Peter resurfacing her discs at the booth, understood the service was not free based on the posted schedule, and said nothing to object while Peter completed the work.
- **D:** A campus nurse treated a student without the student's knowledge or consent after the student fainted in the dormitory hallway.

#### Distilled Core Question

Does conduct-based mutual assent exist when a recipient requests a service, watches it being performed knowing it costs money, and says nothing to object?

#### Remediation Card

**Card ID:** REM-CONTRACTS-FACT-01
**Title:** Implied-in-Fact vs. Mistake & Incapacitated Recipients
**Signal on exam:** You see a bar question where someone watches a service being performed and says nothing — and you're asked whether an implied-in-fact contract exists.
**Your move:** Before concluding "silence = assent," check two things: (1) Was the recipient capable of assenting (conscious, competent)? (2) Did the recipient know or have reason to know the service was not free?
**Tiny rule:** Silence is assent only when it occurs in the face of known non-gratuitous performance and the silent party had a reasonable opportunity to object.
**Trap avoided:** The mistake-about-fee-arrangement distractor — where the "recipient" didn't know they needed to pay.
