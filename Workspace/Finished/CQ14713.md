## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Mary owned a small townhouse and told Timothy that she would be away at a retreat for ten days. She asked Timothy to stop by, feed her fish, and make sure the townhouse looked occupied. Timothy agreed, and Mary gave him a key to use for that purpose. One evening, Timothy hosted a hymn-study dessert night in Mary's townhouse and invited several friends. During the gathering, Stephen, one of the guests and a pickpocket, went into Mary's study, took several antique silver bookmarks from her desk, and slipped them into his coat.

Which of the following is true?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A permitted caretaker hosts guests in the owner's townhouse, and one guest secretly pockets the owner's personal property; who has committed a property crime?

B. Stephen is guilty of larceny and Timothy is not guilty of any crime.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Stephen took Mary's personal property, carried it away at least slightly by putting it in his coat, and acted without Mary's consent. That is larceny. Timothy had Mary's permission to enter the townhouse with the key, and the facts do not show that he intended a theft or felony when he entered, helped Stephen steal, or agreed to Stephen's theft. Gold Key: permission to enter defeats the trespass/burglary move unless the facts give a separate unauthorized entry, criminal intent at entry, or accomplice link. Silver Key: split each compound answer before choosing; a true Stephen clause does not rescue a false Timothy clause.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells a clean split: Stephen stole property, and Timothy misused the key. The Stephen half is true, but the Timothy half overcharges the caretaker as a burglar without an unlawful entry, criminal intent at entry, or accomplice facts. Silver Key: split the compound answer; one true clause plus one false clause is still a false answer.

C is the dominant trap because the dessert night feels outside Mary's instructions. The breaker is the key-and-permission fact: Timothy was allowed to enter the townhouse, and the stem does not add a criminal trespass fact just because he hosted an unauthorized gathering. Gold Key: permission to enter defeats the trespass move unless the facts show the permission was legally revoked or exceeded in a way the tested rule treats as unauthorized entry.

D turns Stephen's later theft into burglary for both people. The theft proves larceny, not burglary, because the facts do not show Stephen entered with intent to steal or that Timothy entered unlawfully with a criminal purpose.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged. The thief is guilty of larceny because larceny is the trespassory taking and carrying away of another's personal property with intent to permanently deprive. Cornell Wex states the common-law larceny rule as an illegal taking of another's property with intent to deprive the owner, and Model Penal Code section 223.2 similarly treats unlawful taking or unlawful control over movable property of another with purpose to deprive as theft by unlawful taking. Stephen's pocketing of Mary's silver bookmarks satisfies that theft/larceny rule.

The burglary and trespass distractors fail for the same reason as the source item. Model Penal Code section 221.1 defines burglary around entry with purpose to commit a crime unless the actor is licensed or privileged to enter; section 221.2 uses the same license-or-privilege idea for criminal trespass. Timothy had a key and permission to enter for caretaking. No facts show he entered with criminal intent, remained after revocation, aided Stephen, or shared Stephen's theft plan. Stephen is guilty of larceny, and Timothy is not guilty of a crime on these facts.

Authority floor: Model Penal Code sections 223.2, 221.1, and 221.2; Cornell Wex, "larceny," https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/larceny; Houston Law Review, "That's Not a Burglary! Classic Crimes and Current Codes," quoting Model Penal Code section 221.1, https://houstonlawreview.org/article/24476-that-s-not-a-burglary-classic-crimes-and-current-codes.
