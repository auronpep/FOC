## 1. Final Question

Timothy and Lydia are the parents of a 10-year-old boy, Daniel. Daniel was diagnosed with acute leukemia. His oncologists told the family that without a bone-marrow transplant, Daniel would likely die within months. ## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Parents do have a protected liberty interest in the care, custody, and management of their children under the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment (Meyer v. Nebraska, 262 U.S. 390 (1923); Pierce v. Society of Sisters, 268 U.S. 510 (1925)). However, that liberty interest is not absolute. The state, acting as parens patriae, has a compelling interest in protecting the life and welfare of children. When a child's life is at stake and medical treatment offers a strong chance of saving that life, the state's interest in preserving the child's life outweighs the parents' right to refuse treatment on religious or other grounds. The court order authorizing the transplant is a constitutional exercise of the state's police power to protect the child (Prince v. Massachusetts, 321 U.S. 158 (1944)).

**Gold Key:** Parental rights under substantive due process are real but not absolute — the state may override them when it has a compelling child-protection interest, especially to save a child's life.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap):** This choice is attractive because parents do have a genuine constitutional liberty interest in directing the care of their children — that part is true. The trap is the word "absolute." The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that parental rights, while fundamental, are subject to state regulation when the state acts to protect the child's welfare. Meyer and Pierce established the liberty interest; Prince confirmed it is not unlimited. A student who recognizes the parental-rights doctrine but forgets the state's countervailing power will pick this answer. The Gold Key — that parental rights are real but not absolute — is the rule that kills this trap.

**B:** This choice reaches the right outcome (the court order is constitutional) but for the wrong reason. The question asks about substantive due process and parental liberty, not the Free Exercise Clause. While the parents' refusal is religiously motivated, the legal issue is whether their substantive due process right gives them an absolute veto — a parental-rights question, not a free-exercise question. This is a misfit: it invokes a neighboring constitutional doctrine that does not govern the call.

**D:** This choice is attractive to students who overcorrect — they know the state can intervene, and they swing too far by denying that parents have any protected liberty interest at all. But Meyer v. Nebraska and Pierce v. Society of Sisters clearly establish that parents do have a fundamental liberty interest in directing the care and upbringing of their children. The issue is the *limits* of that interest, not its existence. This choice flatly misstates the law.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause protects a parent's liberty interest in the care, custody, and management of their children. Meyer v.
