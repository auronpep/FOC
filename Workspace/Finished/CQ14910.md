## Final question

Lydia, a widow and active member of the women's ministry at her church, owned a 40-acre river-bluff pasture that had been in her late husband's family for three generations. The pasture had a spring, good fencing, and a small barn. Lydia and Timothy, a young man who had grown up in the church youth group and was now engaged to be married, entered into a written agreement under which Timothy agreed to buy the pasture for $100,000, its fair market value. The agreement contained all the essential terms of a real estate contract, including a date for closing. Timothy made the required $50,000 down payment. The contract provided that in the event of Timothy's breach, Lydia could retain the $50,000 deposit as liquidated damages.

Before the date set for closing, Timothy died in a sudden illness. On the day that the administratrix of Timothy's estate was duly qualified, which was after the closing date, the administratrix made demand for return of the $50,000 deposit. Lydia responded by stating that she took such demand to be a declaration that the administratrix did not intend to complete the contract and that she considered the contract at an end. Lydia further asserted that she was entitled to retain, as liquidated damages, the $50,000. The reasonable market value of the pasture had increased to $110,000 at that time.

The administratrix brought an appropriate action against Lydia to recover $50,000. In answer, Lydia made no affirmative claim but asserted that she was entitled to retain the $50,000 as liquidated damages as provided in the contract. In such lawsuit, judgment should be for

**A:** Lydia, because the doctrine of equitable conversion prevents termination of the contract upon the death of a party.

**B:** the administratrix, because the death of Timothy terminated the contract as a matter of law.

**C:** Lydia, because the court should enforce the express agreement of the contracting parties.

**D:** the administratrix, because the provision relied upon by Lydia is unenforceable.

## Distilled Core Question

## Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Judgment should be for the administratrix (D). A seller is entitled to keep a buyer's deposit as liquidated damages following the buyer's breach only if the amount is reasonable in light of the seller's anticipated damages at the time of contracting or the actual damages suffered. Here, Lydia suffered no actual damages—she can sell the pasture for $10,000 more than the contract price. Retaining the full $50,000 would give her a windfall unrelated to any loss. The clause is therefore unenforceable as a penalty.

**Gold Key (if anchor-assisted):** Liquidated damages must be a reasonable forecast of anticipated or actual harm; when the non-breaching party has no loss (or a profit on resale), a large deposit functions as a penalty and is unenforceable even if the parties agreed to it.

Even though the contract survived Timothy's death under the doctrine of equitable conversion (the estate stands in the buyer's shoes and the seller could seek specific performance or actual damages), that doctrine does not validate an otherwise unenforceable penalty clause. Lydia is not seeking to force the estate to close the deal; she is asserting a right to keep the deposit after the estate demanded its return. The court will not enforce a penalty simply because it was written into the contract.

## Full wrong-answer explanations

**A (dominant trap — ec_misapplied_for_ld):** Attractive because equitable conversion does keep a land-sale contract alive after the buyer's death, so the estate is bound and the seller could in theory obtain specific performance or damages from the estate. The trap sells the idea that "the deal is still on, so the seller wins." But it is not responsive here: Lydia is not seeking to enforce the contract or recover actual damages; she is claiming the right to keep a $50,000 deposit when she has suffered zero (or negative) actual loss. Equitable conversion does not turn an unreasonable liquidated damages clause into an enforceable one. Silver Key move: when the seller is not forcing performance but instead keeping the deposit after a demand for return, the decisive question is whether the LD clause itself is enforceable under the reasonableness test, not merely whether the contract survived death.

**B (death_terminates_misstatement):** Attractive because death feels like it should end a personal obligation, and many contracts do terminate on death. The trap is the assumption that a land-sale contract is purely personal and dies with the buyer. But land-sale contracts are an exception: under equitable conversion the buyer (and then the estate) holds equitable title from the moment the contract is signed, and the estate stands in the buyer's shoes. The contract does not automatically terminate.

**C (penalty_enforce_trap):** Attractive because the parties expressly agreed to the clause and courts generally respect freedom of contract. The trap sells "they wrote it, so it must be enforced." But liquidated damages clauses are an exception to that principle. Courts will not enforce a stipulated amount that operates as a penalty rather than a reasonable pre-estimate of damages. Public policy against penalties overrides the parties' agreement when the amount is unreasonable in light of actual or anticipated loss.

## Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited answer is correct and the tested rule is unchanged: in a land sale contract, a liquidated damages clause for the buyer's breach is enforceable only to the extent the stipulated amount is reasonable in light of the anticipated harm at contracting or the actual harm at breach. A clause that fixes an unreasonably large amount is unenforceable as a penalty. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 356(1) ("Damages for breach by either party may be liquidated in the agreement but only at an amount that is reasonable in the light of the anticipated or actual loss caused by the breach and the difficulties of proof of loss. A term fixing unreasonably large liquidated damages is unenforceable on grounds of public policy as a penalty.").

When the property has appreciated and the seller can resell for more than the contract price, the seller has suffered no actual loss from the buyer's failure to close. Retention of a deposit that greatly exceeds any actual or reasonably anticipated loss is a penalty. The supplied official explanation correctly notes that the most the seller could retain in these facts is a reasonable amount tied to the $10,000 appreciation (or whatever the jurisdiction's reasonable forecast would support), not the full $50,000.

Equitable conversion (the buyer becomes equitable owner upon signing a binding land contract; risk of loss shifts to the buyer; death of the buyer does not terminate the contract—the estate stands in the buyer's shoes) is correctly identified in the distractors but does not change the outcome. Equitable conversion would allow Lydia to seek specific performance against the estate or actual damages. It does not authorize her to keep an unenforceable penalty deposit after the estate has demanded return and she has treated the demand as repudiation. Lydia made no affirmative claim for specific performance or actual damages; she simply asserted the contractual LD clause. Because that clause is a penalty on these facts, it is unenforceable. The outcome matches the original question exactly.
