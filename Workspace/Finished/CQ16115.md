## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question
A statute provides: "Any person who knowingly sells an intoxicating substance to a person under the age of 21 years shall be guilty of a misdemeanor."

Martha owned a private Christian music cafe called Psalm & Cider. Before her new counter server, Timothy, began his first evening shift, Martha read him the statute and told him to check identification for anyone who appeared to be under 21. She also told Timothy that she would fire him immediately if she ever learned that he violated the statute.

Later that night, a 17-year-old customer who looked 26 ordered a bottle of hard cider. The customer handed Timothy an altered state identification card falsely stating that he was 23. Timothy was not sure whether the phrase "intoxicating substance" included hard cider, but he sold the bottle to the customer. An undercover officer in the cafe saw the sale, recognized the identification card as forged, and arrested Timothy.

Martha is charged with violating the statute, and the prosecutor argues that she should be held vicariously liable for Timothy's act. Which of the following would be Martha's most effective argument in defense?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
If an owner is charged vicariously for an employee's sale to a minor under a statute requiring a knowing sale to a person under 21, what defense matters most when the employee did not know the buyer was under 21?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. The prosecutor is trying to make Martha liable for Timothy's crime, so the first question is whether Timothy committed the crime at all. The statute requires a knowing sale to a person under 21. Timothy sold to a 17-year-old who looked 26 and produced an altered ID saying he was 23, so Timothy's best argument is that he lacked the required knowledge as to the buyer's age. Gold Key: vicarious criminal liability cannot impute an agent crime unless the agent's conduct first satisfies the offense. Silver Key: in a vicarious-liability answer array, check the employee's guilt before checking the owner's personal fault.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because it points to Martha's clean hands and feels like a direct denial of blame. The breaker is that vicarious liability, when available, does not turn on the owner's personal knowledge of the sale; it turns on whether the employee committed an imputable offense.

C is attractive because "scope of employment" is a real phrase in vicarious-liability questions. The breaker is that selling hard cider to a cafe customer during a counter shift is exactly the kind of act Timothy was employed to perform for Martha's business, even if the sale violated the statute.

D is the dominant trap. It is attractive because Martha did the responsible thing by reading Timothy the statute, ordering ID checks, and threatening immediate termination. The breaker is that a principal's warning does not defeat vicarious liability if the agent commits the offense within the job; Gold Key: the stronger defense is upstream, that Timothy's own mens rea is missing, so there is no completed employee crime to impute.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law and Procedure, General Principles, Parties to Crime, Scope of liability, and the verified outline code is `72050400`. The credited outcome is unchanged: the owner's strongest defense is that the server is not guilty of the offense, leaving no underlying employee crime to impute.

The statute requires that the seller act knowingly. Under the Model Penal Code's element-analysis approach, knowledge is evaluated against material offense elements, and a prescribed culpability term generally applies to all material elements unless a contrary legislative purpose appears. See Model Penal Code §§ 2.02(2)(b), 2.02(4). A mistake is a defense if it negatives the required culpability for a material element. See Model Penal Code § 2.04(1)(a). Timothy's strongest argument is that he did not know the buyer was under 21 because the buyer looked older and presented a forged ID showing age 23. That defeats the knowledge element as to the underage-buyer circumstance.

Vicarious criminal liability can impute an agent's offense to a principal in appropriate statutory settings, especially where the agent acts within the scope of employment and for the principal's benefit. The Supreme Court recognized corporate criminal liability for acts of agents in *New York Central & Hudson River Railroad Co. v. United States*, 212 U.S. 481 (1909), including acts within the scope of employment and even against the principal's orders. See https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/212/481/. But that vicarious theory still needs an agent offense to impute. If Timothy is not guilty, Martha has the only defense among the choices that could defeat the prosecutor's vicarious-liability theory.
