## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

A city enacted a "Good Neighbor Shuttle" program for private shuttle companies that use curb lanes outside a city-owned civic center. The program required each shuttle to display a small window decal listing a city phone number for traffic, safety, and noise complaints. A national association of shuttle companies, including companies owned by Lydia, Barnabas, and Timothy, sued the city in federal court, arguing that the program was unfair and unconstitutional. The city agreed not to enforce the decal rule while that litigation was pending, and the rule was ultimately upheld. After the rule was upheld, the association filed a new suit to stop the city from penalizing companies for failing to display the decals while the earlier litigation was pending. The city has not imposed any such penalties. The city moved to dismiss the new claim. How should the court rule?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

Can a federal court decide a pre-enforcement challenge to possible retroactive penalties when the city has not imposed them and may never do so?

D. Deny the motion, because the city program affects companies that operate in interstate commerce.

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

A is correct. The association is asking the federal court to block a penalty scheme that the city has not used and may never use. That makes the claim premature. Gold Key: federal courts decide ripe cases, not fears about contingent future enforcement. Silver Key: when the call asks whether to dismiss, test timing before debating whether the city rule is valid.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

B sells a familiar constitutional phrase and makes the possible retroactive penalty sound like an ex post facto problem. The breaker is timing: before the city imposes or threatens the penalty, the court has no ripe enforcement dispute to decide.

C sells a procedural waiting requirement, as if federal courts must pause until a state court rules first. The breaker is that ripeness is not a state-court-first rule; the missing fact is concrete city enforcement.

D is the dominant trap because interstate movement sounds powerful and the association is national. The breaker is that the current claim is not ready for a merits ruling, even if another challenge might raise commerce-related arguments.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The credited answer is correct under Article III ripeness doctrine. Federal courts may not decide abstract or premature disputes; a claim can be unripe when it rests on contingent future events that may not occur as anticipated or may not occur at all. Congress's Constitution Annotated summarizes the doctrine as a limit tied to Article III's case-or-controversy requirement and cites Texas v. United States, 523 U.S. 296, 300 (1998), for the contingent-future-events formulation. In American Trucking Associations, Inc. v. City of Los Angeles, 569 U.S. 641 (2013), the Court declined to decide a challenge to a port penalty theory in a pre-enforcement posture because it could not know whether the port would use its penalty provision in the feared way.

The transformed item preserves the tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome. The city has not imposed penalties for the interim period, and it may never do so. The court should grant the motion because the claim is not ripe.
