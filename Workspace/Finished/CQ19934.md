## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Daniel, Ruth, and Stephen agreed in April to rob a courier who would be carrying cash receipts from a privately run Bible expo in June. In May, Daniel told Ruth and Stephen that he was out and wanted nothing more to do with the plan. Ruth and Stephen went ahead with the robbery in June without Daniel. Daniel did nothing to warn police or otherwise stop the robbery.

Daniel is charged with (1) conspiracy to rob the courier and (2) the substantive robbery itself. What is the most accurate statement of Daniel's criminal liability after his May withdrawal?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A conspirator tells the others he is out before they commit the planned robbery, then does nothing else; is he liable for the conspiracy and for the later robbery?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is the best answer. The agreement to commit the robbery made Daniel guilty of conspiracy. His later exit did not rewind the April agreement or make the conspiracy disappear.

But the robbery happened after Daniel told the others he was out. Gold Key: withdrawal from a conspiracy does not erase the conspiracy offense; it cuts off liability for later co-conspirator acts. Silver Key: split the two charges in time. The conspiracy charge looks backward to the agreement; the robbery charge looks forward to the post-withdrawal act. Daniel is guilty of the conspiracy but not the later robbery.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because it uses the clean timing fact: Daniel left before the robbery. That timing matters for the robbery, but it does not erase the completed conspiracy agreement. Gold Key: withdrawal is forward-looking for later acts, not backward-looking for the conspiracy already joined.

B is attractive because it reaches for the overt-act idea and tries to place Daniel outside the conspiracy before anything happened. The breaker is that the charged conspiracy rests on the agreement Daniel joined; withdrawal does not undo that membership. The answer also fails to handle the separate robbery charge.

D is the dominant trap. It sells the tougher-sounding rule that Daniel had to stop the robbery, not just get out. The breaker is that the charge for the later robbery turns on post-withdrawal Pinkerton liability, and Daniel's communicated withdrawal cuts that off on these facts. Silver Key: do not merge the two counts into one all-or-nothing answer.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, trap structure, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. The issue is the effect of withdrawal from a conspiracy on (1) the conspiracy conviction and (2) liability for later substantive offenses by co-conspirators.

The governing authority confirms the source key. In Smith v. United States, 568 U.S. 106 (2013), the Supreme Court stated that withdrawal does not negate an element of conspiracy, presupposes the conspiracy offense, and terminates liability for post-withdrawal acts while leaving the defendant guilty of conspiracy. The Court also described withdrawal as affirmative action inconsistent with the conspiracy's goals and communicated to co-conspirators in a way reasonably calculated to reach them. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/568/106/

Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640 (1946), supplies the co-conspirator liability rule: a conspirator can be liable for substantive offenses committed by another conspirator in furtherance of the unlawful agreement while the conspiratorial agency continues. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/328/640

The Department of Justice's archived Pinkerton summary states the same basic theory: substantive liability may rest on actual commission, aiding and abetting, or Pinkerton liability; Pinkerton attribution depends on the conspiracy relationship and acts in furtherance of it. Source: https://www.justice.gov/archives/jm/criminal-resource-manual-2482-pinkerton-vs-aiding-and-abetting

Daniel communicated his withdrawal before the robbery, and the stem gives no independent aiding-and-abetting act. He remains guilty of the completed conspiracy but is not liable for the later robbery.
