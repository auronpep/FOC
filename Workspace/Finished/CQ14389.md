## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Barnabas, a craftsman, contracted in writing to sell Lydia a mobile bread oven for $28,000. Barnabas told Lydia that the oven had been built from bricks salvaged from a well-known Christian camp bakery and that its heat came from a traditional wood-fired hearth. Barnabas knew that both representations were important to Lydia and that both were false.

Lydia used the oven for seven months in her private Daily Bread catering business. She then learned that the bricks were ordinary new masonry and that the oven was heated by electric coils. Lydia immediately sued Barnabas to avoid the contract. A regional food-truck festival had recently made electric bread ovens more valuable than wood-fired ovens in that market, and the electric coils exceed all established safety standards for commercial baking.

Is Lydia likely to prevail?

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. Lydia's assent was induced by Barnabas's knowingly false statements about facts he knew were important to her. The later market value and safety facts do not erase the fraud. Gold Key: fraud that induces assent gives the deceived party a power to avoid; the contract is voidable, not automatically void. Silver Key: once the stem says the buyer sued immediately after discovery, do not count the hidden months as delay against the buyer.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the tempting fairness shortcut: if the oven became more valuable and was safe, maybe Lydia suffered no legally meaningful injury. The breaker is that avoidance for fraud focuses on induced assent, not on whether the bargain later caused economic loss.

C sells the calendar shortcut: seven months sounds too long if counted from contract formation. The breaker is the discovery point. Lydia sued immediately after learning the truth, so the hidden period does not defeat the power to avoid.

D sells the dominant remedy trap: fraud sounds serious enough to erase the contract from the beginning. Gold Key: fraud in the inducement ordinarily makes the contract voidable by the deceived party, not void ab initio.

### 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 164, a contract is voidable by the recipient when assent is induced by a fraudulent or material misrepresentation by the other party and reliance is justified. Restatement section 7 distinguishes a voidable contract from one that is void from the outset: the affected party has a power to avoid or affirm. Restatement section 381 addresses loss of the power of avoidance by delay; the key exam point here is that the buyer acted immediately once the fraud was discovered. Economic fairness after the fact does not defeat the fraud-based power to avoid.
