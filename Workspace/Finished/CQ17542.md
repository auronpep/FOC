## Final question (chosen variation)

During a federal civil trial in a breach-of-contract action between two longtime members of the same small-group Bible study, Peter (plaintiff) sues Barnabas for failing to complete and deliver a set of handcrafted wooden pews that Peter had commissioned for the new fellowship hall at their church. The judge mistakenly excludes a photocopy of a letter Barnabas wrote to Peter two months earlier confirming the delivery date, price, and specifications. Later in the trial the original letter is admitted without objection, and Silas, another member of their Bible study who was present when the letter was discussed, provides undisputed testimony about its exact terms and the agreement. After the jury returns a verdict for Barnabas, Peter moves for a new trial. He argues that the judge's mistaken exclusion of the photocopy by itself requires a new trial, no matter what other evidence came in. How should the court analyze Peter's motion?

## Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)
The court should deny the motion for a new trial. Under the governing standard, a court may grant a new trial for an error in excluding evidence only when the error affected a party's substantial rights. Here the photocopy's contents were fully established by the later-admitted original letter and by Silas's undisputed testimony. The error was therefore harmless.

## Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer (lawyer-confirmation layer only)
The credited answer and the tested rule are unchanged from the source row. The question tests how a federal district court analyzes a timely Rule 59 motion for new trial when the movant claims an evidentiary exclusion error. 

Governing authority: Fed. R. Civ. P. 59(a) (new trial may be granted after jury trial "for any reason for which a new trial has heretofore been granted in an action at law in federal court"). Fed. R. Civ. P. 61 (Harmless Error): "Unless justice requires otherwise, no error in admitting or excluding evidence—or any other error by the court or a party—is ground for granting a new trial, for setting aside a verdict, or for vacating, modifying, or otherwise disturbing a judgment or order. At every stage of the proceeding, the court must disregard all errors and defects that do not affect any party's substantial rights."

The rule is black-letter: trial courts (and appellate courts) disregard harmless errors that do not affect substantial rights. When the same information is admitted through other channels (original document + live undisputed testimony), exclusion of a duplicate or photocopy is the classic harmless error. The source row's official key (A) and explanation are correct; research confirms no change in outcome or rule. No doctrinal-currency hotlist item applies.
