### 6. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. This is a common-law service contract, not a sale of goods. At common law, a modification that changes one party's duty generally needs consideration unless a narrow exception applies. Mary gave up Paul's window-lettering duty, but Paul did not promise any new or different performance in exchange. He would still get the same $14,000 for doing less work. No facts show unanticipated circumstances, statutory authorization, or material reliance that would make the modification enforceable without new consideration. The original written duty therefore remained in place, and Paul breached by refusing to hand-letter the window.

Gold Key: Good-faith/no-consideration modification is a UCC sales-of-goods rule. For a common-law services contract, keep asking what new consideration supports the changed promise unless a Restatement-style exception is triggered.

Silver Key: When a modification answer says "good faith," classify the contract lane first. Goods points toward UCC Section 2-209; services point back to common-law consideration.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because the original deal was written and the later change was oral, so the parol evidence rule sounds like the right gatekeeper. The breaker is timing. The parol evidence rule polices prior or contemporaneous terms offered to vary an integrated writing; it does not bar proof of a later oral modification. Gold Key: the problem with the later oral change is not parol evidence. It is the absence of consideration for a common-law service modification.

B is the dominant trap. It sells a real rule from the neighboring UCC lane: modifications of sales contracts can be binding without consideration if made in good faith. But this contract is for artistic services at a private bookshop. The UCC goods rule does not decide it. Silver Key: first classify goods versus services; after the lane is services, good faith alone cannot save this one-sided modification.

D sounds protective of the written contract and reaches the right breach outcome, but it overstates the law. Contract duties can sometimes be discharged or modified orally. The reason Paul breached is narrower: this specific service-contract modification lacked consideration and did not fit an exception.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The verified outline code is `56050200`, listed in `OUTLINE_CODES_COMPLETE.md` as Contracts, Performance, Breach, and Excuse > Modifications.

The governing common-law rule is the preexisting-duty/consideration rule for contract modification. Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 73 states that performance of a legal duty already owed to the promisor is not consideration unless the performance differs in a meaningful, bargained-for way. Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 89 recognizes limited circumstances in which an executory contract modification may bind without new consideration, including fair and equitable modifications due to unanticipated circumstances, statutory authorization, or enforcement required by material reliance. Sources checked: https://matthewminer.name/law/outlines/1L/1st%2BSemester/LAW%2B505-002%2B%E2%80%93%2BContracts%2BI/R2C%2B%C2%A7%2B73 and https://matthewminer.name/law/outlines/1L/1st%2BSemester/LAW%2B505-002%2B%E2%80%93%2BContracts%2BI/R2C%2B%C2%A7%2B89

The UCC contrast confirms why the dominant trap is wrong. UCC Section 2-209(1) provides that an agreement modifying a contract within Article 2 needs no consideration to be binding, but Article 2 governs transactions in goods. Source checked: https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-209

Those authorities confirm the source key. The original and transformed items test the same issue: whether a one-sided oral modification of a common-law service contract is enforceable without consideration. The credited outcome is unchanged. Paul remained obligated to perform the window-lettering duty and breached by refusing.

## crossover_intersection_map

- UCC Article 2: goods modifications do not need consideration but require good faith.
- Parol evidence: prior or contemporaneous terms, not later modifications.
- Statute of Frauds/no-oral-modification clauses: possible writing issues, but not supplied here.
- Excuse/discharge: related node, but the tested reason is modification without consideration.

## review_truth

A common-law service-contract modification that lets one party give less performance for the same price needs new consideration unless a narrow exception applies.
