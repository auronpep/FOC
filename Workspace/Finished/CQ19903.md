### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Admit. FRE 401 sets the floor: evidence is relevant if it has *any tendency* to make a fact of consequence more or less probable. A recent, sizeable increase in first-party hull insurance on a boat the defendant is charged with sinking is a textbook financial-incentive fact — it makes "Daniel sank the boat to collect" more probable than it would be without the evidence. FRE 402 makes relevant evidence admissible unless some other rule bars it, and FRE 411 — the rule students reach for here — governs **liability** insurance only ("evidence that a person was or was not insured *against liability*"); marine hull coverage is first-party property insurance and is analyzed under ordinary FRE 401 / 402, not FRE 411. The question's "no separate exclusion applies" leaves FRE 403 balancing out of the picture, so the relevance floor alone disposes of admissibility. Motive need not prove the act; it just has to nudge probability upward. The increased coverage does. **Gold Key:** *FRE 411's text carves out exactly the use the prosecution is making — "another purpose, such as proving … bias … ownership, or control." Motive is the same species of non-propensity inference.*

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A — "Admit only if the prosecution first proves the insurer paid Daniel."** The attractive move here sounds like careful "foundation first" reasoning — the student pictures an insurance payout as the trigger that turns a quiet policy change into evidence of fraud. The breaker: relevance for motive lives in the defendant's incentive at the time of the act, not in what the insurer does after the loss. FRE 401 asks whether the fact of consequence is made more probable; the question identifies that fact as Daniel's financial motive at the time of the fire, and the doubled coverage answers it directly. Whether the insurer ultimately paid is a separate, sufficiency-side fact the rule does not require as a precondition to admission. The trap engineers a fake foundation order.

**B — "Exclude; marine hull insurance can never be relevant to prove a vessel's loss."** This is the dominant trap because it borrows the language of FRE 411 ("evidence that a person was or was not insured … is not admissible to prove …") and stretches it into a categorical bar. Two errors are smuggled in at once: (1) FRE 411 governs *liability* insurance, not first-party hull insurance; (2) even where FRE 411 does apply, its second sentence carves out "another purpose, such as proving … bias … ownership, or control," and motive is the same species of non-propensity inference. The "never" is the overclaim. **Silver Key:** *the word "never" is a tell — the moment an answer choice turns on a categorical exclusion, check the rule's text for a carve-out.* **Gold Key:** *FRE 411's second sentence is the unlock; motive sits inside it.*

**D — "Exclude; motive evidence must prove the charged act by itself."** The attractive move is conflating relevance (the admissibility test) with sufficiency (the trial-ending test). The student who picks D treats "some tendency to make the act more probable" as if it were "the only thing standing between the prosecution and a directed verdict." The breaker: FRE 401's floor is the lowest threshold in evidence law — a single nudge in the right direction. Motive is a classic piece of the circumstantial puzzle; it never has to carry the case alone. The "by itself" is the overclaim — a real rule (evidence must tend to prove a fact) pushed to an extreme (must prove the act alone).

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited answer is **C — admit; the policy increase has some tendency to show financial motive.**

- **Governing rule — FRE 401 (Test for Relevant Evidence):** "Evidence is relevant if: (a) it has any tendency to make a fact more or less probable than it would be without the evidence; and (b) the fact is of consequence in determining the action." The "any tendency" language is the lowest threshold in the Federal Rules of Evidence.
- **Governing rule — FRE 402 (General Admissibility of Relevant Evidence):** "Relevant evidence is admissible unless any of the following provides otherwise: … these rules; … Irrelevant evidence is not admissible." First-party insurance on the defendant's own property is not barred by any FRE 401–415 rule, and the question stipulates "no separate exclusion applies" (closing off FRE 403 balancing and any other exclusion argument).
- **Trap rule — FRE 411 (Liability Insurance):** "Evidence that a person was or was not insured against liability is not admissible to prove whether the person acted negligently or otherwise wrongfully. But the court may admit this evidence for another purpose, such as proving a witness's bias or prejudice or proving agency, ownership, or control." FRE 411 is limited to *liability* insurance. First-party coverage (fire, marine hull, livestock, crop, life) is not FRE 411 territory and is analyzed under ordinary FRE 401 / 402 relevance and, where raised, FRE 403 balancing.

**Applied:** The doubled coverage is "any tendency" evidence of Daniel's financial motive to destroy the boat — a fact of consequence in a charge of intentional sinking for insurance gain. The mechanism is the standard financial-incentive inference used in arson-for-insurance and marine-insurance-fraud prosecutions: a recent, sizeable, unexplained increase in coverage on a property the defendant is about to lose is the textbook circumstantial thread. FRE 411 does not bar first-party insurance; its carve-out for "another purpose" (and the fact that it governs liability insurance only) means the evidence is admissible. Motive evidence does not need to prove the act — relevance is not sufficiency.
