### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The oral agreement between Peter and Barnabas established a condition precedent to the *formation* of the contract — the sale of the amplifier by May 1 was required before any enforceable duty came into existence. The parol evidence rule bars extrinsic evidence that would *modify or contradict* the terms of an integrated writing, but it does **not** bar evidence offered to show that no contract was ever formed. ### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is wrong** because it misidentifies the oral agreement as a *modification* of an existing contract. A modification presupposes that a valid contract already exists and that the parties later changed its terms. Here, the oral agreement was made *before* the written contract was signed and concerned whether a contract would come into existence at all — it was a condition precedent to formation, not a modification. The fabricated-rule mold (oral modification of a service contract) is attractive because students know that service contracts can sometimes be modified orally, but the timing is wrong: this was pre-formation, not post-formation.

**B is wrong** because estoppel does not apply. Estoppel prevents a party from asserting the *validity* of a written instrument to then deny its terms. Here, Barnabas is not claiming the written contract is valid and then contradicting it — he is denying that any contract was ever formed. Estoppel runs the wrong direction. The backwards mold is attractive because estoppel is a familiar doctrine, but students must check which party is asserting what.

**D is wrong** (Gold Key: the parol evidence rule has an exception for conditions precedent to formation) because while the parol evidence rule does bar prior oral agreements that contradict a written contract, it contains a critical exception: evidence of a condition precedent to the *effectiveness* of the agreement is admissible. Students who mechanically apply the parol evidence rule without checking for exceptions will pick this trap.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule — Parol Evidence Rule and the Condition-Precedent Exception:**

The parol evidence rule provides that when parties have reduced their agreement to a final integrated writing, extrinsic evidence of prior or contemporaneous agreements is inadmissible to contradict, vary, or add to the terms of the writing. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 213 (1981).

However, the rule contains several well-established exceptions. Critically, parol evidence **is admissible** to establish that no enforceable contract was ever formed — including evidence of a condition precedent to the *effectiveness* of the agreement. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 214(d) (1981) (parol evidence rule does not apply to evidence establishing whether the agreement is integrated or whether it was ever formed); *see also* 3 Corbin on Contracts § 573 (rev. ed.).

A condition precedent to formation is an event that must occur before any contractual duty arises. It is distinct from a condition subsequent (which terminates an existing duty) or a covenant (a promise within an existing contract). Evidence of a condition precedent to formation does not "contradict" the writing — it shows the writing never became operative. *Id.*

**Third-party beneficiary layer:**

Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 302, an intended beneficiary may enforce a contract made for the beneficiary's benefit. However, the beneficiary's rights do not *vest* until the beneficiary: (1) manifests assent to the promise, (2) brings suit to enforce it, (3) materially changes position in reliance on it, or (4) has an express contractual term vesting the rights. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 311 (1981); *see also* Wex, Third-Party Beneficiary, Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School (https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/third-party_beneficiary).

Until vesting, the contracting parties retain the power to rescind or modify the beneficiary's interest without the beneficiary's consent. *Id.*

**Application:**

The oral agreement that Barnabas would sell his amplifier by May 1 or the deal was off constitutes a condition precedent to the formation of the contract. It was agreed upon before the writing was signed and established a prerequisite for the agreement to become enforceable. Because Barnabas could not sell the amplifier, the condition failed, no enforceable contract came into existence, and neither Timothy (whose rights never vested) nor Peter has a breach claim. The parol evidence rule does not bar proof of this condition because it goes to formation, not modification.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 213, 214(d), 302, 311 (1981); 3 Corbin on Contracts § 573 (rev. ed.); LII Wex, Third-Party Beneficiary (Cornell Law School).

#### Distilled Core Question

Two parties sign a written contract. Before signing, they orally agree that a specified event must occur by a deadline or the agreement is void. The event does not occur. Is the oral condition precedent admissible, and does it defeat the contract?

Prediction: the defense should succeed because the oral agreement was a condition precedent to formation, and the parol evidence rule does not bar evidence of such a condition.
