## 7. choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. Yes, because losing housing is a serious consequence

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal**
The choice answers a severity question. It says the consequence is serious. That is not the same as saying the threat satisfies the legal duress threshold.

**(2) Student label**
Economic-seriousness trap.

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like**
“Yes, because Evan faced an imminent threat of death or serious bodily harm and had no reasonable legal alternative.”

**(4) Lawyer confirmation**
A is wrong because economic harm, even severe economic harm, is not the required threat type for common-law criminal duress. The Supreme Court in *Dixon v. United States* described the assumed duress formulation as requiring an unlawful and imminent threat inducing apprehension of death or serious bodily injury, plus no reasonable legal alternative. ([Justia Law][1])

### B. Yes, because the deadline left him no realistic business alternative

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal**
The choice jumps to the “no alternative” idea before proving the qualifying threat. It is using a later element before the gateway element.

**(2) Student label**
Second-element-before-first trap.

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like**
“Yes, because Evan faced an imminent death-or-serious-bodily-harm threat and had no reasonable legal way to avoid it.”

**(4) Lawyer confirmation**
B is wrong because the “no reasonable alternative” inquiry matters only if there is first a qualifying coercive threat. The DOJ Criminal Resource Manual describes reasonable alternatives as defeating duress even when death or serious harm is threatened; here, the problem is earlier because the threat is economic rather than physical. ([Department of Justice][2])

### C. No, because economic pressure is not the type of imminent physical threat required for duress

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal**
This answer points to the exact missing threshold fact: the pressure was economic, not an imminent physical threat.

**(2) Student label**
Threshold-threat survivor.

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like**
This choice is already true and responsive once the duress tiny anchor is supplied.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation**
C is correct. Duress requires more than severe pressure; it requires a qualifying threat of imminent death or serious bodily harm and no reasonable legal alternative. Evan had firing, blacklisting, and feared housing loss, not an imminent physical threat. ([Justia Law][1])

### D. No, because duress is available only when the defendant is charged with homicide

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal**
The word “only” is a warning. The answer makes a sweeping crime-category claim.

**(2) Student label**
Homicide-direction reversal.

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like**
“No, because Evan faced economic pressure rather than an imminent physical threat.”

**(4) Lawyer confirmation**
D is wrong. Duress is generally unavailable for intentional homicide and available for many nonhomicide crimes when the elements are met. Evan loses because the threat was economic, not because fraud is outside the defense.
