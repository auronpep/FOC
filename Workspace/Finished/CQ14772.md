## 7. Right-Answer Explanation

**C is correct.** Esther's testimony recounts Daniel's out-of-court statement that he planned to use a particular pseudonym for an undercover film project. The critical question is the *purpose* for which the statement is offered. The statement is not offered to prove that Daniel actually intended to make a documentary under that name — that is not what matters to the case. It is offered to show a connection between Daniel and the name on the trawler's log: because Daniel told Esther he would use that exact pseudonym, the appearance of that name on the vessel's passenger list is circumstantial evidence that the person on the trawler was Daniel. When a statement is not offered for the truth of the matter asserted, it is not hearsay under FRE 801(c). It is admissible as relevant circumstantial evidence.

**Gold Key:** An out-of-court statement offered to identify the speaker — rather than to prove the content of what was said — is not hearsay. The statement is the key that unlocks an inference, not proof of its own claim.

**Silver Key:** Before labeling anything "hearsay," name the precise fact the proponent is trying to prove with the statement. If the fact-to-prove is not "what the declarant asserted is true," it is not hearsay. Here the fact-to-prove is Daniel's connection to the trawler name — not the truth of his documentary intentions.

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (originally B — 2%):** The party-admission doctrine is an attractive shortcut when you see an out-of-court statement from someone connected to a party. But Daniel is not a party to this lawsuit — Esther is the plaintiff and the insurance company is the defendant. The doctrine requires the declarant to be the opposing party or their agent. Because Daniel is neither, party-admission analysis does not apply. The statement does not need an exception; it is not hearsay at all.

**B (originally D — 21% — DOMINANT TRAP):** The dominant wrong move is to see "out-of-court statement" and immediately stamp "hearsay." Here the statement is offered to prove Daniel's connection to the pseudonym on the log — not to prove that he was actually planning a documentary. **Gold Key:** Apply the purpose test first. Once you see the statement is offered for identity/inference rather than truth, the hearsay label falls away and no exception is needed.

**D (originally C — 1%):** This choice invents a legal threshold: that a missing person must have been absent for more than seven years before testimony about their statements becomes admissible. No such rule governs the admissibility of out-of-court statements. The seven-year period appears in some jurisdictions' presumption-of-death statutes — a separate procedural doctrine entirely unrelated to hearsay analysis. Admissibility turns on the purpose for which the statement is offered, not the duration of disappearance.

## 9. Black-Letter Verification

**Rule tested:** FRE 801(c) defines hearsay as an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted. A statement offered for any other relevant purpose is not hearsay and is admissible under FRE 402 if relevant. *United States v. Muscato*, 534 F. Supp. 969 (E.D.N.Y. 1982), exemplifies the non-hearsay use of statements offered to show the speaker's identity or connection to circumstances rather than the truth of the content. The classic formulation: "Offered not for the truth of the matter asserted" is the key phrase — FRE 801(c)(2).

**Issue unchanged:** Whether an out-of-court statement is admissible when offered not for truth but as circumstantial evidence of the speaker's identity or presence.

**Credited outcome unchanged:** Admissible as non-hearsay circumstantial evidence.

**Attorney review flag:** None. Rule is well-settled. FRE 801(c) and the purpose-of-offer analysis are standard MBE doctrine with no doctrinal-currency hotlist concerns.

## Gold Key(s)

**GK-EVIDENCE-NONHEARSAY-PURPOSE-01**

*Statement:* An out-of-court statement is not hearsay when it is offered not to prove the truth of what was said, but to prove the speaker's connection to a name, event, or circumstance. It becomes circumstantial evidence of identity, not an assertion of fact.

*Type:* distinction

*Unlocks:* The dominant trap of auto-labeling out-of-court statements as hearsay without checking the purpose of offer.

*Trigger:* Stem shows a statement that links the speaker to a name or event appearing elsewhere — and the case needs to prove presence or connection, not the content of what was said.

*Tested_choice:* B

*Last minute review:* true

## Silver Key(s)

**SK-EVIDENCE-PURPOSE-OF-OFFER-01**

*Statement:* Before applying any hearsay label, name the precise fact the proponent wants to prove with the statement. If that fact is not "the content of the statement is true," the statement is not hearsay.

*Type:* call_focus

*Navigates:* The CLASH between C (admissible, non-hearsay) and B (inadmissible, hearsay).

*Trigger:* A choice says "hearsay not within any exception" and the statement could plausibly connect the speaker to an event rather than assert a fact.

*Tested_choice:* B

*Outline_code:* 33040203

*Last minute review:* true

#### remediation_card

**Card:** REM-EVIDENCE-NONHEARSAY-01 — Non-Hearsay Purpose

**Signal:** Out-of-court statement offered to connect speaker to an event or name, not to prove the content of the statement.

**Student move:** Stop before stamping "hearsay." Ask: what is this statement being offered to prove? If the answer is anything other than "the statement's content is true," it is not hearsay.

**Tiny rule:** Hearsay = out-of-court statement offered for truth of the matter asserted. Different purpose = not hearsay = admissible if relevant.

**Trap:** Seeing "out-of-court statement" and reflexively classifying it as hearsay without checking the purpose of offer.

#### wrong_answer_recovery_paths

**If you chose A:** You reached for party-admission doctrine. Check who the declarant is — the opposing party? Daniel is neither plaintiff nor defendant. Party admission has a strict requirement: the opposing party made the statement. When the declarant is a missing third party, this doctrine is unavailable.

**If you chose B:** You stamped "hearsay" before checking the purpose of offer. Go back to the Gold Key: the statement is offered to connect Daniel to the trawler name, not to prove he planned a documentary. Purpose check comes first, always.

**If you chose D:** You imported a presumption-of-death statute concept into hearsay admissibility. These are separate doctrines. Hearsay admissibility turns on the purpose of offer, never on the duration of disappearance.
