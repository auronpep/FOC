### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**C is correct.** Robbery is larceny plus force or threat of force directed at the person who possesses the property. Barnabas took Lydia's tote from her arm while making her believe he had a knife. The umbrella handle did not need to be an actual knife. The point is that the taking was accomplished by fear of immediate force, not by Lydia's voluntary transfer of possession or title.

**Gold Key:** A threatened weapon can satisfy the robbery force/fear element even when the weapon is a bluff, if the victim yields the property because of the threat. **Silver Key:** In theft-offense arrays, first ask how the property moved: grabbed by force/fear, voluntarily handed over by trick, entrusted first, or title transferred.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. Larceny by trick.** This choice is attractive because Barnabas lied about having a knife, and the word "trick" seems to match a false statement. The breaker is the movement of the tote: Lydia did not voluntarily hand over possession in reliance on a harmless fraud; Barnabas yanked it away while using fear. **Gold Key:** A bluff-threat still counts as robbery force/fear when the threat is what makes the victim submit.

**B. False pretenses.** This choice sells the same false-statement bait at a more formal level. False pretenses needs the victim to pass title because of the misrepresentation. Lydia never sold, gave, assigned, or transferred ownership of the tote, tablet, or wallet. She lost possession because Barnabas took the tote by threat-backed force.

**D. Embezzlement.** This choice sounds like a general theft label, but it is built for a different starting point. Embezzlement requires lawful possession first, followed by criminal conversion. Barnabas never had permission, custody, or entrusted control over Lydia's tote. He took it from her.

### 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: a taking from the person or presence of another by force or threat/fear is robbery. The apparent weapon can be enough when it makes the victim yield, because the robbery element is the force or fear used to accomplish the taking.

Lawyer-confirmation authority: *Stokeling v. United States*, 586 U.S. ___, 139 S. Ct. 544 (2019), describes common-law robbery as larceny requiring force or violence sufficient to overcome victim resistance and notes classic purse-taking examples. The Cornell Wex robbery entry states the common-law framing as taking another's property by violent force or threat of such force with intent to permanently deprive. Cornell Wex also confirms the theft-offense distinctions used by the distractors: larceny by trick involves a consensual possession transfer without title; false pretenses involves obtaining title by misrepresentation; embezzlement involves property entrusted to the defendant.
