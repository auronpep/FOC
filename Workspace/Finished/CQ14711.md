## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

B is correct because the laptop was genuinely stolen and was never recovered by police before the sale to the defendant. The fact that the thief's accomplice was an undercover agent does not change the stolen status of the property — the laptop was stolen, it remained stolen, and the defendant received it knowing it was stolen. This is the only scenario where all elements of receiving stolen property are satisfied.

**Gold Key:** For receiving stolen property, the property must actually be stolen at the time of receipt. Police recovery ends stolen status. An undercover agent who participates in the theft (as an accomplice) does NOT recover the property — the property remains stolen.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A:** The laptop was stolen, but the seller is an undercover police agent operating the stall in cooperation with police. This means the laptop was recovered by police before the sale — the agent is working WITH police, implying police custody. Once recovered, the laptop is no longer stolen. The defendant's belief and the seller's truthful statement don't cure the stolen-status defect. **Gold Key:** police recovery ends stolen status.

**Choice C (DOMINANT TRAP):** The laptop was stolen, but police caught the thief as he was carrying it away and he agreed to cooperate by completing the sale. This is the most attractive wrong answer because the sale was "prearranged" — it feels like the defendant is being set up and should be liable. But police caught the thief WITH the laptop, which means they recovered it. Once recovered, it's no longer stolen. The prearranged sale doesn't preserve stolen status absent a statute. **Gold Key:** police recovery ends stolen status, even if police then use the property in a sting.

**Choice D:** The laptop was never stolen at all — the "seller" was the actual owner who filed a fraudulent insurance claim. Receiving stolen property requires property that WAS stolen. Here, the property was never stolen, so the offense fails. The cognitive move: the defendant believed it was stolen and acted on that belief. The breaker: the actus reus element (stolen property) is missing entirely.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule:** Receiving stolen property at common law requires: (1) property that was stolen, (2) the property remained stolen at the time of receipt, (3) the defendant received or concealed it, (4) the defendant knew it was stolen, (5) intent to deprive the owner. The critical distinction in this question is element (2): whether the property was still stolen when received.

**Police recovery doctrine:** When law enforcement recovers stolen property, it ceases to be "stolen" because it has been returned to lawful custody. A defendant who subsequently receives such property from an undercover officer in a sting does not commit the common-law offense because the jurisdictional element — stolen property — is absent. *See* 30 C.J.S. *Receiving Stolen Property* § 2; *see also* Rollin M. Perkins & Ronald N. Boyce, *Criminal Law* (3d ed. 1982).

**Undercover accomplice distinction:** When an undercover officer participates in the theft as an accomplice (rather than recovering the property afterward), the property has NOT been recovered by police. The undercover agent's role as a theft accomplice means the property remains stolen throughout. This is the key distinction between Choice B (correct) and Choices A/C (incorrect).

**Never-stolen property:** Property that was never stolen (e.g., insurance fraud scenario) cannot support receiving-stolen-property liability regardless of the defendant's belief. *See* common-law elements requiring actual stolen status.

**No doctrinal-currency hotlist concern:** Common-law property crime doctrine, not a hotlist area.

**Application:** B is the only scenario where the property was stolen and remained stolen at receipt. A and C involve police recovery. D involves never-stolen property. The credited answer and outcome are unchanged.

## Wrong-Answer Recovery Paths

### Recovery from A
**What the student thought:** The seller told the truth about it being stolen, and the defendant bought it knowing — that should be enough.  
**The miss:** Didn't see that "undercover agent cooperating with police" = police custody = recovery.  
**Recovery:** When the seller is an undercover agent working WITH police, police have control of the property. That's recovery. Recovery ends stolen status.

### Recovery from C (Dominant Trap)
**What the student thought:** The sale was prearranged by police to catch the buyer — that should create liability.  
**The miss:** Focused on the sting setup and the defendant's guilty mind, overlooked that police caught the thief WITH the laptop (recovery).  
**Recovery:** Police caught the thief carrying the laptop = recovery. The prearranged sale is a sting, but the property was already recovered. Without a statute, no liability.

### Recovery from D
**What the student thought:** The defendant believed it was stolen and bought it — that should be receiving stolen property.  
**The miss:** The property was never stolen (insurance fraud by the owner).  
**Recovery:** Receiving stolen property requires property that WAS actually stolen. Insurance fraud doesn't make property stolen.
