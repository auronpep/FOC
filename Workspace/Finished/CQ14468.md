### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** An express condition precedent must be strictly complied with; substantial performance is not enough. However, a party for whose benefit a condition exists may waive it, either expressly or by conduct indicating the party will proceed despite the non-occurrence. Once waived, the condition is excused and the waiving party cannot later rely on the non-occurrence to avoid its own performance.

Here, the oral agreement created an express condition precedent: the contract would be effective only if the pastor gave written notice by April 3. The pastor gave only oral notice on April 2 — technically a failure of the express condition. But the supplier's response — "That's fine. I'll get the system to you on May 1, as we agreed" — was a clear waiver of the written-notice requirement. The supplier knowingly excused the non-occurrence and confirmed the deal. Having waived the condition, the supplier cannot now insist on strict compliance to escape the contract. **Waiver of condition** is the best answer.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (Substantial performance) — DOMINANT TRAP**

This choice is attractive because students learn that "substantial performance" satisfies constructive conditions and allows recovery (minus damages). The pastor did substantially what was required — gave notice, just not in writing. But the doctrine of substantial performance does NOT apply to express conditions. Express conditions must be strictly satisfied. The pastor's oral notice was not strict compliance with the written-notice requirement. The reason this choice fails is not that the pastor didn't perform enough — it's that the legal doctrine invoked is the wrong tool. The correct doctrine is waiver, not substantial performance. **Gold Key:** Express conditions require strict compliance; substantial performance is not a substitute.

**C (Nonoccurrence of a condition subsequent)**

This choice is attractive because it uses the right vocabulary ("condition") and the right outcome (nonoccurrence discharges a duty). But it misidentifies the type of condition. A condition subsequent terminates an existing obligation when a future event occurs. Here, the written-notice requirement was a condition precedent — it had to occur *before* the contract became effective. The contract was not yet binding and then undone; rather, the condition stood in the way of the contract ever coming into force. Because the condition is precedent, not subsequent, this doctrine is inapplicable. Additionally, "excuse by nonoccurrence" applies when a party simply performs despite a condition's failure — that's waiver, not the nonoccurrence-of-a-subsequent-condition doctrine.

**D (Novation of buyers)**

This choice is attractive because "novation" sounds like the kind of technical contract doctrine that could rescue a deal gone wrong. But a novation requires a new party substituted for an original party, with all parties agreeing to release the original obligor. Here, no new party was substituted for either the pastor or the supplier. The same two parties remain. There was no agreement to replace anyone. The supplier simply waived a condition — the parties and their roles never changed.

### 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** Under the Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 224, a condition is an event, not certain to occur, which must occur (unless its non-occurrence is excused) before performance under a contract becomes due. An express condition — one stated explicitly in the contract — must be strictly complied with; substantial performance does not satisfy it. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 224 (1981); *see also* § 238 (concurrent conditions).

**Waiver of Condition:** A condition may be excused by waiver. Waiver is the voluntary relinquishment of a known right. When a party for whose benefit a condition exists manifests an intention to proceed with the contract despite the condition's non-occurrence, the condition is waived. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 84(a) (1981) (condition may be excused by waiver); *see also* E. Allan Farnsworth, *Contracts* § 8.5 (4th ed. 2004) (waiver of conditions).

**Application:** The oral side agreement created an express condition precedent: written notice by April 3. The pastor gave only oral notice. The supplier's verbal response — accepting the oral notice and confirming the May 1 delivery — constituted a waiver of the written-notice requirement. The supplier cannot later invoke the non-occurrence of the written-notice condition to escape the contract.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 224, 84(a) (1981); Farnsworth, *Contracts* § 8.5.

**Verification:** The tested rule (waiver of an express condition precedent), the legal issue (whether the supplier's oral acceptance of non-complying notice excused the condition), and the credited outcome (the pastor wins on waiver) are all unchanged from the original. The official key is confirmed.
