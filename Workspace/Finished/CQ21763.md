## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** At common law, discovering a spouse in the act of adultery constitutes adequate provocation that can support voluntary manslaughter mitigation — but only if the killing occurs before a reasonable person would have cooled off. Here, Hannah did not act in the immediate heat of the moment. She left the scene, drove fifty miles to her sister's house, spent four hours there, repeatedly said she was going to "make him pay," obtained a gun, and then drove back to kill Stephen. A reasonable person would have had ample time to regain composure during that four-hour period. The delay, the distance, and the revenge statements all demonstrate that any passion from the initial discovery had cooled, and what followed was a deliberate act of retaliation — not a killing in the sudden heat of passion. The mitigation fails.

**Gold Key:** Adequate provocation is only one element of the heat-of-passion defense. Even legally sufficient provocation (like discovering adultery) will not reduce murder to voluntary manslaughter if there was adequate time for a reasonable person to cool off between the provocation and the killing. The cooling-time inquiry is objective — it does not matter whether this particular defendant was still angry; what matters is whether a reasonable person in the same circumstances would have regained self-control.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** At common law, voluntary manslaughter is an intentional killing committed in the "sudden heat of passion" resulting from "adequate provocation," without a reasonable cooling-off period, and with a causal connection between the provocation, passion, and killing. *See* Themis Bar Review, Criminal Law § 3 (Voluntary Manslaughter); Matthew Miner, Criminal Law Outline — Voluntary Manslaughter.

**Four elements of heat-of-passion voluntary manslaughter:**
1. **Adequate provocation** — conduct that would arouse sudden passion in a reasonable person. At common law, recognized categories include: discovering a spouse in the act of adultery, being subjected to a serious battery, witnessing serious harm to a close family member, and mutual combat. *Mere words alone* generally do not constitute adequate provocation.
2. **Actual heat of passion** — the defendant must have been actually provoked and acting under the influence of intense emotion at the time of the killing.
3. **No reasonable cooling-off period** — the killing must occur before a reasonable person would have regained composure. This is an **objective** standard. It does not matter whether the defendant was still angry; what matters is whether a reasonable person would have cooled off. *See* Themis Criminal Law Outline: "If there was sufficient time to cool off for a reasonable person even though the defendant himself did not regain self-control, the murder is not mitigated to manslaughter."
4. **Causal connection** — the provocation must have caused the passion, which must have caused the killing.

**Application:** Hannah discovered Stephen in bed with another woman — this is adequate provocation at common law (category: discovering a spouse in adultery). However, she then left the scene, drove fifty miles, spent four hours at her sister's house, made repeated revenge statements ("make him pay"), obtained a gun, and drove back. A reasonable person would have had ample time to cool off during those four hours. The objective cooling-time standard defeats the mitigation defense.

**Authority:** The common-law heat-of-passion framework is well-established. *See* Themis Bar Review, Criminal Law § 3; *see also* California Penal Code § 192(a) (voluntary manslaughter defined as killing "upon a sudden quarrel or heat of passion"); MPC § 210.3(1)(b) (extreme mental or emotional disturbance — broader than common law but illustrative of the doctrine's evolution).
