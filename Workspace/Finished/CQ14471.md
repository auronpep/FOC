## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** When a contract condition is intended to benefit only one party, that party may unilaterally waive the condition — the condition is not a condition to the other party's duty to perform.

C is correct. The contract language made Timothy's obligation to purchase contingent on obtaining a loan at or below 8%. The condition was for Timothy's benefit — it protected him from being forced to buy if financing was too expensive. Courts look beyond the literal words of a condition to determine its purpose. Here, the financing condition clearly benefited Timothy alone. Timothy waived the condition by tendering the full purchase price despite not meeting the rate threshold. Once Timothy waived it, Paul's duty to convey the tractor was unaffected, and Paul must perform.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A** is attractive because the contract did specify 8%, and students may think a written term is an immutable barrier. But the issue is not modification — no one tried to change the contract. The issue is whether Paul's own duty was conditional. Because the financing condition was for Timothy's benefit only, Timothy could waive it, and Paul's duty stands. **Gold Key:** A benefiting party's waiver of a condition does not require the other party's consent.

**B** is attractive because students know that forfeiture can sometimes excuse a condition. But the statement is far too broad — forfeiture avoidance is one doctrine (Restatement § 229), not the only way a condition is excused. Waiver by the benefiting party is an independent, well-established ground. The choice overstates the forfeiture doctrine and ignores the waiver analysis that actually resolves this question. **Gold Key:** Waiver and forfeiture-excuse are separate doctrines; waiver by the benefiting party does not require showing forfeiture.

**D** is attractive because Timothy did change position by taking out a loan and tendering money. But detrimental reliance (promissory estoppel) is not the right framework here. The question is whether Paul's duty to perform was subject to a condition — and it was not, because the condition was for Timothy's benefit and Timothy waived it. The correct analysis is contractual waiver, not reliance-based estoppel.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Rule:** Under the Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 84(a), a party who is the beneficiary of a condition precedent may waive the condition by promising to perform despite its nonoccurrence. Courts interpret conditional language by looking beyond its literal terms to determine the condition's purpose. If the condition was intended to protect or benefit only one party, that party may unilaterally waive it, and the condition is treated as though it was never a condition to the other party's duty. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 84 (Am. Law Inst. 1981); *see also* 8 Corbin on Contracts § 40.2 (Perillo ed., 2017) (waiver turns a conditional duty into an unconditional one without requiring consideration).

**Application:** The contract stated that Timothy's obligation to purchase was conditioned on obtaining a loan at ≤8%. The condition protected Timothy from being bound if financing was unavailable at a reasonable rate. It did not protect Paul — Paul would receive the same $24,000 regardless of Timothy's loan rate. Timothy tendered full payment, waiving the unmet condition. Paul's duty to convey was never subject to that condition. Paul must perform.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 84 (Am. Law Inst. 1981). Corbin on Contracts § 40.2 (Perillo ed., 2017). The ALI Adviser, "Waiver and Estoppel – Part 1" (May 17, 2018), https://www.thealiadviser.org/liability-insurance/waiver-and-estoppel-part-1.

**Verification:** The tested rule (waiver of a beneficiary-only condition), the legal issue (whether the seller's duty was conditional), and the credited outcome (buyer prevails) are unchanged from the original. Research confirms the official key.
