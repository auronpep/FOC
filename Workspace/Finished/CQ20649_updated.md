### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.** The call asks for the added fact most likely to produce judgment for Barnabas. If platforms of this type usually have roll-stop interlocks, then Barnabas may reasonably have expected the interlock to prevent the platform from lurching even after an idle adjustment. That fact attacks Barnabas’s own negligence: it weakens foreseeability of the risk created by the idle adjustment.

**Gold Key:** Negligence turns on unreasonable conduct in light of foreseeable risk. A fact that makes the particular risk not foreseeable can defeat breach.

**Silver Key:** In a “which added fact helps the defendant win” array, prefer the fact that breaks an element of the plaintiff’s claim over facts that merely blame someone else or describe a safer hypothetical.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because it points to another company in the equipment chain and gives the defense someone else to blame. The breaker is that another company’s failure to warn Lydia’s company does not by itself defeat Barnabas’s own negligence if Barnabas’s adjustment foreseeably caused Naomi’s injury.

**B.** This is the dominant trap because the call mentions all-or-nothing contributory negligence, and the choice sounds like Naomi could have avoided the accident. **Gold Key:** contributory negligence requires plaintiff negligence, not merely a safer alternate position. Nothing in the choice says Naomi was negligent for standing beside the platform to start it.

**D.** This choice is attractive because a manufacturing defect feels like a stronger cause than Barnabas’s idle adjustment. The breaker is that another cause does not automatically erase Barnabas’s liability if his own negligence was also a proximate cause of the injury.

### 9. Black-letter verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule and outcome are unchanged. Negligence is evaluated through reasonable care under the circumstances, with foreseeable likelihood and severity of harm among the core factors; a fact showing the relevant safety device is normally present can make the lurching risk less foreseeable to the contractor. Contributory negligence bars recovery only when the plaintiff’s own negligence contributed to the harm; merely showing that the plaintiff would have avoided injury in another position is not the same as showing negligence. ([Open Casebook][1])

The original official explanation identifies A as the only added fact that could produce judgment for the contractor because usual presence of the safety device makes the risk potentially unforeseeable, while B lacks a fact showing plaintiff negligence and C/D merely identify other causes or other parties.

Signal: “Plaintiff would not have been injured if…”

Student move: Ask whether the answer says the plaintiff acted unreasonably.

Tiny rule: No plaintiff negligence, no contributory-negligence bar.

Trap: safer hypothetical = legal fault.

Confidence: anchor-assisted.

### Gold Key(s)

**GK-TORTS-FORESEEABLE-RISK-01**
Negligence turns on unreasonable conduct in light of a foreseeable risk. A fact that makes the specific risk not foreseeable can defeat breach.

**GK-TORTS-CONTRIB-AVOIDABILITY-01**
Contributory negligence requires negligent plaintiff conduct that contributed to the harm. A safer hypothetical is not enough.

### Silver Key(s)

**SK-TORTS-PAGEANT-ARRAY-01**
Sort each added fact by what it attacks: defendant breach, plaintiff negligence, other cause, or other party. Prefer the fact that breaks the plaintiff’s claim against this defendant.
