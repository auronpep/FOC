# CQ14827 — The Fishing Dock Witness

## 1. Final Question

Peter, an experienced commercial fisherman, was charged with making false statements on federal catch reports he had submitted to a federal fisheries agency. At trial, a dock-worker friend testified on Peter's behalf that Peter has a reputation at the harbor for complete honesty. After a sidebar conference at which the prosecutor showed the judge a record indicating that Peter had been suspended from a regional fishing cooperative for falsifying vessel registration records, the prosecutor proposed to ask the dock worker on cross-examination: "Have you ever heard that Peter falsified vessel registration records for his cooperative?" Is the prosecutor's question proper?

---

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A defendant charged with federal fraud called a reputation character witness who testified the defendant is known for complete honesty. The prosecutor has a good-faith basis (a suspension record) to ask the witness: "Have you heard the defendant falsified records at a prior job?" May the prosecutor ask it?

## 6. Right-Answer Explanation (A)

**Gold Key:** Under FRE 405(a), when a defendant calls a character (reputation) witness, the prosecutor may cross-examine that witness with "have you heard" questions about specific bad acts by the defendant. The sole permissible purpose is to test the witness's actual knowledge of the defendant's reputation — not to prove character conformity or guilt.

A is correct. The dock worker testified that Peter has a reputation for honesty. By asking "Have you ever heard that Peter falsified vessel registration records?", the prosecutor is testing whether the dock worker truly knows Peter's reputation at the harbor. If the witness hasn't heard about the falsification, that weakens confidence in the witness's familiarity with the community. If the witness answers "yes," the jury may infer the harbor community sets a low honesty standard. Either way, the question probes the witness's credibility and knowledge — the proper purpose under FRE 405(a). The sidebar record of Peter's suspension supplies the required good-faith basis to ask.

**Silver Key:** Two choices say "Yes, the question is proper." Lock on the one that names the legally permissible purpose: testing the *witness's* knowledge. That purpose points directly to A.

## 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B — No, hearsay**

The hearsay trap runs on reflex: the question involves a prior act, and "hearsay" feels like a safe blocker for anything involving out-of-court facts. But hearsay requires a statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted. The question "Have you ever heard that Peter falsified records?" is not offered for the truth of the falsification — it is offered to test what the witness knows about the defendant's reputation. No matter how the witness answers, the value is in revealing the witness's knowledge (or ignorance). That is not a truth-of-the-matter purpose, so hearsay is off the table.

**C — No, 403 balancing**

This choice performs the right balancing test on the wrong purpose. It frames the evidence as being offered to prove income-tax-style catch fraud — then argues that its relevance to *that* purpose is low and the prejudice is high. But the evidence isn't offered for the crime charged. It is offered to probe the dock worker's knowledge of the community's view of Peter. Once the purpose is correctly identified, the 403 calculus doesn't come out the way C describes.

**D — Yes, but for guilt through character conformity (Dominant Trap)**

D reaches the right conclusion ("Yes, proper") but for an impermissible reason. **Gold Key:** FRE 405(a) expressly limits proof of character by specific instances to cross-examination of character witnesses — and only for the purpose of testing the witness's knowledge, not proving character conformity. Saying the question is proper because it shows Peter is dishonest "and therefore guilty" restates exactly what FRE 405(a) forbids in this posture. The question is proper, but not for D's stated reason.

## 8. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Tested rule:** FRE 404(a)(2)(A) permits a criminal defendant to offer evidence of a pertinent character trait; once offered, the prosecution may rebut. FRE 405(a) governs the method of proof: character may be shown by reputation or opinion testimony, and on cross-examination the witness may be questioned about relevant specific instances of conduct. The cross-examiner must have a good-faith belief that the specific instance occurred. The purpose is to impeach the character witness's credibility — not to prove the defendant's bad character as substantive evidence of guilt.

**Authority:** FRE 404(a)(2)(A); FRE 405(a). The good-faith foundation requirement for cross-examining character witnesses on specific acts is a well-established common-law requirement codified in bar-exam doctrine and confirmed by the Advisory Committee Notes to FRE 405.

**Credited outcome unchanged:** The question is proper. The proper basis is impeaching the character witness (testing the dock worker's knowledge of Peter's actual reputation), which is what A states. C3's residual agrees with the official key. No key disagreement.
