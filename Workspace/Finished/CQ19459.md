### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** Pinkerton liability does not make every conspirator answer for every side crime committed by another conspirator. The co-conspirator offense must be within the scope of the unlawful project, committed in furtherance of it, and reasonably foreseeable as a natural or probable consequence of the agreement.

Here, the agreement was a voucher-fraud scheme. Barnabas's parking-lot punch was a personal detour. It did not obtain catering credits, conceal the forged vouchers, pressure a witness, collect proceeds, or advance the plan in any other way. The stem also tells us the assault was not reasonably foreseeable from the voucher agreement. That breaks Pinkerton liability.

**Gold Key:** Pinkerton is not "conspiracy membership plus any later crime." The later crime must be in furtherance of the conspiracy and reasonably foreseeable from the agreement.

**Silver Key:** In a Pinkerton answer set, do not stop at "still in the conspiracy." Treat membership as the entry ticket, then ask the three limiter questions: scope, furtherance, foreseeability.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A** tempts the student who turns Pinkerton into an automatic transfer rule. The choice sounds decisive because Barnabas was a conspirator and committed a crime while the voucher conspiracy was still alive. The breaker is the word **every**. Pinkerton does not make all later crimes automatically foreseeable; it requires a crime connected to the unlawful project and reasonably foreseeable from it.

**B** is the dominant trap. It uses a true fact: Mary had not withdrawn before Barnabas punched the driver. But that fact only keeps Mary inside the conspiracy. It does not prove that Barnabas's personal assault was within the scheme's scope, in furtherance of it, or foreseeable. **Gold Key:** continued membership is necessary for Pinkerton liability, but it is not enough.

**C** sells the right outcome through the wrong limiter. The voucher conspiracy was nonviolent, and the assault was violent, so the answer feels clean. But Pinkerton does not use a categorical "nonviolent conspiracy" exception. A nonviolent conspiracy can still make a later violent act foreseeable in some settings, such as force used to collect proceeds or silence a witness. This assault fails because it was a personal detour outside the plan and not foreseeable.

### 8. Full Black-Letter Verification and Legal Reasoning

The governing authority is **Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640 (1946)**. Pinkerton permits vicarious liability for a substantive offense committed by a co-conspirator when the defendant was still a member of the conspiracy and the offense was committed in furtherance of the unlawful project. The Supreme Court also described the limiting principle: a different result arises when the later offense is not done in furtherance of the conspiracy, does not fall within the scope of the unlawful project, or could not be reasonably foreseen as a necessary or natural consequence of the unlawful agreement. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/328/640.

The DOJ Criminal Resource Manual's archived Pinkerton discussion states the same exam-relevant limits: Pinkerton is narrower than aiding and abetting, and liability does not extend to offenses outside the conspiracy's scope, not in furtherance of it, or not reasonably foreseeable. See https://www.justice.gov/archives/jm/criminal-resource-manual-2482-pinkerton-vs-aiding-and-abetting.
