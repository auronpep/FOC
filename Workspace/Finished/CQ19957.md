## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Peter was attending a fellowship social in the church hall when he noticed a decorative leather Bible cover left on a folding chair. Tucked inside the cover, Peter found $100 in cash and a small card with the owner’s name and phone number. Peter immediately pocketed the cash, intending to keep it, and placed the Bible cover in the church’s lost-and-found bin without the money.

Is Peter likely guilty of larceny of the cash?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A person finds lost property containing cash and a card identifying the owner. The finder immediately decides to keep the cash and dispose of the other items. Is the finder guilty of larceny?

### 3. Final Answer Choices

(A) No, because currency is not considered personal property for the purposes of common-law larceny.

(B) Yes, because the Bible cover contained clear clues to the owner’s identity and Peter intended to keep the cash when he took it.

(C) No, unless Peter had first entered into a formal agreement with the owner to return any lost items he might find.

(D) No, because lost property belongs to the person who finds it unless the true owner makes a claim within twenty-four hours.

### 5. Correct Answer

B (original key A).

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

(B) is correct. Larceny is the trespassory taking and carrying away of the personal property of another with the intent to permanently deprive the owner of the property. When a person finds lost property, a trespassory taking occurs if (1) the finder intends to steal the property at the time of the find, and (2) there are reasonable clues to the owner's identity. Here, Peter found the Bible cover and immediately formed the intent to keep the $100. Because the cover contained a card with the owner's name and phone number, there were clear clues to the owner's identity. Thus, the elements of larceny are satisfied. Gold Key: Larceny of lost property requires both the intent to steal at the moment of finding and reasonable clues to the owner's identity.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

(A) This choice is attractive because it suggests that cash might be treated differently than other physical goods. However, it is incorrect because "personal property" includes money and currency for the purposes of larceny.

(C) This choice is attractive because it suggests a requirement of a pre-existing duty or contract (baiting the student with a quasi-contract or bailment concept). However, it is incorrect because larceny is a crime against possession that does not require any prior agreement or promise between the thief and the owner.

(D) This choice is attractive because it appeals to the common "finders-keepers" misconception and adds a plausible-sounding but fabricated time limit. However, it is incorrect because the law does not grant ownership of lost property to a finder simply by virtue of discovery when the owner is identifiable.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: common-law larceny of lost property. At common law, a finder of lost property commits larceny if, at the time of the finding, he intends to permanently deprive the owner of the property, provided that he then knows who the owner is or has reasonable clues to the owner's identity. Authority: *Regina v. Thurborn* (1849); *Brooks v. State*, 35 Ohio St. 46 (1878). In this case, Peter formed the intent to keep the cash immediately upon discovery and had a clear clue to the owner's identity (the card with the name and phone number). The outcome (Yes) is consistent with the source item.
