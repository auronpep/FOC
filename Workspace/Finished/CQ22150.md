### 7. Full right-answer explanation

A is the strongest argument. Timothy described a startling event, and the short delay plus the injury context gives the prosecutor a plausible argument that he remained under the stress of the event when Lydia asked what happened. Gold Key: under FRE 803(2), an excited utterance may be admitted even though it is hearsay if it relates to a startling event and is made while the declarant is still under the stress of that event. Silver Key: when all four choices are hearsay labels, pick the label whose timing rule best matches the stem's stress facts, not the label that merely sounds close to the words spoken.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

C is attractive because Timothy was reporting something he had just experienced. The breaker is timing: a present sense impression requires a statement made while perceiving the event or immediately after perceiving it, and this statement came after the event, after Lydia returned, and after Timothy had been taken home.

D is attractive because the statement was prompted by visible marks on Timothy's body. The breaker is content and purpose: Timothy identified the cause and actor, not merely his present pain or bodily condition, and the statement was not made for medical diagnosis or treatment.

B is attractive because Timothy named Miriam. The breaker is that the identification label does not supply a freestanding hearsay exception for this out-of-court accusation in this setting.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is unchanged: the prosecutor needs a hearsay exception for an out-of-court statement offered to prove who caused the injury. Federal Rule of Evidence 803(2) admits an excited utterance: a statement relating to a startling event or condition, made while the declarant remains under the stress of excitement caused by it. Federal Rule of Evidence 803(1) covers statements made while or immediately after perceiving the event, and FRE 803(3) covers then-existing condition but excludes memory or belief offered to prove the remembered fact. FRE 803(4) is the medical diagnosis or treatment exception. The credited answer is still the excited-utterance argument. Authority: FRE 803(1)-(4), available from Legal Information Institute, https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_803.
