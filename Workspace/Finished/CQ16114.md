## 7. Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

Paul intentionally encouraged Hannah to steal by demanding money while she stood before the open cash drawer and then watching her take it. Under accomplice liability, one who incites or encourages another to commit a crime, with the intent that the crime be committed, is guilty as an accomplice to that crime — here, larceny. **Silver Key:** When the answer array names four distinct theft crimes, lock the call first: "of which crime is the defendant guilty" is an identification question, not a comparison. Ask: which crime's elements match the defendant's own acts? Paul's acts — demanding, inciting, watching the taking — match the accomplice path to larceny. The cashier performed the trespassory taking; Paul's intentional encouragement at the scene makes him a principal in the second degree / accomplice.

**Gold Key:** Accomplice liability requires (1) intent to assist or encourage, (2) intent that the crime be committed, and (3) actual assistance or encouragement. Mere knowledge of a crime or mere presence is insufficient — but demanding the thief act while standing at the open cash drawer is active encouragement, not passive presence.

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A — Embezzlement.** The attraction: Paul is an employee who had some relationship to camp funds, so a student might think he "embezzled" by failing to report. The breaker: embezzlement requires lawful possession of the property that is later fraudulently converted. Paul never had lawful possession of the $50 — Hannah took it from the box and handed it to him. The crime he actually committed (accomplice to larceny) does not require prior lawful possession. **Gold Key:** Embezzlement is the fraudulent conversion of property already in the defendant's lawful possession. If the defendant never had lawful possession, the crime cannot be embezzlement — it is larceny (or accomplice to larceny) instead.

**B — Robbery (Dominant Trap).** The attraction: Paul threatened Hannah — "tell the director" — and students often default to robbery when they see a threat used to obtain money. The breaker: robbery requires larceny committed by force or threat of *immediate physical force*. The threat to tell the director is economic or reputational, not physical. The underlying taking is larceny, accomplished through incitement and encouragement, but the threat does not elevate it to robbery.

**D — Fraud.** The attraction: Paul withheld information (he knew Hannah had borrowed money) and used that to get money — that sounds like fraud's "deception" element to a student who hasn't distinguished the precise elements. The breaker: fraud (false pretenses) requires the owner to part with property in reliance on a false representation of material fact. Here, the camp director (the owner) never made any decision about the money — the taking happened by Hannah reaching into the drawer. No misrepresentation induced any owner to part with anything; the transaction was a threat-based extraction, not a deception.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Authority:** Common law accomplice liability (also codified in modern penal codes; see e.g., MPC § 2.06). A person is guilty of an offense if it is committed by another person and the person intentionally aids, solicits, commands, or encourages the other in planning or committing it. The accomplice must have the dual intent: the intent to assist/encourage and the intent that the underlying crime be committed.

Here, Paul intended to obtain $50 — his demand and presence while Hannah took the money was active encouragement of the theft. Hannah committed the larceny (trespassory taking and carrying away of personal property of another with intent to permanently deprive). Paul is liable as an accomplice to larceny.

**Robbery:** Larceny plus force or threat of immediate force. Common law robbery requires the force to be either actual violence or putting the victim in fear of immediate bodily harm. Threatening to tell the boss/director is not threat of force. *See, e.g., State v. Gantt* (distinguishing larceny from robbery by force element).

**Embezzlement:** The fraudulent conversion of property already in the defendant's lawful possession. Because Paul never had lawful possession of the $50 before the taking, embezzlement is inapplicable.

**Fraud (False Pretenses):** Obtaining title to property of another through a false representation of material fact, made with intent to defraud, on which the victim relies. No false representation was made to the camp director, and the director did not part with title in reliance.

#### choice_by_choice_walkthrough

**Choice A — Embezzlement**

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** "Embezzlement is when someone who already has lawful possession of property keeps or sells it for themselves. Paul never had the $50 before Hannah took it from the box — she handed it to him. He never lawfully possessed it. Embezzlement can't happen if the defendant never had possession."

**(2) Student label:** NOT_TRUE — the defendant never had lawful possession, which is the foundational element of embezzlement.

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Embezzlement, because Paul was entrusted with the camp's registration fees and fraudulently converted $50 to his own use." But the facts say Hannah, not Paul, had access to the box.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** Embezzlement is the fraudulent conversion of property already in the defendant's lawful possession. A person who receives stolen money from the thief's hand never had lawful possession — the conversion occurs at the taking, not after. MPC § 223.2(2) distinguishes theft by unlawful taking (larceny) from embezzlement by the possession element. Paul is an accomplice to larceny, not an embezzler.

---

**Choice B — Robbery (Dominant Trap)**

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** "Robbery is larceny plus force or threat of immediate physical harm. Paul threatened to 'tell the director.' That is a threat to his reputation or job, not a threat of hitting her or using a weapon. Without threat of physical force, robbery doesn't work."

**(2) Student label:** NOT_TRUE — the threat was not of immediate physical force.

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Robbery, because Paul threatened Hannah with immediate physical harm if she did not give him the money." But the threat was to tell the director, not to use force.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** Robbery at common law and under MPC § 222.1 requires larceny plus force or threat of immediate serious bodily injury. Non-physical threats (to disclose information, to report misconduct, to fire someone) do not satisfy the force element. Such threats may constitute extortion or blackmail, but not robbery.

---

**Choice C — Larceny (Correct)**

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** "Larceny is taking someone else's property without permission and intending to keep it. Paul didn't physically take the money himself, but he demanded that Hannah take it while she stood at the open drawer and watched her do it. In criminal law, if you intentionally encourage someone to steal and they do it, you are an accomplice — just as guilty as the person who physically stole it."

**(2) Student label:** CORRECT — Paul intentionally encouraged Hannah to steal by demanding the money and watching as she took it.

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the correct answer.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** Accomplice liability under common law and MPC § 2.06: one who intentionally aids, solicits, commands, or encourages another to commit an offense is guilty of that offense. Paul's demand and presence while Hannah took the $50 constitutes actus reus (encouragement) and mens rea (dual intent: intent to encourage + intent the larceny be committed). Hannah's taking satisfies all elements of larceny: trespassory taking and carrying away of personal property (the camp's $50) with intent to permanently deprive.

---

**Choice D — Fraud**

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** "Fraud requires a lie that makes the owner willingly hand over property. Here, Paul didn't lie to the camp director — the director never even knew about this transaction. Hannah took the money from the drawer because Paul threatened her, not because anyone believed a false statement."

**(2) Student label:** NOT_RESPONSIVE — the doctrine is a misfit; no misrepresentation induced the owner's reliance.

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Fraud, because Paul falsely represented to the camp director that the registration records were accurate, inducing the director to leave the box unsecured." But no such representation occurred.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** False pretenses (fraud/theft by deception) requires (1) a false representation of material fact, (2) made with intent to defraud, (3) that causes the victim to pass title to property, (4) in reliance on the misrepresentation. No false statement was made to anyone with authority over the camp's money. The director never parted with property in reliance. MPC § 223.3 (theft by deception) requires that the deception operate on the person from whom the property is obtained.

#### remediation_card

**Title:** Accomplice Liability — Intentional Encouragement
**Signal:** Defendant demands, incites, or watches another commit a theft
**Student move:** Check: did the defendant intentionally encourage the underlying crime with intent it be committed? If yes, defendant is guilty as an accomplice.
**Tiny rule:** Intentional encouragement + intent crime be committed + actual encouragement = accomplice
**Trap:** Thinking the defendant is not guilty because someone else physically took the property
**Confidence:** HARD_STRUCTURAL

## Gold Key(s)

**GK-CRIM-ACCOMPLICE-01** (rule): Accomplice liability requires (1) intent to assist or encourage, (2) intent that the crime be committed, and (3) actual assistance or encouragement. Mere knowledge of a crime or mere presence is insufficient.

**GK-CRIM-EMBEZZLEMENT-01** (distinction): Embezzlement is the fraudulent conversion of property already in the defendant's lawful possession. If the defendant never had lawful possession, the crime is not embezzlement.

**GK-CRIM-ROBBERY-01** (distinction): Robbery requires force or threat of IMMEDIATE PHYSICAL force. A threat to tell the boss, report misconduct, or cause reputational harm is NOT a threat of force.

**GK-CRIM-FRAUD-01** (rule): False pretenses requires: (1) a false representation of material fact, (2) intent to defraud, (3) the victim relies and parts with title. Without owner reliance on a misrepresentation, there is no fraud.

## Silver Key(s)

**SK-CRIM-THEFT-ID-01** (call_focus): When the answer array names four distinct theft crimes, lock the call first: 'of which crime is the defendant guilty' is an identification question, not a comparison. Ask: which crime's elements match the defendant's own acts?

**SK-CRIM-CLASH-ROBBERY-01** (clash): When robbery and larceny both appear and the taking involved a threat, ask: 'Was the threat one of immediate physical harm?' If no — it is not robbery.

**SK-CRIM-CUT-EMBEZZLEMENT-01** (cut): CUT embezzlement immediately by asking: 'Did the defendant ever lawfully possess the property before the taking?' If the defendant received it from someone else's hands, the answer is no — CUT.
