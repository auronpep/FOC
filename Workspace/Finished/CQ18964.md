## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** The officer lawfully stopped Daniel for a traffic infraction (missing rear reflector). During that stop, Daniel repeatedly reached under the seat — a furtive movement suggesting weapon access — and the officer saw a fixed-blade knife sheath bolted to the fender. Together, these facts gave the officer a reasonable suspicion that Daniel was dangerous and could gain immediate control of a weapon. Under *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032 (1983), extending *Terry v. Ohio*, an officer who reasonably suspects danger may conduct a limited protective search of the area where a weapon could be hidden and accessible. The search was confined to under the seat — the exact area Daniel was reaching toward — and did not extend to cargo areas or toolboxes. The scope was properly limited to the protective purpose.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. "No, because Terry frisks apply only to clothing, never vehicles."**
This choice sells the misconception that *Terry* is limited to pat-downs of a person's outer clothing. That's the classic Terry frisk, but the Supreme Court extended the protective-search principle to vehicle passenger compartments in *Michigan v. Long*. The rule isn't "clothing only" — it's "areas where a weapon could be hidden and the suspect could reach." A student who knows *Long* cuts this immediately.

**B. "Yes, because any traffic stop permits a full vehicle search."**
This is the overclaim trap. It sounds right if you're thinking about the broad authority officers have during stops, but it's too broad. Not every traffic stop justifies a full vehicle search. A full search requires either consent, the automobile exception (probable cause of contraband), or — as here — a limited protective search based on reasonable suspicion of danger. The "any stop = full search" framing is the tiered_absolute mold: it takes a real power and pushes it to an unjustified extreme.

**C. "No, because the officer needed probable cause to believe the tractor contained contraband."** ⚠️ DOMINANT TRAP
This is the misfit trap. It confuses two different Fourth Amendment doctrines. The automobile exception (*Carroll v. United States*) lets officers search a vehicle without a warrant if they have probable cause to believe it contains contraband. But this wasn't an automobile-exception search — it was a *Terry* protective search for weapons based on reasonable suspicion of danger. The officer didn't need probable cause of contraband; reasonable suspicion that the suspect was dangerous and could access a weapon was enough. Students who conflate these two doctrines pick this answer.

## Wrong-Answer Recovery Paths

**If a student picked A:** They believe *Terry* is limited to clothing pat-downs. Remediation: Review *Michigan v. Long* — the Court explicitly extended protective searches to vehicle compartments. The rule is "areas where a weapon could be hidden and accessible," not "clothing only."

**If a student picked B:** They overgeneralized from the traffic-stop context. Remediation: Not every traffic stop justifies a full search. The scope of a protective search is limited to the safety concern that justified it. "Any" and "full" are almost always wrong on the MBE.

**If a student picked C (dominant trap):** They confused two Fourth Amendment doctrines. Remediation: Distinguish *Terry* (reasonable suspicion + safety + weapons) from the automobile exception (probable cause + evidence + contraband). The facts here show danger indicators (reaching + sheath), not contraband indicators. The standard is reasonable suspicion, not probable cause.
