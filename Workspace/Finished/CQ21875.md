### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

A is correct. The porch is part of the home's curtilage, and the officers did more than approach to knock. They brought a trained narcotics dog onto the porch to investigate the home for evidence. Gold Key: under Florida v. Jardines, using a trained detection dog on a home's front porch to investigate the home is a Fourth Amendment search. Silver Key: separate the ordinary visitor license from the investigative tool; the public may walk to the door to knock, but that does not authorize a porch evidence search.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

B is attractive because it looks for a more obvious physical intrusion, like opening the door. The breaker is that the protected area includes the curtilage, and the search occurs when officers physically occupy that area for an evidence-gathering dog sniff.

C sells a familiar dog-sniff shortcut from luggage or vehicle cases. The breaker is the home-curtilage setting: a blanket "never" rule cannot survive when officers bring the dog to the front porch to investigate the home.

D uses a true starting point: ordinary visitors, including officers, may approach a front door to knock. The Silver Key steers off the trap: the implied license is purpose-limited, and bringing a trained dog to conduct an evidence search exceeds that license.

### 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. The item tests the Fourth Amendment search threshold for a dog sniff on the home's curtilage. A front porch is part of the home's curtilage. Officers may generally approach a front door to knock, but that implied license does not permit them to enter the curtilage with a trained detection dog for the purpose of investigating the home for evidence.

Authority floor: Florida v. Jardines, 569 U.S. 1 (2013), held that the government's use of a trained police dog to investigate a home and its immediate surroundings is a Fourth Amendment search. The Court reasoned that the front porch is a classic curtilage area and that the ordinary knock-and-talk license does not extend to entering the curtilage to conduct a search. The Fourth Amendment itself supplies the constitutional provision, and Jardines confirms the source key: the porch dog sniff was a search even though officers did not open the door.
