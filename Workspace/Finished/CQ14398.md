### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**D is correct.** Hannah should invoke unconscionability (both procedural and substantive). At the time she agreed to the harvest contract, Hannah was in desperate financial straits—widowed, facing high family medical bills and a binding commitment to fund a short-term mission trip—while holding only land rights and possessing little knowledge of current timber values. Simon, a sophisticated commercial operator with a large integrated enterprise, presented the only offer at a cash price 70% below the regionally prevailing rate for comparable rights. Hannah effectively had no meaningful bargaining power and no realistic ability to walk away or negotiate. This combination of procedural unfairness in formation (desperation + ignorance + lack of power + surprise) and substantive unfairness in the terms (grossly inadequate price creating oppression) renders the contract unconscionable. **Gold Key:** Unconscionability under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 208 requires both (1) procedural unconscionability (absence of meaningful choice due to bargaining-power disparity, desperation, or unfair surprise) and (2) substantive unconscionability (terms so one-sided as to be oppressive or unfairly surprising, such as a 70% price disparity). Courts may refuse enforcement of the unconscionable contract or term.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Bad faith.**

This is the dominant trap (highest wrong-answer selection in the source data). It feels attractive because the price is so obviously one-sided and "unfair" that students reach for a moral or good-faith label. The breaker is that a low price alone, even a very low price, does not establish bad faith. Bad faith in the performance or enforcement of contracts typically requires affirmative misconduct, deceit, or unreasonable commercial behavior beyond merely driving a hard bargain. Simon did nothing deceptive or oppressive in the negotiation process itself; he simply made the only offer Hannah received. Without more (e.g., concealment of value, false statements, or coercive tactics), a claim of bad faith fails. **Silver Key:** When the stem shows only a lowball offer from the sole responder and no affirmative misconduct, "bad faith" is usually a distractor for the real formation defect—unconscionability.

**B. Equitable estoppel.**

This choice is attractive because estoppel is a familiar equitable safety valve and the facts involve a later change of heart after "acceptance." The breaker is that equitable estoppel requires (1) a clear promise or representation by the party to be estopped, (2) reasonable reliance by the other party, and (3) detriment from that reliance. Simon made no promise or representation that Hannah relied on to her detriment; he simply received her acceptance of his offer. Hannah's own change of circumstances after signing does not create an estoppel against Simon. The doctrine does not rescue a party from a bad bargain she made with open eyes (even if desperate).

**C. Duress.**

This is a classic formation defense that students reach for when one party is under financial pressure. The breaker is that duress requires a wrongful act or threat by the other party that overcomes the victim's free will. Simon made no threat, engaged in no wrongful conduct, and applied no improper pressure beyond making a low offer. Hannah's desperation arose from her own medical bills, family obligations, and mission pledge—not from any act by Simon. Economic pressure or unequal bargaining power alone is not duress; it is evidence that may support an unconscionability claim instead.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 14398, a Contracts item on defenses to enforceability, specifically unconscionability (procedural + substantive) in a one-sided commodity-rights transaction with a desperate, uninformed seller and a sophisticated buyer offering a grossly inadequate price. The official key is C (unconscionability).

The governing authority is Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 208: "If a contract or term thereof is unconscionable at the time the contract is made a court may refuse to enforce the contract, or may enforce the remainder of the contract without the unconscionable term, or may so limit the application of any unconscionable term as to avoid any unconscionable result." Comment 1 explains that the determination is made at the time the contract is made and that unconscionability has both procedural and substantive aspects. Procedural unconscionability focuses on the bargaining process—absence of meaningful choice, unfair surprise, or oppression arising from unequal bargaining power, lack of knowledge, or desperation. Substantive unconscionability focuses on the terms themselves—whether they are so one-sided, oppressive, or unfairly surprising as to shock the conscience (classically shown by extreme price disparity).

Courts and the MBE routinely require a showing of both elements for a successful unconscionability defense, though the relative strength of one can compensate for weakness in the other. The facts here present a textbook illustration: Hannah's desperation (medical bills + mission commitment), ignorance of timber values, and lack of any other offers created a complete absence of meaningful choice (procedural). The 70% below-market cash price on a take-it-or-leave basis created the substantive oppression. The outcome is identical to the original: unconscionability affords the best prospect of cancellation before performance begins. (Note: because the subject matter is standing timber to be severed and removed, UCC § 2-302 supplies a parallel unconscionability rule for transactions in goods in many jurisdictions; the common-law analysis under the Restatement is sufficient and matches the source explanation.)

No research contradicts the supplied official key. The doctrine remains current; no hot-list doctrinal shift (e.g., Dobbs, Bruen, or FRE amendments) affects this classic contracts defense.

**review_truth**  
A contract is unconscionable, and may be avoided by the adversely affected party, when there is both procedural unconscionability in formation (desperation, ignorance, and absence of meaningful choice) and substantive unconscionability in the terms (grossly inadequate price creating oppression or unfair surprise).

## Gold Key(s)

**Gold Key: GK-CONTRACTS-UNCON-01**  
Unconscionability requires BOTH procedural unconscionability (absence of meaningful choice due to desperation, ignorance, or bargaining-power disparity) AND substantive unconscionability (grossly unfair or oppressive terms, classically shown by extreme price disparity).  
Trigger: stem shows a desperate, uninformed seller accepting a sole 70%-below-market offer from a sophisticated buyer.  
Tested choice: A (bad faith).  
Last-minute review: When you see desperation + ignorance + sole offer + huge price gap, test both prongs before picking bad faith, duress, or estoppel.

## Silver Key(s)

**Silver Key: SK-CONTRACTS-UNCON-01 (trap_spotting)**  
When the stem shows only a lowball offer from the sole responder and no affirmative misconduct, "bad faith" is usually a distractor for the real formation defect—unconscionability.  
Trigger: moral outrage at a grossly low price with no lies, threats, or concealment alleged.  
Tested choice: A.  
Last-minute review: Low price alone is not bad faith; look for the two prongs of unconscionability instead.
