## Full Right-Answer Explanation

A is correct. **Silver Key:** Spot the unilateral-mistake trigger: the non-mistaken party's knowledge. A contract may be rescinded on grounds of unilateral mistake if the mistake relates to a fundamental assumption of the contract that has a material effect on the exchange, the party asserting mistake should not bear the risk of the mistake, **and** either (i) the non-mistaken party has reason to know of the mistake, or (ii) the effect of the mistake would make enforcement of the contract unconscionable. Here, Esther discovered a $3,000 computational error in her tractor valuation. If Barnabas had reason to suspect this error before relying on the offer, Esther's defense is greatly supported because this directly satisfies the "reason to know" prong of the unilateral mistake test. The error goes to a fundamental assumption (the tractor's value), is material ($3,000 is significant), and Esther should not bear the risk of Barnabas's potential knowledge. This is a C3_SOLVE item once the student recognizes that knowledge of the mistake by the non-mistaken party is the decisive axis.

## Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B is incorrect.** This choice attracts students who focus on contract formation formalities. The argument that Esther's offer lacked consideration for being irrevocable is **legally_true_but_irrelevant**. Under the unilateral mistake doctrine, the absence of consideration for a firm offer does not defeat a mistake defense. Courts may find a bid irrevocable on a reliance theory even without separate consideration for an option promise. The issue is Esther's computational error, not the enforceability of her offer absent mistake. **Diagnostic:** This is a classic bait_doctrine move — it pulls students into the wrong contractual neighborhood (formation) instead of focusing on the defense (mistake).

**C is incorrect.** This choice appeals to students who fixate on the offer's terms. The fact that Esther's text message requested acceptance within 10 days **does not weaken her mistake defense**. This is simply a term of the offer and has no bearing on whether Esther can rescind due to her unilateral computational error. **Diagnostic:** This is the **dominant trap** — a wrong_element distraction that looks like it matters (offer terms) but is completely irrelevant to whether Barnabas knew or should have known of the mistake. It earned the highest wrong-answer pick rate (12%) in the original because students over-value temporal conditions in contract problems.

**D is incorrect.** This choice tempts students with economic reasoning. The fact that Barnabas's farm expansion remains profitable after paying $28,000 **does not address the unilateral mistake test**. Profitability goes to damages or mitigation, not to whether Esther's mistake defense is valid. The unconscionability prong of unilateral mistake requires more than mere economic hardship — it requires enforcement that would be oppressive or shocking. **Diagnostic:** This is a classic wrong_element play — proving a fact that doesn't dispose of the issue. Students trained on efficiency arguments may find this appealing, but it misses the doctrinal point entirely.

## Full Black-Letter-Law Verification AND Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 155):**

A contract may be rescinded on grounds of unilateral mistake if:
1. The mistake relates to a **fundamental assumption** on which the contract is made;
2. The mistake has a **material effect** on the agreed exchange;
3. The party seeking relief (Esther) is **not at fault** and should not bear the risk of the mistake;
4. **AND** either:
   - (i) The other party (Barnabas) **had reason to know** of the mistake, OR
   - (ii) The effect of the mistake is such that **enforcement would be unconscionable**.

**Application:** Esther's $3,000 computational error in valuing her tractor goes to a fundamental assumption (the asset's worth) and has a material effect ($3,000 on a $25,000 transaction = 12%). Esther is not at fault — she discovered the error and promptly revoked. The decisive question is whether Barnabas had reason to know of the mistake or whether enforcement would be unconscionable.

**Original Q14387:** The wallpaper hanger's $4,000 error in computing his sub-bid. The general contractor used the figure in her own sealed bid. The wallpaper hanger revoked before bids were opened. Contractor sues for $4,000 difference after hiring another at $18,000.

**This Variation:** Esther's $3,000 error in tractor valuation. Barnabas used the figure for bank financing. Esther revoked before financing was finalized. Barnabas sues for $3,000 difference after buying another at $28,000.

**Rule Verification:** The tested rule is **IDENTICAL** — Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 155 on unilateral mistake. The credited answer (D in original → A here) turns on the same legal principle: whether the non-mistaken party had reason to know of the mistake. The outcome is unchanged. The doctrine is current and uncontroversial.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 155 (Unilateral Mistake); see also Lenawee County Bd. of Health v. Messerly, 331 Mich. 293 (1951) (classic unilateral mistake case where bidder's computational error known to offeree supported rescission).
