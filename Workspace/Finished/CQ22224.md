While walking between the prayer hall and the lodging cabins at a large regional Christian retreat, the plaintiff was struck by a ministry shuttle van and severely injured. In a subsequent negligence action against the retreat organizer, the plaintiff asserted that there was a dangerous blind bend on the internal service road just south of the place where the accident occurred, and that the organizer was negligent in failing to post signs warning pedestrians and drivers of the danger. 7. Full right-answer explanation

`C` is right because the defense laid the missing foundation that makes this kind of negative evidence relevant. `Gold Key:` testimony that no prior accidents were known is admissible only if the proponent shows substantially similar conditions and a witness or reporting source likely would have known about prior accidents if they had happened. Here, the road was substantially unchanged, it carried heavy traffic, and Lydia's job brought all reported incidents to her. `Silver Key:` when the array splits between one answer naming a foundation fact and other answers attacking jury weight or courtroom instructions, admissibility turns on foundation first. Once that foundation is laid, the evidence comes in and the jury decides how much to trust it.

8. Full wrong-answer explanations

`A` sells the student who sees the weakness in negative evidence and converts that weakness into a bar to admissibility. The breaker is that the possibility of missed reports is a weight argument for cross-examination and closing, not a reason to exclude the testimony once the road was shown to be unchanged and the witness was shown to be in a position likely to know of prior incidents.

`B` sells the student who remembers that "negative evidence" can be risky and turns that caution into an absolute rule. `Gold Key:` there is no blanket rule that this evidence is always inadmissible. The foundation problem is what matters, and the foundation was supplied here.

`D` sells the student who latches onto the judge's instruction because the stem spotlights it. `Silver Key:` a jury instruction about how to handle a type of evidence does not create the admissibility foundation for that evidence. The instruction concerns how jurors should weigh the proof, not whether the missing predicate for admission exists.

9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited outcome is unchanged from the source item: the testimony should be admitted. Under `FRE 401` and `FRE 402`, evidence is admissible if it tends to make a consequential fact more or less probable, unless another rule excludes it. Under `FRE 602`, a witness may testify only if there is enough evidence to support a finding that the witness has personal knowledge of the matter. Evidence that no prior accidents occurred, sometimes described as negative evidence, is relevant to whether a place was dangerous, but only if the proponent lays a foundation showing substantially similar conditions and a witness or reporting source likely would have known of prior accidents if they had occurred. `D.L. v. Huebner`, `110 Wis. 2d 581, 622-23, 329 N.W.2d 890` (1983), states that negative evidence is admissible when the witness is in a position to testify from personal knowledge or experience that the event did not occur.

That foundation exists here. The witness testified that the road was substantially unchanged during her tenure, which answers the similarity problem. She also testified that the road was busy and that all reported incidents came to her attention because of her position, which supports a finding that if prior accidents had been reported, she likely would have known. The possibility that some accidents went unreported is a classic argument about the strength of the inference, not about admissibility. The special instruction does not supply the foundation; it only helps the jury weigh the evidence after admission. Authority: `FRE 401`, `FRE 402`, and `FRE 602` at Cornell LII (`https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_401`, `https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_402`, `https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_602`), plus `D.L. v. Huebner`, `110 Wis. 2d 581, 329 N.W.2d 890` (1983).
