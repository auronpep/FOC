# CQ14578 — Batson Equal Protection / Peremptory Challenge

---

## Pass‑1 Transform Report

### 1.1 Final Christian Variation

**Question**

A community organizer for a faith-based rescue mission led a peaceful prayer vigil on the steps of City Hall to protest the city council’s refusal to grant permits for a homeless shelter. When police ordered the group to disperse because the gathering blocked a public walkway, the organizer refused and was arrested for disorderly conduct. At trial, the prosecution used its peremptory challenges to remove every prospective juror who mentioned attending church regularly, arguing to the court that religious observers would automatically side with the organizer because of her faith-based mission. If the organizer is convicted by a jury without any regular churchgoers and appeals, claiming a violation of her constitutional rights, the court should

A. affirm the conviction, because the organizer was not a member of the class discriminated against.

B. affirm the conviction, because peremptory challenges of religious observers did not deny the organizer the right to an impartial jury.

C. reverse the conviction, because religiously based peremptory challenges violate equal protection of the law.

D. reverse the conviction, because the organizer was denied the right to have her case heard by a fair cross section of the community.

**Correct Answer: C**

### 1.5 Right‑Answer Explanation (C3)

**CUT → CLASH → CALL**

**CUT — A and B are out because they point toward “affirm.”**
- The call asks what the court “should” do on appeal after the prosecution removed every religious juror.
- If the removal violated an individual juror’s equal protection rights (violation of the Fourteenth Amendment), the conviction must be reversed.
- A and B both tell the court to *affirm* the conviction, which means they’re claiming the removal was permissible. That directly contradicts the Batson rule.
- **Structural move:** A choice that says “affirm the conviction” when the defendant complained of a constitutional violation must overcome a very high bar; you need a true legal principle that says the violation did *not* occur. Neither A nor B overcomes that bar.

**CLASH — C vs. D both say “reverse.” Now isolate the exact reason.**
- **C** grounds reversal in *equal protection* — the Batson principle that peremptory challenges may not be used to strike jurors based on group membership (race, religion, etc.).
- **D** grounds reversal in the *fair cross section* requirement — the Sixth Amendment/ jury-pool doctrine from Taylor v. Louisiana.
- The Batson protection is a settled, Equal Protection rule. If a prosecutor removes jurors because of their religion, that is a classic Batson violation. The “fair cross section” applies to the *venire* (the pool from which juries are drawn), not to the *petit jury* ultimately empaneled. See Taylor v. Louisiana, 419 U.S. 522 (1975). Therefore, **D** misapplies the doctrine.

**CALL — The governing principle is Batson/Powers Equal Protection.**
- Batson v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 79 (1986): peremptory challenges based on group status (originally race, now extended to other protected categories, including religion) violate the Equal Protection Clause.
- Powers v. Ohio, 499 U.S. 400 (1991): *any* defendant may raise a Batson challenge, not just defendants who share the excluded jurors’ group membership.
- Thus, the conviction must be reversed because religiously based peremptory challenges violate Equal Protection.

### 1.6 Wrong‑Answer Explanations (C3)

**A — "Affirm, because the organizer was not a member of the class discriminated against."**
- **failure_mode:** EAR_FALSITY / contradiction
- **why_wrong:** Powers v. Ohio held that a defendant *need not* share the excluded jurors’ race (or religion) to raise a Batson challenge. Standing to object is universal for defendants in criminal cases.
- **surface_attraction:** Students who remember “standing” issues in civil rights suits think the plaintiff/defendant must be in the protected class. Here, Batson/Powers gives the defendant standing regardless of membership.
- **method_class:** heuristic_structural (the “standing” intuition is a common trap, but Powers is a taught tiny anchor in modern CrimPro)

**B — "Affirm, because peremptory challenges of religious observers did not deny the organizer the right to an impartial jury."**
- **failure_mode:** EAR_DISTORTION / colloquialism
- **why_wrong:** It conflates “impartial jury” with “no violation.” Even if the resulting jury were arguably impartial, the Equal Protection Clause is violated by the *process* Fritz of striking jurors on group status. The harm flows from the discriminatory use of peremptories, not from the ultimate impartiality of the seated jurors.
- **surface_attraction:** Sounds intuitive — “if the jury is impartial, where’s the harm?” — but ignores that the Equal Protection Clause protects the *excluded jurors* and the integrity of the selection process, not just the defendant’s trial outcome.
- **method_class:** heuristic_structural (the colloquial “impartial jury” notion distracts from the structural Equal Protection point)

**D — "Reverse, because the organizer was denied the right to have her case heard by a fair cross section of the community."**
- **failure_mode:** ISSUE_SENSE / misfit (correct result, wrong doctrine)
- **why_wrong:** The fair-cross-section requirement applies to the *venire* (jury pool), not the petit jury. Peremptory challenges do not implicate the cross-section requirement; they implicate Equal Protection. C gets the doctrine right; D names the wrong constitutional provision and wrong stage.
- **surface_attraction:** “Fair cross section” is a familiar phrase from Sixth Amendment/Taylor v. Louisiana, so students who have learned the jury-pool doctrine try to force it into the peremptory-strike context.
- **method_class:** heuristic_structural (D is a classic doctrine-swap trap)

### 1.7 Black‑Letter Verification (Lawyer‑Confirmation Layer)

**Governing authority:** Batson v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 79 (1986); Powers v. Ohio, 499 U.S. 400 (1991).
- Batson held that a prosecutor’s peremptory challenge used to strike prospective jurors solely on account of race violated the Equal Protection Clause.
- Powers extended Batson to any criminal defendant — a defendant does not need to share the race of the excluded jurors to have standing to object.
- Later cases (e.g., J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B., 511 U.S. 127 (1994)) extended Batson to peremptory strikes on the basis of gender; lower courts and treatises (e.g., LaFave, Criminal Procedure § 21.4; Wright & King, Federal Practice and Procedure § 384) analogize religious strikes to Batson-type Equal Protection claims.
- The trial judge’s stated reason — “religious observers would automatically side with the organizer” — is the classic stereotype that Batson forbids. The strike is presumptively discriminatory and the burden would shift to the prosecution to articulate a race‑/religion‑neutral reason.
- **Fair cross section:** Taylor v. Louisiana, 419 U.S. 522 (1975) (Sixth Amendment requires jury *pool* to be drawn from a fair cross section of the community); Duren v. Missouri, 439 U.S. 357 (1979). The fair-cross-section requirement does *not* apply to the petit jury ultimately seated.

**Conclusion:** The credited answer (C) is correct.

### 1.10 Review Truth

A prosecutor may not use peremptory challenges to remove prospective jurors because of their race, religion, or other protected group status; any criminal defendant may object to such a strike under the Equal Protection Clause, regardless of whether the defendant belongs to the excluded group.
