### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is the best answer. Stephen's strongest defense is not that the robbery was gentle, not that Barnabas was a co-felon, and not that Stephen personally lacked an intent to kill. The strongest defense is that the fatal act was Lydia's justified defensive shot, not an act by Stephen or Barnabas. Gold Key: in agency-theory felony-murder jurisdictions, felony murder generally does not make a surviving felon guilty for a co-felon's death caused by a victim or police officer justifiably resisting the felony. Silver Key: in a "most effective argument" call, choose the answer that attacks the felony-murder link itself, not an answer that merely softens the facts.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells the no-violence agreement. Stephen did demand toy pistols and a promise against violence, but felony murder supplies malice from the intent to commit the predicate felony in jurisdictions that apply the rule. Gold Key: the missing link is not Stephen's subjective wish to avoid violence; the useful defense is that the fatal act came from the resisting victim.

B sells the co-felon-status shortcut. Barnabas was not the robbery victim, but victim status alone is not the felony-murder limit. A death during a felony can support felony murder even when the person killed was not the intended victim; the better argument focuses on who caused the death and why.

C sells the toy-gun foreseeability move. Toy pistols lower Stephen's moral blame, but pointing realistic-looking guns during a robbery predictably invites armed resistance. Silver Key: do not let a softer weapon fact distract from the fatal actor. The store owner's defensive shot, not the toy status alone, is the useful defense.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. Felony murder generally treats malice as supplied by the intent to commit an enumerated or inherently dangerous felony, and robbery is the classic predicate felony. Model Penal Code section 210.2(1)(b) reflects the modern felony-murder formulation by creating a rebuttable presumption of extreme indifference when a homicide occurs during robbery and other listed felonies. The harder issue is causation and agency when the person who kills is not a felon.

People v. Washington, 62 Cal. 2d 777 (1965), is a leading agency-theory authority: a robber was convicted after a robbery victim shot and killed the robber's accomplice, and the California Supreme Court reversed the murder conviction because the killing was committed by the victim, not by the defendant or an accomplice. Commonwealth v. Redline, 391 Pa. 486, 137 A.2d 472 (1958), is another leading co-felon-death authority limiting felony murder when the fatal shot comes from lawful resistance to the felony. Some jurisdictions use a proximate-cause theory and may reach a broader result, but on this MBE-style "most effective argument" call, the justified-victim-resistance argument is the only listed defense that directly attacks felony-murder liability for the co-felon's death.

Authority layer: Model Penal Code section 210.2(1)(b); People v. Washington, 62 Cal. 2d 777 (1965), https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/people-v-washington-24520; Commonwealth v. Redline, 391 Pa. 486, 137 A.2d 472 (1958).
