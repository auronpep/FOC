# CQ22730 — The Cedar Beams Trust

## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question — stem + call

Lydia is a timber merchant and a citizen of State B. She contracted to buy a load of cedar beams from a family trust that operated a sawmill in State A. The trust's trustee is Barnabas, a citizen of State C. When the beams arrived warped and unusable, Lydia sued Barnabas, as trustee, for breach of contract in federal court in State C, invoking diversity jurisdiction.

Barnabas was not home when the process server arrived, so the server left the summons and complaint with Barnabas's adult daughter at his residence, as permitted by the applicable Federal Rule of Civil Procedure. However, both the rule of State C, where the court sits, and the rule of State A, where the contract was performed, required that a trustee be served personally, in hand.

Barnabas moved for summary judgment, arguing that service was invalid because Lydia failed to personally serve him with the complaint. How should the court rule?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

In a diversity case, the method of serving process is governed by a Federal Rule that conflicts with a state rule demanding a stricter method. Which controls, and how does the court rule on a motion attacking the federal-rule service?

### 3. Final answer choices (A–D, post-shuffle)

**A.** Deny the motion, because the service rule is arguably procedural.

**B.** Grant the motion, because the federal court must apply the law of the state in which it is sitting.

**C.** Deny the motion, because granting summary judgment would affect Lydia's substantive rights.

**D.** Grant the motion, because the federal court must apply the law of the state in which the claim arose.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**A is correct.** A federal court sitting in diversity applies state *substantive* law but its own *procedural* law. When a Federal Rule of Civil Procedure is directly on point and collides with a contrary state rule, the Federal Rule governs so long as it is valid — that is, arguably procedural and within the Rules Enabling Act. The Federal Rule here authorizes leaving the summons and complaint with a co-resident of suitable age at the defendant's dwelling; that rule is on point and arguably procedural, so it controls over the State C and State A in-hand-service rules. Service was valid, and the motion must be denied.

**Gold Key:** in diversity, a valid Federal Rule on point controls over conflicting state procedure if it is arguably procedural; a federal court applies state substantive law but federal procedural law. **Silver Key:** A and C both say "Deny" — credit the one resting on the operative reason (the service rule is procedural), not the one citing a truth ("affects substantive rights") that decides nothing.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B — Grant, because the federal court must apply the law of the state in which it is sitting.** This is the engineered pull: it echoes the headline of Erie — "federal courts in diversity follow state law" — and feels like the safe, well-learned rule. The word doing the damage is "must apply the law": a diversity court applies state *substantive* law, but not state *procedural* law where a valid Federal Rule is on point. **Gold Key:** the Erie command reaches substance, not procedure; a Federal Rule that is arguably procedural displaces the conflicting state service rule.

**C — Deny, because granting summary judgment would affect Lydia's substantive rights.** This lands the correct outcome, which makes it feel safe, and it sounds protective of the plaintiff. But its reason is non-responsive: almost any dispositive motion "affects substantive rights," and that observation says nothing about whether the disputed *service* rule is substantive or procedural — the only question that decides the motion. **Silver Key:** when two choices share the verdict, the one citing a true-but-non-operative reason still loses.

**D — Grant, because the federal court must apply the law of the state in which the claim arose.** "Where the claim arose" sounds like a real choice-of-law hook and invites you to reach for State A. But the place the claim arose is irrelevant here: the dispute is about which *service* rule governs, a procedural question answered by the Federal Rules — not by the substantive law of any state, and certainly not selected by where the contract was performed.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested rule (unchanged from the original):** A federal court sitting in diversity applies state substantive law and federal procedural law; under the two-track Erie/Hanna analysis, where a Federal Rule of Civil Procedure directly conflicts with state law, the Federal Rule applies if it is valid — arguably procedural and within the Rules Enabling Act.

- **Hanna v. Plumer, 380 U.S. 460 (1965)** — on facts materially identical to this item (diversity suit; defendant served by leaving copies with a co-resident at the dwelling per the Federal Rule; state law required in-hand personal service), the Supreme Court held the Federal Rule controls; a Rule valid under the Rules Enabling Act applies notwithstanding incidental effects on litigants' rights. The Federal Rule governs if it "really regulates procedure" / is arguably procedural.
- **Rules Enabling Act, 28 U.S.C. § 2072** — authorizes the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure; they may not "abridge, enlarge or modify any substantive right," but incidental effects do not invalidate a Rule.
- **FRCP 4(e)(2)(B)** — service on an individual by leaving a copy at the dwelling with someone of suitable age and discretion who resides there (the on-point Federal Rule; Hanna construed its predecessor, Rule 4(d)(1)).
- **Erie R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938)** — diversity courts apply state substantive law; the command is limited to substance (kills choice B's overbreadth).
