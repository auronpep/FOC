## 1. Final question
A young pastor named Timothy rented a small storefront in a struggling downtown area to plant Grace Community Church, a new outreach ministry focused on neighborhood Bible studies and mercy work. Under the lease, Timothy was obligated to effect and pay for repairs to the leased property caused by vandalism or storm damage. Six months before the lease would expire, vandals smashed the large front plate-glass window that displayed the church's name and a simple cross. Timothy promptly contracted with Kingdom Glass, a local Christian-owned company run by Priscilla, to replace the window for $2,400, due 30 days after satisfactory completion of the work. The landlord was then unaware of the Timothy-Kingdom Glass contract. Priscilla's company was aware that the space was leased for ministry use, but dealt entirely with Timothy.

Forty-five days after Kingdom Glass's satisfactory completion of the window replacement, and prior to the expiration of the lease, Timothy's church plant ran out of funding, ceased operations, and vacated the storefront. In so doing, Timothy forfeited under the lease provisions his right to the return of a $2,400 security deposit with the landlord. The deposit had been required, however, for the express purpose (as stated in the lease) of covering any damage to the leased property except ordinary wear and tear. The only such damage occurring during the occupancy was the smashed window. Kingdom Glass's $2,400 bill for the window replacement is wholly unpaid.

Upon vacating the storefront, Timothy mailed a $1,200 check to Kingdom Glass bearing on its face the following conspicuous notation: "This check is tendered in full and final satisfaction of the $2,400 window replacement invoice." Without noticing this notation, Priscilla's bookkeeper cashed the check. Kingdom Glass now sues Timothy for the $1,200 difference. If Timothy's only defense is accord and satisfaction, is Timothy likely to prevail?

**Answer A:** No, because Kingdom Glass failed to notice the notation on the check.  
**Answer B:** Yes, because by cashing the check Kingdom Glass impliedly agreed to accept the $1,200 as full payment of its claim.  
**Answer C:** No, because the amount owed by Timothy to Kingdom Glass was liquidated and undisputed.  
**Answer D:** Yes, because Kingdom Glass failed to write a reservation-of-rights notation on the check before cashing it.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)
**C is correct.** Timothy's offer of a lesser sum as "full satisfaction" could not create an enforceable accord and satisfaction because the underlying debt was liquidated (fixed at the contract price of $2,400) and undisputed. An accord requires consideration or a bona fide dispute over the claim; payment of a smaller sum on an admitted, fixed obligation supplies neither. Cashing the check, even with the conspicuous notation, does not supply the missing consideration or create a dispute after the fact. The security deposit forfeiture and the lease repair obligation are red herrings; they do not create a genuine dispute between Timothy and Kingdom Glass over the window invoice itself.

**Silver Key (navigation):** When the call is "is accord and satisfaction a defense," first CUT any choice that treats cashing a check or "implied agreement" as sufficient by itself. Then CLASH on whether the stem shows a bona fide dispute over the amount or liability. Here the debt was fixed by the tenant-contractor agreement with no facts of disagreement on price, quality, or who owed it — that single fact kills every "Yes" choice.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
**A (original A mechanic — "failed to notice"):** Attractive because students often fixate on the conspicuous-notation facts and assume the creditor must have actual knowledge for any discharge rule to apply. Breaker: The notation was conspicuous; the deeper failure is that even perfect notice would not create an accord here because the debt was liquidated and undisputed. The "notice" point is not the reason the defense fails.

**B (original C mechanic — dominant trap "cashing = implied agreement"):** Attractive because it appeals to everyday fairness/common-sense ("they cashed it, so they accepted") and sounds like contract formation by conduct. This is the classic autonomy_appeal / colloquialism trap on accord and satisfaction. Breaker (Gold Key / Silver Key): Cashing can complete a satisfaction *only if* a valid accord already exists, which requires either new consideration or a genuine dispute over the claim. A fixed-price repair contract with no facts of disagreement supplies neither. The notation plus cashing is irrelevant without that predicate.

**D (original D mechanic — "no reservation-of-rights"):** Attractive because students remember UCC 1-207 or "under protest" language from other contexts and assume the creditor must affirmatively reserve rights on the instrument itself. Breaker: Reservation language (or striking the notation) matters in *disputed* claim cases under some formulations of the rule; it is irrelevant when there is no bona fide dispute to begin with. The debt here was liquidated and undisputed, so the defense fails for lack of consideration/accord, not for lack of protest language.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule is unchanged from the source: an accord and satisfaction by use of a "full satisfaction" instrument requires that the claim be unliquidated or the subject of a bona fide dispute. Payment of a lesser sum on a liquidated, undisputed debt does not discharge the obligation even if the creditor cashes the check with knowledge of the notation.

**Governing authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):**
- Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 281 (Accord and Satisfaction) and comments (an accord is a contract requiring consideration or a substitute; a claim that is liquidated and undisputed cannot be satisfied by a lesser payment).
- UCC § 3-311 (Accord and Satisfaction by Use of Instrument) — the discharge rule in (b) is expressly conditioned on the claim being disputed or unliquidated in the common-law sense the statute codifies; subsections (c) and (d) provide safe harbors and exceptions that presuppose a disputed claim.
- Classic common-law rule (Pinnel's Case doctrine, followed in the overwhelming majority of U.S. jurisdictions for liquidated claims): a debtor's payment of a lesser sum than is admittedly due, even with "payment in full" language and creditor acceptance, does not discharge the debt for want of consideration. See also *Foakes v. Beer* line of authority in the U.S. (most states).

The source facts, legal issue (accord and satisfaction on a fixed repair debt), credited outcome (defense fails), and trap structure are identical. No doctrinal-currency issue (this corner of contracts law is stable). No key disagreement with the supplied official answer.
