### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

The order is properly denied. The court can require a defendant to display a physical characteristic to assist the jury in a courtroom identification without implicating any constitutional right.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIMINAL-5TH-NONTESTIMONIAL-01):** The Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination protects only testimonial or communicative evidence — the contents of the accused's mind. Compelled physical demonstrations (blood draw, handwriting exemplar, voice sample, displaying a physical characteristic) are non-testimonial and do not implicate the privilege, regardless of how incriminating the result turns out to be.

**Silver Key (SK-CRIMINAL-NONTESTIMONIAL-TELL-01):** When an objection targets a court-ordered physical display, ask one question: does the court want the contents of the defendant's mind, or a physical fact about the defendant's body? Body-fact → non-testimonial → Fifth Amendment is not in play.

Holding out Esther's hands to show a missing finger reveals a physical fact about her body, not a thought, belief, or communication. The privilege does not reach it. The identification procedure is squarely within the court's authority, and the objection fails.

### 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Tested rule (unchanged from original):** The Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination is limited to testimonial or communicative evidence. Compelled physical demonstrations — displaying a bodily characteristic, providing a blood sample, giving a voice exemplar, submitting a handwriting sample — are non-testimonial and fall outside the privilege's protection.

**Legal issue (unchanged):** Whether a court-ordered physical display of a bodily characteristic for in-court identification purposes implicates the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination.

**Credited outcome (unchanged):** The objection is denied; the demonstration is properly ordered.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer):**
- *Schmerber v. California*, 384 U.S. 757 (1966): the Fifth Amendment privilege applies to testimonial or communicative evidence, not to blood draws or other compelled physical procedures that are non-testimonial in character. This is the foundational case for the testimonial/non-testimonial distinction.
- *Holt v. United States*, 218 U.S. 245 (1910): ordering a defendant to put on a blouse to show the jury how it fit was not a violation of the privilege against self-incrimination; the privilege protects against compelled testimonial communications, not compelled physical exhibitions.
- *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218 (1967): lineups and identification procedures are non-testimonial; the Fifth Amendment privilege is not implicated.
- U.S. Const. amend. V.
