## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The parol evidence rule prohibits the introduction of extrinsic evidence of prior or contemporaneous agreements when offered to contradict, vary, or modify the terms of an unambiguous written contract that the parties intended as a complete and final expression of their agreement (a "complete integration"). Here, the written contract specifies "one first-century Galilee oil lamp — $8,000." The collector's testimony about a prior oral agreement for a Judean scroll case directly contradicts the subject matter of the written contract. The contract appears unambiguous on its face, and the collector's proffered testimony is classic parol evidence offered to contradict the writing. The court should sustain the dealer's objection.

**Gold Key:** The parol evidence rule applies to evidence of prior *and* contemporaneous agreements — not just prior ones. The key trigger is that the evidence is offered to *contradict, vary, or modify* an integrated writing. If the writing is unambiguous and integrated, the evidence is barred regardless of when the oral statement was made.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### Choice A (Dominant Trap)

**What makes it attractive:** Students know the parol evidence rule concerns "prior" agreements. Choice A correctly identifies that the conversation happened before signing — a true fact — and then draws a seductive but backwards conclusion: that this timing makes the evidence admissible. A student who half-remembers the rule ("parol evidence = prior agreements") but has the direction reversed will lock in here.

**The breaker:** The fact that the conversation occurred *before* the written contract is exactly what brings it within the scope of the parol evidence rule, not what exempts it. The rule bars prior oral agreements that contradict an integrated writing. The timing makes the evidence *more* vulnerable to exclusion, not less. **Gold Key:** The parol evidence rule applies to prior *and* contemporaneous statements offered to contradict the writing — the timing is the trigger for the rule, not an escape from it.

### Choice B

**What makes it attractive:** Contra proferentem (ambiguity construed against the drafter) is a real and well-known contract doctrine. A student who spots a potential ambiguity in the contract language may reach for this familiar tool.

**The breaker:** The parol evidence rule, not contra proferentem, governs the admissibility of extrinsic evidence. The contract here is unambiguous on its face — it specifies a "first-century Galilee oil lamp." Even if there were ambiguity, the threshold question is whether the extrinsic evidence is admissible at all, and the parol evidence rule answers that question first. Contra proferentem applies to interpretation of ambiguous terms, not to the admissibility of prior oral agreements.

### Choice D

**What makes it attractive:** The fact that there were no other witnesses feels relevant — it highlights the "he said, she said" nature of the dispute and may make a student think the evidence is being excluded for reliability reasons.

**The breaker:** The number of witnesses has nothing to do with the parol evidence rule. The rule is about whether extrinsic evidence can be used to contradict a written contract — it's an admissibility rule based on the integrity of the writing, not a sufficiency-of-evidence or corroboration rule. Whether there are zero witnesses or fifty, the analysis is the same.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Rule:** The parol evidence rule provides that when parties have reduced their agreement to a final, integrated writing, extrinsic evidence (oral or written) of prior or contemporaneous agreements or negotiations is inadmissible to contradict, vary, or modify the terms of that writing. *Restatement (Second) of Contracts* §§ 213, 215; *Masterson v. Sine*, 68 Cal.2d 222, 225 (1968); Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 1856.

**Application:** The written contract for "one first-century Galilee oil lamp — $8,000" is unambiguous on its face. The collector's testimony about a prior oral agreement for a Judean scroll case is a classic prior-or-contemporaneous oral agreement offered to contradict the subject matter term of the writing. The parol evidence rule bars its admission.

**Authority:** *Restatement (Second) of Contracts* § 213(1) (effect of integration: binding agreement supersedes prior agreements); *Masterson v. Sine*, 68 Cal.2d 222, 225 (1968) (parol evidence inadmissible to contradict integrated writing); Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 1856(a) (terms of integrated agreement may not be contradicted by prior or contemporaneous oral agreements).

**Confirmed:** The tested rule (parol evidence rule), legal issue (admissibility of prior oral agreement contradicting written contract), and credited outcome (sustain the objection) are unchanged from the original.

## Program Frame

This item tests the **parol evidence rule** — one of the most frequently tested doctrines on the MBE Contracts exam. The parol evidence rule is an admissibility rule: it determines whether a party can introduce evidence of a prior or contemporaneous oral agreement to change the terms of a written contract. The rule is triggered when (1) the parties have a written contract that is (2) unambiguous and (3) integrated (intended as a complete and final expression of their agreement). If those conditions are met, extrinsic evidence offered to contradict, vary, or modify the writing is barred.
