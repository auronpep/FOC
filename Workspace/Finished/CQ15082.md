## 1. Final Question (Revised Stem + Call)

**Stem:**  
A woman died and devised a parcel of farmland she owned in another state to her nephew, who was 17 years old at the time. A nearby farmer, who owned the property immediately adjacent to the farmland, wrongfully began possessing the land at that time. For 24 of the next 25 years, the farmer planted and harvested crops on the land, grazed his cattle there, and stored farm equipment on it. However, in the sixth year after he first took possession, the farmer neither planted crops nor grazed cattle nor stored equipment on the land because he spent that entire year on a missionary trip to another country. The farmer built a small equipment shed on the land, but he never built a residence on it. When the nephew was 28, he was declared mentally incompetent and had a conservator appointed to oversee his affairs. Since then, he has continuously resided in a care facility. The applicable statute of limitations provides: "An ejectment action shall be brought within 21 years after the cause of action accrues, but if the person entitled to bring the cause of action is under age 18 or mentally incompetent at the time the cause of action accrues, it may be brought by such person within 10 years after attaining age 18 or after the person becomes competent."

**Call:**  
If the nephew's conservator wins an ejectment action against the farmer, what will be the most likely explanation?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A wrongful possessor used land for 24 of 25 years but skipped one entire year in the middle. The owner was a minor when possession began but became an adult before the statutory period would have run, and later became incompetent. The statute tolls only if disability exists when the cause of action accrues. Why does the owner still win?

## 3. Final Answer Choices (Post-Shuffle)

**B:** Because the nephew is mentally incompetent, the statute of limitations has been tolled.

**C:** The farmer never built a residence on the land.

**D:** The farmer was not in continuous possession of the land for 21 years.

## 6. Right-Answer Explanation

**D is correct.** Adverse possession requires **continuous** possession for the full statutory period (21 years in this jurisdiction). The farmer's one-year absence in year 6 — during which he planted no crops, grazed no cattle, and stored no equipment — broke the continuity of his adverse possession. Because the statutory clock reset after that gap, the farmer never completed the required 21 consecutive years. The conservator can therefore eject him.

> **Gold Key:** Continuous possession means **uninterrupted** possession for the entire statutory period. A single gap — even if the possessor returns afterward — resets the clock to zero.

## 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect.** The nephew's minority at the time the farmer took possession does not save him here. The tolling statute extends the time only until 10 years after reaching age 18. The nephew turned 18 in year 2, so his extension period gave him until year 12 to bring ejectment. But the farmer's continuity broke in year 6, before the tolling period even expired — so the nephew's ejectment claim succeeded on the continuity defect, not on tolling.

**B is incorrect (dominant trap).** This choice is highly attractive because students see "mentally incompetent" and think "tolling." But the statute tolls **only if the disability existed when the cause of action accrued**. Here, the cause of action accrued when the farmer first took possession (year 1), at which time the nephew was a minor (not mentally incompetent). His later incompetence in year 12 does not restart or extend the tolling period. The nephew's minority tolled the statute, but that tolling ended 10 years after he turned 18 — and the farmer's continuity broke before that deadline anyway.

> **Silver Key:** Disability tolling looks **only at the moment the cause of action accrues**. Later disabilities do not count.

**C is incorrect.** Adverse possession does not require the possessor to build a residence or make improvements. The elements are actual, open/notorious, exclusive, continuous, and hostile possession — not construction. A shed is evidence of possession but not a legal requirement.

## 8. Black-Letter Verification (Lawyer Confirmation)

**Tested rule:** Adverse possession requires continuous possession for the full statutory period. A break in possession resets the statutory clock. Disability tolling applies only if the disability exists when the cause of action accrues.

**Authority floor:**
- *Howard v. Kunto*, 3 Wash. App. 383, 477 P.2d 210 (1970) — continuity requirement; seasonal use may suffice for certain property types, but a complete abandonment breaks continuity.
- *Marengo Cave Co. v. Ross*, 212 Ind. 624, 10 N.E.2d 917 (1937) — continuous possession means uninterrupted use consistent with the nature of the property.
- Restatement (First) of Property § 227 (1936) — continuity element of adverse possession.
- Statutory tolling for disabilities is standard; see, e.g., N.Y. Real Prop. Acts. Law § 543 (disability must exist at accrual).

**Doctrinal currency check:** No superseding authority affects adverse possession continuity or disability-tolling rules. The black-letter rule remains stable.

**Outcome confirmation:** The farmer's one-year gap broke continuity. The nephew's later mental incompetence does not toll because it did not exist at accrual. D is correct.

**Verdict:** PASS — authority supports the credited answer; no contradiction found.
