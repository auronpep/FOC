### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**A is correct. Gold Key:** an intended beneficiary takes the contract as written. The same written agreement that gives Hannah the right to claim the benefit also caps Lydia’s liability for audio-work claims at $12,000. Hannah may not enforce the beneficiary promise while treating the same contract’s limit as if it does not travel with the promise.

**Silver Key:** when a third-party-beneficiary answer set offers “nothing,” “full amount,” “cap,” and “assignment,” locate the source of the right and the source of the limit. If both come from the same contract, the cap answer is the package answer.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B.** This answer sells a familiar transfer-route idea: assignment is a real way someone can receive contract rights. The breaker is route mismatch; Hannah is suing as the person the original service contract was written to benefit, so the issue is third-party-beneficiary enforcement, not whether Peter later assigned the contract to her.

**C.** This answer sells the old privity reflex: Hannah was not one of the two contracting parties, so a student may want to shut the claim down completely. The breaker is the word “never”; a direct-benefit beneficiary is not categorically barred from enforcing a service contract.

**D.** This is the dominant trap. It sells the nonparty intuition: Hannah did not sign the cap, so the cap feels like Peter and Lydia’s private limit. **Gold Key:** an intended beneficiary cannot use the contract as the source of her right and reject the same contract’s liability limit; the right and cap travel together.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

The source row tests the common-law third-party-beneficiary rule that an intended beneficiary’s rights are measured by the contract that creates them, including the liability cap.  The transform preserves that issue: Hannah is named as the person the audio work is for, so intended-beneficiary enforcement is the route; the same contract caps Lydia’s audio-work liability at $12,000. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 302 recognizes intended beneficiaries when recognition of a right to performance is appropriate to effectuate the contracting parties’ intent, and the circumstances show the promisee intended to give the beneficiary the benefit of performance. ([LexisNexis][1]) Restatement § 309 supports the cap result because beneficiary rights are subject to the contract-level terms and limitations “as provided by the contract.” ([Matthew Miner][2]) Cornell’s Wex summary also states that intended beneficiaries may enforce the contract, but their rights come through the contract relationship rather than from a freestanding outsider status. ([Legal Information Institute][3])
