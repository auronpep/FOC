## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

Complete self-defense requires that the force used be proportionate to the threat. Timothy's shove and raised hand, while unlawful, threatened at most nondeadly force — a slap from an unarmed, smaller person. Lydia responded by stabbing him in the chest with a garden knife, which is deadly force. Deadly force is justified only when the defendant reasonably faces imminent death or serious bodily injury (or a qualifying violent felony). A shove and threatened slap do not cross that threshold. Because the response was disproportionate, Lydia's complete self-defense claim fails. The rule does not require Lydia to retreat here — the problem is proportionality, not retreat.

**Gold Key:** Deadly-force self-defense requires an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury (or kidnapping/sexual assault under the MPC). An unlawful shove and threatened slap are nondeadly force; responding with a lethal weapon is disproportionate as a matter of law.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (originally B):** This choice sells the idea that honest fear alone is enough. It feels right because Lydia was in a confrontation and may genuinely have been afraid. But self-defense has both a subjective and an objective component: the belief in the need for force must be reasonable, and the level of force must be proportionate to the threat. Honest fear of a slap does not make a lethal stabbing reasonable. The Gold Key on deadly-force thresholds shows this trap: the threat must be death or serious bodily injury, not an unwanted touch.

**B (originally A) — DOMINANT TRAP:** This is the most attractive wrong answer. Timothy was about to touch Lydia unlawfully — that is factually true, and students instinctively feel that an unlawful touch should justify a defensive response. The trap is that this choice answers the wrong question. The issue is not whether Timothy's act was unlawful (it was), but whether his unlawful act justified *deadly* force. This choice proves a non-dispositive element (unlawful contact) while ignoring the proportionality requirement that actually decides the call. The Silver Key move — "name the axis: was the threat deadly or nondeadly?" — would steer a student off this trap.

**D (originally D):** This choice invents a categorical rule: self-defense never applies when the defendant uses a blade. That is overbroad. A knife (or any weapon) can be justified if the defendant reasonably faces a deadly threat — for example, if Timothy had been charging at Lydia with a machete. The problem here is not the weapon; it is the disproportionality between the threat and the response.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Governing rule:** A person may use deadly force in self-defense only if the person actually and reasonably believes that such force is necessary to prevent imminent death, serious bodily injury, kidnapping, or sexual assault (MPC § 3.04(2)(b)). Nondeadly force does not justify a deadly response. The force used must be proportionate to the threat faced.

**Authority:** Model Penal Code § 3.04(2)(b); *People v. Goetz*, 68 N.Y.2d 96 (1986) (objective reasonableness component of self-defense); *Beard v. United States*, 158 U.S. 550 (1895) (proportionality principle). The Restatement (Second) of Torts § 65 similarly limits deadly force to situations involving death or serious bodily harm.

**Application:** Timothy's shove and raised hand constituted, at most, a threatened battery (a slap). Even accepting that the shove was unlawful, the threat was nondeadly. Lydia's response — stabbing Timothy in the chest with a garden knife — is paradigmatically deadly force. The disproportion is clear: a threatened slap does not create a reasonable belief of imminent death or serious bodily injury. Complete self-defense fails.

**Verified:** The tested rule (deadly-force proportionality), legal issue (complete self-defense), and credited outcome (No, self-defense fails) are unchanged from the original. The official key (C) is confirmed correct.
