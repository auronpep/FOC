## Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**B is correct.** A witness who voluntarily gives testimony on a subject in a civil case cannot use the Fifth Amendment as both sword and shield to block fair cross-examination on that same subject. The waiver is limited to the subject matter opened by the voluntary testimony. The stem shows Peter chose to testify favorably about the pledge money on direct; the cross questions stay inside that exact subject (electronic transfers from the same pledge account). The court may therefore treat the direct testimony as a limited waiver and require answers on cross.

**Silver Key (call focus):** When the call asks for the "most proper" ruling on a privilege invocation after voluntary testimony, first locate the precise subject the witness opened on direct, then test whether the cross stays inside that subject. Only the choice that matches the limited, subject-matter scope survives.

## Full wrong-answer explanations

**A (global waiver) is attractive** because students often over-apply the idea that "once you talk, the privilege is gone for everything" or confuse civil cases with the broader waiver rules sometimes seen in criminal proceedings. It fails because testimony on one subject does not waive the privilege for every possible incriminating question in the case. The waiver is limited to the subject matter opened.

**C (one-sided testimony allowed) is attractive** because it appeals to a fairness intuition ("he should be allowed to tell his side without being forced to incriminate himself further"). It fails the True & Responsive test: the rule does not permit a witness to give voluntary direct testimony on a subject and then invoke the Fifth to block all fair cross-examination on the identical subject. That would let the witness use the privilege as both sword and shield.

**D (dismiss the complaint) is attractive** to students who treat any Fifth Amendment invocation as case-dispositive or who misremember rules about striking pleadings. It is not responsive to the call (the question is about the proper ruling on the cross-examination questions after voluntary testimony). Invocation on cross does not automatically dispose of the opposing party's civil claim.

## Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited answer (limited subject-matter waiver) is correct and unchanged from the original row. A witness who voluntarily testifies on a subject in a civil proceeding waives the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination to the extent of fair cross-examination on that same subject. The witness may not selectively disclose favorable information and then invoke the privilege to prevent testing of that disclosure.

Governing authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):  
- Rogers v. United States, 340 U.S. 367 (1951) (voluntary testimony constitutes a waiver of the privilege as to the matters covered by the testimony).  
- The principle is routinely applied in civil cases: the waiver is not global and is limited to the subject matter opened on direct. See also Brown v. United States, 356 U.S. 148 (1958) (discussing scope of waiver by testimony).
