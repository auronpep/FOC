### 7. Full right-answer explanation

A is correct. Barnabas purported to convey the coastal wedding-venue property to Stephen by a warranty deed with no exceptions, even though Hannah — the actual owner — was still alive and Barnabas had no present interest in the property. Hannah later died and the will devising the property to Barnabas was duly admitted to probate, so Barnabas acquired the title. At that moment, the after-acquired title doctrine (estoppel by deed) operated: because Barnabas warranted the title in a general warranty deed, he is now estopped from denying the truth of that warranty against the very grantee (Stephen) to whom he made it. The after-acquired title automatically inured to Stephen, and the grantee prevails in the quiet title action. Gold Key: when a grantor conveys by warranty deed without owning the property and later acquires the title, the after-acquired title passes to the grantee by estoppel by deed; the grantee wins a quiet title suit against the grantor. The friend's status as a BFP and the recording of the deed are bonus facts but are not the source of the right.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

B is attractive because it states a true-sounding real-property principle — that a person cannot convey what they do not own — and it fits the surface fact that Barnabas had no interest at the time of the conveyance. The breaker is that the warranty deed carries a present covenant of seisin and right to convey, which is the hook for the after-acquired title doctrine: the grantor is estopped from later asserting the very title he warranted he had. Silver Key: when a warranty-deed grantor later acquires the title, the "no present interest" rule has an estoppel-by-deed exception that automatically inures the after-acquired title to the grantee. Gold Key: GK-RP-ESTOPPEL-01 (after-acquired title / estoppel by deed) kills this trap. Recording is irrelevant to the quiet title claim; the source of Stephen's right is the warranty deed itself, not the recording.

C is attractive because it ties the result to a recordation fact that sounds lawyerly and the stem did mention that Stephen "promptly recorded." The breaker is that recording only protects against a later BFP from the same grantor, and even then only when the recording statute actually applies. In a quiet title action between the original warranty grantor (Barnabas) and the grantee (Stephen), the source of Stephen's right is the warranty deed's estoppel effect, not the fact that it was recorded. Recording neither creates title nor substitutes for the estoppel-by-deed doctrine. Silver Key: SK-RP-ESTOPPEL-02 — when the call is a quiet title suit between the original grantor and the grantee, ignore recording and a possible missed title search; the warranty deed's estoppel is what makes the grantee win.

D is attractive because it sounds like a real-estate due-diligence rule and the stem even says Stephen "did not conduct any title search." The breaker is twofold: (i) the law does not require a buyer to perform a title search before accepting a warranty deed, and (ii) Hannah had not recorded the will (wills are typically not recorded in the real-property records), so even a title search would likely not have revealed the risk. The correct path is to focus on what the warranty deed does once the grantor later gets title, not on what the grantee should have searched for before the conveyance.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested issue remains Real Property, Titles, Estoppel by Deed, and the verified outline code is `84040206` (verbatim in `OUTLINE_CODES_COMPLETE.md`: "Titles, Deeds, and Conveyancing > Conveyance by Deed > Estoppel by deed"). The doctrinal core is the after-acquired title / estoppel by deed rule: when a grantor conveys real property by warranty deed without holding title at the moment of conveyance, and later acquires the title, the after-acquired title automatically inures to the grantee. The grantee wins a quiet title action against the grantor, who is estopped from asserting the very title he previously warranted he had. See William B. Stoebuck & Dale A. Whitman, The Law of Property § 11.5 (3d ed. 2000) (warranty deed triggers after-acquired title doctrine; quitclaim does not); *King v. Gilson's Adm'x*, 32 Ill. 348, 354-55 (1863) (the covenants of seisin and of good right to convey are present covenants broken, if at all, when the deed is delivered, but the doctrine of estoppel by deed operates to pass the after-acquired title to the grantee); *Wallace v. Pruitt*, 1 Tex. Civ. App. 231, 234, 20 S.W. 728, 728 (1892) (the maker of a deed is estopped to deny the truth of recitals therein); *W. E. Coldwell Co. v. Cowart*, 138 Ga. 233, 75 S.E. 425, 427 (1912) (a recital works an estoppel only in an action founded on a deed, or brought to enforce rights arising under it — satisfied here, because the quiet title action is a suit arising out of the deed). The Restatement (Fourth) of Property, Volume 5, Division Three, Chapter 4 ("Present Warranties of Title") is the modern ALI framing of these warranties (approved at the 2024 Annual Meeting as Tentative Draft No. 5; not yet adopted as a final Restatement, so the cited authority remains the case law and the Stoebuck treatise).
