## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**C is correct.** To be convicted as an accomplice, a person must, with the intent that the crime be committed, aid, counsel, or encourage the principal *before or during* the commission of the crime. Daniel and Timothy did nothing during Peter's assault to assist or encourage him. They did not cheer him on, hold the victim, block the door, or do anything else that would constitute actus reus of accomplice liability. Their mere presence at the scene, without more, is insufficient. After the crime was complete, they left with Peter — but post-departure conduct does not retroactively make them accomplices to the manslaughter itself. The general rule of criminal law is that a bystander has no affirmative legal duty to intervene to prevent a crime or aid a victim, even if they easily could. Because Daniel and Timothy neither assisted nor encouraged Peter during the beating, they cannot be convicted as accomplices.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (EAR_FALSITY — backwards):** This choice reverses the modern rule on a critical threshold fact. It says the companions must be acquitted because Peter was not convicted. The actual rule is the opposite: under modern accomplice statutes, an accomplice *can* be prosecuted and convicted even when the principal has not been convicted. A hung jury (mistrial) does not bar prosecution of accomplices. An outright acquittal of the principal might bar accomplice prosecution in some jurisdictions, but that is not what happened here. The choice gets the dependency relationship exactly backward.

**B (EAR_DISTORTION — half_truth):** This choice latches onto a real fact — Daniel and Timothy *did* urge Peter to leave — and inflates it into accomplice-level encouragement. The missing element is *timing and target*. Accomplice liability requires aid or encouragement directed at the commission of the crime, given before or during the crime. Urging someone to flee *after* the victim has fallen and the assault is over is not encouragement to commit the assault; it is post-crime conduct that might support a separate charge (e.g., obstruction of justice or accessory after the fact), but it does not make them accomplices to the manslaughter itself.

**D (EAR_DISTORTION — colloquialism):** This choice appeals to the common-sense moral intuition that people who watch someone get beaten to death and do *nothing* deserve punishment. But criminal law does not impose liability for pure inaction absent a legal duty. Daniel and Timothy had no special relationship to the victim, no legal duty to protect the clerk, and no duty to physically intervene. Their failure to act, however morally regrettable, does not satisfy the actus reus requirement for accomplice liability. This choice substitutes moral common sense for the legal test.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Tested rule:** Accomplice liability requires (1) an actus reus of aiding, counseling, or encouraging the principal, (2) done before or during the commission of the crime, (3) with the mens rea of intending that the crime be committed. Mere presence at the scene of a crime, without affirmative assistance or encouragement, does not establish accomplice liability. Additionally, a bystander generally has no legal duty to intervene to prevent a crime or aid a victim absent a special relationship. Finally, under modern law, an accomplice may be prosecuted even if the principal has not been convicted.

**Authority:**

- **Model Penal Code § 2.06(3)(a):** A person is an accomplice of another in the commission of an offense if, with the purpose of promoting or facilitating the commission of the offense, he aids, agrees to aid, or attempts to aid the other in planning or committing the offense. (MPC requires purposeful assistance directed at the offense — post-departure flight encouragement does not qualify.)
- **No general duty to rescue/Restatement rule:** The common law imposes no affirmative duty on a bystander to act for the protection of others absent a special relationship (parent-child, spouse, employer-employee, etc.). See Restatement (Second) of Torts § 314 (no duty to control conduct of another).
- **Modern accomplice prosecution without principal conviction:** Most modern statutes abolish the common-law distinction between principals and accessories, allowing accomplices to be prosecuted as principals even if the primary actor has not been prosecuted or convicted. See, e.g., 18 U.S.C. § 2 (federal aiding and abetting); California Penal Code § 31.
- **Post-crime assistance is a separate offense:** Harboring or assisting a criminal after the crime is complete is typically charged as a distinct crime (e.g., accessory after the fact, obstruction of justice), not as complicity in the underlying offense. See 18 U.S.C. § 3 (accessory after the fact); California Penal Code § 32.
