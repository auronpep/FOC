### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is the best answer. The indictment means the Sixth Amendment right to counsel had attached, but attachment does not make every later police interview impossible. Esther received warnings, said she understood, signed a waiver, and then confessed. Gold Key: after the Sixth Amendment right attaches, a defendant may still waive the right for police questioning if the waiver is knowing, intelligent, and voluntary; police initiation alone is not an automatic suppression trigger. Silver Key: when the motion gives only one reason for suppression, test that reason only; here, the only reason offered is police initiation after indictment, and the waiver fact answers it.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

B is the dominant trap. It sells the old automatic-bar instinct: once indictment has happened, any police-started interview feels forbidden. The breaker is the waiver fact. Gold Key: the current rule allows a charged defendant to waive the Sixth Amendment right during police-initiated questioning if the waiver is valid.

D sounds careful because Miranda and the Sixth Amendment are distinct rights. The trap is turning that distinction into a never rule. Miranda warnings can ordinarily supply the information needed for a knowing waiver of counsel during post-indictment questioning, so the answer overclaims.

A sells lawyer-control formalism. It feels protective to require counsel's written permission once a lawyer has been appointed. The breaker is that the defendant personally holds the right and may waive it; the lawyer's written permission is not a required condition.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are preserved. The source row tests whether a charged defendant's post-indictment confession must be suppressed solely because police initiated the interview after the Sixth Amendment right attached, even though the defendant received warnings and signed a waiver. The answer remains no automatic suppression: a valid knowing, intelligent, and voluntary waiver can permit questioning about the charged offense.

Lawyer-confirmation layer: the Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches after the initiation of adversary judicial proceedings, including indictment. Once attached, the right may be waived if the relinquishment is knowing, intelligent, and voluntary. In Patterson v. Illinois, 487 U.S. 285 (1988), the Court held that Miranda warnings can be sufficient to inform an indicted defendant of the right to counsel during questioning and the consequences of waiving that right. In Montejo v. Louisiana, 556 U.S. 778 (2009), the Court overruled Michigan v. Jackson's automatic suppression approach and confirmed that police initiation after appointment or attachment does not by itself invalidate a later waiver. Sources checked: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/487/285/ ; https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/556/778/ ; https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/amdt6-6-3-3/ALDE_00013439/

Those authorities agree with the supplied official key. Esther was indicted, so the right had attached. But she received warnings, stated understanding, signed a written waiver, and confessed. Because her suppression motion rests solely on police initiation after indictment, the best answer is that the statement may be admissible if the waiver was valid.
