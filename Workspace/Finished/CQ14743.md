### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is correct. Paul committed larceny when he trespassorily took Stephen's phone from Stephen's pocket with intent to steal it and carried it away, even briefly. Gold Key: larceny's carrying-away element is tiny; slight movement of the property is enough. ### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

B. Robbery sells the knife fact. It invites the jump from "Paul started to draw a weapon" to "Paul used force or fear." The breaker is that Stephen was unconscious before the taking, so no violence or intimidation produced the taking. Gold Key: robbery is larceny plus force or fear applied to the victim.

A. Attempted robbery keeps attention on Paul's first move toward the knife. That feels serious because it points toward a more aggravated offense. The breaker is the call: the question asks for the most serious crime of which Paul properly could be convicted, and completed larceny is available on the finished taking and slight movement.

C. Attempted larceny has the same "he dropped it" attraction. It treats abandonment before escape as if the theft stayed incomplete. The breaker is asportation: once Paul took the phone and started away, the carrying-away element was satisfied by slight movement.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The credited answer remains larceny. Common-law larceny requires a trespassory taking and carrying away of the personal property of another with intent to steal. The asportation requirement is satisfied by slight movement. The U.S. Department of Justice Criminal Resource Manual section 1639 describes larceny under 18 U.S.C. 641 as wrongful taking and carrying away of another's personal property with intent to deprive, citing United States v. Barlow, 480 F.2d 1245, 1251 (D.C. Cir. 1972). Robbery is an aggravated larceny requiring force, violence, or putting in fear; Carter v. United States, 530 U.S. 255 (2000), discusses the common-law relationship between robbery and larceny.

Paul took Stephen's phone from Stephen's pocket, started away with it, and intended to steal it. That completes larceny even though Paul later dropped the phone. Robbery is not complete because Stephen was unconscious and no force, violence, or intimidation caused the taking. Attempt answers are less serious and unnecessary once the completed larceny is established. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged.
