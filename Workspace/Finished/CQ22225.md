7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

B is correct. Lydia's statement that her ankle was throbbing right then described a present physical sensation, so it fits the then-existing physical condition exception. Gold Key: a statement of current pain is admissible as a statement of then-existing physical condition. Silver Key: when one answer tracks what the speaker felt at that moment and another answer pivots to why the doctor was hired, lock onto the time-of-sensation cue first. The privilege detour also fails because the common law does not recognize a general physician-patient privilege, and even in jurisdictions with a statutory privilege the objection belongs to the patient rather than the opposing party.

8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A. A sounds lawyerly because Rule 403 language often rides along with motions to strike. The breaker is that nothing in this record shows any special unfair-prejudice problem; the statement is being attacked on hearsay and privilege theories, and the answer choice answers a different question.

C. C sells the confidentiality instinct. Gold Key: common law does not recognize a general physician-patient privilege, and even where a statutory privilege exists it is ordinarily the patient's protection to assert or waive, not the opponent's.

D. D is the engineered trap because it matches the cross-examination reveal that Dr. Mary examined Lydia only for trial. Gold Key: a statement of present pain falls within the then-existing physical condition lane, and the Advisory Committee Note to Rule 803 rejects the old limitation for statements to a doctor consulted only to testify. Silver Key: do not let a distractor swap "what the plaintiff felt right now" for "why the doctor was consulted."

9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited outcome remains unchanged from the original row: the motion should be denied. Fed. R. Evid. 803(3) admits a statement of the declarant's then-existing physical condition, including present pain or bodily health. Lydia's statement that her ankle was throbbing right then fits that rule because it described a current physical sensation rather than a memory of past pain. The Rule 803 Advisory Committee Note also states that the rule rejects the older limitation that excluded statements to a physician consulted only for the purpose of testifying. That defeats the litigation-only-exam trap.

The privilege detour does not change the answer. Fed. R. Evid. 501 sends privilege questions to common law unless another source controls, and the Supreme Court has said that the physician-patient evidentiary privilege is unknown to the common law. Whalen v. Roe, 429 U.S. 589, 602 n.29 (1977). So a jurisdiction following the common-law rule on confidential communications between patient and physician does not supply a general physician-patient privilege that Barnabas can use to strike the testimony.
