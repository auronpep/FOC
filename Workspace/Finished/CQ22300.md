**A is correct.** Conspiracy requires an agreement to commit the target crime. ## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**D - Conspiracy to commit burglary.** This is the strongest trap because Lydia opened a locked door and Peter entered without permission. The breaker is the common-law burglary frame. **Gold Key:** common-law burglary is not every unlawful entry; the classic elements require a dwelling and nighttime.

**C - Conspiracy to commit larceny.** This answer is attractive because the plan is dishonest and meant to take value from the tutor's private binder. The missing piece is the taking and carrying away of property with intent to permanently deprive. Photographing the pages while leaving the binder behind does not match common-law larceny.

**B - Both conspiracy to commit burglary and conspiracy to commit larceny.** This choice stacks the two surface wrongs: a locked-office entry and a plan to copy private material. But both target crimes fail on their own elements. The office lunch entry is not common-law burglary, and photographing without carrying away the property is not common-law larceny.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule:** Conspiracy requires an agreement to commit a criminal offense. If the agreed plan does not satisfy the elements of the charged object crime, the defendant is not guilty of conspiracy to commit that particular crime. Common-law burglary required breaking and entering the dwelling house of another at night with intent to commit a felony inside. Common-law larceny required the trespassory taking and carrying away of another's personal property with intent to permanently deprive the owner of it.

**Authority:** Model Penal Code section 5.03 states the agreement-based conspiracy framework. `Quarles v. United States`, 139 S. Ct. 1872, 1877 (2019), describes common-law burglary as breaking and entering a dwelling at night with intent to commit a felony, citing Blackstone. The federal government's merits brief in `Carter v. United States`, 530 U.S. 255 (2000), states the traditional common-law larceny definition as taking and carrying away personal goods of another with intent to permanently deprive. See https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/18pdf/587us2r51_0971.pdf and https://www.justice.gov/osg/brief/carter-v-united-states-merits.

**Confirmation:** The credited outcome is unchanged from the source: neither conspiracy conviction is proper. The legal issue is unchanged: the agreement did not target common-law burglary because the entry was into an office during the day, and did not target larceny because the plan was to copy information without carrying away the original property. Not on the doctrinal-currency hotlist. Authority floor satisfied by MPC section 5.03 for conspiracy structure and Supreme Court/common-law authority for burglary and larceny definitions.
