# CQ22866 — Rule 35 Physical/Mental Exams, Christian-School Van Variant

## 1. Final question

Lydia drives a church-van route for a small Christian school, delivering choir members to a regional hymn festival. During a rainstorm, she backed the school van into a parked feed truck outside the auditorium. A choir parent injured in the van sued the school and the truck owner in federal court for damages. The truck owner filed a motion asking the court to order Lydia to submit to vision and psychological examinations, saying only that she was either unable to see well enough to drive or not emotionally steady enough to operate the van.

How should the court rule on the motion?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

In a federal civil case, can a party force someone to take vision and psychological exams based only on conclusory allegations that the person was visually or emotionally unfit?

## 3. Final answer choices (A–D, post-shuffle)

A. Deny the motion, because the truck owner made only conclusory allegations and did not show good cause that Lydia's physical or mental condition is in controversy.

B. Deny the motion, because the requested examinations would violate Lydia's privacy.

C. Grant the motion, because the truck owner alleged that Lydia was visually impaired or emotionally unfit to drive.

D. Grant the motion, because Lydia's physical or mental health may be relevant to the truck owner's defense.

## 5. Correct answer

Correct answer: A (original key D).

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Gold Key: For a Rule 35 physical or mental examination, the condition must be genuinely in controversy and the movant must show good cause; conclusory allegations do not clear that threshold.

Silver Key: When two answers deny the exam, choose the denial grounded in the Rule 35 threshold rather than the privacy intuition.

Choice A is the correct residual. The truck owner asked for a court order compelling vision and psychological examinations, but the only support described is conclusory: Lydia was allegedly unable to see well enough or emotionally steady enough to drive. Relevance alone is not the Rule 35 gateway. The court should deny the motion because the movant did not make the required affirmative showing that Lydia's physical or mental condition is in controversy and that good cause exists for the exams.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

### Choice B

The appeal: this answer feels protective because physical and mental exams can feel invasive.

Breaker: privacy may be a policy concern, but the tested Rule 35 question is whether the movant made the required threshold showing. The cleaner denial is that the motion lacks good cause and an in-controversy showing.

### Choice C

The appeal: this answer mirrors the movant's allegation, so it tempts students to treat “the party said she was impaired” as enough.

Breaker: Gold Key: conclusory allegations do not establish the Rule 35 threshold. The court needs more than a conclusory claim that vision or emotional steadiness might be involved.

### Choice D

The appeal: this answer sounds practically useful because the driver's condition may matter to liability or a defense.

Breaker: relevance is not the same as Rule 35 eligibility. Silver Key: the call asks whether the motion satisfies the exam-order threshold, not whether the condition could someday be relevant.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

Authority floor: Fed. R. Civ. P. 35(a) provides that a federal court may order a party whose mental or physical condition is in controversy to submit to a physical or mental examination, and the order may be made only on motion for good cause and notice. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_35. The Supreme Court applied that threshold in Schlagenhauf v. Holder, 379 U.S. 104 (1964), holding that conclusory allegations that a bus driver was not mentally or physically capable did not justify Rule 35 examinations when the driver had not placed his condition in controversy and the movants had not made the required affirmative showing. See https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/379/104/.

Application: Lydia's condition was not described as something she asserted as part of her own claim or defense. The truck owner merely alleged possible visual or emotional unfitness. That may make the subject relevant, but it does not by itself show that Lydia's physical or mental condition is in controversy or that good cause exists for the requested exams.

Student version Black Letter Law Answer Explanation: A federal court can order a physical or mental exam only when the condition is genuinely in controversy and the moving party shows good cause. Bare allegations that someone might have been impaired are not enough.
