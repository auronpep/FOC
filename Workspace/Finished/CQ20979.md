### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**Gold Key:** Under UCC § 2-314(2)(c), goods are merchantable only if they are "fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods are used." The key word is *ordinary*. A product that causes an allergic reaction in one person out of all users is arguably fit for ordinary use by ordinary persons. The implied warranty of merchantability does not guarantee that a product will be safe for every single user — only that it is fit for the purposes to which it is ordinarily put. ### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original B) — Dominant Trap:** This choice tempts students who believe that a manufacturer is only liable to the party it sells to directly. It feels right: why should the manufacturer care about a sale it didn't make? But the law is the opposite. Under the UCC, all members of the chain of commercial distribution — manufacturer, wholesaler, retailer — are liable for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability. The independent distributor in this choice is irrelevant. **Gold Key:** The implied warranty of merchantability runs through the entire chain of distribution; the plaintiff can sue any commercial seller in the chain.

**B (original C):** This choice appeals to students who conflate warranty liability with negligence. Reasonable testing sounds like the right thing to do, and it might show the product was safe for "normal" skin. But warranty liability is strict — it does not depend on whether the manufacturer acted reasonably or conducted testing. Moreover, the "normal skin" framing is a subtle trap: the ordinary-use standard does not exclude users with sensitive skin from the definition of ordinary purposes. **Gold Key:** Warranty liability is strict; the defendant's conduct (reasonable or not) is not the test.

**C (original D):** Government approval feels like it should matter — if the FDA signed off, the product must be safe, right? ### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** UCC § 2-314 (Implied Warranty: Merchantability; Usage of Trade)

Under § 2-314(1), a warranty that goods shall be merchantable is implied in a contract for their sale if the seller is a merchant with respect to goods of that kind. Under § 2-314(2)(c), goods are merchantable only if they are "fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods are used."

**Authority:** UCC § 2-314(2)(c) (available at LII: https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-314). See also Nebraska UCC § 2-314 annotations (Mennonite Deaconess Home & Hosp. v. Gates Eng'g Co., 219 Neb. 303, 363 N.W.2d 155 (1985)) (to establish breach, plaintiff must prove deviation from the standard of merchantability at the time of sale; goods must be "fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods are used").

**Application:** The plaintiff's claim is that the lotion was not merchantable. The manufacturer's best defense is that the product *was* fit for ordinary purposes. If Martha's allergic reaction was the only one ever reported, the product was, by definition, fit for ordinary use by the vast majority of users. The implied warranty does not require a product to be safe for every conceivable user — only for ordinary use.

**Distractor verification:**
- **Chain of distribution (A):** All members of the chain of commercial distribution are liable for breach of implied warranty of merchantability. This is a well-established UCC principle. The presence of an independent distributor does not shield the manufacturer.
- **Reasonable testing (B):** Warranty liability is strict, not fault-based. The manufacturer's conduct (reasonable or not) is irrelevant. Additionally, the "normal skin" framing is misleading because the ordinary-use standard is not limited to users with "normal" skin.
- **Government approval (C):** Compliance with government regulations does not bar common law warranty liability. A product can be FDA-approved and still breach the implied warranty of merchantability.

**Conclusion:** The tested rule (implied warranty of merchantability, UCC § 2-314), the legal issue (what constitutes an effective defense to a merchantability claim), and the credited outcome (the unusual-allergy fact is the best defense) are all unchanged from the original. **PASS.**
