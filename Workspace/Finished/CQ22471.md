### 1. Final question
Lydia and Barnabas own a private Christian recording studio that keeps a small display of family music artifacts in its lobby. After a concert, someone stole Lydia's late grandmother's handwritten hymnbook from a locked road case. Lydia and Barnabas posted a livestream announcement offering a $75,000 reward to anyone who identified the thief and returned the hymnbook.

A few days later, Daniel, an on-duty city police officer who had heard about the reward, received a tip from an informant that the thief was using a rented warehouse behind a closed print shop. Daniel watched the warehouse for several hours. Near midnight, he saw Stephen walk outside carrying the hymnbook. Daniel arrested Stephen and returned the hymnbook to Lydia and Barnabas. When Lydia thanked him, Daniel requested the reward. Lydia and Barnabas refused to pay. Daniel sued for the reward.

Should the court rule for Daniel?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. A reward offer can usually be accepted by doing the requested act, and Daniel knew about the reward before he acted. But Daniel was on duty, and the act he performed was ordinary police work: finding a thief, making an arrest, and returning stolen property. Gold Key: an on-duty public officer cannot give contract consideration for a reward by doing the same arrest or recovery work the officer is already legally obligated to do. Silver Key: in a reward-offer question, split acceptance from consideration before choosing a Yes answer. Daniel may have satisfied the ordinary acceptance facts, but his official duty prevents the reward promise from becoming enforceable against Lydia and Barnabas.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because it sounds like the missing step in contract formation: Daniel never expressly told the offerors he was accepting the reward. The breaker is that a unilateral reward offer is accepted by performance, so a separate pre-arrest acceptance notice is not required. This answer invents a communication requirement that would distract from the real barrier.

B is attractive because it points to a fact the stem deliberately gives: Daniel knew about the reward before he began the surveillance. Gold Key: knowledge of the offer can matter for acceptance by an ordinary offeree, but it does not supply consideration when the person claiming the reward is already legally obligated to do the work. Silver Key: this is the acceptance gate, not the official-duty gate.

D is attractive because it states the ordinary unilateral-contract move: a person accepts a reward offer by completing the requested act. The breaker is that performance alone proves only the acceptance side of the problem. It does not defeat the preexisting official-duty rule, which is the reason Daniel cannot enforce the reward promise.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue is Contracts, Formation, Consideration, and the verified outline code is `55020204 Formation > Consideration > Preexisting duty`. The source row listed `55020205`, but `OUTLINE_CODES_COMPLETE.md` contains the deeper exact node `55020204 Formation > Consideration > Preexisting duty`, which fits the tested rule better than the broader sufficiency-of-consideration node.

The governing rule is the preexisting duty rule as applied to public officers and reward offers. Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 73 states the general contract principle that performance of an existing legal duty is not consideration when the duty is not doubtful or honestly disputed. The reward-offer application is confirmed by *Denney v. Reppert*, 432 S.W.2d 647 (Ky. 1968), where the court recognized that employees and public officials acting within the scope of their employment or official duties cannot claim a reward for doing those duties, while an officer acting outside his jurisdiction may be eligible. Research sources checked: https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/11720-bruckner-howard-law-contracts-2024/resources/2.6.5-restatement-second-of-contracts-73/ and https://law.justia.com/cases/kentucky/court-of-appeals/1968/432-s-w-2d-647-1.html.

Here, Daniel was an on-duty city police officer. The facts do not place him outside his jurisdiction, outside his shift, or outside the scope of ordinary police work. He apprehended a thief and returned stolen property, the same public-service work he was already obligated to perform. His performance therefore did not provide fresh consideration for Lydia and Barnabas's reward promise. The tested rule, legal issue, trap structure, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source question.
