### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Premeditation and deliberation are heightened, specific-intent mental states required for first-degree murder in this jurisdiction. Voluntary intoxication — if severe enough to prevent the defendant from reflecting on or planning the killing — is admissible to negate those specific-intent elements. If the jury finds that Timothy was too intoxicated to premeditate or deliberate, the first-degree murder charge fails. However, Timothy still intentionally killed Paul. That intentional killing satisfies the malice-aforethought requirement for at least second-degree murder (or voluntary manslaughter, depending on the jurisdiction's framework). Voluntary intoxication does not erase malice murder because malice is not a specific-intent element that intoxication can negate. The evidence may therefore reduce the degree of the homicide but does not produce a complete acquittal.

**Gold Key:** Voluntary intoxication is a defense only to *specific-intent* crimes — it can negate purpose, knowledge, and (critically for homicide) the premeditation and deliberation required for first-degree murder. It is *not* a defense to general-intent crimes, malice crimes, recklessness, or negligence. The distinction between first-degree murder (specific-intent element) and second-degree/malice murder (general intent) is the doctrinal hinge.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap):** This choice is attractive because students who learn that voluntary intoxication "is a defense" sometimes overgeneralize and conclude it wipes out all criminal liability. The trap is the leap from "can negate a specific element" to "complete acquittal." Voluntary intoxication may negate premeditation, but the defendant still intentionally killed — which satisfies malice murder. The intoxication defense reduces the degree; it does not erase the crime. **Gold Key:** Voluntary intoxication is a specific-intent defense only — it cannot negate malice, recklessness, or negligence.

**B:** This choice is attractive to students who memorize the rule that "voluntary intoxication is not a defense to general-intent crimes" and then incorrectly conclude it has *no relevance* to first-degree murder. But first-degree murder, by definition in this jurisdiction, requires premeditation and deliberation — specific-intent elements. Intoxication evidence is directly relevant to whether those elements were formed. The choice flatly misstates the law by treating all murder as general-intent and ignoring the specific-intent overlay of premeditation.

**D:** This choice conflates two entirely different doctrines: voluntary intoxication and insanity. Insanity requires a mental disease or defect that prevents the defendant from understanding the nature of their act or distinguishing right from wrong at the time of the offense. A defendant's later inability to remember the event — a blackout or amnesia — is not insanity. The choice misfits the doctrine to the facts.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Rule:** Voluntary intoxication may be admissible to negate the specific-intent elements of premeditation and deliberation required for first-degree murder, but it does not provide a complete defense and does not negate malice murder (second-degree).

**Authority:**
- **Model Penal Code § 2.08(1):** Evidence of voluntary intoxication is admissible to negate purpose or knowledge — the mental states required for specific-intent crimes. Premeditation and deliberation are treated as specific-intent overlays on the general-intent crime of murder.
- **Common law:** *Rex v. Carroll* (1835) 7 C. & P. 145 (voluntary drunkenness may be considered on the issue of premeditation); *Regina v. Cruse* (1838) 8 C. & P. 541 (gross intoxication may disprove specific intent).
- **State v. Keeton**, 272 S.E.2d 817 (W. Va. 1980) (voluntary intoxication may negate premeditation and deliberation for first-degree murder).
- **State v. Robinson**, 20 W. Va. 713 (1882) (same).
- **17-A M.R.S.A. § 37** (Maine) (intoxication may raise reasonable doubt as to required culpable mental state, except for recklessness).
- **Themis/Criminal Law outline** (LexisNexis): "Voluntary intoxication is a defense to specific intent crimes if it prevents the formation of the required intent. For example, intoxication may prevent the formation of the premeditation required for first-degree murder, but not second-degree murder."
