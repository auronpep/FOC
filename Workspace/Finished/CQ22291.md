### 7. Full right-answer explanation

The motion should be granted. The officer may have had enough suspicion to stop Daniel briefly and ask questions, but a frisk is a different step with a narrower purpose. A Terry frisk is a safety search for weapons, not a shortcut to feel for evidence. Here, the officer patted Daniel down to locate the missing Scripture case, and the stem gives no fact showing she feared Daniel was armed and dangerous. That makes the frisk invalid at inception. Gold Key: a lawful frisk exists to protect the officer from weapons, not to recover contraband or stolen property. Silver Key: when an answer choice celebrates what the officer found, back up and ask why the officer was allowed to touch the suspect at all. Because the frisk was evidence-driven from the start, the pistol was the fruit of an unlawful search and must be suppressed.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because it borrows the plain-feel idea: if an officer is lawfully patting down for weapons and immediately recognizes contraband or another incriminating object, seizure may follow even if the officer was not originally looking for that exact item. The breaker is the doorway problem. That rescue rule never starts unless the frisk itself is valid. Gold Key: no lawful weapons frisk, no plain-feel rescue.

**B.** This choice is attractive because it tracks the stop half of stop-and-frisk doctrine. Reasonable suspicion of theft can justify a brief detention and questions. The breaker is that suspicion of crime does not itself authorize a frisk for evidence. The frisk step needs a safety reason grounded in possible weapons, not just a belief that the suspect may have the stolen item.

**D.** This choice is attractive because a gun really was found during a patdown, which tempts students to bless the search by its result. The breaker is timing. The Fourth Amendment judges the frisk by the officer's justification when it began, and this officer started the patdown to recover the missing Scripture case. Silver Key: do not let the discovered gun rewrite the officer's original purpose.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and outcome are unchanged. The question still asks whether police may conduct a warrantless frisk for evidence during a brief investigative stop, then use a weapon found during that frisk.

- U.S. Const. amend. IV bars unreasonable searches and seizures.
- In *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), the Supreme Court held that an officer may briefly stop a person on reasonable suspicion and may conduct a carefully limited outer-clothing search only when the officer reasonably concludes the person may be armed and presently dangerous. The frisk is justified by officer safety, not by a general desire to search for evidence. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/392/1
- In *Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366 (1993), the Court explained that contraband detected through touch may be seized only during a lawful protective patdown that stays within Terry's bounds. The Court also reiterated that a protective search is not meant to discover evidence of crime and that fruits of a search that exceeds Terry must be suppressed. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/508/366/

Applied here, the stop itself may be arguable because the officer had a recent radio alert and saw Daniel leaving quickly. But the frisk was not justified on the facts given. The officer did not frisk because she thought Daniel was armed; she frisked to feel for the missing Scripture case. That purpose exceeds Terry's narrow weapons-safety rationale. Because the patdown was unlawful at inception, the pistol found inside Daniel's pocket is suppressible. The official key remains correct.
