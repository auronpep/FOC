### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B** is the answer. The motion should be denied because the requested exclusion is premature at the grand-jury stage. The point is not whether the office assistant acted as a government agent or whether the seizure could later support suppression at trial. **Gold Key:** the grand jury may consider evidence without a pre-indictment exclusionary-rule mini-trial. **Silver Key:** when the call is about barring grand-jury consideration, cut answers that litigate the search first.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This answer is the dominant trap because it dives straight into the Fourth Amendment agency issue. The breaker is timing. **Gold Key:** even if the search argument might matter later, Calandra keeps the exclusionary-rule fight out of the grand jury proceeding.

**C.** This answer reaches the correct denial but gives the wrong reason. The breaker is the police-agent fact. The assistant was persuaded by detectives, so the cleaner path is not private-citizen doctrine; the motion is premature.

**D.** This answer sells a probable-cause screen for the grand jury. The breaker is the grand jury's broad evidence-gathering function. The suppression motion does not become available just because the defendant questions probable cause.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are preserved. The original and this variation both ask whether a defendant can use an exclusionary-rule motion before indictment to bar allegedly illegal fingerprint evidence from the grand jury.

Under **United States v. Calandra, 414 U.S. 338 (1974)**, the exclusionary rule does not require suppression of illegally obtained evidence in grand-jury proceedings. The rule is a judicially created trial remedy aimed at deterrence, not a personal right to keep such evidence away from a grand jury. Therefore the motion is denied as premature.
