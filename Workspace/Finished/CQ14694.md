## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Peter is guilty of attempted murder. The crime of attempt requires (1) specific intent to commit the target crime and (2) a substantial step toward its commission. Peter had the specific intent to kill Barnabas — he armed himself, lay in wait, and fired at the spot where he believed Barnabas would be. Firing the weapon is a substantial step. The fact that Barnabas was actually out of range does not matter. This is a case of **factual impossibility** — the crime was impossible due to facts unknown to Peter — and factual impossibility is **not** a defense to attempt. Only **legal impossibility** (where the defendant's intended conduct is not actually criminal) would be a defense. Because Peter was unaware of the range limitation, his intent and his step are both fully present.

**Gold Key:** Factual impossibility is never a defense to attempt. Legal impossibility is the only impossibility defense.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Attempt** at common law requires: (1) **specific intent** to commit the target crime (general intent is insufficient; the defendant must have the purpose of bringing about the criminal result) and (2) a **substantial step** toward commission of the crime (more than mere preparation; conduct that strongly corroborates the defendant's criminal purpose). See *People v. Rizzo*, 246 N.Y. 334 (1927) (attempt requires intent + act beyond preparation); Model Penal Code § 5.01 (substantial step test).

**Factual impossibility** occurs when the defendant's intended criminal objective is impossible to achieve due to facts unknown to the defendant. It is **not** a defense to attempt. *People v. Lee Kong*, 95 Cal. 666 (1892) (defendant fired at a hole in a roof where he believed the victim was standing; victim had just moved — factual impossibility, still attempt). *State v. Moretti*, 52 N.M. 182 (1965) (defendant agreed to perform an abortion on an undercover officer who was not pregnant — factual impossibility, still attempt).

**Legal impossibility** occurs when the defendant's intended conduct, even if completed, would not constitute a crime. It **is** a defense. Example: receiving property the defendant believes is stolen, but which is not actually stolen — no crime of receiving stolen property because the property was not stolen.
