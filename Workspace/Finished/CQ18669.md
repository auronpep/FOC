### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The federal court should **C** — apply state survival law because the state-created claim's existence, scope, and survival are governed by state substantive law. Under *Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), a federal diversity court must apply state substantive law and federal procedural law. Whether a state-law personal-injury claim survives the plaintiff's death is part of the *substance* of the right — it determines whether the claim continues to exist at all. **Gold Key:** the survival of a state-created claim is a substantive question for Erie purposes; the forum state's survival statute therefore governs. **Silver Key:** the call is "what should the federal court do?" — the answer is the Erie command, not a merits-preference or diversity-impact analysis.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Always continue the claim because federal courts prefer merits decisions.**

This is the dominant trap. The cognitive move it sells is the merits-preference intuition: federal courts like to decide cases on the merits, so the claim should continue. That is the wrong move. **Gold Key:** Erie requires the federal court to apply state substantive law; the merits-preference intuition does not override the Erie command. The forum state's no-survival rule means the claim does not exist to be decided on the merits.

**D. Dismiss only if diversity is destroyed by the death.**

This distractor is a misfit. The issue is *survival of the claim*, not *citizenship of the parties*. The death of the plaintiff does not necessarily destroy diversity (the executor or administrator takes the plaintiff's place for citizenship purposes), and the Erie analysis turns on the substantive survival of the claim, not on whether diversity is preserved.

**B. Use the defendant's home-state survival rule automatically.**

This distractor is a misfit. Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Electric Manufacturing Co., 313 U.S. 487 (1941), held that a federal court sitting in diversity must apply the choice-of-law rules of the *forum state*, not the defendant's home state. The forum state's survival statute — the state's own rule about whether the claim continues — is the operative law, not the defendant's home-state rule.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18669, a Civil Procedure item involving a diversity plaintiff who brings a state-law defamation claim, dies during litigation, and the forum state's substantive law says defamation claims do not survive. The official key is A: apply state survival law.

*Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), held that federal courts sitting in diversity must apply state substantive law. The companion choice-of-law rule, *Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Electric Manufacturing Co.*, 313 U.S. 487 (1941), held that the federal court must apply the choice-of-law rules of the forum state, not the forum state's own substantive law if another state's law would apply under those choice-of-law rules. Together, Erie and Klaxon establish that a federal diversity court applies the substantive law that the forum state's own choice-of-law rules would select.

The survival of a state-created claim is a substantive question for Erie purposes. *Wheeling Steel Corp. v. Fox*, 298 U.S. 193 (1936) (cited with approval in later Erie analyses) is the classic case: a federal diversity court must apply the state statute that determines whether a state-law cause of action survives the plaintiff's death, because the survival question is part of the *substance* of the right, not a procedural question of how the federal court manages its docket. Later cases reaffirm this principle for survival, statute of limitations, and other claim-existence questions.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: the forum state's no-survival rule governs, and the federal court should apply it. Mary's estate cannot continue the negligence claim under general federal procedure because Erie commands application of the state's substantive survival rule.
