### 1. Final question

Four men — Peter, John, James, and Andrew — are charged in a single count with conspiracy to commit a series of grain-elevator thefts in three rural Iowa counties. Six successful grain-elevator thefts took place during the period of the charged conspiracy. Because the thieves wore coveralls, hooded jackets, and heavy work gloves, and because they broke and carried off the on-site surveillance hard drives, no direct identification of the thieves by the elevator staff has been made. Some circumstantial evidence ties each of the men to the overall conspiracy, including overlapping phone-tower pings, common storage-unit rentals, and partial fingerprint lifts on a cut padlock recovered at the fourth site.

During cross-examination, a prosecution witness testified that Peter was in county jail on an unrelated probation violation during four of the six thefts. Peter's lawyer has moved for a judgment of acquittal at the close of the government's case.

Should the motion be granted?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation (C)

The motion should be denied. Conspiracy is complete upon the formation of the agreement plus, in most jurisdictions, a single overt act in furtherance by any one of the conspirators. The substantive crimes the conspirators go on to commit are not elements of the conspiracy itself; the agreement is. The fact that Peter sat in county jail during four of the six grain-elevator thefts therefore says nothing about whether (i) Peter was a party to the original agreement, or (ii) the conspiracy ended the moment he was booked. It does not. *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640, 646–48 (1946), holds that the overt acts of one conspirator done in furtherance of the unlawful project are attributable to all; that principle presupposes that presence is not required to be a conspirator in the first place. And under 18 U.S.C. § 371 (and the parallel rule in the great majority of state codes), the arrest or incarceration of one conspirator does not terminate the conspiracy so long as at least two members continue to pursue the unlawful objective. Peter's absence from some overt-act crimes is therefore legally irrelevant to the conspiracy count. The court should deny the Rule 29–style motion, leaving it to the jury to weigh the agreement evidence.

**Gold Key:** *The crime of conspiracy is the agreement, not the acts.* Incarceration of one conspirator does not end the conspiracy as to the rest, and a conspirator is guilty of conspiracy even if absent from every overt act. *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640 (1946); 18 U.S.C. § 371.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A — "Yes, regardless of the alibi rule, because the government is bound by exculpatory evidence elicited during its case in chief."** (originally D — dominant trap)

The trap is the intuition that the prosecution is "stuck" with what its own witness said. That intuition is borrowed from civil procedure (the "party bound by uncontradicted testimony" idea) and it does not apply in a criminal case. In a criminal prosecution, the government is *never* "bound by" exculpatory evidence that surfaces during its case-in-chief; it is free to argue the witness is mistaken, hostile, lying, or simply one piece of a larger mosaic. The constitutional disclosure duty is *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963) — a duty to *turn over* material exculpatory evidence, not a duty to *concede* the case whenever its own witness opens an exculpatory door. The student move that picks A is treating cross-examination testimony as if it were a binding admission. Beyond that, even if the government were somehow "bound," the only thing bound is the witness's testimony about Peter's location — that still does not establish that the *conspiracy* ended, and the conspiracy charge has its own sufficiency standard. The motion would still be denied on the conspiracy count. **Gold Key: in a criminal case the government is not "bound by" testimony of its own witnesses — it may always contest credibility; that is the rule that defeats this trap.**

**B — "Yes, provided the defendant has complied with the rule requiring pretrial notice of alibi."** (originally C)

The bait here is a real procedural rule (Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.1 and its state analogues) that *does* exist and *does* govern criminal cases — but it governs the admissibility of undisclosed alibi *witnesses*, not the sufficiency of the evidence for a judgment of acquittal. The alibi rule answers the question "May the defendant put on an alibi witness whose name was not previously disclosed?" The motion in this question asks "Is the evidence as presented sufficient to convict?" Those are different questions. Even if Peter had fully complied with every alibi-notice requirement in the book, the prosecution would still be entitled to a sufficiency ruling on the conspiracy count, and the evidence of the agreement plus the four non-jail thefts plus the third party's testimony about Peter is sufficient for that count. The alibi rule is therefore a real rule in the wrong venue. **Silver Key: the call here is a *sufficiency* challenge, not an admissibility challenge. Sort the choices by what the call actually asks before weighing any procedural rule.**

**D — "No, because a conspirator is not required to agree to carry out every theft in the series."** (originally A)

The trap here is the temptation to grab a true-sounding conspiracy principle and call it dispositive. It is true, as a general matter, that a single conspiracy may have multiple criminal objectives and that a given conspirator need not sign on to each one — but that principle is irrelevant to the defense actually raised. The defense is a presence-based one: "I was in jail, so I could not have been at the thefts." The right rejoinder is *Pinkerton*: presence at the overt acts is not required. The "agree to all objects" point does not reach the question of whether Peter was at the thefts, because the question is not about agreement at all — it is about presence, which the agreement principle does not address. The student move that picks D answers a question the call did not ask.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification

**Tested rule.** A defendant may be convicted of conspiracy if the government proves (i) an agreement between two or more persons, (ii) to commit an unlawful act, (iii) with specific intent to achieve that objective, and (iv) in most jurisdictions, an overt act in furtherance of the agreement by any one of the conspirators. The substantive crimes the conspirators later commit are not elements of the conspiracy; the agreement is.

**Authority floor.**

- *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640 (1946) — "If that can be supplied by the act of one conspirator, we fail to see why the same or other acts in furtherance of the conspiracy are likewise not attributable to the others for the purpose of holding them responsible for the substantive offense." 328 U.S. at 647. The opinion's premise is precisely that a co-conspirator is liable on the strength of the agreement, *with or without personal presence* at the substantive acts.
- 18 U.S.C. § 371 — federal general conspiracy statute: requires an agreement to commit a federal offense or to defraud the United States, plus an overt act by at least one conspirator. The overt-act requirement is satisfied by the act of any single conspirator; the others need not participate, be present, or even know of the specific act. The arrest of one conspirator does not, by itself, terminate the conspiracy as to the rest.
- *United States v. United States Coin & Currency*, 401 U.S. 715, 721–22 (1971) (general principle that a conspiracy continues until its objectives are abandoned or achieved, or until it is otherwise terminated — *not* until one participant is detained).
- Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.1 — governs *admissibility* of undisclosed alibi *witnesses*; it does not govern sufficiency of the evidence for a judgment of acquittal and does not enter the sufficiency analysis on a Rule 29 motion.

**Right-answer legal reasoning.** The government has charged Peter with conspiracy, not with substantive grain-elevator theft. The fact that he was in jail during four of the six thefts is probative only of whether he *physically participated* in those particular thefts — a fact that matters for a substantive larceny charge, not for the conspiracy count. On the conspiracy count, the question is whether the evidence is sufficient for a reasonable jury to find an agreement that Peter joined, plus an overt act in furtherance by any one of the four co-conspirators. The third party's testimony that Peter was in jail for four thefts does not negate either the agreement or the overt-act element: the agreement can predate the incarcerations, and the other three conspirators' overt acts are imputed to the conspiracy as a whole. *Pinkerton*, 328 U.S. at 647. The motion for acquittal is therefore properly denied.

### 2. The 17-section student case study

#### program_frame

A criminal case in which four men are charged with a single count of conspiracy. The defense moves for a judgment of acquittal at the close of the government's case on the ground that one of the four — Peter — was in county jail during four of the six grain-elevator thefts the conspiracy produced. The question is whether that fact is fatal to the conspiracy charge. The MBE is testing whether the student knows that conspiracy is the agreement (not the acts), that presence at each overt-act crime is not required, and that the incarceration of one co-conspirator does not end the conspiracy as to the rest. The dominant trap is the "government is bound by exculpatory evidence" framing, which smuggles a civil-procedure intuition into a criminal case. The alibi-rule bait is a real procedural rule, but it is the wrong procedural rule for a sufficiency challenge.

#### choice_by_choice_walkthrough

**A. "Yes, regardless of compliance with the alibi rule, because the government is bound by exculpatory evidence elicited during its case in chief."** *(originally D, dominant trap)*

1. *Student-accessible C3 signal.* The phrase "the government is bound by exculpatory evidence" is a civil-procedure reflex. In a criminal case, the government is never "bound by" testimony of its own witnesses; it can attack credibility, argue the witness is mistaken, or simply put on additional evidence. The constitutional disclosure duty is *Brady v. Maryland* — a duty to *turn over* material exculpatory evidence, not a duty to *concede* the case.
2. *Student label.* "civil-procedure reflex imported into a criminal case."
3. *True-and-responsive version would look like.* "Yes, because the prosecution failed to disclose material exculpatory evidence in violation of *Brady v. Maryland*, requiring reversal under the appropriate prejudice standard." (That would be a *Brady* claim, not a sufficiency claim, and is not what this call asks.)
4. *Lawyer confirmation.* *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), imposes a disclosure duty; it does not create a "bound by" rule. The criminal rule (Fed. R. Evid. 607 and the parallel common-law rule) allows the government to impeach its own witnesses. The choice's premise reverses the criminal rule. **Gold Key: in a criminal case the government is not "bound by" testimony of its own witnesses — it may always contest credibility.**

**B. "Yes, provided the defendant has complied with the rule requiring pretrial notice of alibi."** *(originally C)*

1. *Student-accessible C3 signal.* The alibi rule is a real procedural rule (Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.1 and its state analogues), but it governs the *admissibility* of undisclosed alibi *witnesses*. The call here is a sufficiency challenge to a conspiracy charge — a different question. Even full compliance with the alibi rule does not entitle a defendant to acquittal on a conspiracy count if the agreement evidence is otherwise sufficient.
2. *Student label.* "importing the alibi-notice rule into a sufficiency challenge."
3. *True-and-responsive version would look like.* "The defendant's alibi witness will be excluded for failure to comply with the alibi-notice rule under Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.1(e)." (That would be a 12.1(e) ruling, not a Rule 29 ruling.)
4. *Lawyer confirmation.* Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.1 governs the disclosure of alibi witnesses; it does not govern the sufficiency of the evidence for a judgment of acquittal. The choice cites a real procedural rule that does not reach the call. **Silver Key: sort the choices by motion outcome first, then by whether the stated reason actually addresses a sufficiency challenge.**

**C. "No, because a conspirator need not be present at the commission of each theft."** *(originally B, correct)*

1. *Student-accessible C3 signal.* Conspiracy is the agreement. A co-conspirator is liable on the strength of the agreement even if absent from every overt-act crime. Incarceration of one conspirator does not end the conspiracy as to the rest, so long as the others continue the unlawful project.
2. *Student label.* "correct — conspirator's presence is not required."
3. *True-and-responsive version would look like.* "No, because a conspirator need not be present at the commission of each substantive crime and the incarceration of one co-conspirator does not terminate the conspiracy as to the rest." (That is the choice.)
4. *Lawyer confirmation.* *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640, 646–48 (1946) (overt acts of one conspirator done in furtherance of the conspiracy are attributable to all; this presupposes that presence is not required to be a conspirator). 18 U.S.C. § 371 (federal general conspiracy statute — agreement + overt act by any one conspirator). The arrest of one conspirator does not, by itself, terminate the conspiracy as to the rest.

**D. "No, because a conspirator is not required to agree to carry out every theft in the series."** *(originally A)*

1. *Student-accessible C3 signal.* The agreement-to-all-objects principle is a real conspiracy doctrine, but it addresses the *scope of the agreement*, not the *presence* of the defendant at each overt act. The defense raised is a presence defense ("I was in jail, so I wasn't at the thefts"), and the right rejoinder is the Pinkerton presence rule, not the agreement-scope rule.
2. *Student label.* "wrong axis — agreement to all objects, not presence at each overt act."
3. *True-and-responsive version would look like.* "No, because presence at each overt-act crime is not required for conspiracy liability." (That is choice C, not D.)
4. *Lawyer confirmation.* *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640 (1946) supplies the right rejoinder — that presence is not required for conspiracy liability, regardless of how the agreement's scope is defined.
