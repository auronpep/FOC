## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (C)

**Gold Key:** Under the objective theory of contract formation, mutual assent is determined by the parties' outward, objective manifestations — not by their subjective, unexpressed intentions. A contract is formed on the terms that a reasonable person in the offeree's position would understand from the offeror's words and conduct.

Here, Timothy's letter objectively offered $85,000. Peter, knowing that comparable plots sold for $80,000–$90,000, reasonably understood the offer to be $85,000. Peter neither knew nor had reason to know that Timothy intended $80,000. Because Timothy's outward manifestation was an offer of $85,000, and Peter accepted that offer, a contract was formed at $85,000. Timothy's subjective mistake does not defeat contract formation.

---

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap) — "No enforceable contract; mutual mistake/rescission":** This is attractive because the word "mistake" is in the fact pattern, and students reflexively associate mistake with mutual mistake doctrine. But mutual mistake requires *both* parties to share the same erroneous basic assumption about a material fact (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 152). Here, Peter was not mistaken about anything — he understood the offer as $85,000 and that is what he accepted. Only Timothy was mistaken. This is a *unilateral* mistake, and a unilateral mistake does not warrant rescission when the non-mistaken party neither knew nor had reason to know of the error. **Gold Key:** Rescission for mutual mistake requires both parties to be mistaken about the same basic assumption; a clerical error known only to one party is unilateral mistake, which does not support rescission when the other party was unaware of it.

**B — "No contract; parties attached materially different meanings":** This invokes the rule from Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 20(1), which provides that there is no mutual assent when parties attach materially different meanings to a term. But that rule applies only when *both* parties know or have reason to know of the ambiguity. Here, Peter had no reason to know Timothy meant $80,000. Timothy's secret intention does not create a "different meaning" that Peter attached to the price. Only one party was mistaken, so the § 20 misunderstanding rule does not apply.

**D — "Contract at $80,000":** This is attractive because it tracks Timothy's actual subjective intent — he *meant* to offer $80,000. But contract formation is governed by objective manifestations, not subjective intent. Timothy's outward act — the letter — said $85,000. A reasonable person in Peter's position would have understood $85,000. The law binds Timothy to what he objectively communicated, not what he privately intended.

## 9. Black-Letter Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Rule:** Under the objective theory of contract formation, mutual assent is determined by the parties' objective, outward manifestations of assent, not by their subjective, unexpressed intentions. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 20(1) (1981) ("There is no manifestation of mutual assent to an exchange if the parties attach materially different meanings to their manifestations and neither party knows or has reason to know the meaning attached by the other."); *id.* § 20 cmt. a ("Not mutual assent but a manifestation indicating such assent is what the law requires."). *See also* Embry v. Hargadine, McKittrick Dry Goods Co., 127 S.W. 647 (Mo. 1910) (objective standard controls even if subjective intent differs).

**Unilateral mistake:** A unilateral mistake — where only one party is mistaken about a term and the other party is unaware of the mistake — does not defeat contract formation. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 153 (unilateral mistake may support enforcement if the mistake is known or should be known to the other party, or if enforcement would be unconscionable). Here, Peter neither knew nor had reason to know of Timothy's clerical error. The price ($85,000) was within the range of comparable sales ($80,000–$90,000), so Peter's understanding was entirely reasonable.

**Mutual mistake inapplicable:** Mutual mistake under § 152 requires both parties to be mistaken about the same basic assumption. *See* Sherwood v. Walker, 66 Mich. 568 (1887) (both parties believed cow was barren). Peter was not mistaken about any fact. Only Timothy was mistaken. Therefore, mutual mistake doctrine does not apply.

**§ 20 misunderstanding inapplicable:** The "materially different meanings" rule under § 20(1) requires that both parties attach different meanings *and* that neither knows or should know the other's meaning — or, under § 20(2), if one party knows the other's meaning, that meaning controls. Here, Peter did not attach a "different meaning" — he understood the offer at face value. Timothy's secret intention is not a "meaning" Peter attached. The rule is inapplicable.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 20, 152, 153 (1981). The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original.
