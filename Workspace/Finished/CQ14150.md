## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question
Ruth, a Christian rancher, brought a suit in federal court against the manufacturer of her livestock transport trailer and the mobile service center that serviced it. She sued the manufacturer for negligent design of the ventilation system and automatic loading ramp because the ramp did not deploy under circumstances when it should have. She sued the service center for negligence in its repeated failures to fix the trailer's brake controller, which frequently failed to work properly. The manufacturer and service center then moved for dismissal on the ground of misjoinder. Should the court grant the defendants' motion to dismiss?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
In a federal suit, when claims against two defendants arise from distinct problems with the same piece of equipment (negligent design of one system versus repeated repair failures in a different system), are the defendants misjoined under Rule 20, and if so, is dismissal the proper remedy or must the court use severance instead?

### 6. Student black-letter-law answer explanation
C is correct. The claims against the manufacturer and service center were misjoined because they did not arise out of the same transaction, occurrence, or series of transactions or occurrences, and there is no common question of law or fact. The design-defect claims concerning the ventilation and ramp systems are entirely distinct from the repeated-repair claims concerning the brake controller. However, under FRCP 21, misjoinder of parties is not a ground for dismissing an action. The proper remedy is severance of the claims. The court should deny the motion to dismiss but may sever on just terms.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is the dominant trap. It is attractive because students correctly spot that the two sets of claims involve different mechanical systems and therefore do not share a common transaction or series, but it sells the false remedy that misjoinder kills the whole case. The breaker is the explicit text of FRCP 21: misjoinder is not a ground for dismissal; the court may sever instead. Gold Key: "Misjoinder of parties is not a ground for dismissing an action. ... The court may also sever any claim against a party." Fed. R. Civ. P. 21.

B is attractive because students may think that if the problems are all on one trailer, they must be part of a single series and therefore properly joined. The breaker is that "one piece of equipment" is not enough; the claims must arise from the same transaction or series and share a common question of law or fact. Here the design of the ramp/ventilation and the repeated brake repairs are separate problems with no indicated common legal or factual question that would make joinder efficient.

D is attractive because it feels like a fairness or vicarious-liability point (manufacturer should not be dragged in for the service center's mistakes). The breaker is that FRCP 20 joinder of defendants does not require that the defendants be liable to one another or for each other's acts; it requires only that the claims arise from the same transaction/series and share a common question. The rule is about efficient resolution of related claims, not about cross-liability.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested black-letter rule is unchanged: under Fed. R. Civ. P. 20(a)(2), defendants may be joined in one action if the claims against them arise out of the same transaction, occurrence, or series of transactions or occurrences AND any question of law or fact common to all defendants will arise in the action. Under Fed. R. Civ. P. 21, "Misjoinder of parties is not a ground for dismissing an action. On motion or on its own, the court may at any time, on just terms, add or drop a party. The court may also sever any claim against a party."

Research confirmation (current as of 2026): The text of FRCP 21 has remained stable since the 2007 stylistic restyling and continues to provide exactly that misjoinder is not a ground for dismissal and that severance is an available remedy. See Fed. R. Civ. P. 21 (available at https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_21 and official U.S. Courts rules compilations; tracking parameters stripped). No doctrinal shift affects the rule or the credited outcome. The original key (D, now mapped to C) remains correct because the claims are misjoined (distinct systems, no common Q of law/fact, one trailer insufficient to create a "series") but dismissal is forbidden; severance is the remedy. The transform preserves the legal substance, the misjoinder finding, and the remedy distinction exactly.
