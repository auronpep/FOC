## Final Question

During a noisy setup for a Bible-quiz fundraiser, Stephen shoved Paul and swung first. Paul knocked Stephen backward onto a stack of gym mats. As Stephen pushed himself up, Paul grabbed a heavy brass handbell from the display table and drove it into Stephen's face, intending to keep Stephen from rejoining the scuffle. The blow destroyed sight in Stephen's right eye and left permanent scarring around the socket. Paul later said he only meant to keep Stephen down, not to blind or scar him.

Under common law, which element analysis is most critical to determining whether Paul committed mayhem?

## Distilled Core Question

When a defendant intentionally uses force that permanently disables or disfigures another person, is mayhem decided by the permanent injury alone or by whether the defendant had the required maiming mental state?

## Final Answer Choices

A. Whether Stephen started the fight, because provocation is a complete defense to mayhem.

B. Whether Paul intended to maim or permanently disfigure Stephen, as opposed to merely intending to keep Stephen out of the scuffle.

C. Whether the injury was permanent, because permanent loss of sight or disfigurement satisfies all elements of mayhem without any mental state inquiry.

D. Whether Paul acted with reckless disregard for the risk of permanent injury.

## Correct Answer

## Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. The fact that Stephen suffered a classic mayhem-type injury gets the question into the right offense, but it does not end the element analysis. The critical question is Paul's mental state when he used the handbell. Gold Key: common-law mayhem is not a mere bad-result crime; the prosecution must prove unlawful, malicious maiming, commonly tested as intent to maim or disfigure, or at least a willful attack made with awareness that such disabling injury would result. Silver Key: when the array offers "mental state" against "permanent injury alone," count elements before reacting to the worst fact.

If Paul merely intended a temporary restraint and did not act with the required maiming mental state, the mayhem charge is vulnerable even though a serious permanent injury occurred. If the factfinder infers from the deliberate blow to the eye that Paul meant to blind, scar, or disable Stephen, the mental-state element is satisfied.

## Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because Stephen started the fight, and students want the first wrongdoer to lose the protection of the criminal law. The breaker is that provocation is not a categorical defense to mayhem; at most, the fight-starting fact may affect a separate proportional self-defense analysis.

C is attractive because the injury is dramatic, permanent, and exactly the kind of harm mayhem cares about. The breaker is the Gold Key: mayhem still requires the required malicious maiming mental state, so permanence supplies the injury element but not every element.

D is attractive because reckless disregard sounds like a serious enough mental state for a brutal fight injury. The breaker is that common-law mayhem asks for malicious maiming, not mere recklessness as the decisive mens rea.

## Black-Letter Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, trap structure, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item: common-law mayhem requires a qualifying permanent disabling or disfiguring injury plus the required malicious maiming mental state; the key element dispute is intent/malice, not recklessness, provocation, or permanence alone.

Authority layer: Blackstone describes common-law mayhem as the violent deprivation or disabling of a member that makes the victim less able to fight, including disabling or weakening a hand or finger and striking out an eye. See 4 William Blackstone, Commentaries, ch. 15, hosted by Yale Avalon Project: https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/blackstone_bk4ch15.asp. United States v. Cook, 462 F.2d 301 (D.C. Cir. 1972), discussing Blackstone and common-law mayhem, confirms the historical focus on disabling injuries and permanence. See https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/591497a6add7b049345f7143. Cornell Wex summarizes mayhem as a common-law felony requiring intent to maim or cause bodily harm with unlawful force causing permanent dismemberment or disablement, while noting jurisdictional variation on general versus specific intent. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/mayhem.

Applied here, loss of sight and permanent scarring satisfy the qualifying-injury lane. But the most critical remaining analysis is whether Paul acted with the malicious maiming mental state. The handbell blow may support that inference, but the stated purpose of only keeping Stephen down makes mental state the fight.
