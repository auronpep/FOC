### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** The Sixth Amendment right to counsel is offense-specific. Appointment of counsel on the robbery charge does not automatically attach the Sixth Amendment right to the separate, uncharged invoice-forgery scheme. Because Esther received Miranda warnings and waived them before discussing the uncharged invoice matter, the Sixth Amendment robbery counsel right does not itself suppress the invoice-scheme statement.

**Gold Key:** The Sixth Amendment counsel right attaches only after adversary proceedings begin and is offense-specific. Counsel on one charged offense is not a blanket shield against questioning about a different uncharged offense.

**Silver Key:** Split the question into two columns: which right and which offense. Custody points you toward Miranda/Fifth Amendment; a charged case with counsel points you toward the Sixth Amendment, but only for that charged offense and any same offense.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (custody makes the Sixth Amendment apply to all questioning)** attracts the student who correctly notices custody but routes it to the wrong constitutional trigger. Custody is the Miranda/Fifth Amendment signal. It does not make the Sixth Amendment attach to every topic detectives ask about. **Silver Key:** Keep the right separate from the setting: custody asks whether Miranda was honored; the Sixth Amendment asks whether adversary proceedings have begun for that offense.

**B (admissible only if the invoice scheme occurred before the robbery)** sells a timeline rule that sounds orderly but does not answer the Sixth Amendment problem. The relevant question is not which crime happened first. The relevant question is whether the charged robbery and the uncharged invoice scheme are the same offense for Sixth Amendment purposes, and they are not.

**C (appointment of counsel bars all uncharged-offense questioning)** is the dominant trap because it converts a real protection into an absolute. The Sixth Amendment does protect an accused after adversary proceedings begin, but the protection is offense-specific. **Gold Key:** Appointment of counsel on the robbery does not invoke a universal Sixth Amendment bar against questioning about every other uncharged crime.

### 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

**Governing rule:** The Sixth Amendment provides a right to counsel in criminal prosecutions, but that right attaches only after adversary judicial criminal proceedings begin and is offense-specific. In *McNeil v. Wisconsin*, 501 U.S. 171 (1991), the Court treated the Sixth Amendment right as tied to the particular charged offense and allowed statements about uncharged offenses despite attachment on a separate charge. In *Texas v. Cobb*, 532 U.S. 162 (2001), the Court reaffirmed that the right is offense-specific and extends only to charged offenses and offenses that would be the same offense under the *Blockburger* same-elements test. Miranda warnings can also be sufficient to support a knowing waiver of counsel rights during questioning. *Patterson v. Illinois*, 487 U.S. 285 (1988).

**Application:** Esther had counsel appointed only on the robbery charge. The invoice-forgery scheme was unrelated and uncharged. Nothing in the variant makes the invoice scheme the same offense as robbery under the same-elements test. The detectives gave Miranda warnings, and Esther waived them. The Sixth Amendment right attached to the robbery case therefore does not itself require suppression of the invoice-scheme statement.
