# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question — stem + call

Barnabas Boatworks, LLC was sued in the federal district court in Ashford. Barnabas
Boatworks' attorney filed an answer that stated: "Defendant denies the allegations of the
complaint. Defendant reserves all defenses, including but not limited to any defenses
relating to jurisdiction." Four months later, Barnabas Boatworks attended a scheduling
conference, produced thousands of pages of documents in discovery, and took three
depositions. Before the close of discovery, Barnabas Boatworks filed a motion to dismiss
for lack of personal jurisdiction. The plaintiff argues the motion is untimely because the
company waived personal jurisdiction by engaging in discovery. Has Barnabas Boatworks
waived its personal-jurisdiction defense?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A company's answer generically "reserves all defenses, including jurisdictional defenses,"
then the company litigates the merits for months (scheduling conference, document
production, depositions) and only afterward moves to dismiss for lack of personal
jurisdiction. Has it waived the PJ defense?

**D.** Yes, because although the answer referred to jurisdictional defenses only

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**D is correct.** Two independent failures stack into waiver. First, under Rule 12(h)(1)
the personal-jurisdiction defense must be *specifically* raised in the first Rule 12 motion
or the first responsive pleading; a vague "reserves all defenses, including jurisdictional"
clause is not a specific assertion of the objection. Second, even crediting the reservation,
four months of merits litigation — appearing at the scheduling conference, producing
thousands of pages, and taking three depositions — without ever pressing the objection is
extensive merits engagement that courts treat as implied consent to the court's authority.
The credited answer is the one that ties *both* threads together. **Gold Key:** a generic
jurisdictional reservation is not a specific PJ assertion, and sustained post-answer merits
litigation is implied consent that waives PJ — Rule 12(h)(1) governs and the
special/general-appearance distinction is gone. **Silver Key:** when two choices share the
credited outcome ("Yes, waived"), the sweeping/automatic one is the trap and the precise
fact-combining one is the answer — choose **D** over **C**.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A — "filed before discovery closed, so it's timely."** It sells a tidy timing intuition:
the company moved *during* discovery, so surely it acted in time. The breaker: PJ timing is
measured from the first Rule 12 motion or first responsive pleading, never from a later
milestone like the discovery cutoff. **Gold Key:** "before discovery closed" is not a
recognized safe harbor for a PJ motion — the clock runs from the pleading stage, and the
defense had to be specifically raised and pressed there.

**B — "the reservation preserved it indefinitely."** It sells the comfort of a magic clause:
the lawyer wrote "reserves all defenses, including jurisdictional," so the objection feels
locked in for the whole case. The breaker: an over-absolute "for as long as the case
remained pending" ignores that waiver is a totality determination — a generic reservation
neither counts as a specific assertion nor immunizes the defendant against waiver by later
conduct. **Silver Key:** a choice that hangs the entire result on one talismanic phrase in a
waiver question is over-absolute — cut it.

**C — "discovery is a general appearance that waives PJ." (dominant trap)** It sells the
right outcome through a familiar rule: the company litigated, so it must have "appeared" and
forfeited jurisdiction — and it even lands on "Yes, waived." The breaker: federal practice
abolished the special/general-appearance distinction; discovery participation does not
*automatically* waive PJ, and the credited answer turns on the *combination* of a
non-specific pleading defense and extensive conduct, not a per-se rule. **Gold Key:** there
is no automatic "general appearance" waiver in federal court — Rule 12(h)(1) plus
conduct-based implied consent govern. **Silver Key:** between two "Yes" choices, reject the
automatic/per-se one and take the precise combination.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

**Tested rule (unchanged).** Personal jurisdiction is a waivable individual right. Under
**FRCP 12(h)(1)**, the defenses of lack of personal jurisdiction, improper venue,
insufficient process, and insufficient service of process are waived if not raised in a
Rule 12 motion or included in the first responsive pleading — and the assertion must be
specific. Beyond the pleading rule, a party may waive or forfeit PJ through litigation
conduct: *Insurance Corp. of Ireland v. Compagnie des Bauxites de Guinée*, 456 U.S. 694
(1982) (PJ is a personal right subject to waiver, including by a party's conduct in the
litigation). Federal courts have abandoned the old special/general-appearance distinction;
Rule 12 supplies the framework.

**Application.** Here both prongs cut the same way: (a) the answer's generic "reserves all
defenses, including jurisdictional" is not the specific assertion Rule 12(h)(1) requires, and
(b) four months of scheduling-conference participation, document production, and three
depositions without pressing the objection is extensive merits engagement amounting to
implied consent. The credited answer (new **D**) rests on that combination. The tested rule,
legal issue, and credited outcome are **unchanged** from the source item (original key C, a
"Yes, waived — combination" answer). `attorney_review: false` for the authority line —
FRCP 12(h)(1) and *Insurance Corp. of Ireland* are confidently real and on point.
