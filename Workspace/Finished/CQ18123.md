### 6. Right-Answer Explanation

The moment Barnabas paid and loaded the grain, two facts were locked in: (1) he exercised control over stolen property, and (2) he knew it was stolen. The dock worker's own statement — "I pulled these off a shipment before it was logged in" — told him so directly. The cut identification tags independently confirmed the theft. Both elements were satisfied at the instant of receipt.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIMINAL-RECV-STOLEN-01):** Receiving stolen property is complete the moment a person receives or exercises control over stolen goods while knowing or believing they are stolen. No later sale, transfer, or other disposition is required.

**Silver Key (SK-CRIMINAL-RECV-01):** When a "No, unless [future act]..." answer appears in a receiving-stolen-property question, that answer is inventing a post-receipt element. The call asks about guilt at the moment of receipt. Lock onto that moment and the future-conduct condition disappears.

### 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A — "No, because a low price is never relevant to knowledge"**

The appeal here is that $30 alone might not prove Barnabas knew the grain was stolen — price seems like thin proof. But "never relevant" is an absolute, and absolutes almost always overclaim. A suspiciously low price is classic circumstantial evidence that a buyer knew or believed goods were stolen; courts rely on it alongside admissions and tamper evidence regularly. The absolute collapses the moment a single counterexample exists, and here low price is one of three independent indicators of knowledge.

**Choice B — "No, unless Barnabas later sells the grain" (dominant trap)**

This choice sells the idea that receiving stolen property is a "fencing" crime — that active dealing, not just acceptance, completes the offense. That intuition is wrong. Knowingly receiving and exercising control over stolen property is the complete act. What Barnabas does with the grain afterward — sell it, eat it, return it — is legally irrelevant to whether he already committed the offense.

**Gold Key:** Receiving stolen property requires no later disposition. The crime closes the moment Barnabas loaded the grain onto his cart.

**Silver Key:** "No, unless Barnabas later sells" adds a future act to the guilt determination. When a "No, unless [later action]..." choice appears in a receiving-stolen-property question, that condition was invented — cut it.

**Choice D — "No, because only the original thief can commit a property offense"**

The pull here is the intuition that "property crimes" belong to whoever took the property originally. Receiving stolen property exists precisely to criminalize the knowing buyer's side of the transaction. It is a separate offense from the underlying theft, and a defendant need not have participated in the original taking to be guilty of receiving.

### 8. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Rule:** Receiving stolen property requires: (1) receiving or exercising control over (2) property that is stolen, (3) knowing or believing it to be stolen. The offense is complete upon knowing receipt; no further act of sale, transfer, or use is required.

**Application:** The dock worker admitted the grain was pulled from an unlogged shipment (direct statement of theft). The cut identification tags corroborate the admission. The $30 price for a commercial volume of grain further supports the inference of knowledge. Barnabas received control when he paid and loaded the grain. Both elements were satisfied at that moment.

**Legal reasoning — why C is correct:** The question matches the elements precisely. Barnabas had actual knowledge (worker's admission) and corroborating physical evidence (tamper evidence) at the moment of receipt. No further element is missing.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer):** Model Penal Code § 223.6(1): "A person is guilty of theft if he purposely receives, retains, or disposes of movable property of another knowing that it has been stolen, or believing that it has probably been stolen." No later-sale or disposition requirement appears in this formulation or in any standard common-law recitation of the offense. Circumstantial evidence — including price, explicit admissions, and physical tamper evidence — is universally recognized as sufficient to establish the knowledge element. See LaFave, Criminal Law § 20.4 (5th ed. 2010); Perkins & Boyce, Criminal Law 354–58 (3d ed. 1982).

**Card:** RC-CRIMINAL-RECV-STOLEN-01 — *Receiving Stolen Property — Offense Complete on Receipt*

**Signal:** Buyer accepted admitted-stolen goods with physical tamper evidence; a dominant wrong answer adds a future-conduct condition ("unless later sells").

**Student move:** Confirm both elements — control + knowledge/belief — at the moment of receipt. Any answer adding a post-receipt requirement is inventing an element.

**Tiny rule:** Receiving stolen property = control + knowing/believing stolen. Complete at receipt. No resale needed.

**Trap:** Dominant trap invents a fencing/resale requirement.

---

## Gold Key(s)

**GK-CRIMINAL-RECV-STOLEN-01**

Receiving stolen property is complete the moment a person exercises control over stolen goods while knowing or believing they are stolen — no later sale, transfer, or other disposition is required.

- **Type:** rule
- **Unlocks:** Dominant trap B (invented resale requirement)
- **Trigger:** Receiving-stolen-property question where the buyer accepted the goods but has not resold or transferred them; watch for "No, unless he later..." answer choices.
- **Tested choice:** B
- **Authority (lawyer-confirmation only):** MPC § 223.6(1); LaFave, Criminal Law § 20.4 (5th ed. 2010).
- **Last-minute review:** true

---

## Silver Key(s)

**SK-CRIMINAL-RECV-01**

In receiving-stolen-property questions, anchor the guilt analysis to the exact moment the defendant received control. Any "No, unless [future act]..." answer invents a post-receipt element the law does not impose.

- **Type:** call_focus
- **Navigates:** Tension between guilt-at-receipt (C) and invented resale requirement (B, dominant trap)
- **Trigger:** Any "No, unless [later action]..." answer choice in a receiving-stolen-property question
- **Tested choice:** B
- **Outline code:** 73030801
- **Last-minute review:** true

#### wrong_answer_recovery_paths

**Picked A:** Missed the "never" absolute. Recovery: Run EAR — "never/always/only" in a legal claim = red flag. Ask: could price EVER matter? Yes, alongside an admission and tamper evidence, it does. Cut any choice that uses an uncashed absolute.

**Picked B (dominant trap):** Believes receiving stolen property requires later resale. Recovery: Gold Key — the offense is complete at the moment of knowing receipt. The crime is not about what you do with stolen goods; it's about knowingly taking control of them. No later act is an element.

**Picked D:** Believes property crimes are only for the original taker. Recovery: Receiving stolen property is a separate, standalone crime. The dock worker committed theft. Barnabas committed receiving stolen property. Two different defendants, two different offenses, same stolen grain.

#### crossover_intersection_map

- **CRIMINAL/73030100 (Larceny):** Both are property crimes, but larceny requires a trespassory taking by the defendant; receiving requires knowing possession by the recipient. Students conflate them. The dock worker committed larceny; Barnabas committed receiving.
- **EVIDENCE (circumstantial proof of mental state):** The worker's admission, cut tags, and low price are all circumstantial evidence of the knowledge element. Crossover to Evidence: how admissions and physical evidence prove mens rea in criminal cases.

#### review_truth

A person is guilty of receiving stolen property when they receive or exercise control over stolen goods while knowing or believing those goods are stolen — the offense is complete at the moment of knowing receipt.
