## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** Under the rule of Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 38(2), a manifestation of intention not to accept an offer is a rejection *unless* the offeree manifests an intention to take it under further advisement. A reply that merely requests information about an offer's terms is the classic "inquiry" — it expresses no intention to reject and no intention to accept on different terms. The original offer therefore remained open.

Peter's text offering the boat for $40,000 was a definite offer. John's reply asking whether the Minn Kota trolling motor was included was a question about the offer's *scope*, not a counteroffer (which would have to propose definite new terms — see Restatement § 39) and not a rejection. Peter's "It does not" was a clarification, not a revocation; the offer stayed on the same terms. When John later texted "I accept your offer," that was an unequivocal acceptance of the original offer. Peter's attempted revocation came too late — the contract had already been formed by mirror-image acceptance. (Authority: Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 24, 35, 38, 39, 50; *Stevenson, Jacques & Co. v. McLean* (1880) Q.B.D. 346 — the leading case holding that a telegraphic request for clarification is an inquiry, not a counteroffer.)

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

### A — "No, because the offer lapsed before John accepted." (DOMINANT TRAP, 13% inherited)

**What makes it attractive.** Peter told John "the offer is no longer open," and the exchange played out across multiple text messages. Students project a "this has been going on for too long" feeling onto a faceless text thread, and "the offer lapsed" sounds plausible. The mechanic sells the timing intuition.

**The breaker.** An offer's power of acceptance terminates by lapse only at the time specified in the offer, or — if no time is stated — at the end of a *reasonable* time. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 41(1). Peter's text did not include any "reply by" date or duration. The whole exchange happened within minutes, and the text-plus-purchase choreography is exactly what a reasonable-time acceptance looks like. Visible structure: no express deadline in the offer, prompt response in the reply thread. Cut on no-express-time-limit + prompt acceptance.

### B — "No, because John's initial text was a counteroffer." (8% inherited)

**What makes it attractive.** "John said something about the terms of the offer — that must be a counteroffer." Students reflexively label any offeree response that touches the offer's terms as a counteroffer.

**The breaker.** A counteroffer is a *new offer* proposing *definite different terms*. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 39. A question asking whether a specific item is included proposes nothing — it asks for clarification. Restatement § 38(2) preserves "under further advisement" responses from operating as a rejection, and an inquiry is the textbook example. Visible structure: "Does the offer include the trolling motor?" is a yes/no question, not a definite proposal. Cut on question ≠ counteroffer.

### D — "Yes, because John relied on the offer by ordering the trolling motor." (5% inherited)

**What makes it attractive.** John actually did something in reliance — he ordered a new Minn Kota trolling motor. "He relied, so a contract was created" sounds like a clean story. The reliance theory is a real Contracts doctrine (option contracts, Restatement § 87 / promissory estoppel), so the rule the student cites is "true in the right area."

**The breaker.** The call asks whether there is a *contract*, not whether the offer was *irrevocable*. Reliance can convert an offer into an *option contract* (precluding revocation) but it does not, on its own, form a sale contract. The contract here was formed by offer + acceptance — John's "I accept your offer" text. D is right that there is a contract; it is wrong about the *mechanism* that produced one. Restatement § 87 supports an option-contract outcome, not a sale contract. Cut on wrong element for the call. The "I accept" text is the dispositive contract-formation fact; the trolling-motor order is a side fact, not a contract-forming fact.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested rule.** A reply to an offer that asks for information about the offer's terms is a request for information (an "inquiry"), not a counteroffer or rejection; the offer remains open and may be accepted later by unequivocal assent. (Subject: Contracts; subtopic: Offer / Mutual Assent; outline_code 55020101.)

**Governing authority.**
- **Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 38** — Rejection. "(1) An offeree's power of acceptance is terminated by his rejection of the offer, unless the offeror has manifested a contrary intention. (2) A manifestation of intention not to accept an offer is a rejection unless the offeree manifests an intention to take it under further advisement." The "under further advisement" carve-out is the doctrinal basis for the inquiry rule.
- **Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 39** — Counter-offers. "A counter-offer is an offer made by an offeree to his offeror relating to the same matter as the original offer and proposing a substituted contract differing from the one proposed by the original offer."
- **Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 41** — Lapse of Time. "(1) An offeree's power of acceptance is terminated at the time specified in the offer, or, if no time is specified, at the end of a reasonable time."
- **Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 50** — Acceptance of Offer Defined; Acceptance by Promise; Acceptance by Performance.
- **Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 24** — Offer Defined.
- **Stevenson, Jacques & Co. v. McLean**, 2 Q.B.D. 346 (1880) — leading case. The offeree telegraphed the seller asking whether he would accept a different price, payment terms, and "reply tomorrow morning." The court held that the telegram was a request for information, not a counteroffer, and the original offer therefore remained open for acceptance. The original offer was later accepted and a contract was formed.

**Doctrinal-currency check.** This is a general common-law doctrine on contract formation; no recent Supreme Court case or Restatement amendment has altered the rule. The rule is consistent with UCC § 2-207 (a reply that is not an acceptance is not governed by § 2-207's "additional terms" analysis; the offer remains open). No currency flag.

**Regime note.** The source is a real-property sale (common law); the new variation is a boat (tangible goods, UCC Art 2). The inquiry/counteroffer distinction operates the same way under both regimes, because a request for information is neither an acceptance-with-different-terms (UCC § 2-207(1)) nor a counteroffer (§ 2-207(3)). The rule being tested is regime-agnostic. No new legal issue.

#### Distilled Core Question

Does a buyer's reply to a seller's offer, asking whether a particular item is included, terminate the offer as a counteroffer, or is it a request for information that leaves the offer open for a later acceptance?

#### Call and prediction

**Call:** "Is there a contract for the sale of the boat?"
**Prediction:** Yes, contract formed.
**Why this prediction fits:** John's first text asked a question, not proposed new terms. Peter's "It does not" was a clarification, not a revocation. John's "I accept your offer" was an unequivocal acceptance of the original offer. No time limit + prompt response = no lapse. No new terms in the reply = no counteroffer. The contract was formed by offer + acceptance.

#### Trigger facts

- "John asked whether the trolling motor was included" — classifies the reply as an inquiry (not a counteroffer, not a rejection).
- "Peter said 'It does not.'" — clarification, not revocation; the offer stayed on the same terms.
- "John then ordered a new Minn Kota trolling motor" — side fact; supports the reliance distractor (D) but does not itself form a contract.
- "John texted 'I accept your offer'" — unequivocal acceptance of the original offer → contract formed.
- No express time limit in the offer — kills the lapse trap (reasonable time applies).
- The whole exchange was within minutes — reinforces reasonable time.
- Peter refused, claiming the offer was no longer open — attempted revocation, came after acceptance, ineffective.

#### Choice-by-choice walkthrough

**A. "No, because the offer lapsed before John accepted."**

1. *Student-accessible C3 signal.* Did the offer say "reply by" or have an express time limit? No. Then lapse requires *reasonable* time, and the whole exchange was within minutes. Visible structure: no deadline + prompt reply. No lapse.
2. *Student label.* Lapse misconception.
3. *What a true/responsive version would look like.* "No, because the offer expressly expired by its own terms (e.g., 'Reply within 48 hours') and John accepted after that window." The stem doesn't support that.
4. *Lawyer confirmation.* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 41(1): the offeree's power of acceptance is terminated at the time specified in the offer, or, if no time is specified, at the end of a reasonable time. Here: no time specified + prompt acceptance = no lapse. Also: Peter's revocation came after John's acceptance, so the offer had already been exercised before any "lapse" was even available.

**B. "No, because John's initial text was a counteroffer."**

1. *Student-accessible C3 signal.* Did John propose *definite* new terms? No — he asked a yes/no question. A question is not a counteroffer. A counteroffer would say something like "I'll take it for $35,000" or "I accept if you include the motor." John's text proposed nothing.
2. *Student label.* Inquiry mislabeled as counteroffer.
3. *What a true/responsive version would look like.* "No, because John's initial text proposed a substituted term (e.g., 'I'll take it for $35,000' or 'only if you include the motor'), which terminated Peter's offer as a counteroffer." The stem doesn't support that.
4. *Lawyer confirmation.* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 39: a counter-offer is an offer made by an offeree to the offeror relating to the same matter as the original offer and proposing a substituted contract differing from the one proposed. A request for information proposes nothing. Restatement § 38(2) carves out "under further advisement" responses from the rejection rule. See *Stevenson, Jacques & Co. v. McLean* (1880) Q.B.D. 346.

**C. CORRECT. "Yes, because John's initial text merely asked for information."**

1. *Student-accessible C3 signal.* John's text was a question, not a counteroffer, not a rejection. The offer remained open. John's next text "I accept your offer" was an unequivocal acceptance. Contract formed.
2. *Student label.* Inquiry preserved the offer; acceptance formed the contract.
3. *What a true/responsive version would look like.* This is the true, responsive answer. No rewrite needed.
4. *Lawyer confirmation.* Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 24 (offer), 38(2) (rejection excludes "under further advisement"), 39 (counteroffer requires substituted terms), 50(1) (acceptance by promise). Classic statement: *Stevenson, Jacques & Co. v. McLean* (1880) Q.B.D. 346 (request for information is not a counteroffer).

**D. "Yes, because John relied on the offer by ordering the trolling motor."**

1. *Student-accessible C3 signal.* The call asks "Is there a contract." Reliance can create an *option contract* (an irrevocable offer), but it does not by itself form a sale contract. D reaches the right outcome for the wrong reason. The contract here was formed by offer + acceptance. CUT on wrong element.
2. *Student label.* Reliance ≠ sale contract.
3. *What a true/responsive version would look like.* "Yes, because John's purchase of the trolling motor was the type of substantial reliance on Peter's offer that the seller reasonably could have expected at the time of the offer, converting Peter's offer into an irrevocable option contract; and John subsequently accepted." (Note: even this rewrite would be a half-truth — the contract here was formed by offer + acceptance, not by reliance. The original official key rejected the reliance rationale.)
4. *Lawyer confirmation.* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 87 (option contract) / § 90 (promissory estoppel) supports offer irrevocability by reliance, not sale-contract formation. The dispositive contract-forming fact here is John's "I accept your offer" text. The trolling-motor order is a side fact.
