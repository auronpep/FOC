## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

At a homeschool co-op picnic, Lydia mentioned that she planned to sell her privately owned lakeside retreat cabin and the fenced prayer-garden tract around it. Peter was interested. After several weeks of emails, Lydia and Peter signed a written contract for Peter to buy the realty for $412,000, with title to be delivered no later than November 20. Lydia also promised that the cabin would be empty when title was delivered.

The contract provided: "The risk of loss from nonnegligent causes remains with Lydia until delivery of title."

On November 6, lightning struck the cabin and it burned down. Neither Lydia nor Peter was at fault. Lydia was still using the cabin for weekend retreats, and title had not yet been transferred. The jurisdiction does not recognize the doctrine of equitable conversion.

After the fire, if Peter seeks to rescind the contract, may he do so?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

When a land-sale contract expressly keeps nonnegligent risk of loss on the seller until title is delivered, may the buyer rescind after the building is destroyed before title passes?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. Yes, because the jurisdiction has rejected the doctrine of equitable conversion.

B. No, because the risk of loss shifted to Peter as soon as he signed the real estate contract.

C. Yes, because the parties agreed that the risk of nonnegligent loss would remain with Lydia until title was delivered.

D. No, because the jurisdiction does not recognize the doctrine of equitable conversion.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. The contract itself allocated the risk of nonnegligent loss to Lydia until title was delivered. The cabin burned before title was delivered, and neither side was at fault, so Lydia still bore the risk. Gold Key: default land-sale risk-of-loss doctrines yield to an express risk-allocation clause. Silver Key: when a stem gives a direct contract rule and then adds a default doctrine fact, apply the direct contract rule first.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because the jurisdiction's rejection of equitable conversion points away from making Peter the equitable owner at contract formation. The breaker is priority: the parties wrote their own risk-of-loss rule, so the default doctrine is not the reason Peter may rescind.

B is attractive because equitable conversion often says a land buyer bears risk once the contract is formed. The breaker is twofold: this jurisdiction rejects that doctrine, and the parties expressly kept the risk on Lydia until title was delivered.

D is attractive because it uses the same jurisdictional fact that appears in the stem. The breaker is direction: rejecting equitable conversion does not make Peter bear the loss; it cuts against a no-rescission answer.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited outcome is unchanged: Peter may rescind because Lydia bore the risk when the cabin was destroyed. In land-sale contracts, equitable conversion is a default doctrine that may place risk on the buyer after contract formation, while Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act jurisdictions generally keep risk on the seller until title or possession passes. But both default frameworks yield to an express contractual allocation of risk. Here, the contract stated that nonnegligent risk of loss stayed with Lydia until delivery of title. Title had not been delivered, Lydia still had possession, and the fire was nonnegligent, so the contractual condition for shifting risk had not occurred.

Lawyer-confirmation authority: New York General Obligations Law section 5-1311, an enactment of the Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act, states that land-sale risk duties apply "unless the contract expressly provides otherwise" and keeps the risk on the vendor when neither title nor possession has transferred. North Carolina General Statutes section 39-39 uses the same express-contract carveout and the same title-or-possession risk allocation. These authorities confirm the exam rule that default land-sale risk-of-loss rules do not override the parties' express risk clause.
