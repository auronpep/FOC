## 6. Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Article III, § 2, clause 2 of the U.S. Constitution provides that the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction exists "with such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the Congress shall make." This Exceptions Clause gives Congress broad authority to carve out categories of cases that the Supreme Court may not hear on direct appeal. The statute here creates a targeted exception: it removes the Court's appellate jurisdiction over a specific class of state-court judgments (final criminal and quasi-criminal proceedings). This is precisely the kind of exception Congress is empowered to make. The structure of Article III itself contemplates that Congress will define — and may narrow — the Court's appellate reach. *See Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 506 (1869) (upholding Congress's repeal of Supreme Court appellate jurisdiction over habeas corpus appeals); *see also* U.S. Const. art. III, § 2, cl. 2.

**Gold Key (GK-CONLAW-JUDREV-01):** *Exceptions Clause unlocked —* When you see a question asking whether Congress can prevent the Supreme Court from hearing a certain category of appeals, the answer almost always traces to the Exceptions Clause of Article III, § 2: "the Supreme Court shall have appellate jurisdiction, both as to Law and Fact, with such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the Congress shall make." This is one of Congress's most potent structural tools. The Credited Answer is C.

## 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

### A — Why it's attractive and why it fails

Criminal law is indeed primarily a state concern, and the statement that state courts have broad authority over their own contempt powers is a true general proposition. A student who spots the word "traditionally" and senses a federalism theme may be drawn to this answer because it *feels* right in the abstract. But the question is not about whether state courts have contempt power — it's about whether **Congress** can shield those state court judgments from Supreme Court appellate review. The state-sovereignty truism doesn't answer the structural question about congressional authority. It's a misfit: the doctrine invoked (state primacy in criminal law) is not the doctrine at issue (Congress's Exceptions Clause power). **NOT_RESPONSIVE.**

*Silver Key (SK-CONLAW-JUDREV-01) applies: When the call asks what best defends a federal statute's constitutionality, discard any choice that states a true general principle but doesn't engage the structural power at issue.*

### B — Why it's attractive and why it fails

Habeas corpus is an important post-conviction remedy, and students learn early that it provides a federal forum for challenging unlawful detention. The attractiveness here lies in the veneer of sophistication — it sounds like a lawyerly observation about the correct procedural path. But the statement is misleading as a defense of the statute's constitutionality. Habeas is a **collateral** civil challenge to unlawful imprisonment — it is not a form of direct appellate review of a state court judgment. Saying "the proper means is habeas" implicitly concedes that direct appeal is the normal mechanism and habeas is a workaround, which actually undercuts the statute's premise. Moreover, the observation doesn't answer *whether Congress has the power* to strip the direct appeal. The choice is a half-truth distortion. **NOT_TRUE.**

### D — Why it's attractive and why it fails (DOMINANT TRAP)

This is the most dangerous distractor because it contains a true statement presented in a framing that sounds like it could be the answer. It is indeed true that state court contempt order review does **not** fall within the Supreme Court's *original* jurisdiction. A student who fixates on "original jurisdiction" may select this answer because the statement is technically correct and it uses proper jurisdiction terminology. But the call is about **appellate** jurisdiction — the whole point of the statute is to restrict the Supreme Court's appellate reach, not its original jurisdiction. By reframing the issue around original jurisdiction, this choice tempts students who confuse the two or who grab onto a true-sounding procedural point without checking whether it matches the call. The Gold Key (GK-CONLAW-JUDREV-01) helps here: when the question is about Congress stripping the Court's appellate pathway, the answer must engage the Exceptions Clause — not a tangential observation about original jurisdiction. **NOT_RESPONSIVE.**

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** Article III, § 2, clause 2 of the U.S. Constitution (the Exceptions Clause) provides:

> "In all Cases... the supreme Court shall have appellate Jurisdiction, both as to Law and Fact, with such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the Congress shall make."

**Authority:** *Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 506 (1869). In *McCardle*, the Supreme Court upheld Congress's repeal of the Court's appellate jurisdiction to hear habeas corpus appeals under the Habeas Corpus Act of 1867. Chief Justice Chase, writing for a unanimous Court, held that the Constitution confers appellate jurisdiction on the Court "with such Exceptions and under such Regulations as Congress shall make," and that Congress's exercise of this power was valid. The Court dismissed the case for lack of jurisdiction, expressly acknowledging Congress's authority to withdraw appellate jurisdiction even after a case had been argued.

**Additional authority:** Congressional Research Service, *Congress's Power over Court Decisions: Jurisdiction Stripping and the Rule of Klein*, CRS Report R44967 (citing *McCardle* and noting Congress's broad authority under the Exceptions Clause); *see also* U.S. Const. art. III, § 2, cl. 2.

**Doctrinal notes (lawyer-confirmation layer):**
- Congress may eliminate specific avenues for Supreme Court appellate review without eliminating all appellate jurisdiction.
- There are scholarly debates about outer limits (e.g., whether Congress could deny *all* federal review of constitutional claims, raising due process concerns), but for the MBE, the black-letter rule is that Congress has broad power under the Exceptions Clause.
- The Court's *original* jurisdiction (Art. III, § 2, cl. 1 — ambassador cases, state-as-party cases) cannot be expanded or contracted by Congress; but appellate jurisdiction is subject to congressional exceptions.
- Habeas corpus original jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 2241 is distinct from appellate jurisdiction stripping cases.

**Verification against original:** The tested rule (Congress's Exceptions Clause power to limit SCOTUS appellate jurisdiction), the legal issue (constitutionality of a statute stripping appellate review), and the credited answer's legal substance (Congress can make exceptions) are all **unchanged** from the original. The transform is doctrinally sound.
