# Pass-1 Transform Report — Q19596 (Criminal · Homicide Causation)

## 1. Final Question

During an argument after a weekend retreat, Lydia snapped a rubber prayer wristband against Stephen's forearm once, leaving a faint red mark. Stephen walked away unaided and unharmed. Fifteen minutes later, while standing alone beside a public plaza two blocks away, a loose metal awning from a closed bakery tore free in a sudden gust and struck Stephen, killing him. The wristband snap did not affect where Stephen went or his ability to avoid danger.

Is Lydia guilty of homicide for Stephen's death?

**A.** Yes, because an unlawful battery followed by a death is manslaughter.

**B.** No, because the falling awning was an independent event unrelated to the risk Lydia created.

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** Homicide liability requires the defendant's conduct to cause the death. Lydia's wristband snap may be a minor battery, but it did not put Stephen beside the bakery, impair him, expose him to the falling awning, or increase the risk that the awning would strike him. The death came from a later independent event outside the risk Lydia created.

**Gold Key:** Misdemeanor manslaughter still requires causation. An unlawful battery is not converted into homicide merely because a death occurs afterward; the unlawful act must actually and proximately cause the death.

**Silver Key:** Read the chain, not the clock. The question is not whether Lydia touched Stephen before he died; the question is whether Lydia's act moved him into the death risk or made him less able to avoid it.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### Choice A — Dominant Trap

**Why it is attractive:** This choice sounds like the unlawful-act manslaughter rule. It invites the student to treat "battery first, death later" as the whole homicide analysis.

**The breaker:** The unlawful act must cause the death. Here, the falling awning was independent of the wristband snap and unrelated to the risk created by that snap. **Gold Key:** Misdemeanor manslaughter still needs actual and proximate causation; sequence alone is not enough.

### Choice C

**Why it is attractive:** This choice reaches the right bottom-line "No" answer and may appeal to a student who thinks the touch was too minor to matter. It tries to solve the case by minimizing the first act.

**The breaker:** Battery can be criminal. The reason Lydia is not guilty of homicide is not that battery is legally impossible; it is that the later death was not caused by the battery.

### Choice D

**Why it is attractive:** The statement that Lydia committed a battery may be true. It gives the prosecution a real wrong to point to before Stephen's death.

**The breaker:** A prior battery is not the same as homicide causation. **Silver Key:** The clock order does not answer the call; the chain must connect Lydia's risk to Stephen's death.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule:** Criminal homicide requires causation. The defendant's conduct must be an actual cause of death and must also satisfy legal or proximate causation. A later independent event that is unrelated to the defendant's risk and that replaces, rather than flows from, that risk can break the causal chain.

**Authority floor:** Model Penal Code § 2.03 supplies the rule-number floor for criminal causation: conduct must be a but-for antecedent and must satisfy any additional causal requirements of the offense; where the result is too remote or accidental to fairly bear on liability, causation fails. Source checked: H2O Open Casebook reproduction of MPC § 2.03, https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/6141-tanaka-criminal-law-casebook/resources/8.1.1-model-penal-code-mpc-203-causal-relationship-between-conduct-and-result-divergence-between-result-designed-or-contemplated-and-actual-result-or-between-probable-and-actual-result/. The common-law homicide causation frame is also consistent with *Rogers v. Tennessee*, 532 U.S. 451 (2001), which discusses homicide liability in terms of death by the defendant's act and the year-and-a-day causation limitation; source checked: Library of Congress U.S. Reports PDF, https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep532/usrep532451/usrep532451.pdf.

**Right-answer legal reasoning:** Lydia's act was not a factual or proximate cause of Stephen's death. The facts expressly remove any causal bridge: the wristband snap did not affect Stephen's location, condition, movement, or ability to avoid danger. The awning's collapse was a separate event outside the risk of a minor battery. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row: the defendant is not guilty of homicide when the death is caused by an independent superseding event unrelated to the defendant's act.

**Key verification:** The researched rule confirms the supplied key. There is no disagreement between the legal authority and the official answer.
