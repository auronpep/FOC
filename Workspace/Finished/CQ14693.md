## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.** Murder fails because Daniel's shots did not cause Lydia's death. She had already died from the dock fall before he fired. But Daniel still believed Lydia was alive and shot her with the purpose of killing her, so he committed attempted murder rather than murder. Martha intentionally hired and procured that attempted killing, so she can be convicted of attempted murder as an accomplice.

**Gold Key:** a dead victim defeats murder causation, not attempt, when the would-be killer believes the victim is still alive and acts to kill.

**Gold Key:** once the hired killer takes a substantial step toward the killing, the person who solicited and paid for it is not capped at solicitation alone; she can be liable for the offense the killer attempted.

**Silver Key:** this is a descending-seriousness ladder. Test the top rung first. If murder fails, stop at the first lower rung whose elements still fit; do not slide all the way down to conspiracy or solicitation once attempt is satisfied.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Conspiracy.**
This answer is attractive because the agreement and the surveillance are real, and conspiracy is unquestionably in the case. The breaker is the call. The question asks for the **most serious** crime. Once Daniel moved beyond agreement and took the attempted-killing step, conspiracy became a true but lower rung.

**B. Murder.**
This is the dominant trap because a real death occurred and Daniel fired real shots. That makes completed murder feel emotionally right. The breaker is causation. Lydia was already dead before Daniel fired, so the shooting did not cause her death. **Gold Key:** no causation means no murder, even though attempt remains.

**D. Solicitation.**
This answer sells the earliest clean offense in the sequence: Martha clearly hired Daniel to kill Lydia. The breaker is that the ladder did not stop there. Daniel bought the gun, stalked the alley, and fired at the person he believed alive. **Silver Key:** when later conduct satisfies a higher inchoate rung, do not answer with the opening rung.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

I infer from the source row's lack of a named jurisdiction that the bank is testing the general bar-exam rule reflected in standard attempt and accomplice authorities.

Attempt law looks to the circumstances as the actor believes them to be. Model Penal Code section 5.01 treats attempt liability that way, and *People v. Dlugash*, 41 N.Y.2d 725 (1977), is the classic dead-victim example: the actor may be guilty of attempted murder if he believed the victim was still alive and acted with intent to kill. That same framework explains why murder fails here. Murder requires causation of death, and Lydia was already dead before Daniel fired.

Martha's liability rises above solicitation because accomplice doctrine reaches the offense she intentionally procured. Model Penal Code section 2.06 states that a person is an accomplice if, with the purpose of promoting or facilitating the offense, she solicits another person to commit it. Martha hired Daniel to kill Lydia, and Daniel then took the attempted-killing step. That supports attempted-murder liability for Martha through Daniel's attempt. Conspiracy and solicitation are also satisfied on these facts, but they are lower on the seriousness ladder than attempted murder.
