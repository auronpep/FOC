### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. The detective did not ask a neutral recordkeeping question like name, address, or date of birth. He asked the robbery suspect for the same handle witnesses had connected to the prize-table robbery. Gold Key: the routine booking exception protects administrative biographical questions, not questions police should know are reasonably likely to produce an incriminating answer. Silver Key: do not let the place where the question is asked decide the answer; ask whether the question is routine administration or investigation. Because this question targeted the charged robbery alias before warnings, Martha's answer is excluded from the prosecution's case-in-chief.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
**A.** This choice is attractive because it reaches the right "No" result and sounds protective of the Fifth Amendment. The breaker is the word "every." Alias evidence is not categorically barred; routine, noninvestigative identity questions can be asked during booking, but this particular alias question was investigative.

**C.** This choice is attractive because handles and nicknames can look like ordinary identifying information. The breaker is the context. Here the detective asked for the exact handle witnesses had tied to the robbery, so the "biographical" label does not make it routine as a matter of law. Gold Key: administrative identity questions are different from questions reasonably likely to draw out incriminating identity evidence.

**D.** This is the dominant trap because the word "booking" is loud in the stem and in the exception's name. The breaker is Silver Key: the location and paperwork label do not control; the purpose and likely effect of the question do. A robbery detective asking for the robbery handle is not doing neutral intake.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue is Miranda interrogation during booking, not the substantive elements of robbery. The verified outline code is `75080100`, which appears in `OUTLINE_CODES_COMPLETE.md` as "Fifth and Sixth Amendments--Statements, Confessions, and Identifications > Statements and Confessions." The source row listed `73030400` for robbery, but the actual rule tested is the Miranda routine-booking boundary.

Under the Fifth Amendment and *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), the prosecution generally may not use statements from custodial interrogation unless Miranda safeguards were given. *Rhode Island v. Innis*, 446 U.S. 291 (1980), defines interrogation to include express questioning or its functional equivalent when police should know it is reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. *Pennsylvania v. Muniz*, 496 U.S. 582 (1990), recognizes a routine-booking category for biographical data needed for booking or pretrial services, while preserving the limit that investigative or incriminating questions do not become routine merely because they occur at booking. Sources checked: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/384/436/, https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/amdt5-4-7-4/ALDE_00013689/, and https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/496/582/.

Martha was in custody, no warnings had been given, and the detective's question was directly tied to the robbery identifier witnesses supplied. The answer "Candle Crown" was testimonial and incriminating because the prosecution offered it to identify Martha as the robber. The routine booking exception does not make that answer admissible in the prosecution's case-in-chief. Research agrees with the official key, so the validation verdict is PASS.
