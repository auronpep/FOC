### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**B is correct.** Search incident to arrest is narrow. It covers the arrestee and the area the arrestee could reach for a weapon or destructible evidence at the time of arrest. Timothy was handcuffed at the kitchen table with an officer beside him. A bedroom wardrobe and closed storage bin down the hall were not within his immediate control. The stem gives no consent, exigency, warrant, or valid protective-sweep facts, so the pistol and cash pouch should be suppressed.

**Gold Key:** Search incident to arrest inside a home is not a search-the-house rule; it is limited to the arrestee and the reachable area where the arrestee could grab a weapon or destroy evidence. **Silver Key:** In a result-plus-reason array, do not stop at "Yes" or "No." The winning answer must give the correct Fourth Amendment reason.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. Probable cause to search the cottage.** This choice is attractive because armed robbery evidence probably was in the cottage and the officers found exactly the kind of items they were looking for. The breaker is the home-search warrant rule. Probable cause alone does not authorize a warrantless evidence search of a home; the police still need a warrant or a recognized exception.

**C. Protective sweep first.** This choice reaches the right suppression result, which makes it easy to keep too long. The reason is the trap. A protective sweep is a limited safety inspection for people who might pose a danger, not a required first step that unlocks an evidence search. **Silver Key:** The two "Yes" answers clash on the reason; choose the yes answer tied to immediate control, not the one tied to a sweep that the facts do not support.

**D. Entire dwelling incident to arrest.** This choice sells the broadest and most common SITA myth. The breaker is the Gold Key: a home arrest does not let officers search every room, wardrobe, drawer, or container for evidence. The search is limited to Timothy and the area he could actually reach.

### 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: a search incident to lawful arrest reaches the arrestee and the area within the arrestee's immediate control, meaning the area from which the arrestee might gain possession of a weapon or destructible evidence. It does not authorize a full search of the premises. Because Timothy was handcuffed in the kitchen and the officer searched a bedroom wardrobe and closed storage bin down the hall, the searched area was outside the permissible SITA zone. No other exception appears.

Lawyer-confirmation authority: *Chimel v. California*, 395 U.S. 752 (1969), is the governing search-incident-to-arrest authority for an in-home arrest and rejects a general house search incident to arrest. *Maryland v. Buie*, 494 U.S. 325 (1990), confirms that a protective sweep is a quick, limited safety search for persons when justified by specific danger facts; it is not an evidence search and does not replace the Chimel immediate-control limit. The Fourth Amendment supplies the constitutional warrant baseline.
