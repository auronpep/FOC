### 1. Final question

In a jurisdiction that follows the minority alter-ego rule, Peter is leaving a chapel parking lot after a youth-group hymn night when he sees plainclothes Officer Lydia pinning John against a van. Under the alter-ego rule, does Peter have a defense-of-others claim?

If a bystander reasonably but mistakenly uses force to help a person who actually had no right to resist a lawful arrest, does the bystander have defense of others under the alter-ego rule?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is the best answer. The stem supplies the minority alter-ego rule. Under that rule, Peter does not win just because his mistake was reasonable. He gets only the defense John actually had. John was being lawfully arrested and had no right to resist, so Peter has no defense-of-others claim. Gold Key: alter-ego defense of others asks whether the protected person actually had a self-defense right, not whether the intervener reasonably believed one existed. Silver Key: when the stem names a minority rule, apply that rule before reaching for the modern reasonable-belief instinct.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is the attractive answer because Peter's mistake is expressly reasonable. The breaker is that reasonable belief is the modern-rule move, while the stem forces the minority alter-ego rule. Gold Key: alter ego makes Peter borrow John's actual legal position.

B sounds decisive because it avoids rewarding Peter for hitting an officer. The breaker is that defense of others is a recognized doctrine; Peter loses because this version of the doctrine is limited, not because the doctrine does not exist.

C has surface appeal because Peter is a passerby helping someone he thinks is in danger. The breaker is that the problem is not Peter's relationship to John. Under alter ego, Peter's claim depends on whether John actually had the right to resist.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: in a minority alter-ego jurisdiction, defense of others depends on whether the person defended actually had the right to use self-defense. A reasonable mistake by the intervener would matter under the modern reasonable-belief rule, but it does not save the intervener under the supplied alter-ego rule.

State v. Cook, 78 S.C. 253, 59 S.E. 862 (1907), is a primary example of the alter-ego approach; the court treated the intervener's right as no greater than the right of the person defended. The accessible vLex page identifies Cook as South Carolina's adoption of the alter-ego rule: https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/state-v-cook-930540522. South Carolina's Attorney General opinion summarizes Cook and explains that the intervener stands in the shoes of the person protected: https://www.scag.gov/wp-content/uploads/2013/12/96aug23keel.pdf. That opinion also cites State v. Sales, 285 S.C. 113, 328 S.E.2d 619 (1985), for the related point that an intervenor assumes the protected person's rights and limitations. Finally, Town of Springdale v. Butler, 299 S.C. 276, 384 S.E.2d 697 (1989), supports the lawful-arrest premise by rejecting force against officers where the arrest was lawful: https://law.justia.com/cases/south-carolina/supreme-court/1989/23059-2.html.

Peter therefore loses. John had no actual right to resist Lydia's lawful arrest. Under alter ego, Peter can claim no greater defense than John had.
