## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** When the initial taking of property is trespassory (no permission), the trespass is treated as *continuing* through the defendant's possession. If the defendant later forms the intent to permanently deprive the owner, the ongoing trespassory taking and the newly formed intent concur at that moment — satisfying every element of larceny.

Lydia took Daniel's guitar without permission, so her possession was trespassory from the start. She initially intended to return it, so larcenous intent was absent at the moment of taking. But when she decided to keep the guitar permanently, the continuing-trespass doctrine treats the original trespass as still "occurring." The trespassory taking (actus reus) and the intent to permanently deprive (mens rea) therefore concurred at the moment she formed that intent. All elements of larceny were complete at that point.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Elements of larceny (common law):** (1) trespassory taking; (2) and carrying away (asportation); (3) of personal property; (4) of another; (5) with the intent to permanently deprive the owner thereof.

**Concurrence requirement:** The actus reus (trespassory taking) and mens rea (intent to permanently deprive) must coincide — the intent must exist at the time of the taking.

**Continuing-trespass doctrine:** Where the initial taking itself is trespassory (no consent, no permission), the trespass is treated as continuing throughout the defendant's possession. If the defendant later forms the intent to permanently deprive, the ongoing trespassory taking and the newly formed intent are deemed to concur at that moment, satisfying the concurrence requirement. *See* State v. Coombs, 55 Me. 477 (Maine 1868); 52 Am. Jur., Trespass § 18; Wikipedia, "Continuing trespass" (citing Coombs).

**Application:** Lydia took Daniel's guitar without permission — trespassory taking, element (1) satisfied. She carried it away — asportation, element (2). It was Daniel's personal property, elements (3)–(4). She initially intended to return it, so element (5) was absent at the moment of taking. However, when she decided to keep it permanently, the continuing-trespass doctrine treats the original trespass as ongoing. The trespassory taking and the newly formed intent to permanently deprive concurred at that moment. All five elements were satisfied. Larceny was complete when she formed the intent to keep the guitar.

**Embezzlement distinction:** Embezzlement is the fraudulent conversion of another's property by one who is in *lawful* possession. Lawful possession means the owner voluntarily transferred possession to the defendant (e.g., by entrustment, employment, or bailment). Lydia's possession was never lawful — she took the guitar without permission. Therefore, embezzlement does not apply. *See* the original question's explanation and standard criminal law treatises.

**Verification:** The tested rule (continuing-trespass doctrine for larceny), the legal issue (timing of concurrence between trespassory taking and intent to permanently deprive), and the credited outcome (larceny committed at the moment intent was formed) are all unchanged from the original. Research confirms the doctrine is well-established and the official key is correct.

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

#### Program Frame

This item tests the continuing-trespass doctrine in larceny — specifically, how the common law resolves the concurrence problem when a defendant takes property without permission but forms the intent to permanently deprive *after* the taking. The item is anchor-assisted: the student needs a taught doctrinal key (the continuing-trespass rule) to eliminate the two most attractive distractors and to confirm the credited answer.

#### Student Access Test

A student who has learned the continuing-trespass Gold Key and the embezzlement-vs-larceny distinction Gold Key can solve this item. The C3 structural signals (absolute language in A, the "no intent" truth in D) allow partial progress via CUT, but the CLASH between B and C requires anchor knowledge.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Lydia borrowed her neighbor Daniel's acoustic guitar without asking, planning to practice with it for one evening and return it before Daniel noticed. After playing it for several hours, Lydia decided the guitar sounded far better than hers and resolved to keep it permanently, never returning it. Daniel had never given Lydia permission to take the guitar.

**Call:** At what point, if any, did Lydia commit larceny?

**Choices:**
- (A) Lydia committed larceny when she first took the guitar, because any unauthorized taking is larceny.
- (B) Lydia committed larceny when she decided to keep the guitar, because the continuing-trespass doctrine supplies the required concurrence at that moment.
- (C) Lydia committed embezzlement, not larceny, because she had lawful possession when she formed the intent to keep the guitar.
- (D) Lydia committed no larceny because she lacked intent to steal when she first took the guitar.

#### Distilled Core Question

A defendant takes another's personal property without permission, initially intending to return it, but later decides to keep it permanently. At what point is larceny committed?

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks "at what point" — a timing question. The student should recognize this as a concurrence problem: the act (taking) and the intent (to permanently deprive) don't happen at the same time. The prediction is that the answer involves a doctrine that bridges the timing gap.

#### Trigger Facts

1. **"Borrowed without asking"** → trespassory taking (no permission). This is the foundational fact.
2. **"Planning to practice and return it"** → no larcenous intent at the time of taking.
3. **"After playing for several hours, decided it sounded far better"** → triggering event for change of intent.
4. **"Resolved to keep it permanently, never returning it"** → intent to permanently deprive formed.
5. **"Daniel had never given Lydia permission"** → reinforces trespassory nature; kills any embezzlement theory.

#### Governing C3 Lane

**CUT** → eliminate choices with visible structural defects (absolute language, true-but-incomplete reasoning).
**CLASH** → when two survivors remain, name the axis (lawful vs. trespassory possession) and find the splitting fact.
**CALL** → apply the Gold Key to resolve the axis.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A:**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The word "any" is a structural red flag. "Any unauthorized taking is larceny" is an absolute claim. Absolute claims in MBE answer choices are almost always overbroad.
2. **Student label:** The overclaim trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Lydia committed larceny when she first took the guitar, because she took it without permission and intended to permanently deprive Daniel of it." (But this is factually wrong — she intended to return it.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Larceny requires specific intent to permanently deprive at the time of taking. Lydia intended to return the guitar. The crime was not complete at that point.

**Choice B:**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice names the continuing-trespass doctrine — the exact doctrinal tool for a concurrence-timing problem. If the student has the Gold Key, this is the answer.
2. **Student label:** The correct answer.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This choice IS true and responsive. It correctly identifies the timing (when she decided to keep it) and the doctrine that makes it work.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The continuing-trespass doctrine treats the original trespassory taking as continuing. When Lydia formed the intent to keep the guitar, the ongoing trespass and the new intent concurred, completing larceny.

**Choice C:**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice swaps the crime from larceny to embezzlement. The key question: was Lydia's possession lawful? The stem says "without asking" and "never given permission" — that's trespassory, not lawful.
2. **Student label:** The crime-swap trap (dominant).
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Lydia committed embezzlement because Daniel had entrusted the guitar to her and she later converted it." (But this is factually wrong — Daniel never entrusted anything.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Embezzlement requires lawful possession at the outset. Lydia's possession was trespassory from the start.

**Choice D:**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice states a true fact (no initial intent) and draws a conclusion that seems logical ("no intent = no crime"). But it ignores the possibility of a doctrine that bridges the timing gap.
2. **Student label:** The doctrine-ignore trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Lydia committed no larceny because she lacked intent to steal when she first took the guitar, and no subsequent events changed her mental state." (But this is factually wrong — she did subsequently form the intent.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The continuing-trespass doctrine exists precisely for this scenario. The original trespass continues, and when intent is later formed, larceny is complete.

#### Residual Answer

**B** — Lydia committed larceny when she decided to keep the guitar.

When she decided to keep it permanently, the continuing-trespass doctrine treats the original trespass as ongoing. The trespassory taking and the newly formed intent to permanently deprive concurred at that moment. Larceny was complete when she decided to keep the guitar. **Answer: B.**
