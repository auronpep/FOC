## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Deputies lawfully arrest Naomi for a burglary at a private Christian bookstore. During the arrest, an officer removes Naomi's smartphone from her jacket pocket, powers it down, and places it in an evidence pouch. No emergency exists. Without getting a warrant, a detective later opens the phone's photo gallery and finds pictures of boxed study Bibles hidden in a storage unit.

Should the photos be suppressed?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

After a lawful arrest, may officers search the digital photo gallery of a secured phone without a warrant or emergency?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. No, because a smartphone is treated like a wallet or pocket notebook once officers lawfully seize it.

B. Yes, but only if the bookstore-burglary charge is later dismissed.

C. No, because search incident to arrest allows officers to inspect every item taken from the arrestee.

D. Yes, because officers generally need a warrant to search the digital contents of a secured phone after arrest.

### 5. Correct answer

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is correct. Police may seize and secure the phone during a lawful arrest, but the digital contents are different. Gold Key: Riley requires a warrant before police search the digital data on a cell phone seized from an arrestee, unless a real emergency or another exception applies. Silver Key: split the object from the data; taking the phone is not the same as opening the phone. The detective opened a secured phone's photo gallery without a warrant and with no emergency, so the photos should be suppressed.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells the old-container analogy. A wallet or pocket notebook can invite search-incident instincts, but a modern phone is not treated as an ordinary container for its digital contents. Gold Key: Riley separates physical seizure of the device from searching the data inside it.

B sells a timing-and-remedy detour. Suppression turns on whether the search was lawful when it happened, not on whether the burglary charge later survives. A later dismissal does not supply the Fourth Amendment rule.

C sells the broadest search-incident shortcut. The arrest allowed officers to take and secure the phone, but it did not automatically authorize a digital rummage through the gallery. Silver Key: search incident does not mean every item plus every file.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is unchanged. The Fourth Amendment generally requires police to get a warrant before searching digital information on a cell phone seized from an arrested person. Riley v. California, 573 U.S. 373 (2014), holds that the search-incident-to-arrest exception does not, by itself, permit officers to search digital phone contents; officers may secure the device while seeking a warrant, and exigent circumstances can still justify urgent action. Here the phone was secured and no emergency existed. Opening the photo gallery without a warrant exceeded search-incident authority, so suppression is the credited outcome.

Authority layer: U.S. Const. amend. IV; Riley v. California, 573 U.S. 373 (2014).
