## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Lydia passed the state examination required for a license as a physical therapist and applied to the State Board of Physical Therapy for her license. While volunteering after evening Bible study at a winter coat drive, city police arrested Lydia after a dispute over a borrowed delivery van. At Lydia's arraignment, the judge dismissed the charge with the public prosecutor's consent. The Board refused to issue Lydia the license solely because of that arrest.

Lydia challenged the refusal in state court and lost. She then filed an action in federal district court seeking an order directing the Board to issue the license.

Should the federal court hear Lydia's claim?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

May a federal court hear a constitutional due process claim when a state licensing board denies a professional license solely because of an arrest that was dismissed?

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is the intended credited answer. Lydia is challenging state action that denied her a professional license after she satisfied the exam requirement, and the stated basis was only an arrest that was dismissed at arraignment. Gold Key: due process does not turn on the label "privilege"; when state law gives a person a legitimate claim to a professional license, the state must use constitutionally adequate reasons and process before denying it. Silver Key: keep the forum word "hear" tied to the federal constitutional reason, not to the administrative-law label that merely sounds unfair. On the intended MBE lane, a federal due process claim supplies the federal question and the reason the court may hear the claim.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because the call asks whether a federal court should hear the claim, so a student may look for a jurisdictional cutoff. The breaker is that Lydia asserts a deprivation under the Fourteenth Amendment by a state licensing board; that is a federal constitutional claim, not a purely state-law complaint.

C is attractive because it reaches the same "yes" result and the Board's action feels unfair. The breaker is that "arbitrary and capricious" is the wrong frame for this answer array; the tested reason is deprivation of a protected license interest without due process, and the federal-court hook is the constitutional claim.

D is the dominant trap because it uses the old privilege-versus-right language many students remember. Gold Key: the state cannot avoid due process by calling a professional license a privilege; once the applicant has an entitlement or valid qualification, the state needs a constitutionally valid basis and process before denying the license.

### 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

Authority floor: Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause; 28 U.S.C. § 1331; Schware v. Board of Bar Examiners, 353 U.S. 232 (1957); Board of Regents v. Roth, 408 U.S. 564 (1972). Supporting civil-rights remedial statute: 42 U.S.C. § 1983.

The intended rule is that a state cannot deny entry into a licensed occupation for reasons that violate due process, and the right/privilege label does not defeat constitutional protection. Schware is especially close: the Court held that a state may impose valid professional qualifications, but those qualifications must have a rational connection to fitness, and arrests not resulting in trial or conviction were not enough to justify exclusion from practice. Roth supplies the property-interest frame: a protected property interest exists when rules or understandings create a legitimate claim of entitlement rather than a mere unilateral hope. A federal claim alleging deprivation of property without due process arises under federal law for 28 U.S.C. § 1331 purposes.

Current-law validation caveat: the source stem says the applicant already sued the Board in state court and lost before filing in federal district court. Modern doctrine may treat that fact as a Rooker-Feldman or preclusion problem if the federal action seeks district-court review or rejection of a state-court judgment. Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Saudi Basic Industries Corp., 544 U.S. 280 (2005), confines Rooker-Feldman to state-court losers complaining of injuries caused by state-court judgments and inviting district-court review of those judgments. Migra v. Warren City School District Board of Education, 465 U.S. 75 (1984), applies 28 U.S.C. § 1738 preclusion principles to later federal civil-rights litigation after a state-court judgment. Because the source answer choices do not address that posture and the official explanation ignores it, this item is marked NEEDS_HUMAN for bank validation rather than PASS.
