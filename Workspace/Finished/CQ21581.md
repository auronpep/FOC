### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** A misrepresentation claim has two proof requirements that students routinely conflate or incompletely recall: (1) the defendant's fault regarding falsity — the defendant knew or should have known the statement was false (scienter for fraudulent misrepresentation, or at minimum negligence for negligent misrepresentation), and (2) actual damages resulting from the plaintiff's justifiable reliance. Both are independent, essential elements; proving one without the other is insufficient.

**D is correct.** To prevail on a misrepresentation claim, the husband must establish: (a) the adjuster made a false statement of material fact; (b) the adjuster knew or should have known it was false; (c) the adjuster intended to induce the husband's reliance; (d) the husband actually and justifiably relied; and (e) the husband suffered damages as a result. The stem establishes the false statement, intent to induce reliance (the adjuster contacted the husband directly to induce settlement), and actual reliance (the husband settled). The two gaps the husband must additionally fill are: (1) the adjuster's knowledge of falsity (the adjuster knew or should have known the safety-violation reports contradicted the "no violations" claim), and (2) damages (the underlying suit was worth more than the settlement — here, proving the suit would have yielded a judgment exceeding $1,000 establishes the husband was worse off by relying on the misrepresentation). D correctly identifies both required additional facts.

**Silver Key:** When a question asks what a plaintiff "must prove" and the answer choices each isolate different elements, lock the call first: the question is testing whether you know ALL required elements, not just one. Any choice offering only a single element when two are independently required is a half-truth cut.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original B):** This choice correctly identifies one required element — the adjuster's knowledge or reckless disregard of the falsity — and it is genuinely attractive because scienter is the element that distinguishes misrepresentation from an innocent mistake. However, it is only half the picture. Even if the adjuster knowingly lied, the husband still must prove he was actually damaged by the reliance — that the underlying wrongful death claim was meritorious and worth more than the settlement he accepted. **Gold Key:** Both the fault element AND the damages element are independently required; proving one without the other fails.

**B (original A):** This choice correctly identifies the damages element — the husband must show his underlying suit would have succeeded and yielded a recovery exceeding $1,000 — and it is attractive because damages feel like the most concrete, intuitive requirement in a fraud case. But it omits the husband's burden to prove the adjuster's fault: that the adjuster knew or should have known the "no safety violations" statement was false. Without proving the adjuster's mental state regarding falsity, the claim fails even if the damages are crystal clear. **Gold Key:** Both the fault element AND the damages element are independently required; proving one without the other fails.

**C (original D):** This choice is the "get out of jail free" answer — it tells the husband he doesn't need to prove either element. It is attractive to students who assume that once a false statement and reliance are shown, the claim is established. But misrepresentation requires proof of both fault (scienter or negligence) and actual damages. The husband cannot recover without showing both that the adjuster was at fault for the falsity and that the husband suffered a real financial loss from relying on it. This choice flatly misstates the law.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule — Elements of Misrepresentation:**

Under the Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 525 (fraudulent misrepresentation/deceit) and 552 (negligent misrepresentation), and consistent with general tort law principles tested on the MBE, a plaintiff alleging misrepresentation must prove:

1. **A false statement of material fact** (or omission where a duty exists);
2. **Scienter or negligence regarding falsity** — the defendant knew or should have known the statement was false (for fraudulent misrepresentation, scienter = knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard; for negligent misrepresentation, the defendant failed to exercise reasonable care in ascertaining truth);
3. **Intent to induce the plaintiff's reliance**;
4. **Actual and justifiable reliance** by the plaintiff; and
5. **Damages** — the plaintiff suffered pecuniary loss proximately caused by the reliance.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 525, 552; *Derry v. Peek* [1889] 14 App Cas 337 (scienter requirement for fraud); *Ultramares Corp. v. Touche*, 255 N.Y. 170 (1931) (negligent misrepresentation duty and damages).

**Application to this question:** The stem supplies elements 1 (false statement: "no safety violations"), 3 (intent to induce: the adjuster contacted the husband to induce settlement), and 4 (actual reliance: the husband settled). The call asks what the husband must *additionally* prove. The two remaining elements are element 2 (knowledge/should-have-known of falsity) and element 5 (damages — the underlying suit would have yielded a judgment exceeding $1,000, establishing pecuniary loss from the reliance). Both are independently required; neither alone is sufficient.

**Verification against original:** The tested rule (elements of misrepresentation), the legal issue (what additional facts must be proved), and the credited outcome (both the knowledge element AND the damages element must be proved) are unchanged from the original. The original key (C) and the transformed key (D) both represent the same legal substance: the conjunctive answer requiring both elements.

#### Review Truth

To establish a misrepresentation claim, a plaintiff must prove both that the defendant knew or should have known the statement was false and that the plaintiff suffered actual damages from justifiable reliance on the false statement.

#### Gold Key(s)

**GK-TORTS-MISREP-01**
"A misrepresentation claim always requires proof of two independent elements beyond the false statement itself: (1) the defendant's fault regarding falsity (knew or should have known), and (2) actual damages from the plaintiff's reliance. Proving one without the other fails."
