## 1. Final Question

The United States owns and operates Manna Hall, a federal records annex in a humid coastal state. The annex is preparing a public-history exhibit of antique hymnals and handwritten sermon notebooks. A new silent dehumidifying system usually protects the paper, but during a few summer nights it cannot keep the archive dry enough.

The federal appropriation statute that funded the system permits the operators to run an older diesel generator when backup power is needed for the dehumidifiers. When the generator runs, about three nights in any year, its exterior fan exceeds the state's nighttime noise-control standards.

May state authorities successfully prosecute the federal operators for violating the state's noise-control standards?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

D. Yes, because protecting residents from excessive nighttime noise is a legitimate exercise of the state's police power.

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is the best answer in the constitutional-law frame. The operators are running equipment at a federally owned and operated facility, and the federal appropriation statute permits that backup operation. A state police-power rule cannot be used to punish that authorized federal operation unless Congress has clearly allowed the state to do so.

Gold Key: A state may not control or punish authorized federal operations merely because the state has a valid general regulatory interest; congressional consent or an officer acting outside lawful federal authority is the needed unlock.

Silver Key: Read the answer array as two "yes" reasons and two "no" reasons. Once the stem gives authorized federal operation, choose the no answer based on federal immunity, not the no answer based on small harm.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning For The Right Answer

The transformed item preserves the source item's constitutional rule, issue, answer-array geometry, and credited answer substance: absent clear congressional consent, a state may not prosecute federal operators for conduct authorized as part of federal operations.

Authority floor: the Supremacy Clause, U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2, makes valid federal law supreme over conflicting state control. In In re Neagle, 135 U.S. 1 (1890), the Court held that a federal officer acting in discharge of federal duty could not be held for a state-law prosecution based on that authorized conduct. In Johnson v. Maryland, 254 U.S. 51 (1920), the Court reversed a state conviction of a federal postal employee because the state could not impose a licensing condition on the employee's performance of federal duties. Supporting verification links: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/135/1/ and https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/254/51/.
