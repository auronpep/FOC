### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation
Attempted murder is a specific intent crime. To be convicted of an attempt, the defendant must act with the specific intent to commit the target offense—in this case, the intent to kill. If James believed at the time he struck the blow that the guard was already dead, he could not have formed the specific intent to kill a living person. Without the required mens rea (specific intent), James cannot be guilty of attempted murder, even if the guard was actually alive.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations
- **A:** This choice is attractive because it involves an "impossible" attempt (poisoning with a harmless substance). However, factual impossibility is not a defense to attempt. If James had the specific intent to kill and took a substantial step (administering the substance), he is guilty of attempt regardless of whether the substance was actually lethal.
- **B:** This is the dominant trap because it presents a scenario where the "murder" is physically impossible (the victim is already dead). Students often confuse factual impossibility with a lack of intent. Because James believed the guard was alive, he still possessed the specific intent to kill. Under modern law and the Model Penal Code, factual impossibility (shooting a corpse believing it is alive) does not prevent a conviction for attempt.
- **D:** This choice is attractive because it involves an "impossible" weapon. Like the harmless poison, a decorative blade makes the crime factually impossible to complete. However, because James believed the blade was real and intended to kill, he has the requisite specific intent for an attempt conviction.

### 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification
**Authority:** MPC § 5.01(1); *United States v. Thomas*, 13 C.M.A. 278 (1962).
Attempt requires the specific intent to commit the target crime and a substantial step toward its completion. Factual impossibility—where the defendant is unable to complete the crime due to a physical or factual condition unknown to them—is not a defense. However, "legal" impossibility (or a lack of intent based on a mistaken belief that no crime is being committed) can be a defense. Here, the issue is purely the presence or absence of specific intent. If the defendant believes the victim is dead, they cannot intend to kill. The transform preserves the rule, issue, and credited outcome of the original item.
