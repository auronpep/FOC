## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Timothy bought a hand-illuminated psalter from a rare-book dealer in State A. Under State A law, a buyer from that kind of dealer obtains good title to the book even if it had been stolen from a previous owner. At a private Christian history expo in State B, Lydia recognized the psalter as one stolen from her family collection. Under State B law, the owner of stolen personal property may recover it from an innocent possessor.

Lydia sued Timothy in a State B court to recover the psalter. Timothy defended on the ground that State A law gave him good title. The State B court applied State B law, entered judgment for Lydia, and Timothy did not appeal. The sheriff delivered the psalter to Lydia. Several months later, Lydia moved to State A with the psalter. Timothy then filed a new suit against Lydia in a State A court, claiming that the State B court should have applied State A law. Lydia appeared and moved to dismiss.

What should the State A court do?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Timothy already litigated the title dispute in State B, lost, and did not appeal. The new State A suit is not a fresh choice-of-law exam; it is an attempt to reopen a final sister-state judgment. Gold Key: Full faith and credit requires State A to honor the State B judgment with the same force it would receive in State B, even if Timothy believes State B chose the wrong law. Silver Key: once the stem gives a completed sister-state lawsuit and no appeal, start with the judgment, not the location of the property.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is the dominant trap because State A law and State A location both feel powerful: Timothy bought there, Lydia later brought the psalter there, and State A law favors Timothy. The breaker is timing. Silver Key: after final judgment, the court is not choosing the better property rule from scratch; it is deciding whether a later forum may disregard a sister-state judgment.

C is attractive because interstate movement sounds like federal jurisdiction. The breaker is that a private property recovery dispute does not become a federal-question case merely because the object crossed a state line, and the State A court's job is not to send the case away to relitigate the merits.

D is attractive because the psalter was bought from a dealer and moved across state lines, so a student may reach for a national sales rule. The breaker is that there is no freestanding federal law of movable-goods title that displaces the final State B judgment in this posture.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is unchanged. The source item and this variation both ask whether a later State A court may let the losing party relitigate a final State B judgment by arguing that the State B court should have applied State A law. The credited outcome remains dismissal.

Authority floor: Article IV, Section 1 of the U.S. Constitution commands full faith and credit for the public acts, records, and judicial proceedings of every state. The Full Faith and Credit Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1738, likewise provides that authenticated state judicial proceedings receive the same full faith and credit in every court within the United States that they have in the rendering jurisdiction. Constitution Annotated summarizes the Supreme Court doctrine as requiring states to give out-of-state judgments conclusive effect and the same effect they have at home.

Applied here, the State B court rendered a final judgment in a suit between Lydia and Timothy over the psalter. Timothy appeared, asserted State A law, lost, and did not appeal. The alleged legal error was for appeal in State B's system, not for a second suit in State A. State A must therefore dismiss the new action. The interstate movement of the psalter does not create federal sales law, federal-question jurisdiction, or a new chance to choose State A law.
