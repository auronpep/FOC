## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The confession is admissible because the police engaged in no coercive conduct. Under *Colorado v. Connelly*, 479 U.S. 157 (1986), "coercive police activity is a necessary predicate to the finding that a confession is not 'voluntary' within the meaning of the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment." Peter walked into the station on his own, was read his rights, and signed a waiver. His mental illness, while real and severe, does not by itself make the confession involuntary under the Constitution. The Due Process Clause targets police misconduct, not a defendant's internal motivations.

**Gold Key:** Mental illness alone ≠ involuntary confession. Police coercion is the constitutional floor. Without state action, the confession stands.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original B)** is attractive because students sense that Miranda is in play and latch onto the custody element — if Peter wasn't in custody, Miranda doesn't apply, so the confession is admissible for that reason. But the call is about admissibility generally, and the custody analysis is a misfit: the real issue is whether the Due Process Clause excludes the confession, not whether Miranda was triggered. Even if Peter was in custody (which he likely was once the interrogation began), the absence of police coercion is what matters. This choice proves a non-dispositive element.

**B (original D)** is attractive because students know that a Miranda waiver must be "knowingly, intelligently, and voluntarily" given, and a psychiatrist found Peter couldn't understand his rights. That sounds like a failed waiver. But this is a half-truth: the Miranda waiver analysis and the due-process voluntariness analysis are distinct. The Constitution does not require suppression of a confession merely because mental illness impaired the defendant's decision-making — only police coercion does. The choice smuggles a Miranda-waiver rule into a due-process question. **Gold Key:** A mentally ill defendant's waiver is not automatically invalid; without police coercion, the confession is constitutionally voluntary.

**D (original C)** is attractive because it feels right — a psychotic person who confesses because of voices is not acting voluntarily in any commonsense way. This is the colloquialism trap: moral intuition ("he couldn't help it") replaces the legal test. The law requires coercive police conduct, not just internal compulsion. **Gold Key:** Internal compulsion from mental illness, without police coercion, does not render a confession involuntary under the Fourteenth Amendment.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule:** Under the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, a confession is "involuntary" and therefore inadmissible only when it is the product of coercive police conduct. *Colorado v. Connelly*, 479 U.S. 157 (1986). A defendant's mental illness, standing alone, does not render a confession involuntary under the federal Constitution, even if the illness substantially impaired the defendant's ability to make rational choices or understand his rights.

**Authority:** *Colorado v. Connelly*, 479 U.S. 157 (1986) (holding that coercive police activity is a necessary predicate to a finding of involuntariness under the Due Process Clause; respondent's psychotic state, absent police coercion, did not render his confession involuntary).

**Application:** Peter approached police voluntarily, was Mirandized, signed a waiver, and confessed. No threats, promises, deception, or exploitation of his mental state by officers appears in the facts. The psychiatrist's finding that psychosis "induced" the confession goes to Peter's internal motivation, not to police conduct. Under *Connelly*, the confession is constitutionally voluntary.
