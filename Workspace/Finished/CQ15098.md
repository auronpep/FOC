**Vicarious liability** — A defendant is vicariously liable for another's tortious act only when a special relationship (employer-employee, principal-agent, joint enterprise) exists between the defendant and the tortfeasor. ## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** For the retreat center to be held liable for John's battery, vicarious liability must apply. Vicarious liability imputes liability from one person to another based on a special relationship between them — most commonly employer-employee or principal-agent. Here, John was a retreat attendee, not an employee, agent, or servant of the retreat center. No special relationship existed between the retreat center and John that would impute John's intentional tort to the retreat center. The battery was committed by John alone, and without vicarious liability, the retreat center is not responsible for his actions.

**Gold Key**: Vicarious liability requires a special relationship (employer-employee, principal-agent, joint enterprise). A business is not vicariously liable for the intentional torts of a mere customer, patron, or attendee — even if the business's own employee was negligent in serving that person alcohol.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect.** This is an appealing answer because Peter's conduct in serving wine to John may well have been reckless or negligent. But the call of the question asks whether the retreat center is liable for John's battery — not whether Peter was negligent. Negligence by the retreat center's own employee is a separate claim; it does not make the retreat center vicariously liable for an attendee's intentional tort. Vicarious liability requires a special relationship between the defendant and the tortfeasor, which does not exist here. **Gold Key**: The volunteer's negligence is a red herring — it does not create vicarious liability for a patron's intentional act.

**B is incorrect.** This answer correctly states the elements of battery: John intentionally caused harmful or offensive contact with Martha. As applied to John himself, this would be correct. But the call asks whether Martha can recover from the *retreat center*, not from John. To hold the retreat center liable, Martha would need to establish vicarious liability — that the retreat center is responsible for John's tortious conduct due to a special relationship. No such relationship exists; John was merely an attendee. **Gold Key**: Right doctrine, wrong defendant — battery elements are satisfied against the tortfeasor, but vicarious liability does not impute the tort to the business.

**D is incorrect.** This answer reaches the wrong conclusion for the wrong reason. It is true that Martha will not prevail against the retreat center — but not because she cannot prove a permanent injury. Battery does not require proof of any actual injury, let alone a permanent one. Nominal damages are available for battery even without actual harm; the tort is complete upon harmful or offensive contact. The real reason Martha will not prevail is that vicarious liability does not attach to the retreat center for an attendee's intentional tort.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Vicarious liability** (also called imputed liability) holds one party responsible for the tortious acts of another based on a special relationship between them. The doctrine is rooted in *respondeat superior*: an employer is liable for the torts of its employees committed within the scope of employment. See Restatement (Third) of Agency §§ 2.04, 7.07. The relationship must be one of control or right-to-control — employer-employee, principal-agent, or joint enterprise. A business is **not** vicariously liable for the intentional torts of a mere customer, patron, or attendee with whom no such relationship exists. *See* LII, Vicarious Liability, https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/vicarious_liability.

**Battery** requires (1) intentional (2) harmful or offensive (3) contact with the plaintiff's person. Actual injury is not required; nominal damages suffice. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 13, § 18.

## Program Frame

**Subject**: Torts — Other Torts  
**Subtopic**: Vicarious Liability for Acts of Others  
**Outline Code**: 62060100  
**Difficulty**: Core  
**Method Class**: hard_structural  
**Case Study Verdict**: C3_SOLVE  

This item tests whether the student can distinguish between (a) a tortfeasor's direct liability for battery and (b) a business's vicarious liability for that tort. The key structural move is recognizing that the call asks about the *business's* liability, not the *tortfeasor's* — and that vicarious liability requires a special relationship.

## Student Access Test

## Question Data

**Stem**: Martha and John were attendees at a weekend retreat at a Christian retreat center. The retreat center had chartered a bus to transport attendees to the retreat grounds. Before boarding, the retreat center's volunteer coordinator, Peter, served wine to attendees at a dinner. By the time the bus departed, John was visibly intoxicated. During the bus ride, John tried to strike up a conversation with Martha, who ignored him. John became angry and suddenly struck Martha in the face, giving her a black eye.

**Call**: If Martha asserts a claim for damages against the retreat center based on battery, she will

**Choices**:
- **A**: not prevail, because Peter acted recklessly in serving wine to John before the bus departed.
- **B**: prevail, because John's conduct constituted an intentionally inflicted harmful or offensive contact.
- **C**: not prevail, because John was not acting as an agent or employee of the retreat center.
- **D**: prevail, because Martha cannot establish any permanent injury from the contact.

## Distilled Core Question

A retreat center's attendee — visibly intoxicated after wine served by the center's volunteer — strikes another attendee on a chartered bus. Is the retreat center vicariously liable for the attendee's battery?

## Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

### Choice A: "not prevail, because Peter acted recklessly in serving wine to John before the bus departed."

**Part 1 — Student-accessible C3 signal**:  
This choice reaches the right conclusion (not prevail) but for a reason that doesn't answer the call. Peter's recklessness is about *direct* negligence by the retreat center's employee — not about whether the retreat center is *vicariously liable* for John's intentional tort. The fact proved (Peter's recklessness) is not the fact that decides the call (vicarious liability).

**Part 2 — Student label**: The negligence trap — true but irrelevant.

**Part 3 — What a true/responsive version would look like**: A true-and-responsive "not prevail" choice would state the correct reason vicarious liability doesn't attach — e.g., "not prevail, because John was not an agent or employee of the retreat center."

**Part 4 — Lawyer confirmation**: Negligence by a defendant's own employee is a direct liability theory. The call asks about vicarious liability for the tortfeasor's act. These are distinct legal theories.

---

### Choice B: "prevail, because John's conduct constituted an intentionally inflicted harmful or offensive contact."

**Part 1 — Student-accessible C3 signal**:  
This choice states a legally correct proposition — John did commit battery. But it answers the wrong question. The call asks about recovery from the *retreat center*, not from John. This is a misfit: the doctrine (battery elements) is correct but not responsive to the call (vicarious liability).

**Part 2 — Student label**: The right-doctrine-wrong-defendant trap (dominant trap).

**Part 3 — What a true/responsive version would look like**: A true-and-responsive "prevail" choice would need to establish both that battery occurred AND that vicarious liability attaches to the retreat center — e.g., "prevail, because John was acting as an agent of the retreat center when he committed the battery."

**Part 4 — Lawyer confirmation**: Battery elements are satisfied as to John, but the question is whether the retreat center is vicariously liable. No special relationship exists. This is a misfit — correct doctrine, wrong defendant.

---

### Choice C: "not prevail, because John was not acting as an agent or employee of the retreat center."

**Part 1 — Student-accessible C3 signal**:  
This choice reaches the right conclusion (not prevail) for the right reason (no special relationship = no vicarious liability). John was an attendee — not an employee, agent, or joint enterprisor. The reason directly answers the call.

**Part 2 — Student label**: Correct answer.

**Part 3 — What a true/responsive version would look like**: This choice IS true and responsive. It correctly identifies that vicarious liability requires a special relationship and that no such relationship exists.

**Part 4 — Lawyer confirmation**: Vicarious liability requires employer-employee, principal-agent, or joint enterprise relationship. Restatement (Third) of Agency §§ 2.04, 7.07. John was a mere attendee.

---

### Choice D: "prevail, because Martha cannot establish any permanent injury from the contact."

**Part 2 — Student label**: The permanent injury myth.

**Part 1 — Student-accessible C3 signal**:  
This choice invents a legal requirement that doesn't exist. Battery does not require permanent injury — or any actual injury at all. The choice is built on a flat misstatement of law. Additionally, it says "prevail" when the correct outcome is "not prevail."

**Part 3 — What a true/responsive version would look like**: A true-and-responsive version would correctly state that battery requires only harmful or offensive contact, not permanent injury — and would reach the correct outcome (not prevail) for the correct reason (no vicarious liability).

**Part 4 — Lawyer confirmation**: Battery requires only harmful or offensive contact. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 13, § 18. Nominal damages suffice without any actual injury.
