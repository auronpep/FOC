7. Full right-answer explanation

`B` is right because the Fourth Amendment problem is not whether Lydia built a high wall or whether the courtyard counts as curtilage in the abstract. The decisive question is whether society recognizes as reasonable an expectation that the area will remain free from naked-eye observation from public airspace. `Gold Key:` fenced curtilage is still unprotected from ordinary aerial viewing when officers observe it from lawful public airspace at an altitude members of the public commonly use. `Silver Key:` when one answer says "curtilage plus no warrant" and another says "no reasonable expectation from the vantage point used," the clash is objective reasonableness of the vantage point, not the homeowner's subjective desire for privacy. Because the deputy saw the mushrooms from the helicopter before any physical entry and from a lawful public vantage, the observation was not a Fourth Amendment search, the warrant was properly obtained, and suppression should be denied.

8. Full wrong-answer explanations

`A` sells the student who correctly notices curtilage and then stops too early. Curtilage matters, but it does not end the analysis when the police used naked-eye aerial observation from public airspace. `Silver Key:` this array tests whether the vantage point defeats the privacy claim, so curtilage plus lack of a preexisting warrant is only a half-answer.

`C` sells the student who wants police motive to do constitutional work. The breaker is that the officer's purpose does not create a search when the officer only looked from a place where the public could lawfully be and saw what any member of the public could have seen from that vantage.

`D` sells the student who senses the result should be denial and overstates the rule. `Gold Key:` the law is not that residential curtilage is categorically exempt from the warrant requirement. The narrower point is that this particular observation was not a search at all, so the warrant requirement never attached at the moment of viewing.

9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited outcome is unchanged from the source item: suppression should be denied because the aerial observation was not a Fourth Amendment search. In `California v. Ciraolo`, `476 U.S. 207` (1986), the Supreme Court held that officers did not conduct a search when they flew over a fenced backyard at 1,000 feet and saw marijuana with the naked eye, because the respondent had no reasonable expectation that the yard was protected from visual observation from public navigable airspace. In `Florida v. Riley`, `488 U.S. 445` (1989), the Court again held that naked-eye observation of curtilage from a helicopter did not violate the Fourth Amendment where the helicopter was in public airspace at an altitude sufficiently regular for public travel and the defendant showed no contrary facts.

That is the rule this item tests. Lydia clearly tried to preserve privacy with a solid wall, and the courtyard is part of the curtilage. But the Supreme Court's aerial-surveillance cases make the controlling distinction objective reasonableness, not subjective efforts alone. On the facts given, the deputy could not see from the road, used a helicopter, saw the contraband from above, and then got a warrant. The item, like `Ciraolo` and `Riley`, assumes a lawful public-airspace vantage and a naked-eye observation no more intrusive than what a member of the public could have seen from the same location. That defeats the suppression motion. The "only purpose" point does not matter, and the "no warrant is ever required for a residential courtyard" statement is too broad. Authority: `California v. Ciraolo`, `476 U.S. 207` (1986), Cornell LII at `https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/476/207`; `Florida v. Riley`, `488 U.S. 445` (1989), Cornell LII at `https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/488/445`.
