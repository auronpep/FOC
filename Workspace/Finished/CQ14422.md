### 6. Full right-answer explanation
D is correct. The acknowledgment repeated the offer's quantity and quality terms and added an arbitration clause in boilerplate. That makes the arbitration clause an additional term, not a contradiction of an existing term. Under the UCC battle-of-the-forms rule, a definite acceptance can still form a contract even though it includes added terms. Between merchants, an additional term can become part of the contract unless the offer limited acceptance to its own terms, the new term materially altered the contract, or the offeror objected.

Paul's best argument is therefore that the arbitration provision did not materially alter the parties' contract. **Gold Key:** under UCC Section 2-207, added boilerplate in an otherwise definite acceptance does not automatically make a counteroffer; for merchant-to-merchant additional terms, the main gate is whether the term materially alters the deal. **Silver Key:** label the clause first. If the offer was silent about arbitration, the clause is an additional term, so "no contradiction" and "counteroffer" are side roads.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because the offer said nothing about arbitration, so the arbitration clause does not visibly collide with a written offer term. The breaker is that noncontradiction is not the final UCC Section 2-207 gate. The seller needs the added arbitration term to become part of the contract, and the answer that directly supports that result is that the term did not materially alter the contract.

B is the dominant trap. It sells the old mirror-image or last-shot instinct: a form with a new clause looks like a counteroffer, and the buyer later accepted the goods. The breaker is the Gold Key. Under UCC Section 2-207, an otherwise definite acknowledgment does not become a counteroffer merely because it adds boilerplate; without an express condition on assent to the added term, the added clause is handled as an additional term.

C is attractive because arbitration may sound practical and businesslike. The breaker is responsiveness. Efficiency is not the legal test for whether an arbitration clause became a contract term.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Contracts, Formation of Contracts, Acceptance, and the verified outline code is `55020102`. The source row's official key is confirmed by UCC Section 2-207.

UCC Section 2-207(1) states that a definite and seasonable expression of acceptance can operate as an acceptance even though it contains terms additional to or different from the offer, unless acceptance is expressly made conditional on assent to those terms. UCC Section 2-207(2) states that additional terms are proposals for addition to the contract; between merchants, those terms become part of the contract unless the offer expressly limits acceptance to the offer's terms, the terms materially alter the contract, or the offeror has objected or objects within a reasonable time. Source: Cornell LII, Uniform Commercial Code Section 2-207, https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-207.

The arbitration clause is an additional term because the offer was silent about arbitration. The seller's acknowledgment did not say acceptance was expressly conditional on Martha's assent to arbitration, so the added clause is not best analyzed as a counteroffer. The seller's best route is the merchant additional-term route: argue that the arbitration clause did not materially alter the contract. Research support also confirms that courts assess material alteration under UCC Section 2-207 case by case, including for arbitration clauses. Source: Timothy Davis, "U.C.C. Section 2-207: When Does an Additional Term Materially Alter a Contract?", 65 Catholic University Law Review 489 (2016), https://scholarship.law.edu/lawreview/vol65/iss3/7/.

No researched authority contradicts the supplied key. The transform preserves the tested rule, legal issue, credited outcome, and answer-array mechanics. The item asks for the best argument supporting arbitration, not for a guaranteed ruling that arbitration must be compelled.
