### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: common-law bilateral conspiracy requires at least two genuine participants who agree and intend the criminal objective. An undercover officer's feigned agreement does not count as a genuine conspiratorial agreement, but it also does not defeat conspiracy when at least two other participants genuinely agree with each other.

Authority floor checked against the traditional plurality rule. The UNC School of Government's criminal-law analysis summarizes the common-law bilateral rule: conspiracy is an agreement between two or more people to do an unlawful act, the traditional rule requires plurality of intent, and when the only other participant is an undercover officer or informant who lacks criminal intent, there is no conspiracy. It also contrasts the Model Penal Code section 5.03 unilateral approach, under which one culpable actor's agreement can be enough even if the other party is feigning. Model Penal Code section 5.03(1) defines conspiracy in unilateral terms for MPC jurisdictions. Sources checked: https://nccriminallaw.sog.unc.edu/2025/01/14/unilateral-conspiracy-and-money-laundering/ and https://www.criminallawweb.net/web1/mpc/PART1/snippets/overtact.htm.

This fact pattern satisfies the common-law bilateral rule because Martha and Stephen are two real participants. The detective is not needed to supply the second guilty mind.
