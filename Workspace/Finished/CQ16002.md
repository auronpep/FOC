### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. Common-law rape is a general-intent crime, so the prosecution does not need to prove that Timothy specifically intended to violate Lydia's consent. Gold Key: the defendant's voluntary intoxication does not excuse a reckless or negligent consent mistake, but the prosecution still must prove that he intentionally engaged in intercourse. Silver Key: sort the choices by what the intoxication negates. Victim intoxication points toward lack of consent, and defendant intoxication about consent is not enough; only defendant intoxication that negates awareness of the intercourse itself attacks the act the charge requires.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because it treats Lydia's intoxication as a reason Timothy might be less blameworthy. The breaker is that a victim's inability to understand the act is a no-consent fact, not an acquittal fact, so it strengthens rather than weakens the common-law rape charge.

B is attractive because mistaken identity sounds like it could become consent. The breaker is timing: the rape is complete at penetration without consent, and a belief that arises immediately after intercourse began does not erase the completed offense.

D is the dominant trap because it sounds like intoxication destroyed Timothy's blameworthy state of mind. Gold Key: common-law rape does not require a specific intent to disregard nonconsent; a drunken, reckless, or negligent belief in willingness does not produce an acquittal when the victim said stop and resisted.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law and Procedure, Other Crimes, Crimes against the Person > Rape, and the verified outline code is `73020700`. The credited answer remains correct. The common-law rape frame requires forced sexual intercourse with a female not the defendant's spouse and without consent. Rape is treated as a general-intent offense for MBE purposes: voluntary intoxication is not a defense merely because the defendant misread or disregarded consent, especially where the facts show resistance. But the state still must prove the defendant intentionally engaged in the intercourse act itself. If Timothy was so intoxicated that he did not realize he was engaging in intercourse at all, that fact most directly defeats the required act-intent proof and is the most likely path to acquittal.

Lawyer-confirmation authority: Oklahoma's criminal jury instruction commentary states that voluntary intoxication is available only for crimes with a specific criminal intent and specifically identifies rape as not having that specific-intent requirement, citing *Boyd v. State*, 1977 OK CR 322, 572 P.2d 276, and *Kitch v. State*, 69 P.2d 411 (Okla. Crim. App. 1937). See https://okcca.net/ouji-cr/8-36/ and https://law.justia.com/cases/oklahoma/court-of-appeals-criminal/1977/1278.html. Model Penal Code section 2.08 also reflects the intoxication principle that intoxication matters only if it negatives an element, while self-induced unawareness of a risk is immaterial where recklessness is enough. Model Penal Code section 213.1 confirms the force/nonspouse structure of rape and separately treats victim unconsciousness or unawareness as inculpatory sexual-offense facts, not acquittal facts. See https://www.criminallawweb.net/web1/mpc/PART1/snippets/213_1.htm. The research does not contradict the supplied official key.
