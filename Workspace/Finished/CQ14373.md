### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The parol evidence rule bars extrinsic evidence that contradicts or adds to a completely integrated written agreement. But there is a key exception: evidence that the parties agreed the written contract would not become enforceable until the occurrence of a stated condition is admissible, because the condition goes to whether any enforceable obligation ever came into existence. Here, Peter and Lydia orally agreed that the deal was conditioned on church session approval. Because the session refused, no enforceable obligation arose. Evidence of this oral condition does not contradict the writing — it shows the writing never took effect. The parol evidence rule therefore does not bar it.

> **Gold Key:** When parties orally agree that a written contract is subject to a condition precedent (i.e., the obligation won't arise unless some event occurs), evidence of that condition is admissible under an exception to the parol evidence rule — regardless of whether the writing is completely or partially integrated. The condition doesn't contradict the writing; it negates the existence of any enforceable duty.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original C) is attractive but wrong** because it finds the complete-integration trigger and stops. A student who spots that the writing looks complete will instinctively reach for the parol evidence rule and exclude the evidence. The trap: the condition-precedent exception applies *regardless* of whether the writing is partially or completely integrated. A finding of complete integration does not defeat this exception. The oral condition doesn't add to or contradict the writing — it shows no enforceable obligation ever came into being.

> **Gold Key:** The condition-precedent exception to the parol evidence rule applies whether the writing is completely integrated, partially integrated, or not integrated at all. A finding of complete integration alone does not bar evidence of an oral condition precedent.

**B (original D) is attractive but wrong** because it states an overbroad rule: that parol evidence is excluded "whether or not" the writing was a complete integration. This sounds like a tough, no-exceptions rule. But it is too broad — the parol evidence rule does *not* bar evidence of a condition precedent even when the writing *is* a complete integration. The "whether or not" framing makes this choice a sweeping exclusion that swallows the exception.

**D (original A) is attractive but wrong** because the oral statement sounds like a side agreement — a "collateral" deal. But the condition (session approval) goes to the very existence of the obligation, not to a separate, independent agreement. A collateral agreement is one that would naturally be made as a separate agreement and doesn't contradict the writing. Here, the condition is integral to whether the contract ever took effect, so the collateral-agreement exception does not apply.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Rule:** The parol evidence rule provides that when parties have reduced their agreement to a final written integration, extrinsic evidence of prior or contemporaneous agreements or negotiations is inadmissible to contradict, vary, or add to the terms of the writing. *Restatement (Second) of Contracts* §§ 213, 215.

**Exception — Condition Precedent to Enforceable Obligation:** Where the parties agree (orally or otherwise) that the written agreement will not become enforceable unless a stated condition occurs, evidence of that condition is admissible. The rationale is that the condition does not contradict the writing; rather, it shows that no enforceable obligation ever came into existence. *Restatement (Second) of Contracts* § 217 ("Integrated Agreement Subject to Oral Requirement of a Condition"): "Where the parties to a written agreement agree orally that performance of the agreement is subject to the occurrence of a stated condition, the agreement is not integrated with respect to the oral condition." *See also* UCC § 2-202 (permitting evidence to explain or supplement a writing, and not barring evidence of consistent additional terms unless the writing was intended as a complete and exclusive statement).

**Application:** Peter's oral statement — that his obligation was conditioned on church session approval — and Lydia's acceptance of that condition, established a condition precedent to the existence of an enforceable obligation. The session's refusal means no enforceable duty arose. Evidence of this condition is admissible under the condition-precedent exception to the parol evidence rule, regardless of whether the written contract was completely or partially integrated.

**Authority:** *Restatement (Second) of Contracts* §§ 213, 215, 217 (1981); UCC § 2-202. *Pym v. Campbell*, 119 Eng. Rep. 903 (Q.B. 1856) (classic case permitting evidence of oral condition that buyer's engineer approve invention before contract took effect).
