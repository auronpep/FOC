## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

In a federal arson prosecution after a retreat-center art studio burned, Lydia testifies that she never saw Peter wheel any paint-thinner jugs into the choir-robe storage room. Before trial, Lydia testified before a grand jury under oath that she saw Peter wheel two paint-thinner jugs into that storage room. Lydia is now on the stand and subject to cross-examination. The prosecutor offers the grand jury testimony.

What is the best ruling?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

Can a testifying witness's sworn grand jury statement that contradicts her trial testimony be admitted both to impeach and as substantive proof?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. Exclude the grand jury testimony because prior inconsistent statements are admissible only in civil cases.

C. Exclude the grand jury testimony because Lydia is available to testify live.

D. Admit the grand jury testimony both to impeach Lydia and to prove that Peter wheeled the paint-thinner jugs into the storage room.

### 5. Correct answer

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is correct. Lydia is testifying now, is subject to cross-examination, and her grand jury testimony directly contradicts her trial testimony. Gold Key: under Federal Rule of Evidence 801(d)(1)(A), a prior inconsistent statement given under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition is not hearsay when the declarant testifies and is subject to cross-examination. Silver Key: do not stop at impeachment once the prior statement was sworn in a qualifying proceeding. A grand jury qualifies, so the testimony may be used both to impeach Lydia and to prove that Peter wheeled the jugs into the storage room.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells a civil-case myth. Prior inconsistent statements under the federal rule are not limited to civil cases; this is a federal criminal prosecution, and the rule still applies. The breaker is that the rule turns on the declarant's trial testimony, cross-examination, inconsistency, oath, and qualifying proceeding, not on civil versus criminal posture.

B sells the dominant format trap. The answer remembers that depositions count, then narrows the rule too far by treating depositions as the only qualifying format. The breaker is the Gold Key: the rule also covers statements made under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, or other proceeding, and a grand jury is an other proceeding.

C sells the availability reversal. Lydia's presence on the stand feels like a reason to rely only on live testimony, but the federal prior-statement route requires the declarant to testify and be subject to cross-examination. The breaker is that live availability opens the Rule 801(d)(1)(A) door; it does not close it.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: a prior inconsistent statement is not hearsay under Federal Rule of Evidence 801(d)(1)(A) when the declarant testifies, is subject to cross-examination about the prior statement, and the statement is inconsistent with the declarant's testimony and was given under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition. The federal rule permits substantive use, not impeachment-only use, when those requirements are met.

The official United States Code version of Rule 801(d)(1)(A) states that the declarant-witness must testify and be subject to cross-examination about the prior statement, and that the prior statement must be inconsistent and given under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition. The official conference notes state that the adopted rule covers statements before a grand jury and that prior inconsistent statements may also be used for impeachment. Source checked: https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&path=%2Fprelim%40title28%2Ftitle28a%2Fnode232%2Farticle8.

Lydia's grand jury testimony was under oath, before trial, and inconsistent with her trial testimony. Because Lydia is now testifying and subject to cross-examination, the grand jury testimony is admissible both to impeach and as proof that Peter wheeled the paint-thinner jugs into the storage room.
