### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Hannah is entitled to collect **100 percent** of her damages from Lydia. The court held the academy, Lydia, and Paul jointly and severally liable for Hannah's damages, and the jury found Hannah free of fault. Under joint and several liability, a plaintiff who has obtained a joint-and-several judgment may enforce the entire judgment against any one defendant, even if other defendants are also liable. The plaintiff is not required to apportion the damages among the defendants; the apportionment problem is the defendants' inter-se contribution claim against each other, not the plaintiff's collection problem. **Gold Key:** joint and several liability lets the plaintiff collect 100% from any one defendant; proportional sharing is the joint-tortfeasors' inter-se problem (contribution), not the plaintiff's problem. **Silver Key:** when the call asks "what portion can the plaintiff collect from one defendant" and the stem says "jointly and severally liable," the answer is 100%.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. 50 percent.**

This is the **dominant trap** — the proportional-liability / contribution confusion. The student confuses the plaintiff's collection right (joint and several = full recovery from any one) with the defendants' inter-se contribution problem (the defendants themselves may have to share the burden). The breaker is the **Gold Key**: joint and several liability lets the plaintiff collect 100% from any one defendant; the proportional split is the defendants' inter-se problem, not the plaintiff's. Hannah may collect 100% from Lydia; Lydia's claim against Paul for contribution is a separate question.

**C. 0 percent.**

This is the "plaintiff can't reach one joint tortfeasor" trap. The student assumes that because multiple defendants are liable, the plaintiff must pursue them together, or that the plaintiff can somehow be blocked from collecting from any single defendant. The breaker is the very definition of joint and several liability: a plaintiff who has obtained a joint-and-several judgment may enforce the entire judgment against any one defendant. Hannah may collect 100% from Lydia even if the academy and Paul are also liable.

**D. 40 percent.**

This is the "specific non-doctrinal percentage" trap. The student latches onto a number that does not correspond to any doctrinal principle and treats it as if it were a recognized share. The breaker is that the only doctrinally grounded options in this stem are 100% (joint and several) and 0% (no liability at all — contradicted by the court's holding). 40% is not a recognized share in any contribution or apportionment doctrine, and the stem supplies no factual basis for picking 40%.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18197, a Torts item involving a passenger injured in a multi-vehicle collision, a court holding that the company, the employee, and the driver are jointly and severally liable, a jury finding the plaintiff free of fault, and a call asking what portion of damages the plaintiff may collect from the employee. The official key is D: 100 percent. The official explanation states that "when tortfeasors are jointly and severally liable, a plaintiff is entitled to collect all his or her damages from any one of them (i.e., 'severally'), or to collect his or her damages from them together (i.e., 'jointly') in any combination whatsoever."

For lawyer confirmation: at common law, joint and several liability is the default rule for multiple tortfeasors whose independent acts concur to cause a single, indivisible injury. Each defendant is individually liable for the full damages, and the plaintiff may enforce the entire judgment against any one of them. See Restatement (Third) of Torts: Apportionment of Liability § 10 (several liability for independent acts); Restatement (Third) of Torts: Apportionment of Liability §§ 1, 4 (joint and several liability for indivisible injury caused by multiple tortfeasors). The plaintiff's right to collect the full judgment from any one defendant is distinct from the defendants' inter-se contribution problem: under the Uniform Contribution Among Tortfeasors Act (UCATA) and the majority common-law rule, a defendant who has paid more than his equitable share may seek contribution from the other tortfeasors. Contribution is the defendants' claim against each other, not a defense or apportionment that the plaintiff must respect. The existence of a comparative-negligence statute is irrelevant here because the jury found the plaintiff free of fault; comparative negligence affects the plaintiff's recovery, not the defendants' inter-se shares. The transformed outcome is identical to the original: Hannah is entitled to collect 100% of her damages from Lydia.

The transformed call ("In enforcing the judgment, what portion of her damages is Hannah entitled to collect from the employee, Lydia?") preserves the original call's function (asking for the portion of damages the plaintiff may collect from one defendant under a joint-and-several judgment) and does not name the governing joint-and-several liability doctrine.
