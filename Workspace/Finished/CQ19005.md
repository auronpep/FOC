### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is the strongest defense because larceny requires the intent to permanently deprive another person of property at the moment of the taking. Barnabas's best argument is not that intoxication erased all criminal responsibility. It is that his intoxicated but honest mistake made him believe the handbell box was his own, so he did not form the specific intent to steal another person's property.

Gold Key: For larceny, an honest mistake that the property is yours negates the specific intent to permanently deprive, even if the mistake looks careless or unreasonable. The next-morning return helps show the mistake was genuine, but the defense works because the intent was missing when the property was taken.

Silver Key: Time-lock the defense at the taking. Post-taking facts can corroborate intent, but they do not replace the required at-taking mens rea.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A attracts the student who sees intoxication and tries to move the problem into actus reus. The breaker is that Barnabas still physically picked up and carried the box; the real fight is not whether his body acted, but whether he had the stealing intent when he acted.

C attracts the student who treats quick repentance as the defense. The breaker is timing: returning property the next day is not a complete larceny defense if the intent to steal existed when the property was taken. Silver Key: the return matters only as evidence of what Barnabas believed at the taking.

D attracts the student who over-applies intoxication. The breaker is that voluntary intoxication does not create a blanket "no criminal intent" defense. Gold Key: intoxication matters here only insofar as it supports the narrower claim that Barnabas honestly mistook the box for his own and therefore lacked larceny's specific intent.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged. The original and transformed items both test common-law larceny: a trespassory taking and carrying away of the personal property of another with intent to permanently deprive. The defense is an honest mistake of fact about ownership that negates the specific intent to steal. Voluntary intoxication is not a general incapacity defense, but it may be relevant to whether the defendant actually formed a required specific intent. Prompt return is not itself a complete defense once larceny is complete, though it may corroborate the absence of intent at the moment of taking.

Authority: In *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), the Supreme Court treated criminal intent as essential to traditional stealing-type offenses and held that intent must be decided from the evidence rather than presumed from the bare act of taking. The opinion also cites common-law larceny principles requiring a felonious taking and intent to permanently deprive. See https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/342/246/. The same mens rea-negation principle is consistent with Model Penal Code sections 2.04(1) on mistake and 2.08 on intoxication as relevant only when it negates an offense element.
