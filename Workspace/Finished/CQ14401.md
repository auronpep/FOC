## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The source row tests the Statute of Frauds for a suretyship promise. The transformed item preserves the same rule, issue, and credited outcome. Ruth promised Peter that she would pay Matthew's debt if Matthew did not. That is a promise to answer for the duty of another, so it falls within the suretyship provision of the Statute of Frauds.

The governing authority is Restatement (Second) of Contracts. Section 110(1)(b) includes a contract to answer for the duty of another within the Statute of Frauds. Section 112 describes the suretyship relation: the promisee is the obligee, the promisor is surety for the other person, and the promisee knows or has reason to know that relation. Section 131 supplies the memorandum rule: the writing must be signed by or for the party to be charged, reasonably identify the subject matter, indicate that a contract has been made or offered, and state the essential terms with reasonable certainty. Section 133 confirms that the writing need not have been made as a formal memorandum of the contract. Section 134 allows a signature to be any symbol adopted to authenticate the writing, and Section 136 allows the memorandum to be made or signed before or after contract formation.

Ruth's note satisfies those requirements. It was signed by Ruth, the party to be charged. It identified Peter as the creditor, Matthew as the debtor, the eight-week $3,600 van-repair bill as the obligation, and Ruth's promise to pay if Matthew did not. The fact that Ruth sent the note to Matthew rather than to Peter does not defeat the memorandum's evidentiary function.

Research authority checked: Restatement (Second) of Contracts chapter on the Statute of Frauds is part of ALI's Contracts Restatement; open summaries reproduce the relevant rules for Sections 110, 112, 131, 133, 134, and 136.
