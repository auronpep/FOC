# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question
Four weeks after a falling out with her neighbor, Martha was angry because the neighbor still had not returned a valuable parchment scroll that he had borrowed from her. She went to his house one night to demand its immediate return. When she arrived, the neighbor was not at home and his door was unlocked. Martha entered to look for her scroll but could not find it. While searching, she noticed that the neighbor had a new cedar reading desk. Thinking that the desk was worth as much as her scroll, she set the desk on fire before leaving. The fire destroyed the desk completely and charred the timber walls and ceiling of the room, although the house itself was not seriously damaged. 

The jurisdiction adopts the common law definitions of burglary, larceny, and arson. If Martha is charged with burglary and arson, she can properly be convicted of:

## 2. Distilled Core Question
If a defendant enters a dwelling at night without the intent to commit a felony, but once inside recklessly or maliciously causes a structural burning, which common law crimes have been committed?

## 3. Final answer choices
A: burglary and arson.
B: arson only.
C: burglary only.
D: neither burglary nor arson.

## 4. Correct answer
B (Original B)

## 6. Full right-answer explanation
(B) is the best response, because Martha committed common law arson but not common law burglary. Common law arson is the **malicious burning** of the **dwelling of another**. "Malice" is satisfied by intent or **recklessness**. "Burning" is satisfied by even the slightest **charring** of the structure itself (the timber walls and ceiling here). Because Martha intentionally set fire to a desk inside a dwelling, her conduct was reckless with respect to the structure, satisfying the malice requirement. 

Common law burglary requires a breaking and entering of the dwelling of another at night with the **intent to commit a larceny or felony therein**. Martha entered with the intent to retrieve her own property (the scroll), which negates the specific intent for larceny under a **claim of right**. Furthermore, she did not intend to commit arson at the moment she entered; the intent to burn the desk arose only after she failed to find her scroll. Because the specific intent did not **concur** with the entry, she cannot be convicted of burglary.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations
(A) is the dominant trap because it correctly identifies the arson but fails to account for the **concurrence of intent** required for burglary. Students often see the entry and the subsequent felony and assume both crimes are complete, forgetting that the intent must exist at the time of the "breaking and entering."

(C) is incorrect because it fails to identify the arson. The charring of the walls and ceiling constitutes a "burning" of the structure under common law, and Martha's reckless act satisfies the malice requirement.

(D) is incorrect because it fails to recognize the arson. Even though the house was not "seriously damaged," the charring of the structure is sufficient to meet the physical element of common law arson.

## 8. Black-letter-law verification
**Authority:** Common Law. Arson requires a malicious (intentional or reckless) burning of the dwelling of another. *See, e.g.*, *Regina v. Faulkner*, 13 Cox C.C. 550 (1877) (recklessness regarding structure). Burglary requires specific intent to commit a felony or larceny *at the time of entry*. *See, e.g.*, *State v. Moore*, 12 N.H. 42 (1841). Claim of right to one's own property negates larceny intent.

## 9. Rigor/Difficulty Note
This item is "Core" rigor. It tests the distinction between general malice (arson) and specific intent (burglary), and the requirement of concurrent intent for burglary. The "charring" rule for arson is a classic MBE testing point.

#### 2. student_access_test

- **Charring of walls/ceiling:** Meets structural "burning" for arson.

#### 8. choice_by_choice_walkthrough
- **Choice A**: **Both.** This is the "completion trap." It incorrectly assumes that committing a felony inside a house makes one a burglar, regardless of when the intent formed.
- **Choice B**: **Arson only.** This is correct. The charring is sufficient for arson, but the lack of concurrent intent kills the burglary.
- **Choice C**: **Burglary only.** This misses the arson. Charring is the classic MBE marker for a completed arson.
- **Choice D**: **Neither.** This fails on both fronts, incorrectly assuming the arson wasn't "serious enough" and the burglary wasn't "unauthorized enough."

#### 9. residual_answer
Choice B is the only choice that respects both the "charring" rule for arson and the "concurrent intent" rule for burglary.

#### 10. legal_leak_audit

#### 12. remediation_card
**Arson vs. Burglary Timing**: Burglary is about why you went in. Arson is about what you burned (structure) and how (maliciously/recklessly).
