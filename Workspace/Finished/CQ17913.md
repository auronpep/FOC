## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Mary, a worship-summit organizer in State A, signed a written speaker agreement with Paul, a Christian conference speaker based in State C, for Paul to deliver the Friday-evening keynote at Mary's summer worship summit in State A. The agreement contains a clause that states:

> "This agreement shall be governed by the laws of State C, without regard to conflict-of-laws principles."

State A's choice-of-law rules enforce some contractual choice-of-law clauses but refuse to enforce others when the chosen state has no substantial relationship to the dispute. Paul later cancels the engagement, and Mary sues Paul for breach of contract in federal district court sitting in State A on the basis of diversity jurisdiction.

How should the federal court decide whether State C law governs Mary's claim?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The federal diversity court should apply the choice-of-law rules of the forum state — here, State A — to decide whether the choice-of-law clause in the speaker agreement is enforceable. **Gold Key:** under *Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Electric Mfg. Co.*, 313 U.S. 487 (1941), a federal court sitting in diversity must apply the forum state's choice-of-law rules as the rule of decision; the contract's own choice-of-law clause is not self-executing. The court therefore looks to State A's choice rules, which (on these facts) test whether State C has a substantial relationship to the dispute, before honoring — or refusing to honor — the State C clause.

**Silver Key:** when the call asks "how should the court decide" a choice-of-law question in diversity, the test is forum-state choice rules, not the contract's choice clause, not federal common law, and not party preference.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Use federal common law because speaker agreements are interstate.**

This is the cleanest Issue-Sense cut. Federal common law is not the rule of decision for state-law contract claims in a federal diversity court. *Erie R.R. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), holds that "there is no federal general common law," and a state-law contract claim in diversity is governed by the substantive law of the state that would apply under the forum's choice-of-law rules — not by a federal free-standing rule. The "speaker agreements are interstate" framing is bait: interstate commerce does not, on its own, generate federal common law for a state-law contract dispute.

**B. Enforce State C law automatically because the contract names it. (Dominant trap.)**

This is the analytically most attractive wrong answer. The student reads the choice clause — "this agreement shall be governed by the laws of State C" — and concludes that the contract controls. The breaker is the **Gold Key:** the contract clause is not self-executing in federal diversity. *Klaxon* sends the court to the forum state's choice rules first. The forum's choice rules may honor the clause, narrow it, or refuse to enforce it (e.g., because the chosen state has no substantial relationship to the dispute). The clause can only operate *through* the forum's choice rules; it does not displace them.

**C. Use the plaintiff's preferred law because the plaintiff chose the forum. (was A in the original — this is choice D, not C, after shuffle. See D below for the original-C distractor.)**

*[N/A — see D.]*

**D. Use the plaintiff's preferred law because the plaintiff chose the forum.**

This answer is attractive to a student who reads the plaintiff's forum choice as a proxy for "the plaintiff gets the plaintiff's preferred law." The breaker is that party preference is not the rule of decision. The choice of forum is a procedural decision driven by jurisdiction and venue; it does not dictate the choice of substantive law. The rule of decision is the forum's choice rules under *Klaxon*, not the plaintiff's preference.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17913, a Civil Procedure item involving a federal diversity contract suit and a contractual choice-of-law clause naming a non-forum state's law. The official key is B — the forum state's choice-of-law rules determine the effect of the clause. The tested rule sits squarely in the Erie/Klaxon line: a federal court sitting in diversity on a state-law claim must apply the choice-of-law rules of the forum state to determine which state's substantive law governs.

The doctrinal authority is well-settled. *Erie R.R. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), held that "there is no federal general common law" and that federal courts sitting in diversity on state-law claims must apply the substantive law of the state that would apply under the forum's choice-of-law rules. *Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Electric Mfg. Co.*, 313 U.S. 487 (1941), held that a federal court sitting in diversity must apply the choice-of-law rules of the state in which it sits, treating the forum state's choice-of-law rules as binding on the federal court. The Rules of Decision Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1652, provides the statutory hook: "The laws of the several states, except where the Constitution or treaties of the United States or Acts of Congress require otherwise, shall be regarded as rules of decision in civil actions in the courts of the United States, in cases where they apply." And Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws § 187(2) supplies the modern contract-specific framework: a choice-of-law clause will be given effect unless the chosen state has no substantial relationship to the parties or the transaction and there is no other reasonable basis for the parties' choice, or unless applying the chosen law would be contrary to a fundamental policy of the forum state. The transformed outcome is identical to the original: the court applies State A's choice rules to decide whether the State C clause is enforceable, and the answer is the choice-rule analysis under *Klaxon* — not automatic enforcement, not federal common law, and not party preference.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. Use federal common law because speaker agreements are interstate.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Issue-Sense misfit. Federal common law is not the rule of decision for a state-law contract claim in federal diversity. The "interstate" framing is bait.
2. **Student label:** Federal common law misfit.
3. **True/responsive version:** "A federal common law rule on speaker agreements would supply the rule of decision in diversity only if the speaker-agreement claim arose in an area of federal common law (admiralty, federal statutory, interstate disputes) — but a state-law contract claim between two private parties in diversity is not such an area."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** *Erie R.R. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938): "There is no federal general common law." A state-law contract claim in diversity is governed by the substantive law of the state that would apply under the forum's choice-of-law rules, not by federal common law. Interstate commerce, on its own, does not generate a federal common law of contracts.

### B. Enforce State C law automatically because the contract names it. (Dominant trap.)

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The contract really does say what it says. The defect is not visible from the C3 frame alone; the **Gold Key** is required to defeat the trap.
2. **Student label:** Contract-clause-as-self-executing (dominant trap).
3. **True/responsive version:** "The contract's choice-of-law clause will be honored only if the forum's choice-of-law rules say to honor it. On these facts, State A's choice rules test whether State C has a substantial relationship to the dispute. The clause is not self-executing."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** *Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Electric Mfg. Co.*, 313 U.S. 487 (1941): a federal court sitting in diversity must apply the choice-of-law rules of the forum state. The contract's choice-of-law clause does not displace those rules. Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws § 187(2) supplies the modern contract-specific framework: the clause is honored unless the chosen state has no substantial relationship to the parties or transaction and no other reasonable basis for the choice exists, or unless applying the chosen law would be contrary to a fundamental policy of the forum state.

### D. Use the plaintiff's preferred law because the plaintiff chose the forum.
