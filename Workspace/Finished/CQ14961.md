### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The outcome turns on two linked rules.

**Rule 1 — Lien priority by recording order.** Under the standard recording-act framework, priority between competing mortgages on the same parcel is determined by the order of recording. Antioch Bank recorded its mortgage first; Lydia recorded second. Antioch Bank is the senior mortgagee; Lydia is the junior.

**Rule 2 — Senior mortgagee's right to foreclose the entire parcel.** A senior mortgagee whose lien covers an entire parcel has the right to subject the whole parcel to the foreclosure sale. The junior mortgagee has no right to compel the senior to foreclose on only a portion of the mortgaged land. The senior's security interest runs to the entire tract, and the foreclosure may encompass all of it.

**Why Lydia's motion fails.** Lydia's motion asks the court to carve out three acres from the senior's foreclosure. But Antioch Bank's mortgage covers all six acres. The court will not compel a senior mortgagee to foreclose on less than the full parcel simply to preserve the junior's security. The junior's remedy, if any, lies elsewhere (e.g., bidding at the sale, seeking a surplus, or asserting a marshalling claim — but marshalling is unavailable here because both mortgages cover the same tract, not different tracts).

**Gold Key (GK-REALPROP-FOREC-01):** *A senior mortgagee may foreclose on the entire mortgaged parcel; a junior mortgagee cannot compel partial foreclosure when both liens cover the same land.*

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (post-shuffle) — "No, because a pro rata foreclosure of the vineyard will not prejudice the rights of Antioch Bank."**

*Attractive because:* Students who correctly sense the answer is "No" may gravitate toward this choice because it sounds like a reasonable fairness argument — splitting the property proportionally seems equitable. The language "will not prejudice" sounds reassuring and lawyerly.

*The breaker:* This choice misstates the legal rule. The issue is not whether a pro rata foreclosure would prejudice the bank. The issue is that the bank has a legal right to foreclose on the entire parcel, and the court will not order a partial foreclosure at the junior's request. The rationale given is wrong even though the conclusion ("No") happens to be correct. This is a classic "right answer, wrong reason" trap. **Gold Key GK-REALPROP-FOREC-01** defeats it: the senior's right to the entire parcel is the governing principle, not a prejudice analysis.

**B (post-shuffle) — "No, because of the 'two funds' rule of marshalling."**

*Attractive because:* Marshalling (the "two funds" doctrine) is a real equitable principle that can compel a senior creditor to satisfy its debt from a fund the junior cannot reach. Students who have studied marshalling may recall that it can sometimes help a junior lienholder and may be tempted to apply it here.

*The breaker:* Marshalling requires that the senior mortgagee have access to **two different funds or tracts**, while the junior has access to only one. Here, both mortgages cover the **same** six-acre vineyard. There is no second, distinct tract from which the senior could satisfy its debt while preserving the junior's security. The "two funds" rule is therefore inapplicable. **Gold Key GK-REALPROP-FOREC-01** confirms: the senior's right to the entire parcel controls, and marshalling does not apply when both liens encumber the same land.

**D (post-shuffle) — "No, because Antioch Bank holds a purchase-money mortgage."**

*Attractive because:* Purchase-money mortgages (PMMs) do enjoy special priority treatment in many jurisdictions. Students who know that PMMs are "super-priority" instruments may select this answer, reasoning that the bank's special status justifies the "No" outcome.

*The breaker:* Antioch Bank's mortgage is **not** a purchase-money mortgage. A PMM is a mortgage given to secure repayment of funds advanced to **purchase** the property. Hannah already owned the vineyard outright when she borrowed from the bank. The bank's mortgage is an ordinary subsequent mortgage, not a PMM. The conclusion ("No") is correct, but the rationale is factually wrong. **Gold Key GK-REALPROP-FOREC-01** defeats this: the correct rationale is the senior's right to foreclose the entire parcel, not PMM status.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** When two mortgages cover the same parcel, the mortgage recorded first has priority. The senior mortgagee may foreclose on the entire mortgaged parcel. A junior mortgagee has no right to compel the senior to foreclose on only a portion of the land.

**Authority:**

- **4 American Law of Property** § 16.145 (1952): Discusses marshalling and the "two funds" doctrine, confirming it applies only when the senior creditor has two funds and the junior has one — not when both cover the same tract.
- **Marquette Law Review** (1959), "The Rights of Junior Lienholders in Wisconsin": Explains the "two funds" doctrine and confirms it does not apply when both mortgages cover the same land. Also confirms the senior mortgagee's right to foreclose the entire parcel.
- **Nelson & Whitman, Real Estate Finance Law** § 8.1: Discusses foreclosure priority and the senior mortgagee's right to subject the entire property to sale.
- **Gislason & Hunter, "Marshaling of Assets: Old Doctrine Learns New Tricks"** (2020): Confirms marshalling requires (1) two creditors with a common debtor, (2) two funds, and (3) the senior creditor has claims to both funds while the second has claim to only one. If the senior has only one fund, marshalling is unavailable.
- **HOA Law Blog, "Lien Priority Explained"** (2011): Confirms that a senior mortgagee's foreclosure wipes out junior liens and the senior may foreclose on the entire property.

**Purchase-money mortgage distinction:** A PMM is a mortgage given to secure the purchase price of the property. *See* 4 American Law of Property § 16.125; **83050102** in the NCBE outline. Hannah owned the vineyard before borrowing from the bank, so the bank's mortgage is not a PMM.

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

#### Program Frame

This item tests **Real Property — Mortgages > Foreclosure > Procedure** (outline code 83050601). The tested rule: a senior mortgagee has the right to foreclose on the entire mortgaged parcel, and a junior mortgagee cannot compel partial foreclosure when both liens cover the same tract. This is a C3_SOLVE item — all eliminations are structurally visible from the stem.

#### Student Access Test

Every claim in the choice walkthroughs is verifiable from the stem facts alone, without outside legal doctrine. The PMM trap is killed by the "purchased with her own savings" fact. The marshalling trap is killed by the "same six-acre vineyard" fact. The pro-rata fairness trap is killed by recognizing that the legal test is the senior's right, not a prejudice analysis. No legal leaks.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Hannah owns a six-acre vineyard that she purchased several years ago with her own savings. She borrowed $200,000 from Antioch Community Bank (first mortgage on entire vineyard) and later $75,000 from Lydia (second mortgage on same vineyard). Both recorded. Hannah defaulted on both. Bank foreclosed. FMV = $400,000. Lydia moved to compel foreclosure on only three of six acres. Bank opposes.

**Call:** Should the court grant Lydia's motion?

**Choices:**
- A: No, because a pro rata foreclosure will not prejudice the bank.
- B: No, because of the "two funds" rule of marshalling.
- C: No, because the entire six-acre vineyard is subject to Antioch Bank's senior mortgage.
- D: No, because Antioch Bank holds a purchase-money mortgage.

#### Distilled Core Question

A mortgagor granted two mortgages on the same parcel — first to Bank, second to Lydia. Both recorded in that order. Mortgagor defaulted. Bank (senior) forecloses on the entire parcel. Lydia (junior) moves to compel foreclosure on only half. Should the court grant the motion?

#### Call and Prediction

**Call:** "Should the court grant Lydia's motion?" — This is a yes/no call asking whether the junior mortgagee's request for partial foreclosure should be granted.

**Prediction:** The answer is "No." The senior mortgagee has the right to foreclose on the entire parcel. The junior cannot compel partial foreclosure. The key distractor will be the "right answer, wrong reason" trap (PMM mischaracterization).

#### Trigger Facts

1. **Hannah purchased the vineyard with her own savings** → Kills PMM trap. The bank's loan was not used to purchase the property.
2. **Antioch Bank recorded first, Lydia recorded second** → Establishes priority. Bank is senior.
3. **Both mortgages cover the same six-acre vineyard** → Kills marshalling. No second tract exists.
4. **Lydia moved to compel foreclosure on only three acres** → Junior cannot compel partial foreclosure of senior's parcel.
5. **FMV ($400K) exceeds total debt ($275K)** → Surplus exists; bank can recover fully from full foreclosure.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "No, because a pro rata foreclosure of the vineyard will not prejudice the rights of Antioch Bank."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice frames the issue as a fairness/prejudice question. But the legal test isn't whether the bank would be prejudiced — it's whether the bank has the right to foreclose the whole parcel. The "pro rata" framing distorts the governing rule.
2. **Student label:** The fairness trap — sounds reasonable but misstates the legal test.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A correct version would state that the bank has the right to foreclose on the entire parcel because its senior mortgage covers all six acres, not that pro rata foreclosure wouldn't prejudice the bank.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The senior mortgagee's right to foreclose the entire parcel does not depend on a prejudice analysis. Authority: 4 Am. Law of Property §16.145.

**Choice B: "No, because of the 'two funds' rule of marshalling."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Marshalling is a real doctrine, but it requires two different tracts or funds. The stem says both mortgages cover the same vineyard. No second tract exists. The doctrine doesn't apply.
2. **Student label:** The marshalling trap — real doctrine, wrong facts.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** Marshalling would only apply if the bank had a mortgage on the vineyard AND on some other property, while Lydia's mortgage covered only the vineyard. That's not the case here.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Marshalling requires the senior creditor to have access to two distinct funds while the junior has only one. Here, both mortgages cover the same six-acre vineyard. Authority: Gislason & Hunter (2020); Marquette Law Review (1959).

**Choice C: "No, because the entire six-acre vineyard is subject to Antioch Bank's senior mortgage."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The bank's mortgage covers all six acres and was recorded first. The senior may foreclose on the entire parcel. The junior has no right to compel partial foreclosure. This states the correct rule.
2. **Student label:** Correct — the senior mortgage covers the entire parcel.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true and responsive answer. The senior's lien covers the whole parcel, and the junior cannot compel the senior to foreclose on less.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** A senior mortgagee whose lien covers the entire parcel may subject the whole parcel to foreclosure. Authority: Nelson & Whitman, Real Estate Finance Law §8.1.

**Choice D: "No, because Antioch Bank holds a purchase-money mortgage."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The stem says Hannah purchased the vineyard with her own savings. The bank's loan came years later. This is not a purchase-money mortgage. The conclusion ("No") is right, but the reason is factually wrong.
2. **Student label:** The PMM trap — right conclusion, wrong reason.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A correct version would state that the bank's mortgage is a regular (not purchase-money) mortgage, but it still has priority because it was recorded first.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** A PMM secures funds advanced to purchase the property. Hannah already owned the vineyard. Authority: 4 Am. Law of Property §16.125.

#### Residual Answer

After CUTting B (marshalling doesn't apply) and D (not a PMM), and CLASHing A vs. C on the rationale axis, **C** is the true-and-responsive residual. C states the correct rule: the senior mortgage covers the entire parcel.
