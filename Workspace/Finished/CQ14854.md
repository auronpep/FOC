## 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. Mary’s loose-latch statement was made after Lydia threatened to sue and in the same conversation in which Mary offered to pay Lydia’s clinic bill only if Lydia released all claims. **Gold Key:** release-of-claims language moves the conversation into compromise negotiations, so Rule 408 protects factual statements made about the claim. **Silver Key:** do not stop at hearsay; run the Evidence layer cascade. Even if the statement could clear the hearsay layer, the compromise-negotiation policy exclusion controls.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because it reaches the same exclusion result as the credited answer and points to Mary’s causation denial. The breaker is the reason. A causation denial is a merits fact, not the evidentiary ground that excludes Lydia’s offered loose-latch statement.

**B.** This is the dominant trap. It sells the common memory that factual admissions can travel with medical-payment language. **Gold Key:** that memory belongs to the narrower medical-expense rule, not to a pay-and-release settlement negotiation. Once the conversation is a compromise negotiation about a disputed claim, Rule 408 protects factual statements made during it.

**D.** This choice is attractive because Mary is the floor manager and the statement concerns the ark latch, a matter within her job. **Silver Key:** clearing the agent-statement hearsay layer does not end Evidence analysis. A separate policy exclusion can still bar the statement.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

Federal Rule of Evidence 408 bars evidence of compromise offers and “conduct or a statement made during compromise negotiations about the claim” when offered to prove or disprove the validity or amount of a disputed claim. The rule also lists limited permissible purposes, such as bias or undue delay, none of which is the purpose here. ([Legal Information Institute][1])

Federal Rule of Evidence 409 is narrower: it excludes offers to pay medical, hospital, or similar expenses when offered to prove liability. Its Advisory Committee note contrasts Rule 409 with Rule 408 and explains that Rule 409 does not extend to incidental factual statements the way compromise-negotiation protection does. ([Legal Information Institute][2])

Federal Rule of Evidence 801(d)(2)(D) supports the trap in D because an agent or employee statement on a matter within the scope of that relationship can be non-hearsay when offered against the opposing party. But non-hearsay status is not the last admissibility layer; Rule 408 supplies an independent exclusion. ([Legal Information Institute][3])

The tested issue, rule, credited legal substance, and outcome remain unchanged: factual statements made during compromise negotiations after a threatened claim, tied to a release of claims, are inadmissible under Rule 408 when offered to prove the claim.
