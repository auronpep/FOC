### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. Paul appears to have satisfied the statute's listed possession elements: he stayed for more than 20 years, used the apron as an owner would, and did so visibly through a cabin, hives, trees, and beds. Gold Key: when adverse possession is claimed against government or municipal land, ordinary possession elements do not finish the question; the last gate is whether that jurisdiction allows title to run against that owner. Silver Key: when the stem gives every listed statutory element and one answer asks about the owner's government status, treat that answer as the remaining gate. Because municipal-land immunity varies by jurisdiction, the outcome most likely turns on whether the jurisdiction permits municipal property to be acquired by adverse possession.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is the dominant trap because tax payment is a real adverse-possession requirement in some statutes. The breaker is the statute in this stem: it lists continuous, hostile, open, and notorious possession, but it does not add a tax-payment condition. Gold Key: the municipal-land gate is still open even when the listed possession elements are met.

B reaches for a familiar adverse-possession phrase, but it answers a different problem. Color of title can matter when a possessor wants constructive possession of land not actually occupied. Paul claims only the apron he actually occupied, so the outcome does not turn on color of title.

D sounds like a notice problem because open and notorious possession is in the statute. The breaker is that open and notorious possession does not require proof that the town council actually knew about Paul; visible possession is enough to charge the owner with notice. Silver Key: compare the answer to the exact listed element, not to a stronger fact the element does not require.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. A possessor who satisfies the ordinary adverse-possession elements can acquire title only if the law allows adverse possession to run against the owner of the land. Public or municipal property is often protected by statute or common-law public-use immunity, but jurisdictions differ on how far that protection reaches. Washington's RCW 7.28.090 is a primary statutory example barring adverse-possession rules from extending to certain public lands and lands held for a public purpose. City of Philadelphia v. Galdo, 217 A.3d 811 (Pa. 2019), confirms the jurisdiction-sensitive nature of the issue by allowing an adverse-possession claim against city land under Pennsylvania's public-use framework while recognizing that public-use land remains protected. Those authorities confirm the official key: once the listed possession elements are met and taxes/color of title/actual knowledge are not required by the supplied statute, the controlling issue is whether this jurisdiction permits adverse possession of municipal property. Sources checked: https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=7.28.090 and https://law.justia.com/cases/pennsylvania/supreme-court/2019/36-eap-2018.html.
