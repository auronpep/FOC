A regional tax collector suspected Tabitha, a local scribe, of facilitating the concealment of tithing records to avoid civil levies. An undercover investigator, posing as a merchant, knocked on Tabitha's door and claimed he needed a scribe to help him document a large grain shipment. ## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation
The motion to suppress should be granted because the investigator’s act of lifting the parchment exceeded the scope of Tabitha’s consent and did not fall under the plain view doctrine. Under the `Gold Key: fourth_amendment_consent_scope`, a search is constitutional if based on voluntary consent, but the search must stay within the bounds of what a reasonable person would understand was granted. Tabitha consented to the investigator being in her workspace, but she did not consent to him manipulating her belongings or looking underneath items. Because the ledger was not in plain view (it was hidden under the parchment), the investigator’s physical act of moving the parchment constituted an unauthorized search.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations
(A) This choice is attractive because the "immediately apparent" nature of evidence is a requirement for a plain view seizure. However, it fails here because the item was not in "plain view" in the first place. The investigator had to physically move parchment to find the ledger, which is a search that requires independent justification (like a warrant or specific consent).

(B) This is the **Dominant Trap**. It is attractive because of the general principle that a person "assumes the risk" when they invite a stranger into their home that the stranger might be an undercover officer. While Tabitha assumed the risk that the officer would *see* things in plain sight, she did not assume the risk that he would perform a physical search of her closed or covered belongings.

(D) This choice is attractive because it feels unfair for the government to lie to gain entry. However, the Supreme Court has held that the Fourth Amendment is not violated when an undercover officer obtains consent to enter a home by misrepresenting their identity or purpose, provided they stay within the physical scope of the invitation. The deceit itself did not make the entry unconstitutional.

## 8. Black-Letter Verification
The Fourth Amendment protects against "unreasonable searches and seizures." An undercover officer does not violate the Fourth Amendment by lying to get inside a home (*Hoffa v. United States*; *Lewis v. United States*). However, once inside, the officer is limited by the scope of the consent and the plain view doctrine (*Arizona v. Hicks*). Lifting an object to see what is underneath constitutes a "search" and requires probable cause/warrant/consent. The tested rule (scope of consent/plain view), legal issue (undercover manipulation of desk items), and credited outcome (suppression) are unchanged from original QID 16182.
