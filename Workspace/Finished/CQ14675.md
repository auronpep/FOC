## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**B is correct.** Stephen intentionally strangled Daniel with a belt — a deliberate, fully volitional act demonstrating both the intent to kill and the premeditation and deliberation required for murder. Daniel's consent and the merciful motive behind Stephen's actions do not negate the legal significance of Stephen's mental state. Under black-letter law, intent to kill combined with premeditation and deliberation constitutes malice aforethought, which is the mens rea of murder. Motive (mercy) is irrelevant to the degree of homicide; it may be considered at sentencing but is not a defense to the charge itself. Stephen is guilty of murder.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A — Involuntary Manslaughter:** This answer is attractive because it frames Stephen's conduct as an unintentional outcome of a compassionate impulse — as if Stephen "merely" caused death without really meaning to kill. The move trades on the intuition that a mercy killing feels less blameworthy. But involuntary manslaughter requires recklessness — an extreme deviation from ordinary care — not a fully intentional killing. Stephen used a belt to strangle Daniel. That is not a reckless act gone wrong; it is a purposeful act carried to its intended conclusion. The mens rea is intent to kill, not recklessness. Cut.

**C — No Degree of Criminal Homicide:** This answer is attractive because Daniel was a competent adult who explicitly requested death, and Stephen honored that request out of love and compassion. The move sells the idea that a victim's willing consent eliminates the criminal nature of the act. But consent is not a defense to homicide in American criminal law. Daniel's right to life ended the moment Stephen decided to take it; Daniel's earlier request did not grant Stephen a legal license to kill. Stephen committed a volitional act (strangulation) with intent to kill. Actus reus is satisfied. Cut.

**D — Voluntary Manslaughter:** This answer is attractive because the scenario is saturated with emotional content — Daniel's suffering, Stephen's reluctance, the many conversations in which Stephen tried to talk Daniel out of it. The move sells the idea that a defendant who kills out of compassion must be acting in the heat of passion that voluntary manslaughter requires. But heat of passion requires an adequate provocation that would cause a reasonable person to lose self-control — not merely a slow, anticipated, requested death. Daniel's repeated, calm requests over many late-night conversations are the opposite of an adequate provocation. Stephen's deliberate compliance shows he was not in the grip of sudden passion. The distinguishing fact is the deliberateness of Stephen's act. CLASH resolves here.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Governing Rule:** Under the common law and MPC approaches to criminal homicide, a defendant who intentionally kills another human being with premeditation, deliberation, and malice aforethought is guilty of murder. Consent of the victim is not a defense to murder. Mercy, compassion, or "good motive" may be considered in mitigation at sentencing but does not negate the requisite mens rea for murder.

**Authority:** This rule is confirmed by the general principle that "consent is no defense to murder" in American criminal law. The doctrine traces to the common law's rejection of the volenti non fit injuria principle in homicide contexts. Leading hornbook authority: *Rollin M. Perkins & Ronald N. Perkins, Criminal Law* (3d ed. 1969) (discussing malice aforethought and the irrelevance of motive to the degree of homicide). The principle is also reflected in the MPC § 210.1 et seq. definition of murder as a homicide committed purposely or knowingly. Additional support: *State v. Brown* (Iowa 1983) (mercy motive does not reduce murder to manslaughter). No NCBE, ABA, or bar-exam authority endorsement is claimed or implied.

**Verified:** The tested rule (consent/motive does not negate murder; premeditation + intent = murder) and the credited outcome (murder) are confirmed unchanged from the original question.

**## review_truth**
