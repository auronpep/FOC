## 1. Final Question

Barnabas owned a small vineyard that he had been cultivating for years. Six months before Lydia's college graduation, Barnabas orally promised Lydia that he would give her the vineyard as a gift on her graduation day. Lydia, anticipating the gift, entered into a valid written contract to sell the vineyard to Martha, with the closing to take place "one week after Lydia's college graduation." Barnabas failed to convey the vineyard to Lydia on her graduation day. On the intended closing date — one week after graduation — Martha first learned that Lydia could not convey the vineyard because Barnabas had broken his promise. Martha considered suing Lydia but realized she could not compel conveyance while Barnabas still held title. Two weeks after graduation, Barnabas died. Under his valid will, Barnabas devised the vineyard to Lydia. Within a week, the executor of Barnabas's estate delivered to Lydia an executor's deed to the vineyard in compliance with state law. Martha promptly learned of this transfer and demanded that Lydia convey the vineyard to her. Lydia refused. Martha sued Lydia for specific performance. Who will likely prevail?

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The valid written contract between Lydia and Martha contained no "time is of the essence" clause. Under the default rule, when a land-sale contract is silent on this point, the closing date is not a strict deadline — the parties are entitled to a reasonable postponement. Lydia acquired the vineyard by devise and executor's deed less than three weeks after the original closing date (two weeks after graduation plus one week for the executor's deed), which constitutes a reasonable period of postponement. Because the contract remains enforceable and Lydia now holds title, Martha is entitled to specific performance.

**Gold Key:** In the absence of a "time is of the essence" clause, equity treats the closing date as a target, not a deadline — performance within a reasonable time satisfies the contract, and the buyer may obtain specific performance once the seller acquires title.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect (devise vs. conveyance).** This choice sells the misconception that the *method* by which the seller acquired title determines whether the contract can be enforced. The source of the seller's title — whether by lifetime conveyance, inheritance, devise, or otherwise — is irrelevant to the buyer's rights under a valid land-sale contract. What matters is that the seller holds title at the time of performance. The contract was valid when signed, and Lydia's subsequent acquisition of title by devise satisfied her obligation. This distractor is attractive to students who conflate title-origin doctrines with contract-enforcement rules. **Gold Key:** A seller's acquisition of title by any means — conveyance, devise, or otherwise — satisfies the seller's obligation under a land-sale contract; the buyer's remedy of specific performance is available regardless of how the seller obtained title.

**B is incorrect (contract void without owner's signature).** This choice fabricates a rule that a land-sale contract is void unless signed by the legal owner. In fact, a person who does not yet own property may validly contract to sell it, provided the seller is able to acquire and convey title by the time of closing. The contract is not void — it is enforceable once the seller obtains title. This distractor preys on the intuition that one cannot sell what one does not own, but the law distinguishes between present conveyance (which requires title) and a contract to convey (which requires only that the seller acquire title before performance). **Gold Key:** A contract to sell land is not void merely because the seller lacks title at the time of contracting; the seller need only acquire title by the time of performance.

**D is incorrect (merger into executor's deed) — DOMINANT TRAP.** This choice reverses the merger doctrine. The merger doctrine applies when a deed is delivered at closing — the contract merges into the deed, and contract claims are extinguished. Here, the executor's deed was delivered to Lydia *by the estate*, not by the closing. The executor's deed conveyed title from Barnabas's estate to Lydia; it did not supersede or merge Lydia's pre-existing contract with Martha. The contract remains enforceable, and Martha's right to specific performance survives. This distractor is attractive because students confuse the executor's deed (which gave Lydia title) with a closing deed (which would have merged the contract). **Gold Key:** The merger doctrine applies only when a deed is delivered *at closing* in fulfillment of the contract — an executor's deed from a third party's estate to the seller does not merge or extinguish the seller's prior contractual obligations.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing Rule:** When a contract for the sale of land does not contain a "time is of the essence" clause, the closing date is not a strict deadline. Equity permits a reasonable postponement of the closing, and the contract remains enforceable if the seller performs within a reasonable time. *See* 7A C.J.S. Specific Performance § 120; Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 242 (reasonable time for performance when the agreement is silent).

**Seller's lack of title at contracting:** A seller who does not own the property at the time of contracting may still enter into a valid and enforceable land-sale contract. The seller's obligation is to acquire and convey title by the time of performance. *See* 71 Am. Jur. 2d Specific Performance § 100; Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 242 cmt. d.

**Specific performance available:** Because real property is deemed unique, specific performance is the standard remedy for breach of a land-sale contract. Once the seller acquires title, the buyer may compel conveyance. *See* 71 Am. Jur. 2d Specific Performance § 16; Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 360.

**Merger doctrine:** The merger doctrine applies only when a deed is delivered at closing pursuant to the contract. A deed from a third party (such as an executor's deed) to the seller does not trigger merger of the seller's prior contractual obligations. *See* 23 Am. Jur. 2d Deeds § 259; 7A C.J.S. Specific Performance § 140.

**Authority class:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 242, 360; C.J.S. and Am. Jur. secondary authority confirming the black-letter rule. The Restatement is the primary authority floor; no fabricated case names are cited.

## 12. Review Truth

## Question Data

**Stem:** Barnabas owned a vineyard. Six months before Lydia's college graduation, he orally promised to give her the vineyard as a gift on her graduation day. Lydia entered into a valid written contract to sell the vineyard to Martha, with closing "one week after Lydia's college graduation." Barnabas failed to convey the vineyard on graduation day. On the closing date, Martha learned Lydia couldn't convey. Two weeks after graduation, Barnabas died. Under his will, he devised the vineyard to Lydia. Within a week, the executor gave Lydia an executor's deed. Martha demanded conveyance. Lydia refused. Martha sued for specific performance.

**Call:** Who will likely prevail?

**Choices:**
- **A:** Lydia, because she received title by devise rather than by conveyance.
- **B:** Lydia, because the contract was not signed by the legal owner and was therefore void.
- **C:** Martha, because the contract remained enforceable within a reasonable period after the proposed closing date.
- **D:** Martha, because the contract merged into the executor's deed.

## Distilled Core Question

A buyer and seller have a valid written land-sale contract with a fixed closing date. The seller doesn't own the land at signing or at closing. The seller inherits the land two weeks after closing and receives an executor's deed. No "time is of the essence" clause. Can the buyer obtain specific performance?

## Call and Prediction

**Call:** "Who will likely prevail?" — This is a standard outcome call. You must determine which party wins and why.

**Prediction:** The buyer (Martha) will likely prevail because the contract is valid, the seller (Lydia) acquired title within a reasonable time, and the contract had no "time is of the essence" clause. The correct answer will state that the contract remained enforceable within a reasonable period.

## Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

### Choice A

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice points to a real fact — Lydia did receive title by devise. But the call asks who prevails on the specific performance claim. The *source* of the seller's title doesn't determine whether the buyer can enforce the contract. This is a red herring that answers a question not in play.

2. **Student label:** Title-source red herring.

3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would say: "Lydia, because the contract was void ab initio" — but that's not what this choice says. The choice as written is factually accurate (she did get title by devise) but legally irrelevant to the enforcement question.

4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The method of acquiring title is immaterial to the buyer's rights under a valid land-sale contract. 71 Am. Jur. 2d Specific Performance § 100.

**Verdict:** NOT_RESPONSIVE — ISSUE_SENSE (misfit). Eliminate.

### Choice B

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice claims the contract is "void" because the seller didn't own title when signing. This overclaims. The law allows a non-owner to contract to sell land — the seller just needs to acquire title by the time of performance. "Void" is the wrong word; the contract is valid and enforceable once the seller obtains title.

2. **Student label:** The "you can't sell what you don't own" trap.

3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would say: "Lydia, because Martha's remedy is damages, not specific performance, since Lydia didn't hold title at closing" — but that's not what this choice says, and it would also be wrong because specific performance is available once the seller acquires title.

4. **Lawyer confirmation:** A seller's lack of title at the time of contracting does not void the land-sale contract. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 242 cmt. d.

**Verdict:** NOT_TRUE — EAR_OVERCLAIM (fabricated_rule). Eliminate.

### Choice C ✅ CORRECT

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The contract had no "time is of the essence" clause. That means the closing date was a target, not a deadline. Lydia acquired title within a reasonable time (about 3 weeks after closing). The contract remains enforceable, and Martha can obtain specific performance.

2. **Student label:** Correct — reasonable postponement, no time-is-of-essence clause.

3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This choice IS the true-and-responsive version. It correctly identifies the rule (reasonable postponement) and applies it to the facts (Lydia acquired title within a reasonable time).

4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Without "time is of the essence," equity permits reasonable postponement. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 242. Specific performance is available once the seller acquires title. 71 Am. Jur. 2d Specific Performance § 16.

**Verdict:** Correct answer. True and responsive.

### Choice D — DOMINANT TRAP

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice gets the OUTCOME right (Martha wins) but for the WRONG reason. It says the contract "merged into the executor's deed." Merger applies when a deed is delivered at closing pursuant to the contract. Here, the executor's deed was from Barnabas's estate to Lydia — a third-party transfer, not a closing deed. The contract doesn't merge; it survives, and Martha's right to specific performance remains.

2. **Student label:** The merger doctrine reversal.

3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would say: "Martha, because the contract remained enforceable" — which is what C says. D's reasoning (merger) is backwards.

4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The merger doctrine applies only when a deed is delivered at closing pursuant to the contract. An executor's deed from a third party's estate does not merge or extinguish prior contractual obligations. 23 Am. Jur. 2d Deeds § 259.

**Gold Key:** The merger doctrine extinguishes the contract only when a deed is delivered AT CLOSING in fulfillment of the contract — a deed from a third party (like an executor's deed) to the seller does NOT merge the seller's prior contractual obligations.

**Verdict:** NOT_TRUE — EAR_FALSITY (backwards). Eliminate.

## Wrong-Answer Recovery Paths

**If you picked A (title by devise):** You confused a factual detail (how Lydia got title) with a legal rule. Ask yourself: "Does the source of the seller's title affect the buyer's contract rights?" The answer is no. Recovery: Remember that contract enforcement depends on the seller's ability to convey at performance time, not on how they acquired title.

**If you picked B (contract void):** You applied the common-sense rule "you can't sell what you don't own" too broadly. The law distinguishes between a present conveyance (requires title now) and a contract to convey (requires title by performance). Recovery: A non-owner can validly contract to sell land; the contract is enforceable once the seller acquires title.

**If you picked D (merger):** You got the outcome right but the reason wrong. The merger doctrine is a real doctrine, but it applies to closing deeds, not to third-party deeds to the seller. Recovery: Merger = deed delivered AT CLOSING under the contract. An executor's deed from a third party's estate is not a closing deed.
