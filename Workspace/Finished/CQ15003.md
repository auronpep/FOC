## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**A is correct.** This is a federal court sitting in diversity. Two rules control which state's substantive law the court must apply to a state-law claim.

**First — Erie/Klaxon.** Under *Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), a federal court exercising diversity jurisdiction must apply the substantive law of the state whose law would govern the action. Under *Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Electric Mfg. Co.*, 313 U.S. 487 (1941), the federal court must also apply the **choice-of-law rules of the forum state** (here, the State A federal court sitting in State A applies State A's choice-of-law rules) — the federal court does not run its own independent choice-of-law analysis.

**Second — the forum's choice-of-law test for a personal claim.** State A's modern choice-of-law approach for tort and fraud claims is the Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws "most significant relationship" test (§ 145). The court identifies the state with the "most significant relationship" to the **occurrence and the parties** by weighing the place of injury, the place of the conduct causing the injury, and the domicile, residence, and place of business of the parties, along with the place where the parties' relationship is centered (§ 145(2)).

**Applied here.** The conduct causing the injury (Paul's misrepresentation and Barnabas's reliance-induced payment) occurred in State B; Barnabas resides in State B; the parties' relationship is centered in State B. Although the mineral rights themselves are located in State A, the **lex situs rule** is the choice-of-law rule for *property* issues (e.g., validity of a deed, validity of covenants running with the land, inheritance of land). It is **not** the rule for a fraud claim seeking **money damages** for reliance — that is a personal, not a property, claim. State B therefore has the most significant relationship to this fraud claim, and State B's substantive law applies.

**Silver Key (CLASH axis):** When the asset sits in State A and the injury sits in State B, ask: "Is the claim about the *property* (lex situs wins) or about a *personal injury from the seller's conduct* (greatest interest wins)?" A claim for **money damages based on the buyer's reliance on the seller's misrepresentation** is personal. Reach for the greatest-interest test, not lex situs.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**C (was A) — "No, because the suit was filed in State A, so State A's substantive laws will apply."**
What makes this attractive: students who remember only "federal court in State A → State A's law" treat the federal forum as if it were a state court bound to apply its own substantive law automatically. That is a forum-court shortcut, not a choice-of-law rule.
The breaker: *Klaxon* requires the federal court to apply the forum state's **choice-of-law rules**, which then point to whichever state has the most significant relationship. The forum of the suit is not, by itself, the substantive-law answer — and the federal court is not free to ignore the forum's choice-of-law analysis and default to its own law. The "where the suit was filed" cue collapses *Klaxon* into nothing.
**Silver Key (cut):** *Klaxon* is a step, not an end-point. If a choice asserts "the federal court in State A applies State A law" without going through the forum's choice-of-law rules, it's a forum shortcut — cut it.

**D (was B) — "No, because the mineral rights are located in State A, so State A's substantive laws will apply."** — **DOMINANT TRAP (65% inherited)**
What makes this attractive: the asset is real property in State A, and the student remembers the **lex situs** rule that property issues are governed by the law of the situs. That is a real rule. It is also the *most common way to be wrong* on this question (65% of the focus group picked it). The trap rewards the surface-cue match.
The breaker: lex situs is the choice-of-law rule for **property** issues — whether a deed is valid, whether a covenant runs with the land, whether an interest has been validly created or transferred. It is **not** the rule for a fraud claim seeking **money damages for reliance on a misrepresentation**. The claim is personal; the situs of the property is one fact among many, not a trump card. The student's job is to notice that the claim is for **damages**, not for a determination about the **property** itself.
**Gold Key (tested choice D):** **Lex situs governs property questions; the most-significant-relationship test governs personal-injury claims (including fraud-for-reliance damages).** A claim for money damages based on the buyer's reliance on a misrepresentation is a **personal** claim — even if the asset is real property.

**B (was D) — "Yes, because State B is where the buyer lives."**
What makes this attractive: the buyer's residence in State B is one of the Section 145(2) factors, and students who remember that "the plaintiff lives in State B" reach for a residency-based answer. The result is right (State B's law applies) but the **reason** is incomplete.
The breaker: the buyer's domicile is **one contact** among the four the Restatement lists; it is not outcome-determinative on its own. The correct answer must account for the **combination** of (i) the place where the injury occurred and (ii) the place where the conduct causing the injury occurred, both of which are also State B, plus the parties' relationship being centered in State B. A pure domicile answer strips out the place-of-injury and place-of-conduct factors, so it misstates the test.
**Silver Key (tested choice B):** When a "Yes" answer points to a *single* state and names only one factor (domicile, situs, or forum), test whether the *other* factors point the same way. Here they do — so the answer is *right in result, wrong in stated reason*, and the rule answer (A) names the test, not just one factor.

## 9. Full Black-Letter Verification AND Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

**The tested black-letter rule.** In a federal diversity action, the federal court applies state substantive law (*Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938)) and applies the **choice-of-law rules of the forum state** to decide *which* state's substantive law governs the claim (*Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Electric Mfg. Co.*, 313 U.S. 487 (1941)). The forum's modern choice-of-law test for tort and fraud claims is the Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws § 145 "most significant relationship" test, which weighs the place of injury, the place of the conduct causing the injury, the domicile, residence, and place of business of the parties, and the place where the relationship between the parties is centered. The **lex situs** rule is a separate, narrower choice-of-law rule that applies to **property** issues (validity, construction, and inheritance of interests in land), not to personal claims such as fraud-for-reliance damages.

**Why the credited answer (A — "Yes, because State B has the greatest interest in the dispute") is correct.** After *Klaxon* sends the question to State A's choice-of-law rules, the Restatement § 145 analysis is:
- **Place where the injury occurred:** State B (Barnabas paid a defrauded price in State B; reliance harm manifested in State B).
- **Place where the conduct causing the injury occurred:** State B (Paul's misrepresentations were made in State B while visiting).
- **Domicile, residence, place of business of the parties:** Paul resides in State A; Barnabas resides in State B. Barnabas's residence is in State B.
- **Place where the relationship is centered:** State B (the negotiations, the misrepresentation, and the payment all occurred in State B).
Three of the four Section 145(2) contacts point to State B; the situs of the mineral rights is a fact about the asset, not about the *occurrence and the parties* in a fraud-for-reliance claim. Under the Restatement "most significant relationship" test, the **place of injury is presumptively applied** unless another state has a more significant relationship to the particular issue — and the situs of the property does not displace the place of injury for a personal claim. State B has the most significant relationship to this fraud claim; State B's substantive law therefore applies.
