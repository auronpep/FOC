### 1. Final question

Daniel, a widowed retired Christian bookstore owner, was gravely ill in a long-term care facility. Daniel owned a rare hymnbook and sermon-manuscript collection that he had told his adult children, Stephen and Lydia, would be shared equally after his death. Stephen visited Daniel after Sunday services and said, "You know I am the one who still comes to read Psalms with you, while Lydia barely comes at all. "That is true," Stephen replied, "but all my visits and prayers should count for something. Besides, if you will not sell it to me for that price, perhaps I will stop coming by too."

### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**B is correct.** Daniel's assent was induced by undue influence. He was gravely ill, isolated in a long-term care facility, and emotionally dependent on Stephen's visits. Stephen was Daniel's adult child and used that trusted relationship to press Daniel into a sale that Daniel knew was far below value. The pressure was not merely a hard bargain. Stephen tied Daniel's continued companionship, prayer visits, and Psalm-reading visits to Daniel's willingness to sign away a valuable collection for $25,000 when both men knew it was worth at least $90,000. Daniel signed because he feared losing Stephen's visits. That combination of vulnerability, trusted relationship, no independent advice, and excessive pressure makes the contract voidable for undue influence.

**Gold Key:** Undue influence is the trusted-relationship pressure defense. The exam signal is a vulnerable person, a trusted or dominant persuader, and pressure that substitutes the persuader's will for the vulnerable person's free choice.

**Silver Key:** When all four choices are defense labels, do not pick the label that merely sounds unfair. Pair each label with its required missing fact: duress needs an improper threat, mistake needs a false belief, inadequate consideration needs no bargained exchange, and undue influence needs vulnerability plus excessive relational pressure.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Inadequate consideration.**

This answer is attractive because $25,000 for a collection worth at least $90,000 feels unfair. The breaker is that ordinary consideration doctrine does not require equal market value. Daniel received a bargained-for promise of money in exchange for the collection, so the consideration requirement is not defeated by the price gap alone. The unfair price matters, but it matters as evidence of excessive pressure and unfair persuasion under undue influence, not as a standalone consideration failure. **Gold Key:** Adequacy of consideration is not price fairness; once there is a bargain, courts usually do not require equivalent values.

**C. Mistake.**

This choice tempts students because a bad-value deal can look like someone must have misunderstood the facts. The breaker is visible in the stem: both Daniel and Stephen knew the collection was worth much more than the contract price. Daniel expressly said it was worth at least $90,000, and Stephen agreed. There was no belief not in accord with the facts. The problem was pressure, not mistake.

**D. Duress.**

This is the dominant trap. It feels powerful because Stephen used a threat-like sentence: if Daniel would not sign, Stephen might stop visiting. The breaker is that duress requires an improper threat of the kind the law treats as coercive, not merely emotionally painful family pressure. Stephen did not threaten violence, crime, tort, bad-faith legal process, or a breach of a duty owed under a contract. His conduct is still legally serious, but it fits undue influence because he exploited Daniel's vulnerability and trusted dependence to obtain assent. **Gold Key:** The duress-vs-undue-influence split turns on the pressure source. Threat-based coercion points toward duress; exploitation of trust and vulnerability points toward undue influence.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The source row tests Contracts, Defenses to Enforceability, specifically undue influence. The supplied official key is undue influence, and the transformed item preserves that outcome.

The governing rule is Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 177. It treats undue influence as unfair persuasion by a person who dominates the victim or occupies a relationship that justifies trust, and it makes a contract voidable when that influence induces assent. That authority fits the item: Daniel was gravely ill and emotionally dependent, Stephen was his adult child, Stephen exploited that relationship, Daniel had no independent advice in the moment, and the resulting sale was sharply below known value.

The duress distractor remains wrong under Restatement (Second) of Contracts Sections 175-176. Duress by threat requires an improper threat that leaves no reasonable alternative. Section 176's examples of impropriety include threats involving crime, tort, bad-faith criminal/civil process, breach of good faith, or similar illegitimate use of power. A family member's statement that he might stop visiting can supply excessive relational pressure for undue influence, but on these facts it does not supply the kind of improper threat needed for duress.

The inadequate-consideration distractor remains wrong under Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 79. Once consideration exists, no extra requirement of equivalent exchanged values applies. The $25,000 price for property worth at least $90,000 is important evidence of unfair persuasion, but it is not a consideration defect by itself. The mistake distractor remains wrong under Restatement (Second) of Contracts Sections 151 and 152 because mistake requires a belief not in accord with the facts, and the stem says both parties knew the value gap.
