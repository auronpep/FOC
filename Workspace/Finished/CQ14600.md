### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Correct answer: B.**

The warrant authorized a search for one specific item: the chisels' case containing counterfeit coins. The agents found and seized that item. Once the sole object of the warrant was recovered, the warrant's authority was spent. The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant "particularly describ[e] … the … things to be seized." A warrant for a particular package does not give agents an open-ended license to rummage through every room, closet, and locked trunk in the house once that package is in hand.

> **Gold Key — Warrant Exhaustion Rule:** When a search warrant authorizes the search for a particular item or items, the warrant's authority ends once those items are found and secured. The officers may not continue searching the premises under the authority of that warrant. See *United States v. Grubbs*, 547 U.S. 90 (2006); *Horton v. California*, 496 U.S. 128 (1990).

The shotgun in the basement trunk was discovered during a continuatied search conducted after the warrant's purpose was fulfilled. It must be suppressed.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations (Diagnostic Register)

**A. "No, because the initial opening of the mailed package was without probable cause."**

*Why it's attractive:* Students who memorize the warrant requirement sometimes overgeneralize. Warrantless = unconstitutional feels safe. The word "probable cause" triggers the reflex — "they opened a package without a warrant, so everything downstream is poisoned."

*The breaker:* **Gold Key — Border/International Mail Exception.** Federal agents may search international mail at the border without a warrant and without individualized probable cause. This is a settled exception to the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement. See *United States v. Ramsey*, 431 U.S. 606 (1977). The predicate search was lawful. This answer attacks the wrong link in the chain.

> **Gold Key cited:** `exception` — Fourth Amendment border/international-mail search exception (no probable cause required).

---

**C. "Yes, because the agents had probable cause to believe more counterfeit coins were hidden in the house, and the shotgun was found during a proper contraband search."** ★ DOMINANT TRAP

*Why it's attractive:* This is the classic "probable cause creep" answer. Students correctly remember that probable cause can justify some warrantless searches — and they intuit that finding some contraband creates probable cause for more. The reasoning has a logical ring: they found coins, so they suspected more, so they kept looking. It *feels* like good police work.

*The breaker:* **Gold Key — Warrant Exhaustion Rule.** Probable cause to search *afresh* is not the question. The agents were already inside under a *warrant*. A warrant for specific items does not become a general search authority just because officers have a hunch about more contraband. The proper path would have been a *second warrant*. Continuing to search the house under the authority of a warrant whose object has already been found converts a particularized warrant into the kind of general warrant the Fourth Amendment was adopted to prohibit. See *United States v. Grubbs*, 547 U.S. 90 (2006).

> **Gold Key cited:** `rule` — Warrant Exhaustion Rule: warrant authority terminates upon seizure of the described items.

---

**D. "Yes, because smuggling recipients are frequently armed, and the search was justified for officer safety."**

*Why it's attractive:* Officer-safety arguments are instinctive. Students picture agents going into a house and wanting to be safe. The phrase "smuggling recipients are frequently armed" sounds like the kind of thing a court would consider. It's emotionally compelling.

*The breaker:* **Gold Key — Protective Sweep Limits.** Officers may conduct only a "protective sweep" — a cursory visual inspection of spaces where a person might hide — and only when they have specific reason to believe someone inside poses a danger. *Maryland v. Buie*, 494 U.S. 325 (1990). A protective sweep does not authorize opening a locked trunk in the basement. And a generalized profile ("smuggling recipients are often armed") is not the specific reasonable suspicion Buie requires.

> **Gold Key cited:** `rule` — Protective Sweep doctrine limits: cursory visual inspection only; no container opening; specific articulable suspicion of danger required.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Tested Rule:** Scope of a search warrant — a warrant that authorizes search for a particular item does not permit a continued general search of the premises once that item has been found and seized.

**Credited outcome:** Motion to suppress is GRANTED. The gun is inadmissible.

**Authority:**

1. **United States v. Grubbs, 547 U.S. 90 (2006).** The Supreme Court addressed anticipatory warrants and reaffirmed that a warrant's authority is defined by its terms. A warrant to search for specified items authorizes no broader search. Once the described items are seized, the warrant is exhausted. *Grubbs* upheld an anticipatory warrant triggered by delivery, confirming that the warrant's limited authorization was tied to the particular package — not an open-ended license to search the premises.

2. **Horton v. California, 496 U.S. 128 (1990).** The Court confirmed that plain-view seizure is lawful only when the officer has a lawful right of access to the item and its incriminating character is immediately apparent. But plain view does not expand a warrant's scope beyond the items described — an officer executing a warrant for Item A may not prosecute a general search for Items B, C, and D.

3. **United States v. Ramsey, 431 U.S. 606 (1977).** Federal agents may open international mail at the border without a warrant or probable cause. The customs/border exception authorizes the initial search of the mailed package.

4. **Maryland v. Buie, 494 U.S. 325 (1990).** A protective sweep of a home is limited to a cursory visual inspection of areas where a person might be hiding, requires specific reason to believe someone present poses a danger, and does not extend to opening closed containers such as a locked trunk.

**Verification:** The tested rule, credited answer, and legal issue are UNCHANGED from the original. The original key is A (suppress); the variant key is B (suppress via "exceeded scope of warrant"). The legal substance is identical: the gun was found during a search that exceeded the warrant's authority. Research confirms the governing rule. No doctrinal drift detected.
