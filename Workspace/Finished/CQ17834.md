### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** A city may regulate land use, and it may sometimes condition a permit on a property dedication that offsets the proposed project's impacts. But a permit condition that demands property is not valid just because the city wants a public benefit. **Gold Key:** a land-use exaction must have an essential nexus to a legitimate land-use interest and must be roughly proportional to the proposed project's impact. Here, the city demanded a public promenade across Lydia's lot while making no individualized finding that a small flour-storage room would create the need for that promenade or for a 20-foot strip. **Silver Key:** when the stem gives "no individualized finding" and "not tied to projected impact," choose the answer that makes the city prove fit, not the answer that treats the permit as a blank check.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A** sells a clean title-transfer rule. It sounds formal because fee simple ownership feels like the strongest version of a taking. The breaker is that the exaction problem does not wait for fee title. A required public access easement or dedication can trigger the same nexus-and-proportionality analysis. **Gold Key:** the test asks whether the government demanded property as a permit price, not whether the city took fee simple title.

**C** sells zoning deference. Students remember that cities have broad zoning power, then let that general power swallow the takings limit. The breaker is the absolute word "immune." Zoning authority does not make permit exactions categorically immune from Takings Clause review.

**D** sells public-benefit common sense. A promenade connecting two plazas sounds useful, and the permit is discretionary, so the trade can feel fair. The breaker is the missing fit. The city must connect the demanded property to the project's impacts; it cannot demand any public benefit simply because the applicant wants a permit. **Silver Key:** do not let a useful civic project erase the stem's no-fit facts.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule.** The Fifth Amendment Takings Clause limits government demands for property in the land-use permitting process. In *Nollan v. California Coastal Commission*, 483 U.S. 825 (1987), the Supreme Court required an essential nexus between the permit condition and the governmental interest that would justify denial of the permit. In *Dolan v. City of Tigard*, 512 U.S. 374 (1994), the Court added that the required dedication must be roughly proportional to the projected impact of the proposed development. *Koontz v. St. Johns River Water Management District*, 570 U.S. 595 (2013), confirms that the Nollan/Dolan framework applies to government demands made in the land-use permit process. Current-law check: *Sheetz v. County of El Dorado*, 601 U.S. ___ (2024), confirms that the Takings Clause does not exempt legislatures or agencies from ordinary unconstitutional-conditions limits on land-use permits.

The credited answer is therefore unchanged: the condition is likely invalid unless the city can show essential nexus and rough proportionality.

**Why the official key is confirmed.** The source row's official answer says the government generally must show essential nexus and rough proportionality between the condition and the projected impact of the development. The researched authority matches that rule and does not contradict the official key.

**Authority and current-law notes.** The lawyer-confirmation layer relies on the Fifth Amendment Takings Clause, *Nollan*, *Dolan*, *Koontz*, and *Sheetz*.
