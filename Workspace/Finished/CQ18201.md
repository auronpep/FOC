### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** A liquidated damages clause is enforceable (and does not constitute a penalty) if, at the time the contract was formed, the stipulated amount was a reasonable forecast of the damages that would result from breach. The test is forward-looking at formation, not backward-looking at actual harm. Here, the court will ask whether $80 per day was reasonably related to what the parties anticipated Peter's additional expenses would be when they signed the contract. If so, the clause is enforceable regardless of what Peter actually spent or whether he could have spent less.

**Gold Key (GK-CONTRACTS-LD-01):** *A valid liquidated damages clause replaces the plaintiff's obligation to prove actual damages. Once the clause is found enforceable, the plaintiff recovers the stipulated amount without showing what was actually lost — and the defendant cannot reduce it by arguing the plaintiff failed to mitigate.*

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 356(1) provides:

> *"Damages for breach by either party may be liquidated in the agreement but only at an amount that is reasonable in the light of the anticipated or actual loss caused by the breach and the difficulties of proof of loss. A term fixing unreasonably large liquidated damages is unenforceable on grounds of public policy as a penalty."*

The MBE test for enforceability of a liquidated damages clause has three prongs:
1. **Anticipated damages were uncertain or difficult to ascertain** at the time of contracting;
2. **The amount stipulated was a reasonable forecast** of just compensation for the breach at the time of contracting; and
3. **The clause is not a penalty** — i.e., the amount is not grossly disproportionate to anticipated or actual harm.

The critical temporal focus is **at the time the contract was formed**, not after the breach. Once a clause is found enforceable, the plaintiff recovers the liquidated amount without proving actual damages, and the defendant cannot reduce recovery by showing the plaintiff failed to mitigate or that actual damages were lower.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 356(1); see also UCC § 2-718(1) (sale-of-goods parallel); Lake River Corp. v. Carborundum Co., 769 F.2d 1284 (7th Cir. 1985) (discussing penalty doctrine and efficient breach).
