## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Choice B — post-shuffle)

**Gold Key:** Under 28 U.S.C. § 1367(a), a federal court may exercise supplemental jurisdiction over a claim only when it forms "part of the same case or controversy" as the claim over which the court has original jurisdiction — the same "common nucleus of operative fact" test from *United Mine Workers v. Gibbs*, 383 U.S. 715 (1966). A permissive counterclaim (one that does not arise out of the same transaction or occurrence as the plaintiff's claim) does not satisfy this test. Because the promissory-note claim here is factually unrelated to the combine-harvester contract dispute, and because the $15,000 amount is below the $75,000 diversity threshold, the counterclaim has no independent jurisdictional basis and cannot ride into court on supplemental jurisdiction.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice D (original A):** This choice is attractive because Rule 13 really does permit counterclaims — both compulsory and permissive. A student who locks onto the word "permits" may feel the pull of a rule they genuinely learned. But the trap is that procedural permission to plead a claim is not the same thing as subject-matter jurisdiction to adjudicate it. A court can have a properly pleaded counterclaim and still lack the constitutional and statutory power to hear it. **Gold Key:** Rule 13 governs pleading; § 1367 and Article III govern jurisdiction. These are separate gates.

**Choice A (original B) — DOMINANT TRAP:** This is the most tempting wrong answer because it appeals to common sense: the parties are already in federal court, the claims involve the same two people, and hearing everything together seems efficient. A student who thinks jurisdiction is about convenience rather than constitutional case-or-controversy limits will pick this. The breaker is that "same parties" does not equal "same case or controversy." The Supreme Court in *Gibbs* required a common nucleus of operative fact — overlapping evidence, witnesses, and events — not merely overlapping party names. **Gold Key:** Two claims between the same parties can still be two entirely different "cases" for Article III purposes.

**Choice C (original D):** This choice is attractive only to a student who has a vague sense that counterclaims are disfavored in federal court. It is a flat misstatement: defendants absolutely may file counterclaims in federal court, provided subject-matter jurisdiction exists (either independently or through supplemental jurisdiction). The rule against counterclaims is a myth; the real rule is that jurisdiction must exist for every claim.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** 28 U.S.C. § 1367(a) provides that in any civil action over which the district courts have original jurisdiction, the district courts shall have supplemental jurisdiction over all other claims that are so related to the action that they form part of the same case or controversy under Article III. The Supreme Court in *United Mine Workers v. Gibbs*, 383 U.S. 715, 725 (1966), held that pendent jurisdiction exists when state and federal claims derive from a "common nucleus of operative fact" such that a plaintiff "would ordinarily be expected to try them all in one judicial proceeding." Section 1367(a) codifies the *Gibbs* standard.

**Application:** A permissive counterclaim — one that does not arise out of the same transaction or occurrence as the plaintiff's claim — does not share a common nucleus of operative fact with the original claim. The promissory-note dispute and the combine-harvester contract dispute involve different facts, different evidence, different witnesses, and different time periods. They are two separate "cases" for Article III purposes. Because the counterclaim is for $15,000 (below the $75,000 diversity amount under 28 U.S.C. § 1332) and does not arise under federal question jurisdiction, it has no independent jurisdictional basis. Supplemental jurisdiction cannot reach it.

**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1367(a); *United Mine Workers v. Gibbs*, 383 U.S. 715 (1966); *Jones v. Ford Motor Credit Co.*, 358 F.3d 205 (2d Cir. 2004) (permissive counterclaim requires independent jurisdictional basis post-§ 1367); LII Wex, "Supplemental Jurisdiction," https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/supplemental_jurisdiction.
