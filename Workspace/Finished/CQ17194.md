### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**B is correct** because the statement was made during compromise negotiations about a disputed claim and is offered to prove the studio's liability — the textbook Rule 408 trigger. FRE 408(a)(2) bars "conduct or a statement made during compromise negotiations about the claim" when offered to prove or disprove the validity or amount of a disputed claim. The protection is the **compromise context**, not the content of any single sentence inside it. A percentage-of-fault statement that surfaces during a settlement meeting is part of the protected negotiation, not a free-standing admission.

**Gold Key (GK-EVIDENCE-SETTLEMENT-FAULT-01):** FRE 408(a)(2) bars "conduct or a statement" made during compromise negotiations about a disputed claim — the protection covers any statement in the negotiation, not just dollar offers. The first sentence of the rule is the trap-killer.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

#### A — "Admit the statement because it is a statement by an opposing party and therefore cannot be excluded by a policy rule."

The attraction is the opposing-party statement. The student hears "opposing party" and assumes FRE 801(d)(2) admission status unlocks whatever Rule 408 would otherwise bar. But FRE 408 is a **public-policy exclusion** that operates **independently** of the hearsay rules. Two rules, two separate gates — passing one does not pass the other. The opposing-party admission rule says "this is not hearsay"; it does not say "and FRE 408 does not apply." The breaker is the **independence of policy and hearsay**: a statement can be a non-hearsay admission and still be excluded under Rule 408(a)(2) when it is offered to prove liability and was made during compromise negotiations. The student who walks through FRE 408's text and notes that it has its own "not admissible" clause, on top of and apart from the hearsay rules, can CUT A cleanly.

#### C — "Admit the percentage-of-fault statement because Rule 408 protects only the dollar check, not factual statements made during the meeting." *(DOMINANT TRAP)*

The attraction is the "split-the-content" temptation. The student parses the negotiation into "dollar offer" + "factual statement" and assumes the rule protects only the dollar. This is the factual-statement myth — the most common Rule 408 misconception on the bar exam. The breaker is the **actual text of FRE 408(a)(2)**, which on its face bars "conduct or **a statement** made during compromise negotiations about the claim." The protection runs to the context, not to the content; any statement made inside the negotiation is protected when offered to prove validity or amount. The student who reads the rule text — "or a statement," not "or the dollar amount" — can CUT C on first pass.

**Gold Key (GK-EVIDENCE-SETTLEMENT-FAULT-01):** FRE 408(a)(2) bars "conduct or a statement" made during compromise negotiations about a disputed claim — the protection covers any statement in the negotiation, not just dollar offers.

#### D — "Exclude only the dollar check but admit the percentage-of-fault statement for liability."

The attraction is the "honor-the-rule" partial. The student wants to exclude the dollar figure (a Rule 408 staple) but feels the fault statement is too probative to lose. But there is **no doctrine** that lets a court peel factual content out of a protected negotiation context. Rule 408 protects the **context**, not the content; it does not slice a single statement into protected and unprotected components. The breaker is the **holistic read of FRE 408(a)(2)**: the rule treats the entire statement as one protected unit. The student who reads the rule as covering "a statement" (singular, full stop) can CUT D.

**Silver Key (SK-EVIDENCE-SETTLEMENT-NARROW-01):** On a 408 item, the dominant trap is the choice that narrows the protection to dollar offers only. A student who sees that narrowing should CUT it. (No authority field — student-accessible navigation move.)

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested rule (unchanged from original):** Federal Rule of Evidence 408 — Compromise Offers and Negotiations. As currently in force (last amended April 26, 2011; eff. Dec. 1, 2011), FRE 408(a)(2) provides that "conduct or a statement made during compromise negotiations about the claim" is "not admissible — on behalf of any party — either to prove or disprove the validity or amount of a disputed claim or to impeach by a prior inconsistent statement or a contradiction." The rule's stated policy is to encourage frank settlement discussions by removing the risk that statements made in negotiation will be used as admissions of liability or amount.

**Verification that the credited answer is correct under the governing rule:** The stem contains all three Rule 408 triggers: (1) **disputed claim** — the studio disputes both liability and amount in response to a written demand letter; (2) **compromise negotiations about the claim** — the statement was made during a settlement meeting; (3) **prohibited offered purpose** — the plaintiff offers the statement to prove the studio's liability. The statement's content (a percentage-of-fault admission coupled with a dollar offer) sits squarely within FRE 408(a)(2)'s "conduct or a statement" language. The trial court must exclude the statement.

**Verification that the legal issue and credited outcome are unchanged from the original:** Original Q17194 tested the same FRE 408 scope question: does Rule 408 protect factual statements made during compromise negotiations, or only dollar offers? The original credited answer (D) was the full-exclude choice. The variant preserves the same legal substance, the same three distractor mechanics, the same dominant trap, and the same credited outcome — the new correct answer is B (the post-shuffle position of the original D), not D. **Outcome unmoved.**

**Authoritative support (lawyer-confirmation layer only — never the student's first-pass basis):** FRE 408(a)(2) (text confirmed at Cornell LII, no tracking parameters); Advisory Committee Notes to FRE 408 (the rule "is ordinarily phrased in terms of offers of compromise" but the policy "must be taken" with respect to "completed compromises" and to all statements made in the negotiation); *Macsherry v. In settlement negotiations over a disputed civil claim, can a party's percentage-of-fault statement be admitted at trial to prove liability — or does Rule 408 cover the entire negotiation, not just dollar offers?

#### Choice By Choice Walkthrough

##### A — "Admit the statement because it is a statement by an opposing party and therefore cannot be excluded by a policy rule."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The student hears "opposing-party statement" and assumes admissions beat policy exclusions. This is a category-confusion trap: FRE 408 is a public-policy exclusion independent of the hearsay-admission rule.

##### B — "Exclude the statement because it was made during compromise negotiations about a disputed claim and is offered to prove liability."

##### C — "Admit the percentage-of-fault statement because Rule 408 protects only the dollar check, not factual statements made during the meeting." (DOMINANT TRAP)
