## 7. Full right-answer explanation (B)

The challenge is that the warrant issued before the contraband reached the home. **Gold Key:** an anticipatory warrant satisfies probable cause when there is PC to believe (1) the triggering condition will occur and (2) if it does, the contraband will be at the described location — probable cause is **forward-looking**, not a snapshot of the moment of issuance. Both prongs are met: the parcel was intercepted and confirmed to contain counterfeit currency, and delivery to the named address was essentially certain. The triggering condition (delivery and acceptance) actually occurred before officers executed. **Silver Key:** do not equate "PC must exist at issuance" with "the contraband must already be present" — the PC that must exist at issuance is PC about the future. The warrant was constitutional; suppression is denied.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A — "acceptance was consent to search the entire residence."** This sells a familiar Fourth Amendment shortcut: a resident let the item in, so it feels like he let the officers in too. The breaker: accepting a delivery is the warrant's triggering condition, not a voluntary, knowing agreement to a search — the search's validity rests on the anticipatory warrant, and consent is the wrong doctrine.

**C — "PC must exist at the moment of issuance, and the contraband hadn't arrived" (DOMINANT TRAP).** This sells the intuitive snapshot view of probable cause and mirrors the suspect's own argument: no warrant before the contraband is on site. The breaker: **Gold Key** — for anticipatory warrants, probable cause is forward-looking; PC to believe the triggering condition will occur and that contraband will then be present is enough (Grubbs), so the contraband's absence at issuance does not defeat the warrant.

**D — "the triggering condition had to appear on the face of the warrant."** This sells the particularity instinct: if a condition controls the search, surely it must be written into the warrant itself. The breaker: **Gold Key** — Grubbs held the Fourth Amendment does not require the triggering condition on the warrant's face; the warrant must describe the place and items with particularity, and the condition may live in the supporting affidavit.

## 9. Black-letter verification + legal reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

The credited answer is correct under anticipatory-warrant doctrine. *Authority:* *United States v. Grubbs*, 547 U.S. 90 (2006). Grubbs upheld anticipatory warrants conditioned on a future event and set the two-part test: probable cause to believe (1) the triggering condition will come to pass and (2) if it does, contraband will be found at the described place — i.e., probable cause is assessed prospectively. Grubbs also held the Fourth Amendment does **not** require the triggering condition to appear on the face of the warrant; particularity attaches to the place to be searched and the items to be seized (U.S. Const. amend. IV), and the condition may be stated in the affidavit. Here both prongs were satisfied (the parcel was confirmed contraband and delivery was near-certain), and execution followed the triggering event, so the search was valid. **Doctrinal-currency check:** Grubbs (2006) remains controlling; not on the currency hotlist. The tested rule (Grubbs anticipatory-warrant test), the legal issue, and the credited outcome (constitutional; suppression denied) are **unchanged** from the source item.
