## 1. Final Question

In a federal civil trial after a shuttle crash following a Christian harvest supper, Lydia testifies that she saw Barnabas leave the fellowship hall, sway into a coat rack, smell strongly of wine, fumble with his keys, and speak in thick, slurred sentences. Barnabas objects when Lydia is asked, "Did Barnabas appear intoxicated?" How should the court rule?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

May a lay witness who personally observed a person's behavior testify that the person appeared intoxicated?

## 3. Final Answer Choices

C. Overrule the objection because the opinion is rationally based on Lydia's perception and helpful to the jury.

D. Sustain the objection because lay witnesses may never give opinions.

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Lydia is not offering a laboratory result or a technical diagnosis. She personally saw Barnabas sway, smell strongly of wine, fumble with his keys, and slur his speech. A lay appearance opinion can package those firsthand observations in a helpful shorthand for the jury. Gold Key: FRE 701 permits lay opinion when it is perception-based, helpful, and not specialized expert testimony. Silver Key: when the witness gives concrete sensory facts first, "appeared intoxicated" is ordinary shorthand, not an expert-only conclusion.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because a blood-alcohol level sounds like the cleanest proof of intoxication. The breaker is that the question asks for an appearance opinion based on what Lydia personally observed, not a chemical measurement.

B is the dominant trap. It sounds lawyerly because intoxication can be tested scientifically, but the objection targets an ordinary appearance opinion. Gold Key: FRE 701 does not require an expert for perception-based shorthand testimony like apparent intoxication.

D sells a broad "no opinions from lay witnesses" rule. That absolute is too strong because the evidence rules allow lay opinions that are rationally based on perception and helpful to the jury.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

Federal Rule of Evidence 701 allows nonexpert opinion testimony if it is rationally based on the witness's perception, helpful to understanding the testimony or determining a fact in issue, and not based on scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge within Rule 702. The current federal rule is published by the United States Courts in the Federal Rules of Evidence and is also reproduced by the Legal Information Institute. Lydia's testimony fits that rule because it rests on firsthand sensory observations and translates them into a common appearance judgment. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item: the court should overrule the objection.
