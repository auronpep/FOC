## 6. Full right-answer explanation

**A is correct.** Daniel caused the dangerous artery wound, and Lydia died from blood loss from that wound. **Gold Key:** in homicide causation, a victim's refusal of medical treatment for religious or personal reasons ordinarily does not supersede the defendant-created mortal wound. **Silver Key:** when the answer array fights over a later treatment refusal, ask whether the later act created a new unrelated danger or merely affected the response to the wound the defendant created.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B.** This is the dominant trap. It treats Lydia's voluntary refusal as the whole causation question. The breaker is the Gold Key: a treatment refusal for religious reasons ordinarily does not supersede the dangerous wound Daniel caused.

**C.** This choice adds a precise-foreseeability condition. Daniel does not need to know Lydia's religious beliefs before causing the dangerous wound. The "only if" phrase is the overclaim.

**D.** This choice invents an untreatable-wound threshold. Causation does not require the original wound to be certain fatal if treated. The wound caused the blood loss that killed Lydia.

## 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule is homicide causation. A result crime requires factual causation and legal or proximate causation. Under Model Penal Code section 2.03, conduct is a cause of a result when it is an antecedent but for which the result would not have occurred and the causal relationship satisfies any additional causal requirement imposed by the offense. The same basic structure is used in common-law homicide: the defendant remains responsible when his act caused or directly contributed to death unless an intervening act is the sole, superseding cause.

The closest U.S. authority found is **State v. Welch**, 135 N.C. App. 499 (1999). The court rejected the argument that a stabbing victim's refusal of a blood transfusion cut off homicide causation; the victim would not have needed the transfusion but for the defendant's stabbing, and the refusal was not the sole cause of death. The classic common-law illustration is **R v. Blaue**, where a stabbing victim's religious refusal of a transfusion did not break the causal chain. The variation preserves that same rule. Daniel caused the artery wound. Lydia refused treatment for religious reasons. The refusal did not create a new unrelated risk; it affected the treatment of the wound Daniel caused.
