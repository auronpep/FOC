## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key — Firm Offer Rule (UCC § 2-205):** A merchant's offer to buy or sell goods is irrevocable — without any consideration — if (1) the offeror is a merchant, (2) the offer is in a signed writing, and (3) the writing gives assurance that the offer will be held open. The offer is irrevocable for the stated period or, if no time is stated, for a reasonable time, but never more than three months.

Here, Daniel is a merchant (he runs a Christian bookstore — a person dealing in goods of the kind). His offer was in a signed writing (the March 1 letter was signed and dated). And it expressly gave assurance it would "remain open for 45 days." All three requirements of § 2-205 are met. The offer is irrevocable for the full 45-day window, and Daniel's attempted revocation on March 22 — three weeks in — was ineffective. Timothy still has time to accept, and his lawsuit should succeed.

This is also why Choice B (no consideration): Under § 2-205, a firm offer does NOT require consideration. That is the entire point — the statute overrides the common-law requirement. Choice B states the opposite of what the firm offer rule provides.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### Choice A (Yes — no acceptance communicated)

**What makes it attractive:** Students instinctively revert to the common-law rule that an offer can be revoked anytime before acceptance, and acceptance must be communicated. That rule is the default mental model for most offer-and-acceptance questions, and it is factually true here — Timothy had not accepted. The pull is strong because it *feels* legally correct based on first-week Contracts material students memorized before learning the firm offer exception.

**The breaker:** Acceptance is irrelevant to the question of whether the *revocation was effective*. The UCC § 2-205 firm offer rule removes the merchant's power to revoke entirely during the stated period. Timothy doesn't need to have accepted yet — he just needs to still be within the 45-day window. **Gold Key: Firm Offer Rule — no consideration needed, and revocation is off the table entirely during the irrevocability period.**

### Choice B (Yes — no consideration) ⚠️ DOMINANT TRAP

**What makes it attractive:** Consideration is the backbone of the common-law option contract. Students learn early that a promise to hold an offer open requires consideration to be binding, and this deep instinct fires hard here. Nothing in the stem says Timothy paid Daniel anything to keep the offer open. The surface facts support this reasoning perfectly — just not the governing rule.

**The breaker:** UCC § 2-205 was specifically designed to eliminate the consideration requirement for firm offers. The statute's text explicitly says the offer "is not revocable, for lack of consideration." The whole statutory purpose is to make merchant firm offers binding *without* consideration. **Gold Key: Firm Offer Rule — the statute overrides the common-law consideration requirement entirely.**

### Choice D (No — revocation not in writing)

**What makes it attractive:** Students who recognize that something is going on with firm offers but don't precisely know the rule may latch onto the fact that the statute requires a *signed writing* for the offer. By that logic, a revocation should also need to be formal or written — it has the surface feel of symmetry.

**The breaker:** Even an in-person or oral revocation would have been just as ineffective. The firm offer rule makes the offer irrevocable regardless of the form of the attempted revocation. The writing requirement applies to the *offer*, not the *revocation*. Choice D defeats the wrong choice for the wrong reason — it produces the right outcome ("No, Daniel could not revoke") but frames it as a form requirement that doesn't exist. **Gold Key: The UCC writing/signature requirement applies to the offer itself, not to the revocation.**

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing Authority:** UCC § 2-205 (Firm Offers)

**Statutory Text (LII / Cornell):** *"An offer by a merchant to buy or sell goods in a signed writing which by its terms gives assurance that it will be held open is not revocable, for lack of consideration, during the time stated or if no time is stated for a reasonable time, but in no event may such period of irrevocability exceed three months."*

**Three Elements Required:**
1. **Merchant offeror** — UCC § 2-104(1): a merchant is a person who deals in goods of the kind or holds himself out as having knowledge/skill peculiar to the goods involved. Daniel runs a Christian bookstore — he deals in books, Bibles, and worship materials.
2. **Signed writing** — The offer must be in writing and signed by the offeror. Daniel's March 1 letter was signed and dated.
3. **Express assurance** — The writing must give assurance the offer will be held open. Daniel's letter stated: "This offer will remain open for 45 days from the date of this letter."

**All three elements satisfied → firm offer created → offer is irrevocable for 45 days → Daniel's attempted revocation on March 22 was ineffective.**

**Confirmed:** The tested rule (UCC § 2-205 firm offer), the legal issue (whether a merchant's written, signed, time-stated offer is revocable without consideration), and the credited outcome (No, the revocation was ineffective) are all unchanged from the original. No doctrinal drift. The firm offer doctrine has not been altered by any recent legislation or case law affecting this core rule.

### CLASH Phase

The tension is Yes-No: Was the revocation effective?

**Yes camp (A and B):** Reason the revocation worked because either no acceptance was communicated (A) or no consideration supported the promise to hold open (B).

**No camp (C and D):** Reason the revocation didn't work because of the 45-day window (C) or the lack of written revocation (D).

**Splitting axis:** Does UCC § 2-205 apply? If it does, the offer is irrevocable and the answer is No. If it doesn't apply, general common-law rules govern and the answer is Yes.

**Fact that resolves it:** Daniel is a merchant (bookstore owner dealing in goods of the kind), the offer was in a signed writing, and it expressly stated it would remain open for 45 days. All three § 2-205 elements are met. The firm offer rule applies. The answer is No.

**Now within the No camp:** C says "because Timothy had 45 days" — this correctly identifies the statutory irrevocability period. D says "because Daniel did not communicate revocation in writing" — this is wrong on the law; the form of revocation is irrelevant under § 2-205. D is a half-right-answer: correct conclusion, wrong reason.

### CALL Phase

Between C and D, the tiebreaker is doctrinal accuracy. The firm offer rule makes the offer irrevocable — it does not impose a writing requirement on revocations. C is the only choice that states the correct outcome AND the correct legal reason.

**Result: C is the credited answer.**

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

#### Program Frame

This item tests the UCC § 2-205 firm offer rule — one of the most tested exceptions to the common-law rule that offers are revocable anytime before acceptance. The item sits at the intersection of two competing mental models: the common-law default (revocable before acceptance, consideration needed for options) and the UCC merchant exception (irrevocable firm offer, no consideration needed). Students who default to common-law rules will miss this item. The Gold Key is the firm offer rule itself.

#### Student Access Test

A student who has learned the C3 method and has been taught the UCC § 2-205 Gold Key can solve this item. The C3 structural moves (identifying the call, spotting the Yes/No split, naming the axis) get the student to the CLASH phase. The Gold Key resolves the CLASH. No outside legal doctrine beyond the taught Gold Key is required.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Daniel (Christian bookstore owner) sends Timothy (church-vendor organizer) a signed letter offering 200 leather-bound hymnals at $12 each, stating the offer will remain open for 45 days from the March 1 letter date. Timothy receives it March 3 but doesn't respond. On March 22, Daniel gets a megachurch offer at $16 each, accepts it, and calls Timothy to revoke. Timothy had been planning a promotional push but hadn't told Daniel. Timothy sues.

**Call:** Did Daniel effectively revoke his offer?

**Choices:**
- A: Yes — Timothy hadn't communicated acceptance
- B: Yes — no consideration to support keeping the offer open
- C: No — Timothy had 45 days to accept
- D: No — Daniel didn't communicate revocation in writing

#### Distilled Core Question

A merchant sent a signed written offer promising to hold it open for a stated period. Before acceptance, the merchant tried to revoke. Can the merchant revoke?

#### Call and Prediction

The call is a straightforward yes/no: Did the revocation work? Before reading choices, a student should predict: "This looks like a UCC firm offer question. If the elements are met, the offer is irrevocable and the answer is No." This prediction anchors the student before the distractors can pull.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: Yes — Timothy hadn't communicated acceptance**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice answers a different question. The call asks whether the revocation was effective, not whether a contract was formed. Timothy's failure to accept is legally irrelevant to whether Daniel could revoke under the firm offer rule.
2. **Student label:** The no-acceptance instinct trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice saying "Yes, because Daniel's offer was not in a signed writing" — that would actually defeat the firm offer.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Under § 2-205, the offer is irrevocable regardless of whether acceptance has been communicated. The common-law rule (revocable anytime before acceptance) is displaced by the firm offer statute.

**Choice B: Yes — no consideration to support keeping the offer open** ⚠️ DOMINANT TRAP

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice invents a requirement that the governing rule specifically eliminates. If the student knows the firm offer Gold Key, this choice is dead on arrival — § 2-205 says "not revocable, for lack of consideration."
2. **Student label:** The consideration over-application trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice saying "Yes, because Timothy is not a merchant" — that would actually be wrong (the merchant requirement applies to the offeror, not the offeree), but it would at least target a real element.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** UCC § 2-205 explicitly overrides the common-law consideration requirement. The statute's text: "not revocable, for lack of consideration." **Gold Key: Firm Offer Rule — no consideration needed.**

**Choice C: No — Timothy had 45 days to accept**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** All three § 2-205 elements are met (merchant, signed writing, express assurance), so the offer is irrevocable for the stated 45-day period. Daniel's revocation came three weeks in — within the irrevocability window — and was therefore ineffective.
2. **Student label:** Correct answer.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true and responsive choice.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** UCC § 2-205: merchant's signed writing that gives assurance it will be held open is not revocable during the stated period. **Gold Key: Firm Offer Rule.**

**Choice D: No — Daniel didn't communicate revocation in writing**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Right conclusion, wrong reason. The writing requirement applies to the offer, not the revocation. Even an in-person or oral revocation would be just as ineffective under § 2-205.
2. **Student label:** The writing-form symmetry trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice saying "No, because the firm offer rule makes the offer irrevocable regardless of the form of revocation."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** § 2-205 imposes a signed-writing requirement on the offer, not on the revocation. The form of attempted revocation is irrelevant.

#### Residual Answer

**C** — the only choice that states the correct outcome AND the correct legal reason.
