### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**C** is correct. Defamation requires a false statement of fact. A statement is **not** defamatory if the **gist or sting** is **substantially true**, even if minor details (such as the exact month) are inaccurate. Here, the defamatory sting is "childcare license revoked for failing a state safety inspection." The license **was** revoked for failing the safety inspection — that is substantially true. The wrong month (May vs. June) is a minor inaccuracy that does not change the sting; the statement is not actionable.

**Gold Key:** defamation is a falsity tort. A statement is not defamatory if the gist or sting is substantially true. Minor inaccuracies that do not change the gist or sting are tolerated.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Absolute privilege, because all private newsletters are protected.**

This is the invented-privilege trap. There is no automatic absolute privilege for all private newsletters. Absolute privilege is reserved for a small set of institutional speakers (legislators, judges, witnesses in judicial proceedings, executive officials speaking on matters of state, etc.). The newsletter is a private publication, not an institutional speaker, so the absolute-privilege route is unavailable. The breaker is the **Gold Key** plus the recognition that absolute privilege has narrow, traditional applications that do not extend to private newsletters.

**B. Opinion, because license status is subjective.**

This is the false-recharacterization trap. License status is a **factual** matter: a state agency either revoked a license or did not, on a specific date, for a specific reason. The statement "your license was revoked in May for failing a safety inspection" is a factual assertion, not an opinion. The opinion defense requires that the statement be one that cannot be proven true or false — a subjective assessment, a loose figurative statement, or rhetorical hyperbola. License revocation is none of these. The breaker is the **Gold Key** plus the recognition that "license was revoked" is a verifiable fact.

**D. No defense, because any factual mistake defeats truth.**

This is the dominant trap. The any-inaccuracy myth is the classic MBE misconception here: a student who half-remembers "defamation requires truth" reasons that any mistake (even a one-month error) makes the statement false. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: defamation requires falsity, but a statement is not false if the gist or sting is **substantially true**. The wrong-month inaccuracy does not change the sting (license revoked for failing a state safety inspection), so substantial truth is the defense.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18982, a Torts item involving a private publication's false-month statement and the defendant's defense to a defamation suit. The official key is A: substantial truth, because the sting of the statement was true despite the wrong month.

Defamation requires (1) a false and defamatory statement of fact, (2) of and concerning the plaintiff, (3) published to a third party, (4) with the requisite fault (negligence for private figures on matters of public concern; actual malice for public figures or matters of public concern under *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254 (1964)), and (5) causing the requisite harm (actual damages or, in some jurisdictions, presumed damages for defamation *per se*). The first element — falsity — is the subject of this question.

The "gist" or "sting" of a statement is the central defamatory imputation that would cause a reasonable listener to think less of the plaintiff. The defamatory sting here is "childcare license revoked for failing a state safety inspection." The license **was** revoked for failing a safety inspection. The month discrepancy (May vs. June) is a minor inaccuracy that does not change the sting; reasonable listeners would not think less of the plaintiff because of the month.

The substantial-truth doctrine is settled. A defendant is not liable in defamation if the statement is substantially true, meaning the gist or sting is true, even if minor details are inaccurate. See, e.g., *Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co.*, 497 U.S. 1, 19-20 (1990) (a statement is not considered false unless it would have a different effect on the mind of the reader from that which the pleaded truth would have produced); *Masson v. New Yorker Magazine, Inc.*, 501 U.S. 496, 513-17 (1991) (a deliberate alteration of a quotation does not necessarily result in substantial falsity if the alteration does not change the meaning conveyed). The doctrine tolerates minor inaccuracies that do not change the gist or sting.

The other defenses fail. Absolute privilege is reserved for a narrow set of institutional speakers (legislators, judges, witnesses in judicial proceedings, executive officials speaking on matters of state); a private newsletter is not within that set. Opinion requires a statement that cannot be proven true or false; a statement that a license was revoked is a verifiable fact. The "any mistake defeats truth" framing is a misstatement of the law.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: substantial truth is the best defense; the wrong-month inaccuracy does not change the sting.

### B. Opinion, because license status is subjective.
