### 7. Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**A is correct.** Battery is a general-intent crime. It requires only the intent to perform the act that causes harmful or offensive contact — not the intent to harm or injure a specific person. Nathan chose to drink throughout the afternoon. He chose to swing the fishing rod. The general intent to perform that physical act is established. His testimony that he thought Philip was not behind him addresses whether he aimed at Philip — but battery does not require that. Whether he subjectively identified his target is beside the point.

**Gold Key:** Voluntary intoxication may negate specific intent (e.g., the premeditated deliberation element in first-degree murder) but it never negates general intent. Once a defendant voluntarily performs the act that causes the contact, the general-intent element is met regardless of his intoxicated belief about the scene.

**Silver Key:** Before deciding any intoxication defense question, classify the charged crime: specific-intent or general-intent? Battery = general-intent. That classification ends the analysis before any sympathetic-facts story can land.

### 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B** (dominant trap): This choice is compelling because Nathan genuinely did not know Philip was standing there. If he didn't know he was striking a person, how could he have "intended" to injure Philip? The trap is in the premise: battery does not require intent to injure a specific victim. It requires only the general intent to do the act — swinging the rod. Nathan's intoxicated belief about what was behind him does not convert his voluntary arm-swing into an involuntary reflex. **Gold Key:** Voluntary intoxication removes neither consciousness nor the volitional character of Nathan's swing. The law fixes on the act, not on who the actor thought he was hitting.

**C**: This choice sounds rigorous — it conditions guilt on a specifically-intended harmful act. But it converts battery into a specific-intent crime, which it is not. The prosecution needs to prove only that Nathan intentionally swung the rod, not that he intended to connect with Philip in particular. The phrase "specifically intended to harm Philip" fabricates an element the law does not impose.

**D**: The reasonable-person standard belongs in negligence, not battery's intent analysis. Whether a sober person might have made the same spatial error during a backcast is irrelevant to the question of whether Nathan intended to perform the physical act. Moreover, Nathan's impairment was self-induced; courts do not treat voluntary intoxication as an excuse that passes a reasonableness filter.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification

At common law, battery is an intentional unlawful application of force to another resulting in harmful or offensive contact. "Intentional" for a general-intent crime means only that the defendant intended to perform the physical act — not that he intended the precise consequences or knew exactly what object or person he was striking. The common-law rule is settled: voluntary intoxication is not a defense to general-intent crimes. It may, in limited circumstances, negate a required specific-intent element, but it does not negate the general intent that battery requires.

The official key (guilty) is confirmed. Nathan voluntarily became intoxicated and voluntarily swung the fishing rod. General intent is established by those two voluntary acts. His belief about what was behind him is legally irrelevant to the elements of battery.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):** LaFave, *Substantive Criminal Law* § 9.5 (voluntary intoxication and general intent); MPC § 2.08 (voluntary intoxication generally unavailable as a defense except to negate a specific mental-state element — consistent result for intentional-act crimes). The common-law bar-exam rule: voluntary intoxication ≠ defense to a general-intent crime. No doctrinal-currency hotlist item is triggered; this rule is stable.
