## 1. Final question

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A** sells the idea that the prosecution failed to prove a substantial risk. It sounds like a factual sufficiency argument. But the stem gives Paul's age (68) and the walker—facts from which a jury can infer a substantial risk. The choice flatly contradicts those facts.

**C** sells the idea that Peter gets a pass because he lacked actual or reasonable knowledge of the victim's age. It sounds like a fairness argument. But the stem defines the required mental state as recklessness, not knowledge or reasonable knowledge. The choice imports a standard that the statute never requires.

**D** sells the idea that the victim's age alone is enough to convict. It reaches the right outcome but skips the mental state analysis entirely. The call is whether Peter could be convicted, which requires the jury to find the statutory mens rea. Because the statute is silent on mental state, recklessness is required; the victim's age is necessary but not sufficient.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The governing rule is MPC § 2.02(3): when a criminal statute does not prescribe a culpability requirement for a material element, the element is satisfied if the person acts purposely, knowingly, or recklessly with respect thereto. Because the MPC's culpability requirements are hierarchical, proof of a higher state satisfies a lower one; as a practical matter, the default standard is recklessness. MPC § 2.02(c) defines recklessness as consciously disregarding a substantial and unjustified risk that the material element exists or will result from the person's conduct.

The state statutes in the question codify this MPC framework. The aggravated-theft statute is silent as to mens rea regarding the victim's age, so the default recklessness standard applies. Even though Peter testified he did not actually know Paul's age, the jury may infer from the totality of the circumstances—Paul's age, the walker, and Peter's admitted indifference—that Peter consciously disregarded a substantial and unjustified risk that Paul was 65 or older. The conviction is proper if the jury so finds.

**Authority:** MPC § 2.02(3) and § 2.02(c); see also Justia, "The Mental State Requirement in Criminal Law Cases" (recklessness as default when statute is silent).
