### 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule is the Fourth Amendment warrant-particularity rule for the place to be searched. The Fourth Amendment requires warrants to particularly describe the place to be searched and the persons or things to be seized. See U.S. Const. amend. IV, https://constitution.congress.gov/constitution/amendment-4/. The leading Supreme Court formulation in Steele v. United States, 267 U.S. 498, 503 (1925), is that a warrant sufficiently describes the place if the officer can, with reasonable effort, ascertain and identify the intended place. See https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/267/498/. Maryland v. Garrison, 480 U.S. 79 (1987), also confirms that warrant-description questions are judged by practical reasonableness under the information available to officers. See https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/480/79/.

Under that rule, the credited answer remains correct. The street number was wrong, but the warrant's detailed physical description pointed to one private cottage and created no realistic risk that officers would choose the wrong location. The technical address error therefore does not invalidate the search solely on the place-description ground. No researched authority contradicted the supplied official key.
