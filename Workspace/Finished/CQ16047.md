## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Naomi committed larceny because she took and carried away Mary's personal property without permission and with intent to permanently deprive Mary of it by destroying it. Gold Key: the victim's unlawful possession of the property does not make the property free for a private person to take; larceny asks whether the taking was trespassory and whether the taker intended permanent deprivation. Naomi is not guilty of burglary under the supplied statute because she entered with the key Mary gave her. Silver Key: split the two charges; larceny turns on the taking, while burglary turns on whether there was breaking plus entry for larceny.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is the dominant trap. It is attractive because Naomi returned with the purpose of taking the crate, so the entry feels like burglary. The breaker is the statute's word "breaking": Naomi used the entrusted key, so the entry did not satisfy the breaking element, even though the later taking was larceny. Gold Key: a theft-purpose reentry does not cure a missing breaking element.

B is attractive because Naomi destroyed illegal fireworks and may look more like a public-safety helper than a thief. The breaker is that larceny is not excused merely because the owner should not have possessed the item; Naomi still made a trespassory taking and meant to permanently deprive Mary of the crate's contents.

D is attractive because it focuses on Naomi's second entry after she already knew the crate was illegal and intended to remove it. The breaker is twofold: the entrusted key prevents burglary under the supplied breaking-and-entering statute, and the taking itself still satisfies larceny.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. Common-law larceny requires a trespassory taking and carrying away of the personal property of another with intent to permanently deprive the owner. Naomi's authority was limited to feeding the cat, not removing Mary's crate. Carrying the crate away and destroying the fireworks shows intent to permanently deprive. The fact that the fireworks were illegal to possess does not defeat the property-of-another or trespassory-taking elements.

Burglary fails under the supplied statute because it requires "breaking and entering." Naomi entered by using the key Mary entrusted to her. Even though Naomi returned with a purpose to commit larceny, the entry does not supply the breaking element under the source rule. The modern generic-burglary cases confirm that burglary analysis is element-specific and turns on unlawful or unprivileged entry plus criminal intent, but this source statute uses the narrower breaking-and-entering formulation.

Authority checked:
- Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), for traditional theft intent and the importance of criminal intent in common-law theft-derived offenses: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/342/246/
- People v. Williams, 57 Cal.4th 776 (2013), summarizing common-law larceny as trespassory taking and carrying away of another's personal property with intent to permanently deprive: https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/ca-supreme-court/1642915.html
- Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575 (1990), and later summaries of generic burglary, for the element frame requiring unlawful or unprivileged entry or remaining plus intent to commit a crime: https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-supreme-court/495/575.html
- LII Wex, Breaking and Entering, for common-law burglary's breaking-and-entering formulation: https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/breaking_and_entering
