### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Ruth got the benefit of instruction, but common-law larceny is not triggered by every unpaid benefit. Gold Key: common-law larceny requires a taking and carrying away of tangible personal property; unpaid services are handled, if at all, by a theft-of-services statute. Silver Key: separate "value received" from "property taken." The stem lists no object Ruth took, so the service answer is the only true and responsive answer.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because the studio really lost the value of one paid seat. The breaker is that lost revenue is not the same thing as Ruth taking and carrying away the studio's personal property.

C is attractive because it reaches the right "no" result and then tries to attach larceny to a familiar robbery-like image. The breaker is the fabricated body-taking rule. Larceny does not require taking from the victim's body; it requires a taking of personal property.

D is the dominant trap because it names the moral wrong in the facts: Ruth got a paid class experience without paying. Gold Key: common-law larceny does not punish unpaid services as larceny unless a statute expands the offense; without a tangible item taken, this answer overreads the theft label.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, trap structure, and credited outcome are unchanged. Common-law larceny requires a wrongful taking and carrying away of another's personal property with intent to deprive. The U.S. Department of Justice Criminal Resource Manual section 1006 states the larceny elements as wrongful taking and carrying away, lack of consent, and intent to deprive, citing *United States v. Barlow*, 480 F.2d 1245, 1251 (D.C. Cir. 1972): https://www.justice.gov/archives/jm/criminal-resource-manual-1006-larceny. Modern theft-of-services statutes confirm the separate statutory category for unpaid services; for example, New Jersey Revised Statutes section 2C:20-8 separately covers purposely obtaining services known to be available only for compensation by means used to avoid payment: https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-2c/section-2c-20-8/.

Ruth received class instruction and access. She did not take a workbook, pen, ink, money, badge, or other tangible item. A theft-of-services statute might cover the conduct, but common-law larceny does not. The official key remains correct.
