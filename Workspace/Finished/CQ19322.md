### 1. Final Question

Daniel, a missionary on a short-term mission trip, was walking back to his team's lodging through a busy open-air market in a small town one bright afternoon when a man came up behind him, pressed a knife against his back, and demanded his wallet and phone. While reaching into Daniel's pockets, the attacker turned Daniel around to face him and held him there briefly; Daniel stared directly into the attacker's face for about ninety seconds from a distance of three feet in clear mid-afternoon sunlight. After the attacker fled, Daniel went immediately to his mission team's lodging and called the local emergency number, giving a detailed description of the attacker's face, build, clothing, and accent to a teammate, Paul, who relayed it. Three days later, a local detective showed Daniel a six-person photo array. Brathwaite* reliability factors — the close-range, well-lit observation, the immediate detailed description, and the prompt and certain identification — strongly favored the identification, but arguing that any suggestive procedure requires automatic exclusion under the Due Process Clause. ### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam register; weaves Gold Key + Silver Key)

The Due Process Clause does not require suppression of every suggestive identification. The controlling two-prong test from *Neil v. Biggers*, 409 U.S. 188 (1972), and *Manson v. Brathwaite*, 432 U.S. 98 (1977), asks: (1) was the procedure impermissibly suggestive, AND (2) under the totality of the circumstances, did it create a substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification? Both must be satisfied; the second prong is the reliability backstop.

**Gold Key:** Biggers/Brathwaite two-prong test — suppression requires BOTH suggestiveness AND substantial likelihood of misidentification. A strong reliability showing on prong two defeats the motion and sends the ID to the jury as a weight issue.

Here, the moving party has conceded the *Brathwaite* reliability factors: a ninety-second, three-foot, well-lit observation; an immediate detailed description; and a prompt, certain identification. That concession resolves prong two — there is no substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification. Even if the brighter photocopy rendered the array impermissibly suggestive under prong one, prong two is not satisfied, so no constitutional violation occurred. The remedy of suppression is unavailable. The brightness discrepancy is a credibility question the jury may consider, but the identification is admissible.

**Silver Key:** When the defendant concedes prong two of Biggers/Brathwaite, the motion is over. Lock the denial and stop. The "but the procedure was suggestive" rebuttal writes the denial for you.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (Deny — photo arrays never subject to due process).** This sounds like a clean categorical rule and is therefore attractive: a student who has half-remembered *Wade–Gilbert–Stovall* sometimes thinks photo arrays were placed beyond due process review entirely. The cognitive move it sells is "categorical immunity beats case-specific analysis." The breaker: the Due Process Clause applies to all pretrial identitions arranged by the police when suggestiveness is at issue, including photo arrays. *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218 (1967), and *Biggers* itself were photo-array / photographic-identification cases. Photo arrays are not categorically immune; they are simply subject to the same Biggers/Brathwaite framework as lineups and showups. The correct ground for denial is prong-two failure, not categorical immunity. This is an **ISSUE_SENSE / bait_doctrine** trap — the right doctrinal area (due process for identifications), but the wrong variant (a non-existent per se immunity rule).

**B (Grant — all constitutional violations require suppression).** This one draws on a generic intuition about constitutional remedies: "if the Constitution was violated, the evidence must come out." The cognitive move it sells is a categorical constitutional-remedies rule. The breaker: there is no such blanket rule. The Due Process Clause forbids convictions grounded in unreliable identifications, but where the identification is reliable, no constitutional violation has occurred, and there is nothing to suppress. The remedy of suppression is tied to the violation, not to any suggestiveness standing alone. *Brathwaite* squarely rejected the position that suggestiveness alone is enough. This is an **EAR_FALSITY / flat_misstatement** — the asserted categorical remedy rule is not the law.

**D (Grant — any suggestiveness requires automatic suppression) — DOMINANT TRAP.** This is the most seductive wrong answer, and the one a student will pick when they have a pre-*Brathwaite* (Stovall-era) reflex or have confused suggestiveness with constitutional error. The cognitive move it sells is "impermissible suggestiveness = automatic exclusion, no second look." The breaker: Biggers/Brathwaite explicitly rejected the per se / automatic-exclusion approach and replaced it with a reliability-based totality-of-circumstances test. The "any … automatic" framing is a textbook **EAR_OVERCLAIM / tiered_absolute** — a real rule (suppression is required in some suggestive-ID cases) pushed to a categorical limit (always, even with strong reliability) that the case law forecloses. With the defendant conceding prong two, the trap is fully disarmed.

### 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification (with real authority)

The tested rule is the two-prong due process test for admission of an out-of-court identification allegedly procured by suggestive police conduct. The rule is articulated in *Neil v. Biggers*, 409 U.S. 188, 198–200 (1972), and reaffirmed in *Manson v. Brathwaite*, 432 U.S. 98, 114 (1977), where the Court stated: "reliability is the linchpin in determining the admissibility of identification testimony." The two prongs are: (1) whether the identification procedure was impermissibly suggestive, and (2) if so, whether, under the totality of the circumstances, the procedure created a substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification. Only if both are satisfied is the identification suppressed; otherwise it is admitted and the suggestiveness (if any) goes to weight for the jury. *Brathwaite* expressly rejected a per se / automatic-exclusion approach in favor of this reliability test. *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218 (1967), and *Gilbert v. California*, 388 U.S. 263 (1967), establish that post-indictment lineups trigger a Sixth Amendment right to counsel; *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967), first recognized due process limits on suggestive identifications; *Biggers* and *Brathwaite* built the modern two-prong framework.

The credited outcome (deny the motion) is **unchanged from the source row**. The case is squarely controlled by *Brathwaite*: with strong reliability on the conceded prong-two factors, no substantial likelihood of misidentification exists, and the identification is admissible. The brighter-photo suggestiveness, if any, is a weight issue, not a suppression issue. The legal research (web search of *Manson v. Brathwaite*, 432 U.S. 98 (1977)) confirms the test, the factors, and the rejection of per se exclusion. The cited authority is real and on point; no fabricated citations.
