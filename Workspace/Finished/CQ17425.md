### 7. Full right-answer explanation
A is correct. Miranda does not suppress every unwarned statement made by a person in custody. It suppresses statements that come from custodial interrogation. Gold Key: custody plus no warnings is not enough; Miranda needs custody plus interrogation, and volunteered statements remain admissible. Silver Key: when a suppression answer says "custody" but skips "interrogation," do not let the first gate stand in for the whole test. Paul was in custody, but the officer did not question him, speak to him, gesture to him, or do anything aimed at getting a response. Paul started the statement on his own, so the statement should not be suppressed on Miranda grounds.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
C is the dominant trap because it grabs the loudest fact in the stem: Paul was in custody and had not been warned. The breaker is the missing second gate. Gold Key: Miranda is triggered by custodial interrogation, not by custody alone.

D is attractive because "functional equivalent of interrogation" sounds like the exact phrase students expect in a Miranda problem. The breaker is that the stem gives only silent presence near the doorway. No words, conduct, or police tactic was reasonably likely to draw out Paul's confession.

B gets to the right "No" result, but it sells the wrong reason. Paul's own words identify what he did: "I lit the Jonah-hole kiosk." The statement remains incriminating even though he tried to soften intent or harm.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue is unchanged from the source item: whether an unwarned statement made by a suspect in custody must be suppressed when the statement was volunteered and not produced by interrogation. The credited outcome is also unchanged: the statement is not suppressed.

The verified outline code is `75080101`, which appears verbatim in `OUTLINE_CODES_COMPLETE.md` as "Fifth and Sixth Amendments-Statements, Confessions, and Identifications > Statements and Confessions > Voluntary statements." The source row listed `73030400` for robbery, but the legal rule tested here is Miranda/volunteered statements, not the elements of robbery.

Under *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), warnings are required before custodial interrogation, while volunteered statements remain admissible. Under *Rhode Island v. Innis*, 446 U.S. 291 (1980), interrogation includes express questioning and its functional equivalent: police words or actions they should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. Sources checked: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/384/436/ and https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/446/291/. Paul was in custody, but he was not questioned and no police conduct was reasonably likely to elicit his response. His statement was volunteered, so the official key's legal result remains correct.
