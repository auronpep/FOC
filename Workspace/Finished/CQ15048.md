# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question

Stephen owned a coastal parcel called Sandpiper Point in fee simple. Stephen executed a properly formatted deed purporting to convey Sandpiper Point to Timothy in fee simple. The deed stated the conveyance was "in consideration of $10 paid and other valuable consideration." Stephen handed the deed to Timothy, and Timothy promptly recorded it. Three months later, Stephen sued Timothy to cancel the deed and quiet title. Stephen proved that Timothy never actually paid the $10 or any other consideration, despite the deed's recital. In this action, Stephen should:

## 2. Distilled Core Question

When a grantor executes and delivers a deed with a recital of consideration that was never actually paid, can the grantor later cancel the deed for lack of consideration?

## 6. Right Answer Explanation

**C is correct.** A deed's validity depends only on proper execution and delivery with present intent to transfer title. Consideration is not required for a deed to effectively convey land. The recital of consideration in a deed is merely customary language and does not create a contractual requirement. Here, Stephen executed the deed in proper form and physically delivered it to Timothy with intent to convey. The fact that no money changed hands is legally irrelevant to the validity of the conveyance. Timothy received valid title upon delivery, and Stephen cannot undo the transfer based on non-payment of recited consideration.

**Gold Key:** A deed is a gift mechanism, not a contract. Execution + delivery with intent to convey = valid transfer. Consideration is never required.

## 7. Wrong Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect.** While parol evidence may contradict a recital of consideration to show non-payment, this fact is legally irrelevant. Even if Stephen proves no consideration was paid, the deed remains valid because consideration is not a requirement for deed effectiveness. The parol evidence rule is a distraction here — the real issue is that the evidence, even if admitted, doesn't help Stephen's case.

**B is incorrect.** This states a true legal principle (recordation alone cannot validate a void deed), but the deed here was never void. It was valid upon execution and delivery. The recordation point is a red herring because the instrument was already effective before recording. Stephen loses not because of recording, but because the deed was valid from the moment of delivery.

**D is incorrect.** Recording does not terminate a grantor's remedies in all situations — for example, recording doesn't bar a claim based on fraud, forgery, or lack of delivery. Stephen's failure here is not procedural (recording) but substantive: he has no valid claim because the conveyance was legally complete without consideration.

## 8. Black-Letter Verification

**Tested Rule:** A deed conveying real property is valid and transfers title upon (1) proper execution (signed by grantor in required form) and (2) delivery with present intent to transfer title. Consideration is not required. Recitals of consideration are customary but not legally operative.

**Authority:** 
- Restatement (First) of Property § 311 (delivery requirement)
- 20 Am. Jur. 2d Deeds §§ 51, 73 (consideration not required for valid deed)
- *Roller v. Brown*, 69 S.E.2d 128 (S.C. 1952) — deed valid without consideration
- *Mullen v. Pantzer*, 56 N.E.2d 707 (Ind. 1944) — recital of consideration is not a contractual term

**Verification:** The official key (B, now C after shuffle) correctly states the black-letter rule. No doctrinal changes affect this area. The rule remains current and well-settled.
