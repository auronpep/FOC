### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Lydia's strongest problem is the **favorable-termination element**. A malicious-prosecution claim requires the plaintiff to show, among other things, that the prior proceeding terminated in a way that affirms the innocence of (or at least does not affirm the guilt / liability of) the malicious-prosecution plaintiff. Paul's breach suit is **still pending** — no judgment, no voluntary dismissal, no settlement that could be characterized as a favorable termination for Lydia. Without a favorable termination, the claim is premature at best, and the other elements (no probable cause, malice) cannot cure that defect. **Gold Key:** the favorable-termination requirement is a threshold element of malicious prosecution. A pending underlying suit is fatal to the malicious-prosecution claim regardless of how strong the malice or lack-of-probable-cause showing would otherwise be.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. Malicious prosecution never applies to a civil suit, regardless of jurisdiction.**

This is a jurisdictional overstatement. Many U.S. jurisdictions do permit a malicious-prosecution claim based on a prior **civil** proceeding (the doctrine originated in criminal cases but the modern Restatement position is that the tort reaches civil proceedings that are resolved in a manner affirming the plaintiff's innocence of the wrong charged, subject to limits). The breaker is that the missing element — favorable termination — controls here, not the civil/criminal distinction. **Silver Key:** the call asks for the "strongest" problem, not a categorical rule. A student who picks B has overread a half-truth into a flat denial.

**C. Paul's alleged spite by itself automatically proves the malicious-prosecution claim.**

This is the dominant trap. Spite is one input to malice, and malice is one element of the claim, but malice is not the only element; the plaintiff must also establish lack of probable cause and (most importantly here) **favorable termination**. The breaker is that "spite" without a favorably-terminated prior proceeding does not make out the tort. **Gold Key:** every element must be satisfied independently; a strong showing on one element cannot substitute for a missing element on another.

**D. Lydia should relabel the claim as a private nuisance action against Paul.**

This is the wrong-tort-frame trap. Nuisance concerns interference with the use and enjoyment of land; a pending contract suit is litigation conduct, not interference with land use. The breaker is that relabeling the claim does not fix the favorable-termination defect, and a nuisance theory would require facts (continuing interference with land) that the stem does not supply.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17956, a Torts item involving a buyer who sued a seller for breach of contract and a seller who, while the breach suit was still pending, sued the buyer for malicious prosecution. The official key is **A**: the prior proceeding has not terminated favorably for the malicious-prosecution plaintiff.

A claim for malicious prosecution (or "wrongful civil proceedings" in the modern Restatement formulation) generally requires the plaintiff to prove: (1) the institution or continuation of a prior civil or criminal proceeding; (2) by or at the instance of the defendant; (3) termination of the prior proceeding in a manner that affirms the plaintiff's innocence of the charge or wrong asserted (favorable termination); (4) absence of probable cause for the prior proceeding; (5) the defendant's purpose was to secure a proper resolution of the underlying claim (i.e., not solely an improper purpose — the modern Restatement position) or, in the traditional majority formulation, malice. The Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 674 and 681 frame the tort. The favorable-termination element is grounded in the policies that (a) every prior proceeding should be allowed to run to a final judgment in the regular course, and (b) a party who has merely been sued has not yet suffered the kind of injury the tort is designed to redress. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 674 cmt. e, and the related commentary, treat favorable termination as a threshold requirement: if the prior proceeding has not yet been finally resolved in a manner affirming the plaintiff's innocence, the malicious-prosecution action does not accrue.

On these facts, the contract suit is still pending. There has been no judgment on the merits for Lydia, no voluntary dismissal that could be characterized as a favorable termination, and no other resolution that would satisfy the favorable-termination element. The presence of malice and an arguable lack of probable cause cannot cure the missing element. The transformed outcome is identical to the original: Lydia's malicious-prosecution claim is not available while the underlying contract suit remains pending.
