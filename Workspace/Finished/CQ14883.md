### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. The overlapping fire-insurance evidence points to motive for the charged arson. The threat to Mary points to consciousness of guilt and an effort to keep damaging testimony out of court. Both are other-act facts, but each is offered for a noncharacter purpose rather than to show that Stephen is a bad person who acted badly again.

Gold Key: FRE 404(b) blocks propensity use, not every other-act use. If the act explains motive, plan, knowledge, identity, absence of mistake, or consciousness of guilt, the first door is open, subject to the Rule 403 balance.

Silver Key: When the choices split two offered items, test each item separately before selecting "only," "both," or "neither." Here, both items have a noncharacter job.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A. The attraction is that threatening Mary feels more directly tied to the prosecution because it targets testimony in this case. That is admissible, but stopping there misses that the extra insurance also has a noncharacter purpose: it supplies a motive to burn the insured property.

C. The attraction is the broad character-evidence reflex: both facts make Stephen look bad, so neither should come in. FRE 404(b) does not require that result when the acts are offered for motive or consciousness of guilt, and the probative value is not substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice on these facts.

D. The attraction is the cleanest dominant trap: insurance on burned property sounds like classic motive evidence, while the threat sounds like a separate bad act. Gold Key: the threat is not being used as "Stephen is violent"; it is being used as evidence of consciousness of guilt and witness suppression, so it is also admissible.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule is unchanged. Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b)(1) bars using another crime, wrong, or act to prove character and action in conformity. Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b)(2) permits other-act evidence for a noncharacter purpose such as motive, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, absence of mistake, or lack of accident. Federal Rule of Evidence 403 still permits exclusion only when unfair prejudice, confusion, delay, or similar dangers substantially outweigh probative value. Huddleston v. United States, 485 U.S. 681 (1988), confirms that Rule 404(b) evidence is admissible when offered for a proper noncharacter purpose and supported sufficiently for the jury to find that the act occurred.

Sources checked: FRE 404(b), https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title28a-node232-article4-rule404; FRE 403, https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=2023&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-2023-title28a-node230-article4-rule403; Huddleston, https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/485/681/.

The overlapping fire-insurance evidence has a direct motive purpose in an arson prosecution. The threat to Mary has a direct consciousness-of-guilt and witness-suppression purpose. Neither item depends on the forbidden inference that Stephen has bad character and therefore committed arson. The official outcome remains B in the transformed item: both pieces of evidence are admissible.
