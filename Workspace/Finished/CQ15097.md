## 1. Final Question

Peter and Daniel are roommates at a Christian study retreat center. One evening, Daniel stays up late to pray and read Scripture. Around midnight, Peter stumbles back to their shared cabin after secretly drinking at a bonfire. ## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** *Apprehension must be contemporaneous with the defendant's act — a plaintiff who is asleep, has his back turned, or is otherwise unaware of the threat at the time cannot satisfy the apprehension element of assault, no matter how frightening the defendant's intent or how real the danger.*

C is correct. To recover for assault, the plaintiff must prove that he reasonably apprehended an imminent harmful or offensive contact at the time of the defendant's act. Apprehension requires awareness — the plaintiff must actually perceive the threat. Here, Daniel was kneeling with his back to the door and his eyes closed. He did not see Peter enter, did not see the flashlight, and had no idea Peter intended to strike him. Because Daniel was completely unaware of the threat at the time, he cannot establish the reasonable apprehension element, and his assault claim fails.

---

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect.** This choice sells the "true but incomplete" trap. It is true that Peter intended to inflict serious bodily harm — that satisfies the *intent* element of assault. But intent alone is not enough. The plaintiff must also prove he reasonably apprehended imminent contact. A student who picks A knows the intent element is satisfied but forgets to check whether the apprehension element is also met. The Gold Key above defeats this trap: intent without contemporaneous apprehension is insufficient.

**B is incorrect.** This choice confuses post-event fear with the contemporaneous apprehension assault requires. Learning the next morning that someone planned to attack you is frightening, but fear *after the fact* does not satisfy the apprehension element. Apprehension must occur at the time of the defendant's act. Daniel's fear upon hearing the story the next morning is legally irrelevant to the assault analysis. This trap pulls students who equate "being scared" with the legal requirement of "reasonable apprehension of imminent contact."

**D is incorrect.** This choice confuses assault with battery. Battery requires physical contact; assault does not. Assault is about the apprehension of imminent contact, not the contact itself. A student who picks D has memorized that battery requires touching but has not internalized that assault is the *threat* of touching. The absence of contact here is legally irrelevant — if Daniel had been aware of Peter's approach, the lack of contact would not defeat the claim.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 21 (1965): An actor is subject to liability to another for assault if (a) the actor acts intending to cause a harmful or offensive contact or an imminent apprehension of such contact, and (b) the other is thereby put in imminent apprehension of such contact.

**Elements of tortious assault:**
1. **Intent:** The defendant must intend to cause a harmful or offensive contact, or intend to create an imminent apprehension of such contact. (Satisfied here — Peter intended to strike Daniel.)
2. **Apprehension:** The plaintiff must actually and reasonably apprehend imminent harmful or offensive contact. (NOT satisfied — Daniel was unaware.)
3. **Imminence:** The threatened contact must be imminent, not a future threat.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 21–22 (1965); see also *Barton v Armstrong* [1969] 2 NSWR 451 (threats must create reasonable apprehension of *imminent* attack); FindLaw, "Elements of Assault Claims" ("if a victim is unaware of the threat of harm, no assault has occurred. For example, an assailant who points a gun at a sleeping person most likely has not committed an assault"); *McLean v McLean*, 2019 SKCA 15 at paras 59–60 (assault is "the intentional creation of the apprehension of imminent harmful or offensive contact").

**Verification:** The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original. The original tests whether a plaintiff who is asleep (unaware) can satisfy the apprehension element. The variant tests whether a plaintiff with his back turned and eyes closed (unaware) can satisfy the apprehension element. Same rule, same outcome, same trap structure. **PASS.**

#### Final Student Script

Daniel will **not** prevail on his assault claim. Assault requires both (1) intent to cause apprehension of imminent harmful or offensive contact, and (2) the plaintiff's reasonable apprehension of such contact. Here, Peter clearly intended to strike Daniel, satisfying the intent element. However, Daniel was kneeling in prayer with his eyes closed and his back to the door — he was completely unaware of Peter's presence and intent at the time. Because Daniel did not apprehend any imminent contact, the apprehension element fails, and the assault claim fails. The fact that Daniel felt afraid after learning of Peter's plan the next morning is legally irrelevant — apprehension must be contemporaneous with the defendant's act. The fact that Peter never touched Daniel is also irrelevant — assault does not require physical contact.
