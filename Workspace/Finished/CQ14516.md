# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question

Lydia operated a small Christian bookstore. A credit union agreed to lend her
$18,000 for one year at 6% interest, with the proceeds to be released within 10
days. Lydia planned to use the money to buy a limited run of hand-carved nativity
sets for resale at a projected profit of $8,400, but she said nothing about that
plan to the credit union. The credit union did not release the funds and refused
to assure Lydia that it would do so. Lydia then obtained a replacement one-year
loan from another lender at 9% interest. By the time Lydia began the replacement
loan application, the nativity sets had been sold to another retailer.

In an action against the credit union for breach of contract, which of the
following amounts is Lydia likely to recover?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A lender breaches a loan promise, the borrower gets a higher-rate substitute
loan, and an undisclosed resale opportunity is lost. Does the borrower recover
the extra borrowing cost or the hidden resale profit?

## 3. Final Answer Choices

- **A.** Nothing, because the lender never tacitly agreed that Lydia could
  recover damages if it breached the loan promise.
- **B.** The extra cost of borrowing from the replacement lender instead of
  borrowing at the promised rate.
- **C.** $8,400, Lydia's foreseeable lost profit from the nativity-set purchase.
- **D.** Nothing, because damages for a lost business opportunity are never
  recoverable.

## 5. Correct Answer

**B** (original key: **C**).

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

Lydia can recover the ordinary expectation loss caused by the failed loan: the
added cost of getting substitute financing. The credit union promised money at
6%, breached, and Lydia had to borrow at 9% instead. That interest-rate spread is
the direct cost of the lender's nonperformance.

The missed nativity-set profit is different. It depends on Lydia's special
resale plan, and she did not tell the credit union about that plan when the loan
contract was made. **Gold Key:** contract law separates ordinary/direct loss from
special consequential loss; special lost-profit damages require that the breaching
party had reason to know the special circumstances at formation. **Silver Key:**
when the answer array offers one direct replacement-cost amount and one downstream
profit amount, ask which loss the breacher could see from the contract itself.

The credit union could see the cost of substitute financing. It could not see the
hidden nativity-set resale profit. **B** is correct.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

- **A. Nothing, because the lender never tacitly agreed that Lydia could recover
  damages if it breached the loan promise.** This sounds technical because it
  uses a private-agreement label for damages. The breaker is that the MBE rule
  does not require a separate tacit agreement before the nonbreaching party can
  recover ordinary contract damages. Lydia can recover the direct cost of the
  substitute loan.
- **C. $8,400, Lydia's foreseeable lost profit from the nativity-set purchase.**
  This sells the most emotionally visible loss: Lydia really lost a profitable
  resale deal. The breaker is the formation-time knowledge fact. Lydia said
  nothing about the nativity-set plan, so the lost profit is not recoverable as a
  special consequential loss. **Gold Key:** the breacher must have reason to know
  the special circumstances when the contract is made.
- **D. Nothing, because damages for a lost business opportunity are never
  recoverable.** This choice catches the student who notices that the hidden
  resale profit is too remote and then swings too far. The breaker is the word
  "never." Lost-opportunity or consequential damages can be recovered when the
  foreseeability rule is satisfied; this case fails only because Lydia kept the
  special resale plan from the lender.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule is unchanged: contract damages generally protect the injured
party's expectation interest, subject to limitations such as foreseeability. The
right answer is the direct extra borrowing cost, not the undisclosed resale
profit.

Authority floor:

- Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 347 measures expectation damages by
  the loss in value caused by nonperformance plus other recoverable loss, less
  costs or loss avoided. Source checked:
  https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/9515-contracts-2023/resources/1.1.2-restatement-2d-of-contracts-347/
- Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 351 limits damages for losses the
  breaching party did not have reason to foresee as a probable result of breach
  when the contract was made. Source checked:
  https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/4201-contracts-2022/resources/1.2.3-restatement-2d-351-unforeseeable-damages/
- Hadley v. Baxendale, (1854) 9 Ex. 341, 156 Eng. Rep. 145, states the same
  ordinary-course / known-special-circumstances limit on damages. Source checked:
  https://law.justia.com/cases/foreign/united-kingdom/9-ex-ch-341-1854.html

Applying those rules, the lender's breach naturally causes the borrower to incur
the higher cost of replacement financing. That is recoverable expectation loss.
The lost profit on the nativity-set resale depends on special circumstances that
were not communicated to the lender. Because the lender had no reason to know
about that resale opportunity at contract formation, the $8,400 profit is too
remote. The official key and the legal research agree. Bank validation verdict:
**PASS**.
