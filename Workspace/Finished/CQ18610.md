### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because it points to the absence of physical contact and turns that into an easy no. The breaker is that duress does not require touching; the real defects are lack of imminence and the presence of legal alternatives.

C captures one real ingredient: bodily harm is the kind of threat duress can recognize. The breaker is that the type of harm is only one part of the test; timing and alternatives still defeat the defense.

D is the dominant trap because Ruth's genuine fear makes the defense feel morally compelling. The Gold Key defeats it: honest fear does not satisfy duress when the threat is future, conditional, and avoidable through reasonable legal alternatives.

### 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. Duress requires coercion by threat of death or serious bodily injury, but the threat must be sufficiently immediate or imminent and the defendant must lack a reasonable legal alternative to committing the offense. A future conditional threat usually fails when the defendant has time to seek protection, escape, or otherwise avoid the harm lawfully.

Authority floor: United States v. Bailey, 444 U.S. 394 (1980), explains that duress and necessity require a lack of a reasonable legal alternative and a direct causal relation between the criminal act and avoidance of the threatened harm. Dixon v. United States, 548 U.S. 1 (2006), describes duress as generally requiring an immediate or imminent threat and that the defendant take advantage of a reasonable opportunity to escape. United States v. Contento-Pachon, 723 F.2d 691 (9th Cir. 1984), states the common federal elements as an immediate threat, a well-grounded fear, and no reasonable opportunity to escape. Those authorities confirm the source key: Ruth's defense fails because the threat was delayed and conditional, and she had reasonable legal alternatives.
