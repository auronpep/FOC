### 7. Right-Answer Explanation

**C is correct.** Attempt is a specific-intent crime: the defendant must have the specific intent to commit the target offense. *See* 2 W. LaFave, *Substantive Criminal Law* § 11.2. Felony murder, by contrast, supplies malice aforethought from the commission of a dangerous felony — it does **not** require intent to kill. The problem with attempted felony murder is structural: felony murder's malice-substitution doctrine works for a *completed* death (where the felony supplies the missing intent), but it cannot supply the *specific intent to kill* that the *attempt* crime requires when no death has occurred. *People v. Bland*, 28 Cal.4th 313 (2002); *State v. Sanders*, 2000 Ohio. Andrew's best argument is that the charge itself is invalid because the intent required for attempt cannot be borrowed from the felony murder rule.

**Gold Key:** Attempt = specific intent to commit the target crime. Felony murder = malice supplied by the felony for a *completed* death. You cannot combine them: the felony supplies malice for murder, but it does NOT supply the specific intent to kill that attempt requires. When the target is murder and no one died, the attempt charge needs its own intent to kill — and felony murder cannot provide it.
