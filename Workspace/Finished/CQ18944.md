## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

A Terry stop and a Terry frisk are separate intrusions with separate justifications. The stop was lawful — officers had reasonable suspicion that Barnabas committed petit larceny. But a frisk requires a separate basis: officers must reasonably suspect the person is armed and dangerous. Here, Barnabas was calm, polite, made no threatening movements, and had no visible weapon bulge. Without that separate safety basis, the frisk was unlawful.

**Gold Key:** Stop and frisk are separate intrusions. Crime suspicion justifies the stop. Armed-and-dangerous suspicion justifies the frisk. One does not automatically include the other.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B (originally D):** Officers needed a warrant — wrong. Terry v. Ohio authorizes limited warrantless frisks when armed-and-dangerous suspicion exists. The problem is the lack of that suspicion, not the lack of a warrant.

**C (originally B) — DOMINANT TRAP:** "Every valid Terry stop includes a frisk." Students conflate stop and frisk because Terry uses both words. But they are separate intrusions with separate justifications. The Gold Key kills this trap.

**D (originally C):** "Petit larceny always involves a risk of violence" — a generalized overbroad claim. The officer needs articulable facts suggesting THIS suspect is armed, not a statistical generalization.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Governing rule:** Under Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), an officer may conduct a brief investigatory stop based on reasonable suspicion of criminal activity. A Terry frisk requires separate reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous. *See also* Minnesota v. Dickerson, 508 U.S. 366 (1993); Arizona v. Johnson, 555 U.S. 323 (2009).

**Application:** Reasonable suspicion of petit larceny justified the stop. But Barnabas showed no armed-and-dangerous indicators. The frisk was unlawful. Official key A confirmed correct.

This question tests whether you understand that a Terry stop and a Terry frisk are two separate constitutional intrusions, each requiring its own independent justification. The bar exam loves to conflate these two in a single scenario to create the "every stop includes a frisk" trap. Your job is to separate the intrusions in your mind, check the justification for each independently, and refuse to let the stop's validity bleed into the frisk's validity.
