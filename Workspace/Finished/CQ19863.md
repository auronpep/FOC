### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. Ruth was lawfully arrested, and the tote was beside her hand before officers moved her away. Gold Key: Search incident to a lawful arrest reaches the arrestee and the area within immediate control, including a reachable container, because the rule is aimed at weapons and evidence preservation. ### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A. The closed-container answer is the dominant trap because a zipped tote sounds private and warrant-worthy. The breaker is the Gold Key: a reachable container in the grabbing area can be searched incident to the lawful arrest, subject to the immediate-control limit.

B. The arrest-warrant answer is attractive because it notices that an arrest warrant is not a search warrant. The breaker is that the search is justified by the arrest-incident exception, not by treating the arrest warrant as a house-search warrant.

D. The entire-home answer is attractive because the arrest happened inside the home. The breaker is scope: search incident reaches the person and immediate-control area, not the whole house.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The governing rule is unchanged. Under Chimel v. California, 395 U.S. 752 (1969), a search incident to a lawful arrest may include the arrestee's person and the area within immediate control, meaning the area from which the arrestee might gain possession of a weapon or destructible evidence. United States v. Robinson, 414 U.S. 218 (1973), confirms that a lawful custodial arrest itself supplies the arrest-incident justification for a search of the person. Arizona v. Gant, 556 U.S. 332 (2009), reinforces that the Chimel rationale is tied to actual reaching distance at the time of the search. Here, the closed tote was beside Ruth's hand at the moment of arrest and was opened before she was moved away, so it was in the immediate-control area. The result does not authorize a full-home search, and the arrest warrant is not being used as a search warrant.
