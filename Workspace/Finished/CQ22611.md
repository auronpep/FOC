### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

A case is "unripe" when the controversy is not yet fully developed — the alleged injury is speculative, conjectural, or premature rather than concrete and objective. The Supreme Court held in *Laird v. Tatum*, 408 U.S. 1 (1972), that a plaintiff's claim that government data-gathering alone creates a "chilling effect" on First Amendment rights is insufficient for justiciability absent a showing of objective harm or a threat of specific future harm. Here, David alleges only that the roster's existence chills his First Amendment rights, but he has never been denied any benefit, disciplined, or threatened. His alleged injury is purely speculative — the kind of subjective chill that *Laird* held does not create a justiciable controversy. The case is therefore unripe and must be dismissed.

**Silver Key:** When a plaintiff claims that the mere existence of a government (or government-adjacent) data-collection practice chills constitutional rights, ask: has anything actually happened to the plaintiff yet? If the answer is no, the case is almost certainly unripe — cut it first.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap):** This choice is attractive because students see a lawsuit against "officials" and reflexively think of sovereign immunity under the Eleventh Amendment. The Eleventh Amendment bars suits against states by citizens of another state, but the *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), doctrine creates a well-established exception: state officials sued for injunctive relief to stop ongoing violations of federal law are not entitled to Eleventh Amendment immunity because their official capacity is stripped when they act contrary to federal law. David's suit seeks an injunction against ongoing conduct claimed to violate the First Amendment — exactly the posture *Ex parte Young* permits. The Eleventh Amendment does not bar this suit.

**D:** This choice is attractive because the case feels speculative — nothing has happened to David yet. But "mootness" and "ripeness" are distinct justiciability doctrines. A case is moot when the controversy has already ended — when no live dispute remains for the court to resolve. Here, the controversy is very much alive: the roster still exists, David still objects, and the legal question is unresolved. The problem is not that the case has concluded; it is that it has not yet ripened into a concrete injury. Mootness is the wrong timing doctrine for this posture.

**C:** This choice sounds lawyerly — "political question" is a term students associate with courts refusing to decide controversial matters. But the political question doctrine applies when the Constitution commits an issue to a coequal branch of government or when there are no judicially manageable standards. A First Amendment challenge to a data-collection practice is a routine constitutional claim that courts decide every day. There is no textual commitment of this issue to the political branches, and courts have clear standards for evaluating First Amendment claims. The political question doctrine does not apply.

#### Wrong Answer Recovery Paths

**A (political question):**
- Why a student picks this: The phrase "political question" sounds like a reason courts dismiss cases. The student may think any controversial case is a "political question."
- Skipped move: The student didn't check whether the political question doctrine's actual requirements are met (textual commitment to coequal branch, no manageable standards).
- Recovery step: Ask — is there a text in the Constitution that says Congress or the President decides this? Are there no standards a court can apply? If no to both, it's not a political question.

**C (Eleventh Amendment):**
- Why a student picks this: The student sees "officials" and reflexively thinks "sovereign immunity." The word "immune" sounds authoritative.
- Skipped move: The student didn't notice that the plaintiff seeks an injunction, not damages — and that injunctions against officials are the classic exception to sovereign immunity.
- Recovery step: Ask — is the plaintiff suing for money damages or for an injunction to stop ongoing conduct? If injunction, the Ex parte Young exception applies and the Eleventh Amendment does not bar the suit.

**D (mootness):**
- Why a student picks this: The case feels like nothing has happened yet, so the student reaches for a timing doctrine. "Moot" sounds like "doesn't matter anymore."
- Skipped move: The student confused "not yet ripe" (injury hasn't materialized) with "already moot" (controversy has ended).
- Recovery step: Ask — is the controversy still live? Does the practice still exist? If yes, the case is not moot. Then ask — has the plaintiff been concretely harmed? If no, the case is unripe.
