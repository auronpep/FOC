### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Daniel clearly invoked his right to remain silent about Martha's garage and the keyboard. The police did exactly what Miranda requires at that moment: they stopped questioning immediately. The later assault confession is not automatically suppressed merely because Daniel invoked silence earlier. The key is whether his right to cut off questioning was scrupulously honored.

Here it was. The officers stopped the first interview, waited four hours, used a different detective, gave fresh Miranda warnings, and questioned Daniel about an unrelated assault rather than resuming pressure about the garage and keyboard. Daniel then waived and confessed. That sequence supports admission of the assault confession.

**Gold Key:** A right-to-silence invocation requires police to stop the interrogation then underway; it does not create the same counsel-only reinitiation bar that follows a request for counsel.

**Silver Key:** Separate the two Miranda invocations before choosing: silence asks whether police honored the stop; counsel asks whether police reinitiated without counsel.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A is attractive** because it captures the first half of Miranda: when a suspect says he will not talk, police must stop. The breaker is the word "never." The right to silence is not a permanent ban on all later questioning. After immediate cessation, a significant break, fresh warnings, and questioning about a different matter can make the later statement admissible.

**C is attractive** because it reaches the correct no-suppression outcome and gives students a quick way to defend it. The breaker is the quote itself. "I do not want to talk about Martha's garage or that keyboard" is a clear silence invocation about that topic. The confession survives because the police honored that invocation, not because Daniel's words were unclear.

**D is attractive** because it sounds like a familiar Miranda protection: once counsel is invoked, police generally may not reinitiate custodial interrogation until counsel is present unless the suspect initiates. The breaker is that Daniel did not ask for a lawyer. **Gold Key:** a silence invocation is governed by the scrupulously-honored rule, not by the counsel-only reinitiation rule. **Silver Key:** the choice changes the right invoked; that is the trap.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is the Miranda right-to-silence rule from **Michigan v. Mosley, 423 U.S. 96 (1975)**. Mosley holds that, after a suspect invokes the right to remain silent, admissibility of a later custodial statement depends on whether the suspect's right to cut off questioning was "scrupulously honored." The Court approved later questioning where police immediately stopped the first interrogation, waited a significant time, gave fresh Miranda warnings, used another officer, and questioned the suspect about a different crime. Source checked: Legal Information Institute, https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/423/96; GovInfo, https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/USREPORTS-423/USREPORTS-423-96.

That authority confirms the credited answer. Daniel's first invocation was clear, and the first officers stopped immediately. The later detective waited four hours, gave fresh warnings, and questioned him only about an unrelated assault. Those facts match the Mosley admissibility pattern. The legal issue and credited outcome are unchanged: the assault confession should not be suppressed merely because Daniel earlier invoked silence about a different matter.
