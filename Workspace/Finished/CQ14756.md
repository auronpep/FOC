## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The cat's behavior—hissing, scratching, arching its back—is not hearsay. Under FRE 801(a), a "statement" is (1) an oral or written assertion or (2) nonverbal conduct of a **person**, if intended by the person as an assertion. FRE 801(b) defines a "declarant" as a **person** who makes a statement. Because a cat is not a person, its behavior does not constitute a "statement" under the hearsay rule. Lydia's expert testimony interpreting the cat's trained response is therefore not based on hearsay and is admissible as substantive evidence of the defendant's guilt, subject to foundation requirements showing the cat's training and reliability.

**Gold Key:** Animal behavior is not hearsay. The Federal Rules of Evidence define a "statement" as an assertion by a person (FRE 801(a)), and a "declarant" as a person (FRE 801(b)). Animals are not persons. Therefore, a trained animal's reactions—whether a drug-sniffing dog's alert, a cat's hissing, or a falcon's dive—do not constitute "statements" and cannot be hearsay. The handler's expert testimony about the animal's behavior is admissible as substantive evidence, not as an opinion based on hearsay. The only evidentiary hurdle is foundation (showing the animal's training and reliability), not a hearsay exception.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing Rule:** Federal Rules of Evidence 801(a)–(c).

- **FRE 801(a):** A "statement" is (1) an oral or written assertion or (2) nonverbal conduct of a **person**, if it is intended by the person as an assertion.
- **FRE 801(b):** A "declarant" is a **person** who makes a statement.
- **FRE 801(c):** "Hearsay" is a statement, other than one made by the declarant while testifying at the trial or hearing, offered in evidence to prove the truth of the matter asserted.

**Analysis:** The cat's behavior (hissing, scratching, arching its back) is nonverbal conduct. However, under FRE 801(a), nonverbal conduct constitutes a "statement" only if it is conduct of a **person** intended as an assertion. A cat is not a person. Therefore, the cat's behavior is not a "statement" under FRE 801(a). Because there is no "statement," there can be no "hearsay" under FRE 801(c). Lydia's expert testimony interpreting the cat's trained response is therefore not based on hearsay and is admissible as substantive evidence of guilt, provided a proper foundation is laid establishing the cat's training and reliability.

**Authority:** FRE 801(a)–(c); *see also* Advisory Committee Notes to FRE 801 (defining "statement" as requiring intent to assert, and limiting nonverbal-conduct assertions to those of a person); CliffsNotes, Evidence, "Animals and machines (watches, radar guns, etc.) are foundation issues, not hearsay issues. A drug sniffing dog identifying a suspect is not hearsay." The same principle applies to any trained animal's behavior.

**Key verification:** The credited answer (admissible) is correct.
