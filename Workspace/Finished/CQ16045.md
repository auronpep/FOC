### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**C is correct.** When a criminal statute is designed to protect a class of persons, a member of that protected class cannot be convicted as an accessory to the very crime the statute creates — even though the protected person's participation was factually necessary for the crime to occur. The statute here targets hiring authorities (people in Lydia's position) who extract value from hired tutors (people in Peter's position). Peter is the person the law was written to shield. Treating him as an accessory would punish the victim the statute was designed to protect. This is the only argument listed that could result in dismissal.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIM-PARTIES-01):** *A member of the class a criminal statute was enacted to protect cannot be convicted as an accomplice to that crime, even when the person's conduct was factually necessary to the offense.* The protected person is treated as the victim, not a co-perpetrator.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. "He did not assist Lydia in committing the crime."**
This sounds like a gut-level fairness argument — Peter might think he was just trying to get a tutor, not "helping" Lydia commit a crime. But the facts kill it: Lydia could not have committed this crime without Peter's payment. His money was the thing of value the statute prohibits. He factually assisted; the argument is simply wrong on the facts.

**B. "A person cannot be tried as an accessory until the principal has first been tried and convicted."**
This is the dominant trap. It triggers a memory of an old common-law sequencing rule that sounds authoritative and procedural. But the majority of jurisdictions have abandoned that requirement. Accessories can now be tried before, after, or jointly with the principal. The joint trial in the facts is perfectly lawful. This rule is a relic.

> **Gold Key callout (GK-CRIM-PARTIES-01):** The old common-law sequencing requirement has been abandoned in most jurisdictions. The Gold Key that unlocks this trap is knowing that modern law permits joint trial of principal and accessory regardless of sequencing.

**D. "No person can be charged as an accessory to a crime that could not have been committed without his participation."**
This is a Wharton's Rule bait. Wharton's Rule says that when a crime necessarily requires two participants, those two cannot be charged with *conspiracy* to commit it (because the agreement is already baked into the substantive offense). But Peter is not charged with conspiracy — he's charged as an accessory to the substantive crime. Wharton's Rule does not apply to accessory liability. The choice is a doctrine misfire: right area of law, wrong procedural context.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing doctrine:** Accomplice liability and the protected-class exception.

**Rule:** A person who is a member of the class that a criminal statute was designed to protect cannot be held liable as an accomplice (accessory) to that crime, even when the person's participation was factually necessary for the crime's commission. The protected person is the victim of the offense, not a co-perpetrator. *See* Model Penal Code § 2.06(6)(a) (providing that a person is not an accomplice in the conduct of another when the conduct is "inevitably incident" to the commission of the offense and the person is the victim or intended beneficiary of the protection of the statute); *see also* LaFave, *Criminal Law* § 6.7(c) (explaining that the victim of a crime cannot be an accomplice).

**Application:** The statute criminalizes a hiring authority receiving value from a hired tutor in exchange for hiring. The protected class is hired tutors (people in Peter's position). The statute was written to prevent hiring authorities from extracting payments from tutors. Peter is a member of that protected class. Even though his payment was factually necessary for Lydia to commit the crime, he is the victim the statute protects — not her co-criminal.

**Authority:** Model Penal Code § 2.06(6)(a); Wayne R. LaFave, *Criminal Law* § 6.7(c) (6th ed.); *see also* Wex Legal Encyclopedia, "Accomplice" (Cornell LII) (noting that victims of crimes are generally not accomplices when the statute was designed to protect them).
