### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Ruth's best defense is federal supremacy. Congress has constitutional power to regulate federal park property, and the federal statutes governing national parks specifically authorize the Interior Department's contract for the vine removal. The state may have a valid plant-protection law in general, but it cannot use that law to punish the federally authorized work inside the federal park when the state rule conflicts with valid federal law.

**Gold Key:** When valid federal law authorizes conduct on federal property, a conflicting state law must yield under the Supremacy Clause.

**Silver Key:** The phrase "contracted with" is not the call. Lock the federal-conflict lane before chasing contract or commerce labels.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. As applied, the state statute unduly interferes with interstate commerce.**  
This choice sells the interstate hook: Ruth lives in another state, so commerce language feels available. The breaker is the Silver Key: the prosecution is not defeated because the state burdened commerce; it is defeated because the state is trying to punish federally authorized park work.

**C. Only the federal government can designate a plant as protected.**  
This choice sounds clean because the federal park work wins, but it overclaims the reason. States can protect plants and wildlife within their police power; the problem is not the state's general power to protect the vine, but the conflict with valid federal authorization inside the park.

**D. As applied, the state statute violates the Obligation of Contracts Clause of the United States Constitution.**

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

Legal-confirmation layer only: Article IV, Section 3, Clause 2 gives Congress power to make rules and regulations respecting property belonging to the United States. Article VI, Clause 2 makes valid federal law supreme over conflicting state law. In `Kleppe v. New Mexico`, 426 U.S. 529 (1976), the Supreme Court confirmed broad congressional power over federal public lands under the Property Clause and held that conflicting state laws must yield to federal legislation enacted under that power.

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are preserved: a state law that is otherwise valid cannot be applied to punish conduct that valid federal statutes authorize on federal property. The state retains ordinary environmental police power, but the Supremacy Clause controls where the state law conflicts with valid federal park regulation.
