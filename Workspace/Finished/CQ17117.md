### 1. Final question

Mary runs a small Christian day school. She hired Paul, a playground-equipment contractor, to build a new playground on the school grounds for $32,000. Six weeks after completion, a five-year-old child broke her arm when a climber panel gave way. Mary sued Paul in federal district court on diversity grounds, asserting negligence and breach of warranty and seeking $40,000 in damages. Before the case was submitted to the jury, Paul moved for judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50(a) on the single ground that the $40,000 damages figure was not supported by the evidence. The court denied the motion. The jury returned a verdict for Mary and awarded $40,000. After the verdict, Paul filed a renewed motion for judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50(b) — his first time challenging causation — arguing that Mary had failed to introduce legally sufficient evidence that the climber's failure caused the child's broken arm.

May the court grant the renewed Rule 50(b) motion on the new causation ground?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The court may **not** grant the renewed Rule 50(b) motion on the new causation ground. A Rule 50(b) motion is a renewal of a pre-verdict Rule 50(a) motion, not a new motion; the post-verdict motion is limited to grounds that were fairly raised in the pre-verdict motion. Paul raised only the damages ground in his Rule 50(a) motion; the causation ground is new and is not properly before the court on a Rule 50(b) renewal. The general rule is that "a party may not urge in support of a renewed motion for judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50(b) any ground not fairly raised in support of the party's earlier Rule 50(a) motion." Unitherm Food Systems, Inc. v. Swift-Eckrich, Inc., 546 U.S. 394, 404 (2006). **Gold Key:** Rule 50(b) renews, rather than invents, a Rule 50(a) legal-sufficiency challenge. **Silver Key:** lock the call to whether the new ground was preserved in the pre-verdict 50(a) motion, not to whether the new ground is doctrinally strong.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Yes, because any pre-verdict Rule 50(a) motion preserves every possible post-verdict Rule 50(b) ground, regardless of whether the ground was raised before submission.**

This is the dominant trap and the classic "Rule 50(a) preserves every Rule 50(b) ground" misconception. The student hears "Rule 50(a) was filed" and assumes any post-verdict challenge is preserved. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: Rule 50(b) is a renewal, not a new motion; the post-verdict motion is limited to grounds that were fairly raised in the pre-verdict motion. The causation ground is new and is not properly before the court.

**C. No, because Rule 50(b) is available only to plaintiffs, not to defendants, after an adverse jury verdict.**

This is the "Rule 50(b) is for plaintiffs only" misframe. The student has reversed the party-availability rule. The breaker is the rule itself: any party may move for judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50, including a defendant after an adverse jury verdict. The renewed motion is the standard vehicle for a defendant's post-verdict legal-sufficiency challenge.

**D. Yes, because causation is more important than damages, and a court may grant renewed JMOL on any ground that is more important than the ground the defendant did raise pre-verdict.**

This is the "importance-priority" fallacy. The student has half-grasped that causation is an element of the claim and concludes that importance can substitute for preservation. The breaker is the rule itself: importance does not substitute for preservation. Rule 50(b) is a renewal motion, not an importance-priority vehicle; a new ground is not properly before the court simply because it is doctrinally more important than the ground the defendant did raise pre-verdict.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17117, a Civil Procedure item involving a defendant who moved for Rule 50(a) JMOL pre-verdict only on damages, the court denied the motion, the jury found for the plaintiff, and the defendant filed a Rule 50(b) motion arguing for the first time that the plaintiff failed to prove causation. The official key is C: the post-verdict motion cannot introduce a new legal-sufficiency ground not raised before submission.

Rule 50 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure governs judgment as a matter of law. Rule 50(a) authorizes a pre-verdict motion for judgment as a matter of law; the court may grant it if "there is no legally sufficient evidentiary basis for a reasonable jury to find for [a] party on [an] issue." Rule 50(b) authorizes a post-verdict renewed motion and is the procedural vehicle by which a party may challenge the sufficiency of the evidence after an adverse jury verdict. Critically, Rule 50(b) is a renewal, not a new motion; the post-verdict motion is limited to grounds that were fairly raised in the pre-verdict Rule 50(a) motion. The Supreme Court so held in *Unitherm Food Systems, Inc. v. Swift-Eckrich, Inc.*, 546 U.S. 394, 404 (2006), reasoning that Rule 50(b) is the renewal of the earlier Rule 50(a) motion, not a freestanding post-verdict vehicle. The Third Circuit's earlier articulation in *Walls v. Armour Standard Corp.*, 820 F.2d 1467, 1471 (10th Cir. 1987), and the consensus of the circuits support the same rule.

The policy rationale is straightforward. The pre-verdict Rule 50(a) motion gives the opposing party notice of the legal-sufficiency challenge and an opportunity to cure any deficiency before the case goes to the jury. Permitting a party to raise a new ground for the first time after the verdict deprives the opposing party of that notice-and-cure opportunity and would invite sandbagging: a party could withhold a known ground, take its chances with the jury, and then spring the withheld ground as a renewed post-verdict motion.

Rule 50(b) is available to any party meeting the Rule 50 requirements. A defendant after an adverse jury verdict is the paradigmatic Rule 50(b) movant. The "available only to plaintiffs" misframe is not the law; Rule 50(a) and Rule 50(b) are both available to plaintiffs and defendants.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original. Paul raised only the damages ground pre-verdict; the causation ground is new; the post-verdict Rule 50(b) motion cannot introduce the new causation ground; the court must deny the motion on the new ground.

A defendant moved for Rule 50(a) JMOL pre-verdict only on damages; the court denied. The jury found for the plaintiff. After the verdict, the defendant files a Rule 50(b) motion on a new causation ground never raised pre-verdict. May the court grant it?

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. Yes, because any pre-verdict Rule 50(a) motion preserves every possible post-verdict Rule 50(b) ground, regardless of whether the ground was raised before submission.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice asserts a flat rule that any pre-verdict Rule 50(a) motion preserves every possible post-verdict Rule 50(b) ground. The doctrinal overclaim is not student-accessibly false without the Gold Key; the unlimited-preservation framing is facially attractive because the student hears "Rule 50(a) was filed" and assumes any post-verdict challenge is preserved.
2. **Student label:** Unlimited-preservation overclaim.
3. **True/responsive version:** "A pre-verdict Rule 50(a) motion preserves only the grounds that were fairly raised before submission. A new ground raised for the first time after the verdict is not properly before the court on a Rule 50(b) renewal."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** *Unitherm Food Systems, Inc. v. Swift-Eckrich, Inc.*, 546 U.S. 394, 404 (2006), squarely held that a party may not urge in support of a renewed Rule 50(b) motion any ground not fairly raised in support of the party's earlier Rule 50(a) motion.

### B. No, because Paul did not fairly raise the causation ground in his pre-verdict Rule 50(a) motion, and a Rule 50(b) motion may not introduce a new legal-sufficiency ground.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the only choice that names the renewal-not-invention rule. It is true and responsive.
2. **Student label:** Residual answer.
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** A Rule 50(b) motion renews, rather than invents, a Rule 50(a) legal-sufficiency challenge. The post-verdict motion is limited to grounds fairly raised pre-verdict; the causation ground is new and is not properly before the court.

### C. No, because Rule 50(b) is available only to plaintiffs, not to defendants, after an adverse jury verdict.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice asserts a party-availability rule that is the inverse of the actual rule. Rule 50 is available to any party meeting the Rule 50 requirements; a defendant after an adverse jury verdict is the paradigmatic Rule 50(b) movant. The cut is structural.
2. **Student label:** Plaintiff-only availability misframe.
3. **True/responsive version:** "A defendant after an adverse jury verdict is the paradigmatic Rule 50(b) movant. Rule 50 is not plaintiff-only."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Rule 50(a) and Rule 50(b) are both available to plaintiffs and defendants. A defendant after an adverse jury verdict is the paradigmatic Rule 50(b) movant.

### D. Yes, because causation is more important than damages, and a court may grant renewed JMOL on any ground that is more important than the ground the defendant did raise pre-verdict.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice converts a doctrinal-strength observation into a preservation rule. Importance does not substitute for preservation. Rule 50(b) is a renewal motion, not an importance-priority vehicle. The cut is structural.
2. **Student label:** Importance-priority fallacy.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Causation may be doctrinally more important than damages, but that does not preserve the causation ground for a Rule 50(b) motion. The new ground is not properly before the court simply because it is doctrinally strong."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Importance does not substitute for preservation. Rule 50(b) is a renewal motion; a new ground is not properly before the court simply because it is doctrinally more important than the ground the defendant did raise pre-verdict.
