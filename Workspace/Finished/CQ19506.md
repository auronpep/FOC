# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## Final Question

Martha operated a network of Christian food pantries known as Mercy Tables that served thousands of families each month. She entered into a written agreement with Faithful Provision, a wholesale food distributor, under which Faithful Provision committed to supply all of Mercy Tables' requirements for staple dry goods — flour, rice, beans, and cooking oil — for a two-year term. The contract set the price each month according to a widely published national agricultural commodities index for those exact goods. Six months into the term, a severe, multi-state drought in the primary grain-growing regions caused the index price for the staples to rise sharply and unexpectedly. Faithful Provision sent Martha a letter stating it would no longer honor the contract at the indexed price and refused to make further deliveries. Martha sued Faithful Provision for breach of contract. The court should rule in favor of

**A:** Martha, because she bound Mercy Tables to purchase its actual requirements in good faith.

**B:** Faithful Provision, because Mercy Tables was not obligated to purchase any specific quantity of goods.

**C:** Martha, because a sharp rise in the commodities index alone does not excuse a seller from a requirements contract.

**D:** Faithful Provision, because the drought and resulting price spike were not foreseeable when the parties signed the agreement.

## Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**A is correct.** Under UCC §2-306(1), a term that measures quantity by the "requirements of the buyer" means the buyer's actual good-faith requirements. Official Comment 2 explains that such contracts do not lack mutuality of obligation because the buyer who determines quantity "is required to operate his plant or conduct his business in good faith and according to commercial standards of fair dealing in the trade so that his ... requirements will approximate a reasonably foreseeable figure." Martha's promise that Mercy Tables would purchase all its actual requirements for the listed staples, coupled with the statutory good-faith obligation to order reasonable quantities, supplied consideration for Faithful Provision's return promise to meet those needs at the indexed price. The dramatic price spike triggered by the drought is precisely the commercial risk the indexed-price requirements contract was designed to allocate; it does not render the contract unenforceable or excuse the seller.

## Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B (dominant trap — illusory promise / no mutuality).** This choice is attractive because the contract does not state a fixed number of bags or cases, so a student may conclude "Mercy Tables could buy zero and the promise is illusory." The breaker is that UCC §2-306(1) and Comment 2 read in the good-faith actual-requirements obligation; that obligation is not illusory and supplies the mutuality the common law once demanded. **Gold Key: "Good faith actual requirements = binding promise."** The tested_choice (B) would have been defeated by recognizing that the statute itself prevents the buyer from acting opportunistically or ceasing operations to avoid purchases.

**C.** This choice is attractive because it correctly states that market price movements alone usually do not excuse performance. The breaker is that it misses the precise legal question the call asks: whether the buyer's promise was sufficient to make the seller's counter-promise enforceable in the first place. The indexed price term is evidence the parties allocated price risk to the contract; the good-faith quantity term is what makes the whole agreement binding.

**D.** This choice is attractive because "unforeseeable supervening event" is a standard excuse doctrine students have memorized (impracticability or frustration). The breaker is that UCC §2-306 requirements contracts exist in part to protect the buyer against precisely these market swings; the seller assumed the risk of index increases by agreeing to the indexed price and the good-faith supply obligation. No fact in the stem shows the drought made performance commercially impracticable in the 2-615 sense; the seller simply no longer wants to sell at the (now higher) indexed price.

## Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule is UCC §2-306(1) and Comment 2: a requirements contract (buyer promises to purchase all its good-faith actual requirements from the seller) is enforceable and supplies mutuality/consideration; the buyer may not demand quantities unreasonably disproportionate to a stated estimate or to normal/prior comparable requirements; the seller may not refuse to supply the buyer's actual good-faith requirements merely because the market price has risen.

Governing authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):  
UCC §2-306(1) (Requirements and output contracts): "A term which measures the quantity by the output of the seller or the requirements of the buyer means such actual output or requirements as may occur in good faith, except that no quantity unreasonably disproportionate to any stated estimate or in the absence of a stated estimate to any normal or otherwise comparable prior output or requirements may be tendered or demanded."  
UCC §2-306, Comment 2: "Requirements and output contracts do not lack mutuality of obligation since under this section, the party who will determine quantity is required to operate his plant or conduct his business in good faith and according to commercial standards of fair dealing in the trade so that his output or requirements will approximate a reasonably foreseeable figure."

The credited outcome is unchanged from the source row: the buyer (airline / Martha) prevails because its good-faith requirements promise binds it and supplies consideration. Research (UCC text + comments + standard treatises) confirms the official key C (now A) is correct; no contradiction. Price-index contracts are the classic commercial device for allocating exactly this risk; neither war nor drought supplies an excuse when the contract itself indexes price and requires good-faith quantity.
