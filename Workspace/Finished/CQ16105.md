### 7. Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**B is correct.** A lesser included offense is one whose elements are entirely subsumed within the elements of a greater offense. Attempting to commit any crime is always a lesser included offense of that completed crime, because all the elements of the attempt are present when the full crime is committed. When Esther agreed to pay John and showed up with the cash, she had already committed bribery in the second degree — the offer was made and accepted, completing that crime. Because she is guilty of the completed offense, she is automatically guilty of all its lesser included offenses, including the attempt. The charge of attempted bribery in the second degree is therefore proven.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIMINAL-DJ-LIO-01):** Attempt is always a lesser included offense of the completed crime. One who commits a crime commits all lesser included offenses simultaneously. Being charged only with the lesser is no obstacle — the completed act proves the lesser beyond doubt.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIMINAL-DJ-MERGER-01):** A lesser included offense "merges" with the greater offense only in the sense that a defendant cannot be convicted of BOTH in the same proceeding. It does not mean the defendant cannot be convicted of the lesser alone. Merger is a double-conviction bar, not a conviction bar.

**Silver Key (SK-CRIMINAL-DJ-MERGER-01):** When a choice argues "not guilty because the attempt merged with the completed crime," test the merger conclusion: does merger bar conviction of the lesser ALONE, or only conviction of BOTH? Merger bars only the double conviction. Being charged only with the lesser eliminates the double-conviction problem entirely.

### 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**C** (dominant trap): This choice identifies that bribery in the second degree sounds like an early-stage, inchoate crime — after all, it is defined as "offering to commit bribery," which sounds like a prelude to the real thing. **A**: This choice correctly identifies that Esther committed bribery in the second degree when she agreed to pay John. That part is true. It then invokes the merger doctrine — also a real doctrine. Who spoke first is beside the point.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification

The lesser-included-offense rule is settled common law and constitutional doctrine: a lesser included offense is one whose elements are a subset of the greater offense's elements. Attempt to commit a crime is always a lesser included offense of the completed crime because the attempt's elements (specific intent + substantial step) are entirely contained within the completed offense. One who commits the completed crime necessarily commits the attempt as well.

The merger doctrine under double jeopardy: under the same-offense test (*Blockburger v. United States*, 284 U.S. 299 (1932)), a defendant may not be convicted of both a greater offense and a lesser included offense in the same proceeding. But this rule (a) operates as a bar to double conviction, not as a bar to conviction of the lesser alone, and (b) does not prevent conviction when only the lesser charge is brought. Because Esther is charged only with the attempt (the lesser), and not with bribery in the second degree (the greater), the merger doctrine is simply not triggered.

The official key (guilty) is confirmed. Esther committed bribery in the second degree by making the offer; that completed act encompasses the attempt as a lesser included offense; the prosecution's decision to charge only the lesser is valid and results in conviction.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):** *Blockburger v. United States*, 284 U.S. 299 (1932) (lesser-included-offense / same-offense test); *Brown v. Ohio*, 432 U.S. 161 (1977) (double jeopardy and lesser included offenses); LaFave, *Substantive Criminal Law* § 11.4 (attempt as lesser included offense of completed crime). No doctrinal-currency hotlist item triggered; lesser-included-offense doctrine under double jeopardy is stable.
