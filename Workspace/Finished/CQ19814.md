### 7. Full right-answer explanation

A is correct. The first jury trial put Lydia in jeopardy, and the appellate court did not reverse for a curable trial mistake. It reversed because the prosecution's evidence was legally insufficient to prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. Gold Key: a reversal for legally insufficient evidence is treated like an acquittal, so the prosecution does not get a second chance to prove the same charge. Silver Key: when the answer array gives three denial reasons and one bare grant, sort the reason for the appellate reversal first; trial error usually permits retrial, but evidentiary insufficiency bars it.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

B is attractive because "reversal" can sound like the first trial gets wiped away. The breaker is that a jury trial still placed Lydia in jeopardy, and an insufficiency reversal is not a nullity rule that erases the first jeopardy. Gold Key: insufficiency is treated like an acquittal, not like a remand for a clean do-over.

C is attractive because it notices that the first prosecution is over. The breaker is the direction of the rule: when jeopardy has attached and ended with an acquittal-equivalent insufficiency ruling, the ending supports dismissal; it does not authorize the second prosecution.

D is the dominant trap because a successful defense appeal often permits retrial after trial error. The breaker is the missing exception. Gold Key: the appeal does not waive the double-jeopardy bar when the reversal rests on legally insufficient evidence. Silver Key: do not stop at "appealed"; classify the appellate reason.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested issue and credited outcome remain unchanged: after a jury conviction is reversed for legally insufficient evidence, double jeopardy bars another trial on the same charge. The Fifth Amendment provides that no person may be twice put in jeopardy for the same offense. The Constitution Annotated states the same-offense protection and explains that successful defense appeals are the ordinary setting for reprosecution questions: https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/amdt5-3-5/ALDE_00000862/. The controlling insufficiency rule is Burks v. United States, 437 U.S. 1 (1978): once a reviewing court finds the evidence insufficient to sustain the guilty verdict, the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes a second trial and the proper remedy is acquittal. Burks also distinguishes trial-error reversals and rejects waiver by requesting a new trial: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/437/1. Crist v. Bretz, 437 U.S. 28 (1978), confirms that in a jury trial jeopardy attaches when the jury is empaneled and sworn: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/437/28/. Those authorities confirm that A is correct and that the denial reasons in B, C, and D do not defeat Lydia's motion.
