### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**D is correct.** Common-law robbery is larceny — the trespassory taking and carrying away of the personal property of another with intent to permanently deprive — plus the taking from the person or presence of another **by force or intimidation**. The intimidation element requires a threat of **immediate, imminent bodily harm** that places the victim in reasonable fear of present, imminent physical injury. Simon's threat to burn Dorcas's oil press at the next harvest is a threat of **future harm**, not immediate harm. This is the hallmark of extortion or blackmail: obtaining property by threat of future damage or injury, as opposed to the present fear required for robbery. Simon is therefore guilty of extortion and larceny, but not robbery.

**Silver Key:** When the call asks about robbery and the threat is anything other than immediate bodily harm, the intimidation prong fails — and the answer is "not robbery" regardless of how scary the threat sounded. Lock the question to the immediacy requirement first.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original D):** The trap here is the word "voluntarily." Handing property over under threat is not legally voluntary — it is a coerced surrender. Robbery law has never required the victim to resist physically; the taking element is satisfied whenever force or intimidation overcomes the victim's free choice. The problem with Simon's conduct is not how Dorcas surrendered the oil, but whether his threat met robbery's immediacy requirement. A coerced handover under a sufficient immediate threat is still a "taking" for robbery purposes.

**B (original A — DOMINANT TRAP):** This choice captures a real finding — Simon intimidated Dorcas and she parted with her property because of it. The attraction is that robbery's name is almost on the screen: he threatened, she complied, property moved. The breaker is the qualifier robber the word "immediate." Common-law robbery does not cover all intimidation leading to property transfers; it limits itself to intimidation by **immediat bodily harm**. Because Simon's threat was of harm "at the next harvest" — not right then and there — it fails the intimidation prong. The conduct may well be extortion, but it is not robbery.

**C (original B):** This choice says that a threat to a person's livelihood counts as a direct physical threat. The attraction is the real-world logic: for a widow with one oil press, the press *is* the livelihood, and threatening it feels as real as threatening the person. But under the common-law rule, the intimidation element requires a threat of bodily harm to the victim or someone present — not destruction of property. This choice reverses the test. (Some modern statutes may expand the definition, but the common-law rule governs here.)

### 9. Black-Letter Verification

**Authority.** Common-law robbery = larceny + force or intimidation (threat of force) used to take property from the person or presence of another. *See* 2 W. LaFave, *Substantive Criminal Law* § 20.2 (3d ed.); *Commonwealth v. Szulewski*, 409 Mass. 641 (1991) (robbery requires threat of imminent harm distinguishing from extortion); Model Penal Code § 222.1 cmt. (noting common-law distinction between robbery's immediate threat and extortion's future threat).

The immediacy requirement is well established: a threat of future harm — "next month," "next harvest," "I'll come back with a weapon" — is characteristic of extortion, not robbery. E.g., *People v. Montoya*, 184 Cal. App. 3d 1147 (1986) (threat of future harm insufficient for robbery; proper charge extortion).

**Signal:** The stem gives you a time-delayed threat ("next week," "next month," "next harvest," "I'll come back with…").

### Recovery from Choice A

### Recovery from Choice B
**Why a student picks this:** This is the strongest trap. Simon clearly intimidated Dorcas. She clearly handed over property. "Intimidation to obtain property" sounds exactly like the definition of robbery.
**Skipped move:** The immediacy check — looking at WHEN the threatened harm would occur.
**Recovery step:** Isolate the threat's timing. "Next harvest" is not "right now." The intimidation prong requires **immediate** bodily harm. The threat here is real but future-dated → extortion, not robbery. This is the item's core distinction. Slow down and check the time qualifier.

### Recovery from Choice C
**Why a student picks this:** The olive press is Dorcas's livelihood — threatening it feels equivalent to threatening her, especially to a widow. The emotional logic is seductive.
**Skipped move:** The common-law rule distinguishes threats to the body from threats to property. Property ≠ person.
**Recovery step:** At common law, robbery's intimidation prong requires a threat of **bodily harm**. Threats to property (even critical property) are not sufficient. This choice overstates the law. If you feel the pull, name the distinction: body vs. property. The rule is narrow.
