### 4. Right-Answer Explanation (Choice C)

**Gold Key:** Specific performance is an equitable remedy available to the *buyer* in a real estate (or unique-goods) contract because the buyer's interest — obtaining the unique item — cannot be satisfied by money alone. The *seller*, by contrast, has a purely monetary interest: receiving the purchase price. Money damages are fully adequate to compensate the seller for the lost sale. Therefore, the seller is entitled to damages, not specific performance.

Timothy's breach of the purchase contract entitles Lydia to recover any damages that flow from his failure to complete the transaction — for example, the difference between the contract price and the price she ultimately obtains from a resale, plus incidental costs. This is the standard expectation-damages remedy for breach of contract.

### 6. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule — Specific Performance and the Seller's Remedy:**

Under contract law, specific performance is an equitable remedy available when money damages are inadequate to compensate the non-breaching party. In real estate contracts, specific performance is commonly available to the *buyer* because each parcel of land is deemed unique, and money cannot replace the particular property the buyer contracted to acquire. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 360 (factors affecting adequacy of damages); *Semelhago v. Paramadevan*, [1996] 2 S.C.R. 415 (S.C.C.) (specific performance not automatic even for land; damages may be adequate).

The critical distinction tested here is *which party* seeks specific performance. The seller of real property (or a unique good) has a purely financial interest — receiving the contract price. Money damages are fully adequate to compensate the seller. The buyer, by contrast, seeks the unique item itself and cannot be made whole by a money judgment. Accordingly, specific performance is available to the buyer, not the seller.

**Impossibility Defense:**

The defense of impossibility excuses performance only when the event rendering performance impossible was unforeseeable at the time of contracting and was not a risk the party assumed. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 261 (impracticability); UCC § 2-615 (commercial impracticability). A party's subsequent illness, disability, or change in financial circumstances is generally considered foreseeable and does not excuse performance. *See* *Krell v. Henry*, [1903] 2 KB 740 (distinguishing frustration of purpose from impossibility); *Patch v. Solar Corp.*, 2008 WL 3896746 (government order excusing performance must be unforeseeable).

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 261, 360; UCC § 2-615.
