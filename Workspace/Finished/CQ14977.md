## 1. Final question (revised stem + call)

Naomi and Stephen owned the Old Mulberry Mill, a large brick workshop building in the
city's warehouse district, in fee simple as tenants in common, each owning an undivided
one-half interest. Naomi lived in a distant city and cared only about realizing a profit
when the building could be sold at the price she wanted. For six years Stephen occupied
the building and ran a pottery-and-woodworking workshop out of it. Stephen never
accounted to Naomi for any income, but he paid all real estate taxes when they came due
and kept the building insured against loss from fire, theft, and storm.

Stephen died intestate, survived only by his sole heir, his niece Hannah. Hannah then
occupied the building and tried to keep the workshop running, but was inexperienced in
the craft, and the result was a financial disaster. Hannah failed to pay the real estate
taxes for two years. The appropriate governmental authority held a tax sale to recover
the taxes due. At that sale Naomi was the only bidder and obtained a conveyance from the
appropriate governmental authority upon payment of an amount sufficient to discharge the
taxes due, plus interest and penalties, and the costs of holding the tax sale. The amount
Naomi paid was one-quarter of the reasonable market value of the building.

Naomi then instituted an appropriate action against Hannah to quiet title in and to
recover possession of the building. Hannah asserted all defenses available to her but
refused to pay Naomi any money. Except for the statutes related to real estate taxes and
tax sales, there is no applicable statute.

In this lawsuit, Naomi is entitled to a decree quieting title so that Naomi is the sole
owner in fee simple of the building

## 2. Distilled Core Question

Two people own land equally as tenants in common. One pays the taxes for years, then dies;
the heir lets the taxes lapse. The other cotenant buys the land at the tax sale for far
below value and sues for sole title. On what condition does she win?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**B is correct.** A cotenant cannot use a tax sale to wipe out the other cotenants and take
the whole estate. Naomi's purchase at the tax sale is treated as a redemption made for the
benefit of the cotenancy, so Hannah keeps her undivided one-half interest **if** she
reimburses her share of the carrying cost. **Gold Key:** the reimbursement is measured by
what the buying cotenant actually *paid* for the tax deed, not by the land's value — and a
cotenant in sole possession who receives no third-party rents owes no accounting, so the
only item to divide here is the single tax-deed expenditure. Because the property generated
no rents to account for and there is exactly one expenditure to split, Hannah retains her
half by paying one-half of what Naomi paid for the deed. Having refused to pay anything,
Hannah forfeits the protection of that redemption, and Naomi is entitled to be quieted in
as sole owner. The decree turns on Hannah's *refusal to contribute her half of the cost* —
which is precisely what B states.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A (dominant trap — fabricated obligation).** It feels right because Stephen had paid every
tax bill and insured the building for years, so the heir who "let it all fall apart" looks
like she broke a duty she inherited. The breaker: nobody was ever obligated to carry the
property alone — Stephen paid voluntarily, and a cotenant's voluntary expenditures create a
right to *contribution*, never a personal *obligation* whose breach forfeits the land.
**Gold Key:** a cotenant's only enforceable carrying-cost remedy is contribution, so an
answer that punishes Hannah for "defaulting on Stephen's obligations" rests on a duty that
does not exist.

**C (survivorship confusion).** It is attractive because Naomi did outlive Stephen, and
"the survivor takes" sounds like ownership law. The breaker: survivorship belongs to *joint
tenancy*; the stem fixes the estate as a **tenancy in common** with undivided one-half
interests, so Stephen's death passed his half to his heir Hannah, not to Naomi. **Gold
Key:** tenants in common have no right of survivorship — death of a cotenant sends the
share to heirs or devisees.

**D (wrong measure of contribution).** It is the most dangerous near-miss because it gets
the *structure* right — Hannah must pay to keep her share — and only the *number* wrong. The
breaker: contribution is keyed to the amount the redeeming cotenant **spent**, not to the
property's market value; pegging it to value would hand Hannah a windfall or a penalty
having nothing to do with the tax debt. **Silver Key:** when two choices agree the party
must pay and differ only on the yardstick, pick the yardstick tied to what was actually
spent and cut the market-value figure.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification + legal reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule (unchanged).** Cotenants stand in a confidential relationship with respect to
the common estate. The majority common-law rule is that one cotenant cannot acquire an
outstanding tax title (or any adverse title arising from the common burden) and assert it
against the other cotenants; the acquisition operates as a redemption that **inures to the
benefit of all cotenants**, subject to the redeeming cotenant's right to **contribution** of
each cotenant's proportionate share of the **amount expended** (taxes, interest, penalties,
costs). A cotenant in exclusive possession who has not ousted the others and has received no
rents from third parties generally owes **no accounting** for the value of personal use, so
the only divisible item here is the tax-deed cost.

**Application.** Naomi (cotenant) bought at the tax sale for taxes + interest + penalties +
costs = one-quarter of value. That purchase redeems for the cotenancy; Hannah may keep her
one-half interest by reimbursing one-half of what Naomi paid. There are no rents to account
for, so accounting is unnecessary. Hannah refused to pay anything, so she does not retain her
interest and Naomi takes sole title. Credited outcome (sole title, conditioned on the heir's
refusal to pay half the deed cost) is **identical** to the original.

**Authority floor.** General common-law rule of cotenant fiduciary duty and contribution;
see 20 Am. Jur. 2d *Cotenancy and Joint Ownership* §§ 95–112 (cotenant cannot acquire
adverse tax title; redemption inures to all; contribution measured by sums expended); J.W.
Looney, *Right of Cotenant to Contribution From Other Cotenants*, Notre Dame Law Review
(equitable contribution / equitable-lien basis); illustrative case *Bankers Trust Co. v.
Woodall* (N.M. Ct. App. 2006) (one cotenant's redemption does not divest fellow cotenants;
they redeem proportionately as tenants in common). The governing proposition is stated
plainly above as the floor; the case is cited as illustration. `attorney_review: true` for
jurisdiction-specific phrasing of the contribution measure.

**Bank validation:** research **confirms** the official key. No key disagreement →
`PASS`.
