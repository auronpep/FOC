Lydia wanted Stephen, the owner of a lakeside cabin she hoped to buy for a private Bible-study retreat, killed before he could sell the cabin to someone else. ### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. Lydia believed she had made an agreement with Timothy to have Stephen killed, and she paid Timothy in furtherance of that plan. Gold Key: in a unilateral-conspiracy jurisdiction, the defendant's believed agreement and criminal purpose can supply conspiracy liability even when the other person is only pretending. Silver Key: the call asks conspiracy, so lock onto agreement; do not grade the answer by attempt's substantial-step language.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because the payment looks like the concrete move that proves guilt. The breaker is call focus: substantial step is attempt language, while this call asks whether a conspiracy exists from the believed agreement.

C sells the idea that the plan could not work because Timothy was a police officer. The breaker is the unilateral rule: Lydia is judged by the agreement she believed she made, not by Timothy's hidden law-enforcement status.

D tracks the most visible undercover-officer fact and treats Timothy's lack of criminal intent as fatal. The Gold Key defeats that trap: unilateral conspiracy does not require the other person to genuinely share the criminal purpose.

### 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. The item tests unilateral conspiracy: when a jurisdiction adopts that approach, a defendant can be guilty if she purposely agrees, or believes she is agreeing, with another person to commit the target crime. The other person's feigned agreement or immunity does not defeat liability.

Authority floor: Model Penal Code sections 5.03(1)(a) and 5.04(1)(b) supply the unilateral-conspiracy structure; State v. Tyma, 636 N.W.2d 620 (Neb. 2002), explains that under the unilateral approach only the defendant need agree and the second party can feign agreement; U.S. v. Valigura, 54 M.J. 187 (C.A.A.F. 2000), describes the MPC unilateral theory as permitting punishment when the actor believes she has agreed with another even though the other person has no purpose to commit the crime. The credited answer agrees with those authorities. The attempt/substantial-step choice remains wrong because it answers a neighboring inchoate offense rather than the conspiracy call.
