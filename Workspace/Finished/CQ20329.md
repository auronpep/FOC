### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct** because Lydia was on a substantial personal frolic at the time she struck the pedestrian. Under respondeat superior, an employer is vicariously liable only for torts committed by an employee acting within the scope of employment. Here, Lydia drove seven miles in the opposite direction from her assigned delivery route to attend a personal social event. That is a substantial deviation in distance, direction, and purpose. The fact that she planned to complete the delivery after the game does not change her status at the time of the accident.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule — Respondeat Superior / Scope of Employment:**

Under the doctrine of respondeat superior, an employer is vicariously liable for a tort committed by an employee acting within the scope of employment. *Restatement (Third) of Agency* § 7.07(1) (2006). An employee acts within the scope of employment when performing work assigned by the employer or engaging in a course of conduct subject to the employer's control. *Id.* § 7.07(2). Conversely, an employee's act is *not* within the scope of employment when it occurs "within an independent course of conduct not intended by the employee to serve any purpose of the employer." *Id.*

**Frolic vs. Detour:**

The classic distinction is between a *detour* (a minor deviation from the assigned task, still within scope) and a *frolic* (a substantial personal departure, outside scope). Factors courts consider include: (1) the time and distance of the deviation, (2) whether the deviation was for the employee's personal benefit, (3) the degree of departure from the assigned route or task, and (4) whether the deviation was foreseeable. A substantial frolic — such as driving several miles in the wrong direction to attend a social event — takes the employee outside the scope of employment until the employee returns to the employer's business. *See* Bloomberg Law, *Frolic and Detour: Tort Defense*; *Restatement (Third) of Agency* § 7.07.

**Intent to return is irrelevant:**

The scope inquiry is temporal — it asks whether the employee was within scope *at the time of the tort*. An employee's subjective intent to resume work later does not retroactively bring the frolic within scope. *See* the discussion in *Wetherbe v. State* (Tex. App. 2016) (citing *Restatement (Third) of Agency* § 7.07 and rejecting the argument that subjective intent to serve the employer controls the scope analysis).

**Authority:** *Restatement (Third) of Agency* §§ 7.07(1), 7.07(2) (American Law Institute 2006).
