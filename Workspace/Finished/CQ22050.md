## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register) — B

The water company's easement was created for the benefit of the company in its business
capacity, not for the benefit of an owner of a specific dominant parcel — so it is an
easement **in gross**. The governing rule is that an easement in gross is alienable **if it
is commercial**: the holder's use produces economic benefit. Providing metered water
service for a fee is commercial, so the water company may license a third party to use the
pipes — especially where, as here, the added use does not increase the burden on the
servient land. **Gold Key:** a commercial easement in gross is alienable, but a purely
personal (noncommercial) easement in gross is not — that distinction is exactly what makes
B accurate and A's universal claim false. **Silver Key:** between two same-area rule choices
that differ only in breadth, the one carrying the real exception ("noncommercial … not …,
commercial … are …") is the credited answer; the unqualified "all" choice is the bait.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A — "All easements in gross are freely alienable."** The choice lands on the right
result — the easement is in gross and is in fact transferable on these facts — so it reads
like the controlling rule. The universal "all" is the breaker: purely personal
(noncommercial) easements in gross are not alienable, so only the commercial-qualified form
states the law. **Gold Key:** commercial easements in gross are alienable, noncommercial
ones are not — knowing the carve-out is what kills the absolute. **Silver Key:** the bare
"all" is the tell; the choice that names the exception wins.

**C — "All easements appurtenant are freely alienable."** It is a genuine black-letter rule
and sits squarely in easement law, so it sounds authoritative and safe to pick. It governs
a different creature: this easement benefits the water company itself, not an owner of a
dominant estate, so it is in gross, not appurtenant — a true statement that does not answer
why Stephen loses.

**D — "Stephen derived a commercial benefit from the easement."** The word "commercial" in
the controlling rule pulls the eye toward whoever profited, and a developer-adjacent grantor
looks commercial. Commercial character is measured by the easement holder's economic use,
not the grantor's benefit — and on these facts Stephen sold the development before granting
the easement, so he gained nothing from it; the point neither states the rule nor answers
the call.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification + right-answer legal reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule (verified):** An easement in gross is created for the benefit of a holder in a
capacity other than as owner of a dominant parcel. A **commercial** easement in gross — one
whose use yields economic benefit — is **alienable/assignable**, and may be licensed to a
third party so long as the transfer does not increase the burden on the servient estate. A
purely personal (noncommercial) easement in gross is generally **not** transferable.

**Authority:** Restatement (First) of Property §489 & cmt. c (1944) (commercial easements in
gross are alienable; commercial character turns on economic, not personal, use); *Miller v.
Lutheran Conference & Camp Ass'n*, 331 Pa. 241, 200 A. 646 (1938) (leading case: commercial
easements in gross are assignable; assignees take "one stock"); Restatement (Third) of
Property: Servitudes §4.6 (2000) (modern rule treats benefits in gross as freely
transferable unless contrary to the parties' intent).

**Doctrinal-currency note (not on the hotlist, flagged for completeness):** The bar-tested
framing is the traditional commercial/noncommercial split (Restatement First §489). The
Restatement (Third) §4.6 modernizes this — all easements in gross are presumptively
transferable absent contrary intent — but the **outcome is unchanged**: a commercial
easement in gross is alienable under both regimes. Superseded framing in some jurisdictions,
same credited outcome → noted here, not a key disagreement. `attorney_review: false` for the
outcome; framing note logged.

and credited outcome (servient owner loses) are **UNCHANGED** from the original. Research
