### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. Lydia did not know the keyboard was stolen when she received it as a gift, so the common-law receiving-stolen-property offense is missing the required timing of guilty knowledge. Gold Key: for common-law receiving stolen property, the knowledge that the property was stolen must exist when the defendant receives it; learning the truth later does not retroactively make the receipt criminal. She also did not commit larceny because accepting a gift without knowing it belongs to someone else is not a trespassory taking with intent to steal. Silver Key: when the answer array stacks crimes, test each named crime separately before accepting a combo answer; if each named crime misses its timing element, the residual can be no crime.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because Lydia kept using the keyboard after learning it was stolen, which makes the fact pattern feel morally complete. The breaker is that larceny needs a trespassory taking with intent to steal at the taking, and Lydia's receipt of a gift was not that kind of taking.

C is attractive because it captures both guilty-looking facts: the item was stolen, and Lydia kept it after the warning. The breaker is that stacking two crimes cannot cure the missing timing elements; common-law receiving stolen property lacks knowledge at receipt, and larceny lacks a trespassory taking with intent to steal.

D is the dominant trap because it uses the strongest fact in the stem: Lydia knew the keyboard was stolen and kept it anyway. Gold Key: the knowledge must be present at receipt for the common-law receiving offense; later knowledge plus continued use does not make the original receipt criminal under this tested frame.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law and Procedure, Crimes against Property, Possession offenses, Receipt of stolen property, and the verified outline code is `73030801`. Under the historical/common-law receiving-stolen-property offense tested by this item, the defendant must receive stolen property with knowledge of its stolen character and with intent to deprive the owner of it. Illinois common-law receiving cases state the same timing requirement: the proof must show that the receiver knew the property was stolen when he received it. See *People v. Mowry*, 125 N.E.2d 471 (Ill. 1955), discussing *People v. Piszczek*, 83 N.E.2d 617 (Ill. 1949), and *People v. Allen*, 407 Ill. 596, 96 N.E.2d 446 (1950), https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/59149f54add7b0493466831a and https://law.justia.com/cases/illinois/supreme-court/1950/31741-5.html. The BIA's historical survey in *Matter of Bahta*, 22 I&N Dec. 1381 (BIA 2000), also recognizes that the receipt offense historically required knowledge at the time of receipt, while noting that the Model Penal Code and many modern statutes broaden the offense to include retaining or possessing stolen property after knowledge. See https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/eoir/legacy/2014/07/25/3437.pdf; Model Penal Code section 223.6.

That modern-statute contrast does not change this MBE-style common-law item because the answer choices name "receiving stolen property" and "larceny," not a separate statutory retaining, concealing, withholding, or possession offense. Lydia acquired the keyboard innocently, so common-law receiving stolen property fails. Larceny also fails because common-law larceny requires a trespassory taking and carrying away of another's personal property with intent to steal at the taking; Lydia accepted a gift without knowing it was another's property. The credited outcome is therefore unchanged: no crime under the listed choices.
