## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Timothy was lawfully arrested for stealing sound equipment from a private Christian retreat center, without an arrest warrant. He was not given Miranda warnings but was immediately taken to a police station, where he and five other men were placed in a lineup to be viewed by Lydia, the retreat volunteer who had been in the equipment room when the thief fled. Each man was required to say the words Lydia heard the thief shout: "Open the cabinet. I need the speaker keys." After all the men in the lineup spoke those words, Lydia identified Timothy as the thief.

Timothy later moved to suppress Lydia's testimony, claiming that the lineup violated his privilege against self-incrimination. At the suppression hearing, Lydia testified that she had not gotten a good look at the thief's face because the thief had been wearing a hood pulled low over most of his face, but that she was certain Timothy was the thief because she had recognized his voice at the lineup.

Should Timothy's motion be granted?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

Does requiring an arrested suspect to repeat a thief's words in a lineup violate the privilege against self-incrimination when the witness identifies him by voice?

### 3. Final Answer Choices

A. No, because testimony of a witness based on firsthand observation is not subject to exclusion as the fruit of the poisonous tree.

B. Yes, because Timothy was never told that he could refuse to make a statement and that any statement could be used as evidence against him.

C. No, because being required to speak at the lineup, while compelled, was not testimonial or communicative.

D. Yes, because Timothy was compelled to speak at the lineup, and this compelled speech led to the witness's identification testimony.

C (original key A).

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Timothy was compelled to speak, but the police used his voice as an identifying physical characteristic. The privilege against self-incrimination protects testimonial or communicative evidence, not the compelled display of physical traits such as voice sound.

Gold Key: speaking can be compelled when the words are used only to identify the physical sound of the voice, not to prove the truth or content of a statement.

Silver Key: when a lineup answer mentions "speech," ask whether the prosecution wants the message or the physical voice.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because Lydia personally heard the thief and later identified Timothy. The breaker is overbreadth: eyewitness identification can be excluded when it flows from an unconstitutional identification procedure, so firsthand observation alone is not a universal answer.

B is the dominant trap because Miranda warnings feel mandatory whenever police make a suspect speak. The breaker is the Gold Key: Miranda protects testimonial custodial interrogation, while this lineup required a voice exemplar used as physical evidence.

D is attractive because it correctly notices compulsion and causation. The breaker is the Silver Key: the compelled act produced a voice comparison, not a testimonial admission or communicated fact.

### 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The credited answer is unchanged: the motion should be denied because compelling a suspect to speak words in a lineup for voice identification is not compelled testimonial communication. United States v. Wade, 388 U.S. 218 (1967), held that requiring an accused in a lineup to use his voice as an identifying physical characteristic did not violate the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination. Pennsylvania v. Muniz, 496 U.S. 582 (1990), likewise distinguishes testimonial communication from physical voice characteristics; a suspect may be required to reveal the physical manner of speech without making a testimonial response. United States v. Hubbell, 530 U.S. 27 (2000), confirms that the privilege applies to testimonial acts that communicate factual assertions, which is not what a voice exemplar does.

The transformed facts preserve the issue, posture, and credited outcome. The defendant is lawfully arrested, placed in a lineup without Miranda warnings, required to repeat the thief's words, and identified by voice. The answer remains that the speech was compelled but not testimonial or communicative.
