### 7. Full right-answer explanation
A is correct. Daniel's claim is not based on his own physical danger or a bodily injury suffered during the rescue. It is based on emotional distress from witnessing a stranger's fatal injury and being unable to save him. Gold Key: a bystander outside the zone of physical danger generally needs a close family or comparable relationship with the injured person before bystander emotional-distress recovery is available. Silver Key: separate the witness-to-stranger lane from the rescue lane before honoring a heroic-sounding rescue phrase. Daniel was across the courtyard when the cart struck the courier, did not know the courier, and had no bodily symptoms, so he is not likely to recover.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
B is attractive because "zone of danger" is a real NIED phrase and can feel like the fastest path to recovery. The breaker is visible in the stem: Daniel saw the collision from the far side of the courtyard, so the cart's negligent movement did not place him in immediate personal danger.

C is attractive because the story makes Daniel the rescuer, and "danger invites rescue" sounds like the law should reward that intervention. The breaker is that the rescue doctrine protects a rescuer's claim for harm suffered in the rescue effort; it does not erase the close-relationship or zone-of-danger limits when the claimed harm is pure emotional distress from a stranger's death. Gold Key: bystander NIED recovery still needs Daniel's own danger lane or a close relationship lane.

D is attractive because Daniel voluntarily entered the aftermath of the accident, so the choice converts his good act into a defense. The breaker is that voluntary rescue is not an automatic assumption-of-risk bar against the negligent actor who created the peril, and this claim fails for the NIED limits instead.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Torts, Negligence, negligent infliction of emotional distress, and the verified outline code is `61020203`. The credited outcome is unchanged: no recovery.

The black-letter rule is that emotional-distress recovery in negligence is limited by control devices such as the zone-of-danger rule, physical harm or symptom requirements in some jurisdictions, and special bystander rules. Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical and Emotional Harm section 48 states the bystander-recovery lane for serious emotional harm from bodily injury to another in terms of contemporaneous perception and a close family relationship. *Thing v. La Chusa*, 48 Cal. 3d 644 (1989), likewise limits bystander NIED recovery to a plaintiff who is closely related to the injury victim, is present and aware of the injury-producing event, and suffers distress beyond that of a disinterested witness. The ALI notes that Oregon adopted Restatement section 48 for bystander recovery in *Philibert v. Kluser*, and Cornell's Wex summary of the zone-of-danger rule confirms that the plaintiff must be placed in immediate risk of physical harm and frightened by that risk.

Those authorities confirm the source key. Daniel was not in the path of Mary's cart when the negligent act occurred, so the zone-of-danger lane does not apply. He also had no close family or comparable preexisting relationship with the courier, so the broader bystander lane does not apply. The rescue doctrine is real, but it does not convert emotional distress over a stranger's death into a recoverable NIED claim when the rescuer was not physically harmed and was not personally endangered. Research sources checked: https://www.ali.org/news/articles/institute-courtsoregon-and-new-jersey-adopt-restatement-sections, https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/thing-v-la-chusa-28548, https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/zone_of_danger_rule, and https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/rescue_doctrine.
