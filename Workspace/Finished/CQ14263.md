### 1. Final question
At a state-run culinary institute's annual Good Samaritan food-drive market, a new institute regulation barred certain hand-lettered messages on booth signs. Institute panels could fine, suspend, expel, or fire students, staff, and instructors who violated the regulation. ### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. The budget section does not merely set a neutral funding priority for the institute. It names Lydia and Barnabas, cuts off the only money available to pay them, and does so because of the legislature's judgment about their prior role in the controversial rule. Gold Key: a legislative act that singles out named people and imposes punishment without a judicial trial is a bill of attainder, and a targeted salary or employment cutoff can count as punishment. Silver Key: when two "No" answers survive, split them by reason; the better reason is named-person punishment without trial, not retroactivity alone.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because full faith and credit sounds like a command to honor official state legal acts. The breaker is that this clause concerns recognition of other states' laws, records, and judgments; it does not require a court to enforce an unconstitutional budget section.

B is attractive because it notices that Lydia and Barnabas were targeted for conduct that happened before the budget section was enacted, so it reaches the right result with a familiar retroactivity smell. The breaker is the Gold Key distinction: ex post facto reasoning is about retroactive criminal punishment, while this stem's clean lane is legislative punishment of named people without trial.

D is attractive because state control over appropriations sounds powerful when the disputed act is a budget section. The breaker is that the Eleventh Amendment is a sovereign-immunity and federal-court jurisdiction doctrine; it does not give a state legislature permission to punish named public employees without trial.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Constitutional Law, Individual Rights, Bills of attainder, and the verified outline code is `44040601`. Article I, Section 10, Clause 1 bars states from passing bills of attainder. Constitution Annotated identifies a bill of attainder as legislation that imposes punishment on a specific person or group without a judicial trial, and it treats the state and federal prohibitions as having the same scope. See U.S. Const. art. I, Section 10, cl. 1, https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/artI-S10-C1-4/ALDE_00001100/; Constitution Annotated, Historical Background on Bills of Attainder, https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/artI-S9-C3-1/ALDE_00013186/.

The salary-cutoff point is directly supported by United States v. Lovett, 328 U.S. 303 (1946), where the Court invalidated an appropriations provision that barred payment of compensation to named government employees. Lovett explained that legislative acts targeting named people or ascertainable groups for punishment without judicial trial are bills of attainder, and that an appropriations cutoff can accomplish that punishment. See https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/328/303/. The official key remains correct: the budget section names Lydia and Barnabas, deprives them of their government salary and practical employment, and supplies no judicial trial.

The main wrong-answer contrast also checks out. State ex post facto doctrine is limited to retroactive criminal or penal laws; retroactivity alone is not the right reason here. See Constitution Annotated, State Ex Post Facto Laws, https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/artI-S10-C1-5/ALDE_00001101/. The Eleventh Amendment and the Full Faith and Credit Clause do not alter the bill-of-attainder result.
