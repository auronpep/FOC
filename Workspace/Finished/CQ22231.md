7. **Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)**

Choice C is the prosecutor's best response because the extra fuel cards and matching licenses support a noncharacter inference that the charged swipe was part of a larger coordinated fraud plan. The prosecutor is not forced to argue, "she had lots of cards, so she is a bad person." Instead, the prosecutor can argue, "she had a ready-made system for repeated fraudulent use." Gold Key: other-acts evidence may be used for plan when it does real work beyond propensity. Silver Key: when the call asks for the prosecutor's best admissibility theory, keep the answer that ties the other acts to the charged offense itself, not just to general criminality. The court would still have to weigh unfair prejudice, but this is the only listed argument that points to a legitimate 404(b)-style use.

8. **Full wrong-answer explanations**

- **A.** This choice feels strong because theft sounds incriminating and the stash is large. The breaker is that stealing the cards is not the fact of consequence the prosecutor needs to prove in this charge. Gold Key: Rule 404(b) blocks the move from "she probably stole cards" to "so she probably used this one fraudulently," because that is still a character-and-propensity chain wearing different clothes.
- **B.** This choice sounds lawyerly because "admission by conduct" is a real evidence idea. The breaker is that mere possession of many cards and IDs does not show the defendant believed herself guilty of the charged swipe in the way flight, concealment, or bribery can. It is the wrong doctrine for these facts.
- **D.** This choice attracts students who feel the stash is so suspicious that silence should count against the defendant. The breaker is that a criminal defendant cannot be forced to take the stand or explain incriminating possession. The prosecutor's best argument must be about admissibility of the evidence, not compulsory self-explanation.

9. **Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer**

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item: other-acts evidence is inadmissible to prove propensity, but it may be admissible for a noncharacter purpose such as plan, subject to Rule 403 balancing. Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b)(1) bars other-act evidence used to prove character-conformity, while Rule 404(b)(2) permits the same evidence for another purpose, including "plan." Rule 403 then allows exclusion only if unfair prejudice substantially outweighs probative value. Here, the officer's testimony about dozens of extra fuel cards and matching licenses gives the prosecutor a noncharacter theory that the charged use was one step in a broader fraud setup. That makes choice C the best argument in opposition to exclusion. Choice A remains wrong because even if the stash suggests theft, that inference does not by itself answer the charged fraudulent-use issue except through forbidden propensity reasoning. Choice D remains wrong because the Fifth Amendment protects the defendant from being compelled to be a witness against herself. Choice B remains wrong because possession of the stash is not the kind of conduct that shows consciousness of guilt of the charged act. Authority floor: Fed. R.
