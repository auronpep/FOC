The next day, an undercover officer offers the recovered bicycle to Lydia, saying, "This was stolen — I can let you have it cheap." Lydia buys it, believing it was stolen. No statute in the jurisdiction treats recovered property used in a sting as still stolen.

Is Lydia guilty of common-law receiving stolen property?

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

At common law, receiving stolen property requires that the property actually be stolen at the time the defendant receives it. Once police recovered the bicycle and took it into secure custody, it ceased being stolen property under the common-law rule. The facts state no statute extends liability to property used in a sting operation after recovery. Without stolen property, the completed offense fails — regardless of what Lydia believed or intended.

**Gold Key:** Property recovered by police is no longer legally stolen. Receiving stolen property requires stolen property at receipt. No "stolen" property = no offense (at common law, absent a statute).

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice B (originally A, DOMINANT TRAP):** This is the trap for students who see "she believed it was stolen" and stop. Belief is indeed required for the mental-state element of receiving, and Lydia satisfies it — she genuinely thought the bicycle was stolen. The attraction: the choice isolates a TRUE element and the student assumes that's enough. The breaker: the offense also requires that the property ACTUALLY BE STOLEN when received. Belief alone doesn't satisfy the actus reus element. The correct answer (D) cites this **Gold Key**: police recovery ended the stolen status.

**Choice A (originally B):** This choice invents a rule — that police can "always preserve stolen status by saying the item is stolen." That's a fabricated preserved-stolen-status doctrine. The stem explicitly says "no statute treats recovered property used in a sting as still stolen," which directly undercuts this. The cognitive move: police authority + sting context = liability preserved. The breaker: police words don't create legal stolen status. The facts specifically negate the mechanism this choice relies on.

**Choice C (originally D):** This choice reaches the right outcome (not guilty) but for the wrong reason — it says "because Lydia did not personally steal the bicycle." That's a true statement, but it's irrelevant. Receiving stolen property is a separate offense from theft. You don't need to be the thief to be a receiver. The cognitive move: "if she didn't steal it, she can't be guilty." The breaker: the offense is about receiving, not about who committed the original theft. This reasoning proves the wrong element.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule:** At common law, the offense of receiving stolen property (sometimes called "receiving stolen goods") requires: (1) property that was stolen, (2) the property was still stolen at the time of receipt, (3) the defendant received or concealed the property, and (4) the defendant knew it was stolen, with (5) intent to deprive the owner. When law enforcement recovers stolen property, the property ceases to be "stolen" because it has been returned to lawful custody. A defendant who receives such property from an undercover officer in a sting operation does not commit the common-law offense because the jurisdictional element — stolen property — is absent. Some modern statutes extend liability to property that "is represented" as stolen, but the facts here explicitly negate any such statute.

**Common-law authority:** *See* 30 C.J.S. *Receiving Stolen Property* § 2 (property must be stolen at time of receipt); *see also* Rollin M. Perkins & Ronald N. Boyce, *Criminal Law* (3d ed. 1982), at common law the property must be stolen and remain stolen at the time of the receiving offense; modern statutes may vary and sometimes extend to property used in stings, but the question specifies no such statute.

**No doctrinal-currency hotlist concern:** This is common-law property crime doctrine, not one of the enumerated hotlist areas. No constitutional overlay requiring updated framing.

**Application:** Police recovered the bicycle and placed it in secure custody. No statute extends liability. The property was therefore not "stolen" when Lydia received it. The credited outcome (not guilty) is unchanged. Legal issue, tested rule, and outcome are all preserved.

**Verdict:** PASS.

## Wrong-Answer Recovery Paths

### Recovery from A
**What the student thought:** Police said it was stolen, so it must still be stolen.  
**The miss:** Assumed police authority to maintain stolen status verbally.  
**Recovery:** Re-read the "no statute" sentence. Police words don't create legal status. The common-law rule controls.

### Recovery from B
**What the student thought:** Lydia believed it was stolen, so she's guilty.  
**The miss:** Stopped at the mental-state element and ignored the property-status element.  
**Recovery:** Receiving stolen property has TWO elements: (1) stolen property + (2) knowing receipt. Check BOTH. Here, element (1) fails because police recovered the property first.

### Recovery from C
**What the student thought:** She didn't steal it herself, so she can't be guilty.  
**The miss:** Confused receiving stolen property with theft.  
**Recovery:** Receiving is a separate offense. The question is whether she received stolen property, not whether she stole it.
