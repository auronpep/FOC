## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is the best answer. The landlord may owe reasonable care for shared areas that tenants and their guests are expected to use, but negligence still needs a breach. Here, the decisive fact is that the landlord had no notice of the macaw's dangerous biting habit and no other reason to know about the risk.

**Gold Key:** a landlord is not automatically liable for a tenant's dangerous pet. Liability turns on ordinary reasonable care, including notice of the dangerous propensity and enough control to do something about it.

**Silver Key:** split duty from breach. A duty to lawful guests in common areas does not equal liability when the risk was not known or reasonably discoverable.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. No, because a landlord owes no duty to a tenant's guests.**
This answer sells a clean no-liability shortcut. It reaches the right bottom line but breaks the reason. **Silver Key:** do not use "no duty" when the injury happens in a shared area the landlord keeps open for tenants and their guests. The better reason is no notice, not no duty.

**C. Yes, because in these circumstances a landlord is strictly liable for injuries caused by dangerous animals in common areas.**
This answer borrows the strict-liability animal rule and hands it to the wrong defendant. **Gold Key:** strict liability for an abnormally dangerous domestic animal belongs to the animal's possessor or keeper who knows or has reason to know of the dangerous propensity. The landlord did not possess the macaw and had no notice of the biting habit.

**D. Yes, because a landlord's duty to protect tenants' guests from dangerous conditions in shared areas is nondelegable.**
This is the dominant trap. It spots the strongest plaintiff-side fact: the injury happened in a shared area under the landlord's control. The breaker is that the answer stops at duty. A reasonable-care duty still needs notice or a discoverable risk before the failure to act becomes negligent.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

**Tested rule.** A landlord who controls common areas owes ordinary reasonable care to tenants and lawful tenant guests, but the landlord is not an insurer against every risk created by a tenant's animal. When the alleged risk is a tenant's dangerous domestic animal, the landlord's liability depends on notice of the animal's dangerous propensity and enough control to take reasonable protective action. Without notice or reason to know, the landlord has not breached the reasonable-care duty.

**Authority.** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 360 states the common-area reasonable-care principle for parts of leased land retained in the lessor's control. A 2025 Colorado Court of Appeals decision collected that rule for tenant guests in common areas and quoted § 360's reasonable-care formulation. See *Willis v. Twin Shores Master Owners Ass'n*, 2025 COA 37, https://www.coloradojudicial.gov/system/files/opinions-2025-04/24CA0369-PD.pdf. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 509 states strict liability for a possessor of a domestic animal with known abnormal dangerous propensities; *Edmonds v. Cailloux* quotes the section and treats knowledge/reason to know as an element. See https://law.justia.com/cases/texas/fourth-court-of-appeals/2006/18992.html. *Uccello v. Laudenslayer* confirms the landlord-specific animal rule: duty arises only when the landlord has actual knowledge of the dangerous animal and the right to remove it; the court emphasized that a duty may not be imposed without proof of knowledge of the dangerous propensity. See https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/44/504.html.

**Credited outcome.** The source item and the variation both test the same legal issue: whether the landlord is liable when a lawful guest is injured in a common area by another tenant's dangerous animal and the landlord lacked notice of the animal's dangerous propensity. The credited outcome is unchanged. The guest does not prevail against the landlord because no notice means no negligent failure to protect.
