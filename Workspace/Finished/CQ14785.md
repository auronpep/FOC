### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

A is correct. Lydia's statement was made moments after the shove, from the curb, to a private listener. That timing makes the statement most naturally fit the excited-utterance exception: it relates to a startling event and is made while the stress of that event is still operating. Gold Key: the excited-utterance path turns on stress and timing, not on the listener having an official or professional title. Silver Key: when every option is a listener role, ignore the title first and ask what purpose or timing fact creates the exception.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

B. The pastoral counseling setting feels safe and serious, and that makes the answer sound trustworthy. The breaker is that counseling, by itself, is not a hearsay exception for proving who committed the assault; if anything, the pastoral setting raises a privilege concern rather than an admissibility path.

C. The police report feels official, and a student may want the law-enforcement route to be cleaner than a family call. The breaker is the timing: Lydia first talked through the incident with Naomi and then made a report on instruction, which weakens the stress-of-event basis that makes an excited utterance work. Silver Key: the title "police officer" does not beat the timing fact.

D. The treatment setting activates a real hearsay exception, so this is the strongest trap. The breaker is that ordinary medical-treatment statements cover symptoms, history, and general cause when reasonably pertinent to care; naming Daniel as the person who shoved Lydia is fault identity, not the treatment-purpose fact. Gold Key: treatment purpose can carry "how I was hurt," but usually not "who is legally at fault."

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The credited answer is legally sound under the Federal Rules of Evidence. A statement is hearsay when offered to prove the truth of what it asserts. Fed. R. Evid. 801(c). Hearsay is not excluded when it is an excited utterance: a statement relating to a startling event or condition, made while the declarant is still under the stress caused by that event. Fed. R. Evid. 803(2). Lydia's statement to Naomi identifies Daniel as the assailant moments after the shove and relates directly to the startling event. That preserves the tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome.

The doctor trap does not defeat the key. Fed. R. Evid. 803(4) covers statements made for, and reasonably pertinent to, medical diagnosis or treatment, including symptoms, medical history, inception, or general cause. The Advisory Committee note explains that fault statements ordinarily do not qualify: a patient saying she was struck may matter to treatment, but the legal fault identity usually does not. A routine statement identifying Daniel as the assailant is therefore not the best route to admission under the medical-treatment exception.

Research sources used: Fed. R. Evid. 801(c), https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_801; Fed. R. Evid. 803(2) and 803(4), https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_803.
