## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation
C is correct. Stephen received permission to use Lydia's walkway, so he had a license. Gold Key: a land-use license is permission, not a land interest; it can be oral and is generally revocable. Gold Key: estoppel requires the user to substantially spend money, property, or labor in reliance on the permission. Silver Key: when the stem tells you who maintained the route, track the spender. Lydia alone maintained the walkway and gate. Stephen used the shortcut, but no fact shows he paid, built, repaired, or otherwise changed position in reliance. Mary therefore may revoke the license.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations
A is attractive because daily use plus a new owner feels like a fairness problem. The trap sells the estoppel exception before its required reliance fact appears. Gold Key: estoppel needs substantial money, property, or labor by the user. Silver Key: the stem says Lydia alone maintained the walkway, so Stephen never gets the reliance fact that would lock the license.

B is attractive because open and notorious use is real language from prescriptive-easement doctrine. The breaker is the permission fact. Stephen was not using the walkway adversely; he was using it because Lydia allowed it. The short 30-month period also does not carry a prescriptive claim.

D is attractive because land rights often need writings, and "oral" sounds like a Statute of Frauds problem. The breaker is the Gold Key: a license is permission to use land, not a conveyance of a land interest. It may be oral. Mary wins because the license is revocable, not because the permission was invalid.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification AND Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer
The tested issue remains Real Property, Rights in Land, Licenses, and the verified outline code is `81020300`. A license is permission to use another person's land without receiving a permanent property interest. An ordinary license may be oral and is generally revocable. `Kitchen v. Kitchen`, 465 Mich. 654 (2002), is repeatedly cited for the rule that an oral license is revocable at will and that a license generally ends on transfer of title; see the summary and quoted rule in `Astemborski v. Manetta`, https://law.justia.com/cases/michigan/court-of-appeals-unpublished/2022/352066.html. The estoppel exception is a reliance exception: Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes section 2.10 recognizes a servitude by estoppel when the owner permitted use and the user substantially changed position in reasonable reliance. `Anastacio v. Palau Public Utilities Corp.`, 18 ROP 22 (Tr. Div. 2011), quotes and applies Restatement sections 1.2 comment g and 2.10, emphasizing substantial expenditures or permanent improvements by the user, https://palaucourts.gov.pw/doc/ROP_Reporters/18ROP/18%20ROP%2022.pdf. Georgia Code section 44-9-4 illustrates the same exam distinction: a parol license is revocable unless the licensee incurs expense under it, in which case it may become an easement running with the land, https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-44/chapter-9/article-1/section-44-9-4/. Here Stephen had permission, not adversity; Lydia alone maintained the route; and Stephen made no reliance expenditure or improvement. Research did not contradict the supplied official key. The credited outcome is unchanged: the grantee prevails because the neighbor held only a revocable license. Authority floor satisfied; attorney_review: false.
