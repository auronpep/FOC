Gold Key: unilateral conspiracy asks whether the defendant believed she was joining an agreement to commit the crime; the other person's hidden lack of criminal intent does not erase the defendant's culpable agreement. ### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule is unilateral conspiracy under the Model Penal Code approach. The verified outline placement is `71040200` (Inchoate Offenses > Conspiracy). The source row's legal issue, credited outcome, and trap structure are unchanged: the defendant is guilty because the jurisdiction uses the unilateral approach and the defendant agreed with criminal purpose even though the apparent partner was an undercover officer.

Authority floor: Model Penal Code section 5.03(1) frames conspiracy in terms of a person who, with the purpose of promoting or facilitating a crime, agrees with another person that they will engage in the criminal conduct. Courts and commentators describe the MPC as adopting a unilateral theory because it focuses on the actor's culpability rather than requiring a true meeting of guilty minds. In *United States v. Valigura*, the Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces described the MPC unilateral theory as allowing punishment of a person who believes he has agreed with another to commit a crime even though the other person has no criminal purpose. In *State v. Pacheco*, 125 Wash. 2d 150 (1994), the Washington Supreme Court rejected unilateral conspiracy for Washington statutes, but the opinion expressly identifies the competing MPC-style unilateral view and confirms why an undercover-officer fact changes the result only in bilateral jurisdictions.
