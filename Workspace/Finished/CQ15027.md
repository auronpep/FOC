# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

Emmaus Court is a private residential development where each family owns its own townhouse lot and also shares a pool, fitness room, and picnic pavilion. After several seasons of maintenance complaints, the community association's board adopted a new front-yard rule for all individually owned lots: owners and occupants may use only raised cedar planter boxes in the front courtyard area. The stated reason was to stop decorative stone fountains, which had become common in the development, because loose gravel from the fountains washed into the courtyard drains and required the board to hire extra maintenance workers after heavy rains.

Daniel, a board member who had voted against the rule, ordered two decorative stone fountains for his own front courtyard and told neighbors he still intended to install them. While bringing a meal to a neighbor, Esther, the board president, saw the fountains being delivered to Daniel's lot. The community's governing documents authorize the board to impose rules on structures and landscaping on individually owned property. If Daniel installs the fountains, may the board take action?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

An association is authorized to make reasonable structure and landscaping rules for individual lots, and an owner plans to violate a new rule. May the board use a reasonable sanction rather than direct self-help removal?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The board may suspend Daniel's privileges to use common recreational facilities. The governing documents authorize the board to make rules about structures and landscaping on individually owned property, and the fountain rule is tied to a maintenance problem affecting the community drains. That makes the rule a valid exercise of the board's private association power. When Daniel violates it, the board may use reasonable enforcement measures, including loss of access to shared recreational facilities.

**Gold Key:** A common-interest community board with authority to regulate individual lots may enforce reasonable rules with reasonable private sanctions, such as fines or loss of common-facility privileges; that enforcement power is not the same thing as permission to enter the owner's lot and remove the item itself.

**Silver Key:** When two answers say "yes," separate the board's power to enforce from the exact remedy chosen. The valid answer uses an association sanction; the trap answer uses direct self-help against the owner's property.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B:** Attractive because a front-yard restriction feels like a property burden, and "taking without compensation" is a familiar land-use phrase. The breaker is the actor. This is a private residential association enforcing private governing documents, not a government taking private property for public use. The Fifth Amendment frame does not answer this call.

**C:** Attractive because Daniel bought into the community before this exact fountain rule appeared, so the timing feels unfair. The breaker is the express rulemaking authority in the governing documents. Those documents already authorize the board to impose structure and landscaping rules on individual lots. A later reasonable rule adopted under that authority can bind existing owners.

**D:** Attractive because Daniel is about to violate a valid rule, so a direct cure sounds efficient and satisfying. This is the dominant trap. The breaker is remedial scope: the board may enforce with reasonable sanctions, but the stated power does not let it treat Daniel's lot as board property and remove the fountains itself. **Gold Key:** association enforcement power means reasonable compliance tools; it does not automatically include direct entry and removal.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is unchanged: in a common-interest community, a private association may adopt and enforce reasonable rules governing individually owned property when the governing documents authorize that rulemaking power. For lawyer confirmation, Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes sections 6.7 and 6.9 govern an association's authority to adopt rules restricting individually owned property, including design-control restrictions, when the authority is grounded in the declaration or governing documents and the rule is reasonable. The American Law Institute's Moretto summary confirms that the Nevada Supreme Court adopted sections 6.7 and 6.9 for association authority over restrictions on individually owned property and treated express governing-document language as the kind of authorization section 6.9 requires. See https://www.ali.org/news/articles/institute-courts-supreme-court-nevada-adopts-sections-restatement-law-third-property.

The enforcement point is also unchanged. Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes section 6.8 authorizes a common-interest community, except as limited by statute or governing documents, to enforce governing documents and rules adopted under section 6.7 through reasonable procedures and sanctions, including fines, penalties, late fees, and withdrawal of privileges to use common recreational and social facilities. A public casebook/excerpt quoting section 6.8 states that withdrawal-of-privileges remedy in those terms. See https://law-journals-books.vlex.com/vid/3-17-notice-1036372831. This supports the credited answer: suspending Daniel's privileges to use common recreational facilities is a permissible private-association enforcement measure.

The direct-removal answer remains wrong. Section 6.8 supports reasonable enforcement procedures and sanctions; it does not convert the board into the owner of Daniel's lot or supply default authority for self-help entry and removal of the fountains from individually owned property. The post-purchase-rule answer remains wrong because the governing documents already authorize rules on structures and landscaping. The Takings Clause answer remains wrong because the Fifth Amendment constrains government takings, not purely private association action. The Constitution Annotated overview frames the Takings Clause as barring the Government from taking private property for public use without just compensation. See https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/amdt5-9-1/ALDE_00013280/.

The legal issue, tested rule, credited answer substance, and trap structure are preserved from Q15027. The source key says the board may withdraw common recreational privileges. The transformed key says the same thing through Daniel's fountain violation.
