### 5. Correct answer

**A. No, because Peter is unusually sensitive to noise during the day.**

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

A is correct.

**Gold Key:** Private nuisance requires a substantial and unreasonable interference judged against ordinary use and ordinary community expectations. A plaintiff cannot win on a nuisance claim where the harm exists only because of the plaintiff’s unusual sensitivity.

**Silver Key:** Do not stop at “interferes with quiet use and enjoyment.” In a nuisance array, look for the community-impact facts. Here, the hum is faint, the shop uses acoustic panels, it operates only in normal daytime hours, and ordinary neighbors do not notice it. Peter’s problem comes from his night-shift schedule, not from an ordinary-person-level nuisance.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B is incorrect.** The daytime schedule helps Lydia, but it is not the deciding reason. If the daytime noise were loud enough to substantially and unreasonably disrupt ordinary neighbors, daylight-only operation would not automatically defeat nuisance. B answers with the wrong element.

**C is incorrect.** Sound traveling past Lydia’s property line is not enough by itself. A nuisance claim needs a substantial and unreasonable interference. Boundary crossing proves only a starting fact.

**D is incorrect.** This is the dominant trap. It uses the right nuisance vocabulary, but it stops too early. **Gold Key:** “Quiet use and enjoyment” is only part of the private nuisance frame; the interference must also be substantial and unreasonable. **Silver Key:** when one choice says “interferes with quiet enjoyment,” check whether the stem shows ordinary-community impact or only a sensitive-plaintiff fact. Here, only Peter is affected.

### 8. Black-letter verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule and outcome are unchanged from the original. The original official explanation states that nuisance liability requires an invasion of use and enjoyment that is both substantial and unreasonable, and that the homeowner’s unusual daytime sensitivity defeats the claim. 

Lawyer confirmation: Restatement (Second) of Torts § 821D defines private nuisance as a nontrespassory invasion of another’s interest in private use and enjoyment of land, and the comments distinguish nuisance from trespass by requiring significant harm and unreasonableness for private nuisance liability. ([LexisNexis][1]) Layton v. Yankee Caithness Joint Venture states that an actionable private nuisance must substantially and unreasonably interfere with another’s use and enjoyment, and specifically applies Restatement § 821F to reject recovery for an exceptionally sensitive plaintiff where the harm would not be suffered by a normal person in the community. ([Justia Law][2]) Cornell Wex states the same exam-relevant rule: private nuisance requires substantial and unreasonable interference, and a court will not find private nuisance where harm comes from the plaintiff’s unique attributes rather than the defendant’s conduct. ([Legal Information Institute][3])
