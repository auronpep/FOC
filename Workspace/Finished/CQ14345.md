## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The board exercises executive power — it issues rules with the force of law regulating an entire industry. Under the Appointments Clause of Article II, Section 2, all "officers of the United States" exercising executive authority must be appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate, unless Congress has vested the appointment of "inferior officers" in the President alone, the courts, or heads of departments. Here, two of the five board members are designated by statute to be the sitting chief legal counsel of congressional committees. They are neither appointed by the President, confirmed by the Senate, nor appointed by a court or department head. Congress may appoint its own internal legislative staff, but it may not appoint officers who wield executive power. *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976). The statute is therefore unconstitutional.

**Gold Key:** The Appointments Clause gives the President — not Congress — the power to appoint officers of the United States who exercise executive authority. Congress can create the office; it cannot fill it (except for its own internal legislative staff).

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is attractive** because the Necessary and Proper Clause is a genuine source of congressional power, and students may reason that if Congress can create a board under the Commerce Clause, it can also decide how to staff it. **The breaker:** the Necessary and Proper Clause authorizes Congress to enact laws to carry out powers granted to *any branch*, but it cannot be used to override an express constitutional limitation — here, the Appointments Clause's specific assignment of the appointment power to the President. A clause that is "necessary and proper" must still comport with the rest of the Constitution. **Gold Key:** the Necessary and Proper Clause does not let Congress do what another constitutional provision expressly forbids.

**B is attractive** because students may recall that Congress has broad spending and regulatory powers and may assume that a "substantial nexus" test validates the appointment scheme. **The breaker:** the "substantial nexus" language relates to Congress's spending power and state taxation of interstate commerce — it has nothing to do with the appointment of executive officers. The general welfare clause does not give Congress authority that the Appointments Clause specifically reserves to the President. This answer invokes the wrong doctrinal framework entirely.

**D is attractive** (dominant trap) because it reaches the correct outcome (unconstitutional) and sounds plausible — students may think that quasi-legislative rulemaking power means Congress should do the appointing. **The breaker:** the reason is backwards. Congress may *create* an agency with quasi-legislative (rulemaking) power, but it may not *appoint* the officers who run it. The Appointments Clause requires executive officers to be appointed by the President with Senate confirmation. Congress appointing its own members to an executive body is precisely what the Appointments Clause forbids. **Gold Key:** Congress creates agencies; the President appoints the officers who run them.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule — Appointments Clause, U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 2:**

> "[The President] shall nominate, and by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate, shall appoint Ambassadors, other public Ministers and Consuls, Judges of the Supreme Court, and all other Officers of the United States, whose Appointments are not herein otherwise provided for, and which shall be established by Law: but the Congress may by Law vest the Appointment of such inferior Officers, as they think proper, in the President alone, in the Courts of Law, or in the Heads of Departments."

**Key authority:** *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976). The Supreme Court held that the appointment of FEC members by Congress violated the Appointments Clause because Congress may appoint its own legislative officers but may not appoint officers of the United States who exercise executive or administrative power. The Court distinguished between officers who carry out internal legislative functions (permissible for Congress to appoint) and officers who exercise enforcement, rulemaking, or other executive powers (must be appointed per Article II).

**Application to this item:** The board here issues rules with the force of law — a quintessentially executive/administrative function. Two members are designated by Congress from among sitting congressional committee counsel, without Presidential appointment or Senate confirmation. Under *Buckley*, these are "officers of the United States" exercising executive power and must be appointed in compliance with Article II. The statute violates the Appointments Clause.

**Verified:** The tested rule (Appointments Clause), the legal issue (whether Congress may appoint executive board members), and the credited outcome (unconstitutional) are all unchanged from the original. The official key is confirmed.

## ### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

## Program Frame

This item tests the Appointments Clause of Article II, Section 2 — specifically, whether Congress may appoint members of a federal board that exercises executive power. The item is a C3_SOLVE: every distractor can be eliminated using visible structural moves without outside legal doctrine. The deciding move is a CLASH between two choices that both say "unconstitutional" — the student must identify the correct *reason*.

## Student Access Test

A smart student who has completed the C3 lessons on Separation of Powers and the Appointments Clause tiny anchor should be able to:
1. Recognize that a board with rulemaking power exercises executive power
2. Recognize that the two congressional-designee members are not appointed per Article II
3. Eliminate the two constitutional choices (wrong doctrine)
4. Choose between the two unconstitutional choices by identifying the correct reason

All four choices are student-accessible. No legal leak.

## Question Data

**Stem:** A federal statute creates a five-member National Lending Standards Board with broad regulatory powers over consumer credit, payday lending, and mortgage brokering, including rulemaking with force of law. Three members are appointed by the President with Senate confirmation (seven-year terms, removable only for good cause). Two members are designated by statute as the sitting chief legal counsel of the Senate and House Committees on Oversight, serving ex officio. A payday lending company subject to the board's rules seeks a declaratory judgment that the statute is unconstitutional.

**Call:** The court should rule that the statute is…

**Choices:**
- **A:** constitutional — Necessary and Proper Clause authorizes Congress to determine appointment means
- **B:** constitutional — substantial nexus between general welfare power and appointment means
- **C:** unconstitutional — all members of federal boards exercising executive powers must be appointed per the Appointments Clause
- **D:** unconstitutional — all members of federal boards with quasi-legislative powers must be appointed by Congress

## Distilled Core Question

A federal statute creates a five-member board with executive rulemaking power. Three members are appointed by the President with Senate confirmation; two are designated by Congress from sitting congressional committee counsel. Is the statute constitutional?

## Call and Prediction

**Call:** "The court should rule that the statute… is" — a constitutional validity call. The student must determine whether the appointment scheme is constitutional.

**Prediction:** The statute is unconstitutional because the two congressional-designee members are not appointed in compliance with the Appointments Clause. The answer is **C**.

## Trigger Facts

1. **Board has rulemaking power with force of law** → This is executive power. The Appointments Clause is triggered.
2. **Two members designated by Congress from sitting committee counsel** → These members are not appointed by the President, not confirmed by the Senate, and not appointed by a court or department head.
3. **Three members properly appointed** → The defect is limited to the two congressional-designee seats; the question is whether this partial defect invalidates the statute.
4. **No challenger/standing clutter** → The original posture is preserved. No taxpayer standing or other extraneous doctrine.

## Governing C3 Lane

**CUT → CLASH → CALL**

- **CUT:** Eliminate A and B (both constitutional — wrong on the law)
- **CLASH:** Between C and D (both unconstitutional — fight on the *reason*)
- **CALL:** C wins because the Appointments Clause, not "Congress must appoint quasi-legislative boards," is the correct reason

## Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

### Choice A

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The Necessary and Proper Clause is a real power, but it cannot override an express constitutional limitation. Here, the Appointments Clause specifically assigns the appointment power to the President. A clause that is "necessary and proper" must still comport with the rest of the Constitution.
2. **Student label:** Necessary and Proper Clause does not override the Appointments Clause.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Constitutional, because the board members are inferior officers whose appointment Congress validly vested in the heads of departments under the Appointments Clause." (This would require the statute to actually do that, which it doesn't.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18; art. II, § 2, cl. 2. *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976).

### Choice B

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** "Substantial nexus" and "general welfare" are doctrines about state taxation and congressional spending — they have nothing to do with appointing executive officers. This answer invokes the wrong area of constitutional law entirely.
2. **Student label:** General welfare / substantial nexus is the wrong doctrinal framework.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Constitutional, because Congress's spending power for the general welfare authorizes it to structure the appointment of board members as it sees fit." (This would require the spending power to apply, which it doesn't in this context.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 1. *United States v. Butler*, 297 U.S. 1 (1936). Not applicable to appointments.

### Choice C ✓

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The board exercises executive power (rulemaking with force of law). Two members are designated by Congress, not appointed by the President or confirmed by the Senate. Under the Appointments Clause, executive officers must be appointed by the President with Senate confirmation (or, for inferior officers, by the President alone, courts, or department heads). This statute violates that requirement.
2. **Student label:** Correct — Appointments Clause requires executive officers to be appointed by the President.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true and responsive version.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 2. *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976).

### Choice D

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice reaches the correct outcome (unconstitutional) but for the wrong reason. It says Congress *must* appoint members of quasi-legislative boards — that's backwards. Congress creates agencies; the President appoints the officers who run them. The Appointments Clause gives this power to the President, not Congress.
2. **Student label:** Correct outcome, wrong reason — Congress does NOT appoint executive officers.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Unconstitutional, because Congress may not appoint officers of the United States who exercise executive power." (This is essentially what Choice C says.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 2. *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976).

## Residual Answer

After CUTting A and B, and CLASHing C vs. D on the *reason*, the residual is **C** — the only choice that is both true and responsive.

## Remediation Card

**Title:** Who Appoints Whom? The Appointments Clause in One Card
**Signal:** Congress creates an agency and also picks some of its members
**Student move:** Stop — Congress can create offices but cannot appoint the officers who run them (unless they're internal legislative staff). The President appoints principal officers with Senate confirmation.
