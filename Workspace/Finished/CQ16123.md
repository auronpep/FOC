# CQ16123

## 1. Final Question

Peter put nearly all of his rent money into an informal cash pool tied to the final round of a televised gospel-trivia tournament. If his pick lost, he expected to lose his apartment. He watched the final round at a cafe that stayed open late for the event. During the close finish, Peter became so anxious that he drank several strong ciders and became drunk.

Peter's pick won at the last moment. As Peter left the cafe, he saw two large men near the doorway who helped run the cash pool. Fearing that the men were angry about his win and were going to hurt him, Peter got into his car and drove away. A police officer saw Peter weaving across lanes and tried to pull him over. A reasonable sober driver would have recognized the car as a police car, but Peter thought the cash-pool helpers were chasing him.

Peter made a sudden turn onto a busy side street. Naomi was crossing the street while looking down at her phone and without looking up. Peter hit Naomi with the car, and Naomi died immediately. Peter was charged with involuntary manslaughter.

Peter should be found

## 2. Distilled Core Question

Is an intoxicated driver guilty of involuntary manslaughter when his drunk, reckless driving causes a pedestrian's death even though he did not intend to kill and the pedestrian was careless?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Peter voluntarily became drunk, drove erratically, misread a police car as a threat because of that intoxication, turned suddenly onto a busy side street, and killed Naomi. Gold Key: involuntary manslaughter does not require an intent to kill; reckless or criminally negligent conduct that causes death is enough. Silver Key: keep the focus on the defendant's risk-creating conduct, not on the victim's careless conduct or the defendant's subjective panic.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the dominant genuine-fear trap. Peter's panic feels human, and the stem says he honestly believed he was being chased. The breaker is that the belief was produced by self-induced intoxication and does not undo the reckless drunk driving that caused a death.

B sells the victim-blame trap. Naomi's phone use and failure to look made the collision feel partly her fault. The breaker is that a victim's carelessness is not a complete defense when the defendant's criminally reckless conduct proximately causes death.

D sells the no-intent trap. Peter did not mean to kill Naomi, and that fact matters for murder. The breaker is that involuntary manslaughter is built for unintentional killings caused by reckless or criminally negligent conduct.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: a defendant who voluntarily becomes intoxicated, drives recklessly, and thereby causes a death can be guilty of involuntary manslaughter even though the death was accidental. Involuntary manslaughter covers unlawful, unintentional killings caused by recklessness or criminal negligence. Drunk driving that creates a serious risk of death can supply that culpability, and the victim's ordinary carelessness does not by itself bar criminal liability.

Model Penal Code section 210.1 treats a homicide as criminal when a person purposely, knowingly, recklessly, or negligently causes another human being's death, and section 210.3 classifies reckless criminal homicide as manslaughter; section 210.4 separately recognizes negligent homicide. Model Penal Code section 2.08 states the intoxication rule: when recklessness is enough, a risk the actor would have recognized sober cannot be avoided merely because self-induced intoxication made the actor unaware of it. Wisconsin Criminal Jury Instruction 926 and Wis. Stat. section 939.14 state the common criminal-law point that a victim's contributory negligence is not itself a defense to a crime. Modern summaries of involuntary manslaughter likewise identify unintentional killing by negligence or recklessness and note that lack of intent is not a defense to that charge. Sources checked: https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/3185-tanaka-criminal-law-casebook/resources/9.1.2-mpc-article-210-criminal-homicide/, https://www.criminallawweb.net/web1/mpc/PART1/snippets/excuse.htm, https://wilawlibrary.gov/jury/files/criminal/0926.pdf, and https://www.justia.com/criminal/offenses/homicide/involuntary-manslaughter/.

Peter's intoxication was a significant cause of his dangerous driving and mistaken belief. Naomi's carelessness may be a fact in the causal story, but it does not make Peter's reckless drunk driving noncriminal. The correct verdict is guilty.
