## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The court should decide for Barnabas (A). The covenant of warranty in the deed only obligates the grantor to defend the grantee or reimburse reasonable defense costs when a third party asserts a lawful claim of superior title that existed at the time of conveyance or for which the grantor bears responsibility. Here, Lydia successfully defended the quiet title action and proved the city man's claim had no legal basis. Because Barnabas conveyed good title and there was no valid claim requiring defense, the covenant was never breached. Lydia's expenses in defeating a meritless claim are not recoverable from Barnabas under the warranty.

**Silver Key (call focus):** When the stem states the grantee "successfully defended" the third-party action, that fact itself signals the claim was not lawful. The covenant does not require the grantor to underwrite defense of every lawsuit that happens to name the parcel — only lawful ones. Read the outcome fact against the call before assuming the presence of the covenant words creates a payment obligation.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B (Lydia, because in effect it was Barnabas's prior ownership that the city man was challenging):** Attractive because the city man's claim did trace back to Barnabas's family line, so it feels like "Barnabas's problem." The breaker is that the covenant of warranty does not turn on whose prior ownership is mentioned in the third-party pleading; it turns on whether the claim itself is lawful and superior. A meritless claim, even one that references the grantor's chain, does not breach the covenant when the grantee defeats it.

**C (Lydia, because Barnabas's deed to her included the covenant of warranty):** Attractive because the deed literally contained the covenant language, and students often treat any post-conveyance title fight as automatically triggering reimbursement. This is the dominant trap. The breaker is the scope of the covenant itself: it covers only lawful claims. The stem's "successfully defended" fact shows this claim was not lawful. Presence of the covenant words without a valid underlying claim creates no reimbursement duty. (Silver Key: the successful-defense outcome fact defeats the assumption that "covenant mentioned = pay.")

**D (Barnabas, because the city claimant may elect which of Barnabas or Lydia to sue):** Attractive because it sounds like a procedural fairness or election rule ("the plaintiff picks the defendant"). The breaker is that this is not how warranty covenants operate. The covenant is a promise running from grantor to grantee; it is not a rule about which party a third-party title claimant may name in a quiet title action. Election of defendants has nothing to do with whether the grantor breached the warranty to this grantee.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested black-letter rule is the scope of the covenant of warranty (also called covenant of general warranty) in a warranty deed: the grantor promises to defend the grantee's possession and title against lawful claims of superior title and to compensate the grantee for losses (including reasonable defense costs) if such a lawful claim succeeds or forces the grantee to settle. The covenant is breached only when there is an actual failure of title — i.e., a valid, superior claim that clouds or divests the title the grantor purported to convey. A grantor has no duty under the covenant to defend the grantee against, or reimburse costs incurred in defeating, a meritless or invalid claim.

This rule is standard in American real property law. See, e.g., the formulation in 1-2 Powell on Real Property § 2.03[2] (covenant of warranty "protects the grantee against lawful claims of title"); standard hornbook statements that the warranty "obligates the grantor to defend only against rightful claims"; and the consistent distinction in deed treatises between lawful/valid claims (covered) and wrongful/meritless claims (not covered). The Restatement (Third) of Property (Servitudes) and state-specific deed covenant authorities align: breach requires a failure of title, not merely the filing of a suit. Title insurance policies use parallel "duty to defend" language limited to covered (i.e., potentially valid) claims for the same reason.

The official key (C in the source row, now A) is correct. The source explanation is accurate and matches the black-letter rule: "The landowner would only be required to defend or reimburse the teacher for lawful or reasonable claims for title. Because the landowner conveyed good title, and did not fail to defend against any valid claims that existed at the time of the conveyance, the landowner should prevail." The stem facts (Lydia "successfully defended" and proved the claim "had no legal basis") establish that the city man's action was not a lawful claim. Therefore no breach occurred, and Lydia cannot recover her defense expenses from Barnabas.
