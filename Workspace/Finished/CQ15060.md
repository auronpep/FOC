## 1. Final Question

Six years ago, Martha owned an empty brick storefront in a small arts district that she planned to turn into a Bible-calligraphy studio. Martha borrowed $32,000 from Ruth and gave Ruth a note for $32,000 due in six years, secured by a mortgage on the storefront. Ruth did not record the mortgage. The fair market value of the storefront was then $32,000.

Four years ago, Martha learned that Ruth still had not recorded the mortgage. In consideration of $68,000, Martha conveyed the storefront to Timothy. The fair market value of the storefront was then $68,000. Timothy knew nothing of Ruth's mortgage, and the building was empty.

Six weeks later, Ruth discovered the sale to Timothy, recorded her $32,000 mortgage, and notified Timothy that she held a $32,000 mortgage on the storefront.

Two years ago, Timothy needed funds for shelving and lighting. Although he told Cedar Trust Bank about the mortgage claimed by Ruth, the bank loaned him $21,000, and Timothy gave the bank a note for $21,000 due in two years, secured by a mortgage on the storefront. The bank promptly recorded the mortgage. At that time, the fair market value of the storefront was $96,000.

The recording act of the jurisdiction provides: "No conveyance or mortgage of real property shall be good against subsequent purchasers for value and without notice unless the same be recorded according to law."

Both notes are now due, and both Martha and Timothy have refused to pay. The storefront is now worth only $68,000. What are the rights of Ruth and Cedar Trust Bank in the storefront?

Owner gives an unrecorded mortgage, later sells to a buyer for value without notice, and the first mortgagee records only after the sale. The buyer then gives a recorded mortgage to a bank that knows of the first mortgagee's claim. Which mortgage is enforceable?

## 7. Right-answer explanation

B is correct. Timothy paid value and had no actual, record, or inquiry notice of Ruth's unrecorded mortgage when he bought the empty storefront. In a notice jurisdiction, that status makes Timothy a protected purchaser, so he took the storefront free of Ruth's prior unrecorded mortgage. Ruth's later recording and later notice to Timothy came too late. Once Timothy owned the storefront free of Ruth's lien, Cedar Trust Bank received an enforceable mortgage from Timothy even though the bank knew about Ruth's already-cut-off claim. Gold Key: in a notice jurisdiction, the protected buyer's no-notice status is measured when the buyer takes; later notice does not revive the prior unrecorded mortgage. Silver Key: first ask whether the middle buyer took free; if yes, the later bank-notice facts are downstream noise.

## 8. Wrong-answer explanations

A is incorrect. The bank's notice looks powerful because Timothy told the bank about Ruth's recorded claim before the bank made its loan. The breaker is timing: the dispositive no-notice moment was Timothy's purchase, not the bank's later loan, and there is no enforceable Ruth lien left for the bank's notice to promote.

C is incorrect. Ruth's earlier recording date looks like it should control once her mortgage appeared in the records before the bank's mortgage. The breaker is the statute type: this is a notice statute, not a race statute, and Timothy had already taken free of Ruth's unrecorded mortgage before Ruth recorded.

D is incorrect. This choice correctly sees that Timothy's innocent-purchaser status protects the bank's side of the dispute, so it feels like a sophisticated priority answer. The breaker is that it leaves Ruth with an enforceable lien, but Timothy's protected purchase cut Ruth's unrecorded mortgage off as against the storefront.

## 9. Black-letter verification with authority

The tested rule is unchanged: under a notice recording act, a subsequent purchaser for value who takes without notice of a prior unrecorded interest prevails over that prior interest. Notice is tested when the protected purchaser takes. Later notice to that purchaser, and the prior mortgagee's later recording, do not restore the prior unrecorded mortgage against that purchaser's title.

Authority floor: the item's quoted statute is the governing notice recording act. As a real-world analog, Fla. Stat. 695.01(1) provides that conveyances and mortgages are not effective against subsequent purchasers for value and without notice unless recorded. Florida's Supreme Court applied that notice-statute rule in Lesnoff v. Becker, 101 Fla. 716, 135 So. 146 (1931), protecting a purchaser without notice of a prior unrecorded mortgage. Research did not contradict the supplied official key. Sources reviewed: https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2023/695.01; https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914a619add7b049346d5558; https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/implied-and-constructive-notice-title-search-fallibility-and-the-rigidity-of-the-constructive-notice-doctrine/.
