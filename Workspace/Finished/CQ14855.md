## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** The court should allow the question. In a civil case, a party may invoke the privilege against self-incrimination if answering could tend to incriminate him, but the civil factfinder may still draw an adverse inference from that refusal. The same direction points even more strongly when the question asks whether the party destroyed relevant evidence: intentional destruction can support an inference that the evidence would have hurt the destroying party. **Gold Key:** civil Fifth silence and spoliation are not invisibility cloaks; they may protect against compelled incriminating testimony, but they do not block civil adverse-inference reasoning. **Silver Key:** keep the two yes answers apart by reason. The winning yes is "destruction supports an adverse inference," not "civil parties have no Fifth."

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because privilege feels like a hard stop: if Daniel can legitimately invoke the Fifth, the jury should not be allowed to hold that against him. The breaker is the civil setting. A civil party may claim the privilege, but the factfinder may draw an adverse inference from the party's refusal to answer when the question is probative. **Gold Key:** civil Fifth silence can carry an adverse inference even though the privilege itself may be valid.

**C.** This choice is attractive because incident reports prepared after an accident often sound like work product, and work product is a familiar discovery shield. The breaker is the overclaim. Work-product protection is qualified, must be asserted through the court, and does not authorize a party to destroy evidence and then use the protection as a no-question shield. The call is not asking whether the report itself must be shown to the jury; it asks whether Mary may ask whether Daniel destroyed it.

**D.** This is the dominant trap because it reaches the correct yes outcome by erasing the privilege. The breaker is that the privilege can be invoked in civil proceedings when the answer could tend to incriminate the witness. **Silver Key:** when a yes/no answer pair contains a correct outcome with a false reason, test the reason. The privilege exists; the adverse inference is the civil consequence.

The tested rule, issue, trap structure, and credited outcome are preserved. Lawyer-confirmation authority: the Fifth Amendment protects against compelled self-incrimination, and Constitution Annotated recognizes that the privilege may be claimed by a party or witness in a civil court proceeding when the answer could incriminate the witness.
