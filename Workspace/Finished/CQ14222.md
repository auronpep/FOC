### 6. Student black-letter-law answer explanation
D is correct. A clerk may enter a default judgment without a judge only when the claim is for a sum certain and the defaulted party has not appeared. Peter is not a no-show party in the sense Rule 55 uses for clerk-entered judgment: he appeared by filing the original diversity complaint. Martha's counterclaim may be for an exact amount, and Peter may have defaulted by not answering it, but those facts do not let the clerk enter judgment. Because Peter appeared, Martha must seek a default judgment from the court.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
D is correct because Rule 55 separates entry of default from entry of default judgment. Entry of default can follow a party's failure to plead or defend, but clerk-entered default judgment is narrower. Under Rule 55(b)(1), the clerk may enter judgment only on a claim for a sum certain or a sum that can be made certain by computation, and only against a defaulted party who has not appeared and is neither a minor nor an incompetent person. In all other cases, Rule 55(b)(2) sends the party to the court. Peter appeared when he filed the original complaint, so Martha's exact-dollar counterclaim still requires court action. Gold Key: exact amount plus default is not enough if the defaulted party has appeared. Silver Key: when the clerk-power answer depends on sum certain, check the appeared-party exception.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because Peter did not answer the counterclaim, and that fact explains why the clerk could enter default. The breaker is that entry of default and clerk-entered default judgment are different steps. Peter's failure to answer supports entry of default, but it does not erase his earlier appearance in the action.

B is attractive because Martha's counterclaim seeks an exact $6,500, which satisfies one clerk-judgment condition. The breaker is that sum certain is only one side of the Rule 55(b)(1) gate. Peter appeared by filing the original complaint, so the clerk cannot enter judgment even though the amount is exact.

C is attractive because it points to timing. The breaker is the four-week interval after service of the counterclaim. Rule 12(a)(1)(B) gives 21 days to answer a counterclaim, so Peter's time had expired.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Civil Procedure, Verdicts and Judgments, Defaults, and the verified outline code is `97100400`. Fed. R. Civ. P. 55(b)(1) provides the clerk-entered default-judgment rule: if the claim is for a sum certain or a sum that can be made certain by computation, the clerk may enter judgment for that amount and costs against a defaulted party who has not appeared and is neither a minor nor an incompetent person. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_55

Rule 55(b)(2) supplies the court route for all other cases. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_55 Fed. R. Civ. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_12

Those authorities confirm the official key. Martha's $6,500 counterclaim is a sum certain, and Peter's failure to answer after four weeks supports entry of default. But Peter appeared in the action by filing the original complaint. Because the defaulted party has appeared, the clerk may not enter the default judgment under Rule 55(b)(1); Martha must apply to the court under Rule 55(b)(2). The credited outcome is unchanged.
