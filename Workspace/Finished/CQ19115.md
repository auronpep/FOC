### 7. Full right-answer explanation
D is correct. The call asks which theory best supports accomplice liability, and D names the three classic forms of aid recognized by the common law and the Model Penal Code: verbal encouragement before and during the offense, lookout duty during the offense, and getaway driving after the offense. Ruth satisfies (1) intent to promote or facilitate the robbery — she agreed to help, gave words of encouragement, watched for trouble, and motored Paul away — and (2) an act of aid, counseling, or encouragement, three times over. Any one of the three, with the required intent, would suffice. Gold Key: mere presence at the scene is not enough, but verbal encouragement during the commission of the crime, standing watch as a lookout, and driving the getaway are textbook examples of accomplice conduct. Silver Key: when the call charges a specific category (here, accomplice) and one answer rejects the category and substitutes a different one (principal), that answer is automatically the wrong category — cut it on sight.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because driving the getaway sounds like the most consequential single act, and students want to reward the "biggest role" with the strongest legal label. The breaker is that principal status is defined by who performed the actus reus of the robbery — the force or threat plus the taking — and that was Paul, not Ruth. Gold Key: the principal/accomplice line tracks who carried out the offense, not whose contribution mattered most. Ruth's importance does not convert her role into a principal; she is properly charged as an accomplice.

B is attractive because Ruth was undeniably at the scene, knew what was happening, and stayed involved from start to finish, which feels like enough for a fact-finder to call her complicit. The breaker is that the common-law rule and MPC § 2.06(6) treat mere presence — even presence with knowledge of the crime — as insufficient without an act of aid, counseling, or encouragement, and an intent to facilitate. Silver Key: "present at the scene" is the classic MBE trap, so it is always the first thing to test against the call — if a choice says presence alone is enough, it is almost certainly the dominant trap. Here, Ruth's presence is real but the trap is the "automatically" framing: presence needs aid + intent to become complicity.

C is attractive because Ruth never stepped off the boat, never brandished a weapon, and never laid a hand on the clerk, so the student wants to give her the benefit of the doubt. The breaker is that the rule requires only an act of aid, counseling, or encouragement, with intent to facilitate — it does not require physical entry into the crime scene or personal contact with the victim. Gold Key: lookouts and getaway drivers have been liable as accomplices since the modern doctrine took shape, even though they were nowhere near the point of the force or the taking. Ruth's lookout and motor-away are textbook aid.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains accomplice liability, the deepest listed node in the supplied outline map is `72050200` (Parties to Crime > Accomplices), and the rule is unchanged from the source row. Under modern criminal law, a person is liable as an accomplice if, with intent to promote or facilitate the commission of an offense, she aids, counsels, encourages, agrees to aid, or attempts to aid another person in planning or committing the offense. See Model Penal Code § 2.06(3)(a). The actus reus may consist of physical assistance, logistical support, or words of encouragement; serving as a lookout, supplying a tool, and driving the getaway vehicle are paradigmatic examples. Aiding-and-abetting statutes and the common law treat all such helpers as punishable as principals for the offense they facilitated. The "mere presence" carve-out is recognized at common law and codified in MPC § 2.06(6) (a person is not an accomplice if his conduct is "inevitably incident" to commission, or — by the great weight of authority — if he is merely present at the scene). The principal/accomplice line, in turn, is fixed by who performed the actus reus of the offense, not by which contribution was most important. Modern statutes routinely charge accomplices as principals, but that charging convention does not change the substantive distinction. The official key therefore remains correct: Ruth's verbal encouragement, lookout role, and getaway motoring are textbook accomplice conduct, and her conduct satisfies every element of liability as charged.
