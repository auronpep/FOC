## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Lydia's out-of-court statement has been admitted through the witness, so Lydia is treated like a witness for credibility attacks. Gold Key: once a hearsay declarant's statement is in evidence, Rule 806 lets the opponent attack the declarant's credibility with evidence that would be admissible if the declarant had testified. Silver Key: classify the attack by purpose before applying a character or foundation rule. Peter is not offering the shove-and-jacket incident to prove Lydia is violent; he is using it to show that Lydia may have had a reason to shade her statement against Peter.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the dominant explain-or-deny trap. It sounds protective because impeachment often raises foundation concerns, but the breaker is the purpose and target of the proof: this is an attack on an admitted hearsay declarant's bias. Gold Key: the declarant's credibility may be attacked as if the declarant had testified, and the bias point does not become improper just because Lydia is not on the stand to explain it first.

B sells the character shortcut. The prior shove might make Lydia look aggressive, but Peter's impeachment theory is not "Lydia is violent, so she lies." Silver Key: ask what the fact is being used to prove; here it points to a reason Lydia might slant her statement against Peter.

D sells an overbroad specific-instance rule. Some specific-act character attacks are limited, but a specific incident can be used when it is relevant to bias. The incident is not offered as general bad character; it is offered as a concrete source of hostility toward Peter.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: when a hearsay statement has been admitted, the declarant's credibility may be attacked by evidence that would be admissible if the declarant had testified, including evidence showing bias or motive to slant the statement.

Federal Rule of Evidence 806 provides that after a hearsay statement is admitted, the declarant's credibility may be attacked, and then supported, by evidence admissible for those purposes if the declarant had testified. Rule 806 also removes the explain-or-deny obstacle for declarant impeachment based on inconsistent statement or conduct. United States v. Abel, 469 U.S. 45 (1984), confirms that bias impeachment is a recognized and relevant credibility attack, and that evidence showing a relationship or fact giving the witness a motive to slant testimony may be admissible for bias. Rule 608(b)'s character-for-truthfulness limits do not defeat evidence offered for bias rather than for general character. Sources checked: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_806, https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/469/45/, https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_608, and https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_613.

Because Lydia's statement about Peter's driving has been admitted, Peter may attack Lydia's credibility even though Lydia is not the witness currently on the stand. The shove-and-jacket incident is relevant because it tends to show possible hostility by Lydia toward Peter, which could explain a biased statement. The proper answer is that the question is proper because it tends to show possible bias.
