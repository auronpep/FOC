# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 0. Source Question (Original)

**Original QID:** 17007

A banking firm hired a computer-banking specialist to update its computer network for $15,000. The specialist then went to work installing hardware and writing special software for the firm. Soon thereafter, to pay off a debt that the specialist owed to a computer programmer, the specialist assigned to the computer programmer her rights to receive payment under the contract. After the work was completed, the firm refused to pay. The computer programmer instituted a claim against the firm for $15,000. Which one of the following additional facts or inferences, if it were the only one true, would be most likely to lead to a judgment for the firm?

- **A:** The programmer was not a specialist in the application of computer technology to the practice of banking.
- **B:** The contract between the specialist and the firm did not contain a clause permitting assignment.
- **C:** The contract between the specialist and the firm contained language that stated, "This contract may not be modified except by a writing signed by both parties hereto."
- **D:** The firm paid $15,000 to the specialist prior to being notified of the specialist's assignment to the programmer.

**Correct Answer:** D

**Correct Answer Explanation:** An obligor is discharged of liability to the obligee's assignee to the extent of any payments made to the original obligee prior to notice of the assignment.

**Wrong Answer Explanations:**
- A: Incorrect because the right to payment for work already delivered is assignable regardless of the assignee's ability to perform.
- B: Incorrect because all rights under a contract are assignable; silence implies permission to assign.
- C: Incorrect because an assignment is a transfer of rights, not a modification of performance obligations.

## PASS-1 CREATIVE TRANSFORM (Six Steps)

### Step 1: Identify the Black-Letter Rule Tested

**Rule:** When a contract right is assigned, the obligor (the party who owes performance) is discharged from liability to the assignee to the extent the obligor pays the original obligor (the assignor) *before receiving notice* of the assignment. After receiving notice, payment to the assignor no longer discharges the obligor.

**Core legal issue:** Timing of obligor's payment to assignor vs. notice of assignment — governs whether the obligor is discharged.

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**Gold Key: An obligor who pays the assignor in full before receiving any notice of the assignment is discharged from liability to the assignee to the extent of that payment — even though the assignment was valid and the assignee has a legitimate claim against the assignor.**

**C is correct.** An assignment transfers the assignor's right to receive payment from the obligor to the assignee. However, until the obligor receives notification of the assignment, the obligor may safely pay the original party (the assignor) and is discharged from any obligation to the assignee for amounts already paid. Here, Silas paid Paul the full $8,000 *before* learning that Paul had assigned the payment right to Stephanas. Because Silas lacked notice at the time of payment, Silas is discharged and Stephanas's claim fails. Stephanas's remedy is against Paul (the assignor), not Silas.

**Why the other choices fail:** See below.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations (Diagnostic Register)

### Choice A (original C) — ISSUE_SENSE / misfit — NOT_RESPONSIVE

**Why attractive:** A no-modification clause sounds like it could block any change to the contractual relationship, and students who conflate "assignment" with "modification" will gravitate to this answer. The language "may not be modified except by a writing" feels like a barrier.

**The breaker:** An assignment is a *transfer of the right to receive payment* — it does not change what either party must do under the contract. Paul still had to deliver the tents; Silas still had to pay. The performance obligations are identical. A no-modification clause restricts changes to the parties' duties, not the transfer of the right to collect money owed. This choice answers a modification question, not an assignment question.

### Choice B (original A) — ISSUE_SENSE / wrong_element — NOT_RESPONSIVE

**Why attractive:** Students may reason that the assignee should be capable of performing the contract — especially since the work has specialized requirements (custom trade-fair tents). It feels intuitively unfair to let someone collect for work they couldn't do.

**The breaker:** Stephanas is not suing to enforce the contract or to perform the work — he is suing to collect money that Paul already earned by completing the work. Paul delivered and set up all 50 tents; the work is done. The right to payment for completed work is assignable regardless of whether the assignee could have performed the work personally. Stephanas is stepping into Paul's shoes as payee, not as performer.

### Choice D (original B) — EAR_OVERCLAIM / fabricated_rule — NOT_TRUE **[DOMINANT TRAP — A]**

**Why attractive:** This is the strongest trap. Many students hold the intuition that contract rights can only be assigned if the contract expressly allows it. The language "did not contain a clause permitting assignment" cues that intuition powerfully.

**The breaker:** Under the default rule, *all contractual rights are assignable unless the assignment would materially change the obligor's duty, burden, or risk, or unless a statute or valid contractual restriction provides otherwise.* Silence on assignment means assignment is permitted — the absence of an authorization clause does not create a prohibition. Silas's obligation (to pay $8,000 for the tents) is unchanged by the assignment; only the payee changes. A clause permitting assignment is not required.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Credited Answer

**Tested Rule:** Discharge of obligor by pre-notice payment to assignor after assignment of contract rights.

**Student-accessible C3 path:** The call asks which fact leads to a judgment for the obligor (Silas). The one fact that protects the obligor is a discharge fact — payment made before notice. The other three choices describe facts that do not discharge the obligor. The C3 structure tests whether the student can distinguish a valid discharge from three irrelevant or legally incorrect defenses.

**Lawyer-Confirmation Layer:**

The governing rule is stated in **Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 338(1)**: "Except as stated in this Section, notwithstanding an assignment, the assignor retains his power to discharge or modify the duty of the obligor to the extent that the obligor performs or otherwise gives value until but not after the obligor receives notification that the right has been assigned and that performance is to be rendered to the assignee."

The obligor's payment to the assignor before receiving notice of the assignment is a valid discharge of the obligor's duty, pro tanto. See also **UCC § 9-406(a)** (parallel rule for account debtors in Article 9 contexts): "an account debtor may discharge its obligation by paying the assignor until, but not after, the account debtor receives a notification... that the amount due or to become due has been assigned."

**Distractor audits:**

- **Choice D (dominant trap):** Anti-assignment restrictions in contracts between the assignor and obligor are generally ineffective to prevent assignment of the right to payment. See **Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 322(2)(a)** (general rule of assignability); **UCC § 9-406(d)** (anti-assignment clause ineffective for accounts). A silent contract permits assignment by default. This choice fabricates a rule requiring express permission. Correct: NOT_TRUE. Mold: EAR_OVERCLAIM / fabricated_rule.

- **Choice A:** Modification and assignment are legally distinct concepts. A no-modification clause does not restrict assignment. The assignor's right to transfer the receivable is not a "modification" of the contract. Correct: NOT_RESPONSIVE. Mold: ISSUE_SENSE / misfit.

- **Choice B:** An assignee acquires "the rights of the assignor" (Restatement § 328). The assignee's capabilities are irrelevant to the right to collect payment for completed performance by the assignor. The question is about payment, not further performance. Correct: NOT_RESPONSIVE. Mold: ISSUE_SENSE / wrong_element.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 322, 338; UCC § 9-406. No doctrinal-currency hotlist items implicated. The rule is well-established and not affected by recent amendments or cases.

**Confirmed:** Tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are UNCHANGED from the original. The credited answer (discharge by pre-notice payment) is the correct legal outcome under the same rule.

#### Program Frame

This Contracts item tests the assignment-of-rights rule from the Nonparties to Contract subtopic. The student must identify which additional fact wins the case for the obligor (Silas). Two structural features matter: (1) the default rule that contractual silence permits assignment, and (2) the discharge rule that protects an obligor who pays before receiving notice of the assignment. #### Question Data

**Stem:**
Silas, a leather goods dealer, hired Paul, a tentmaker, to supply and set up 50 custom merchant tents for a trade fair at Silas's marketplace for $8,000. Paul delivered and set up all 50 tents. Before Silas paid, Paul, to settle a debt he owed to Stephanas for canvas materials, assigned to Stephanas his right to receive the $8,000 payment under the contract. **Call:** Which one of the following additional facts or inferences, if it were the only one true, would be most likely to lead to a judgment for Silas?

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A** — "The contract contained a no-modification clause..."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This answer restricts *modification*, but the issue is *assignment*. Transferring the right to receive $8,000 doesn't change what Paul must deliver or what Silas must pay. The contractual duties stay exactly the same.
2. **Student label:** "Modification clause — wrong frame."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A fact that alters Silas's legal obligation to pay Stephanas — for example, a clause that restricts *assignment* specifically (and even then, such clauses are generally unenforceable).
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) § 336. An assignment is not a modification. A no-modification clause doesn't restrict assignment of the right to payment.

**Choice B** — "Stephanas was not capable of making tents..."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Can Stephanas personally make tents? Probably so, but it doesn't matter. The question is whether Silas owes Stephanas money for tents Paul already delivered. Stephanas's personal skill at tentmaking is irrelevant.
2. **Student label:** "Assignee's skill — not the issue."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Stephanas never received the assignment" (no standing) or "Paul never delivered the tents" (no earned right to payment).
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The assignee acquires the assignor's rights regardless of the assignee's personal capability. Restatement (Second) § 328.

**Choice C** — "Silas paid Paul $8,000 prior to being notified of the assignment."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** **Gold Key #2:** When an obligor pays the assignor before receiving notice of the assignment, the obligor is discharged. Silas gave Paul the money before Stephanas ever told Silas about the assignment — Silas can't be made to pay the same $8,000 twice.
2. **Student label:** "Paid before notice — Silas is discharged."
3. **What a true/responsive version looks like:** This is it. This fact directly triggers the discharge rule.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 338(1). Payment to the assignor before notice = full discharge.

**Choice D** — "The contract did not contain a clause permitting assignment."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** **Gold Key #1:** The default rule is that all rights are assignable unless restricted. If the contract is silent, assignment is allowed. The lack of a permission clause doesn't create a ban.
2. **Student label:** "No permission clause doesn't mean no assignment."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "The contract explicitly prohibited assignment of the right to payment" — but even then, under UCC § 9-406(d) and Restatement § 322, such clauses are generally ineffective for the right to payment.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) § 322(2)(a). All rights are assignable by default. Silence = permission.
