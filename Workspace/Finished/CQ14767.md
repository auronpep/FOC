## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Paul is unavailable because he died. His statement was not just a description of pain or a loose apology; it admitted the fact that would expose him to fault and undermine the claim against Daniel. Gold Key: for an unavailable declarant, a statement that would expose the declarant to civil liability or defeat the declarant's own claim is admissible as a statement against interest. Silver Key: when the choices are hearsay-exception labels, match the statement to the exception's required trigger, not to the emotional setting around the statement.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the dying-declaration shortcut. Paul died soon after the statement, and the statement relates to the fall, so the label feels close. The breaker is the missing trigger: the stem does not show that Paul believed death was imminent when he spoke.

B sells the severe-event shortcut. A fall from a climbing wall is startling, and Paul was still in pain. The breaker is timing and stress: the statement came three days after the fall, with no fact showing Paul was still speaking under the excitement caused by the event.

D sells the dominant state-of-mind trap. "I caused the fall" can sound like what Paul thought or believed. The breaker is the use of the statement: it is offered to prove the past fact remembered or believed, not to prove Paul's then-existing mental condition. Gold Key: the statement fits against-interest because it harms Paul's civil position; Silver Key: do not pick the label that merely sounds psychologically adjacent.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule, issue, credited outcome, and trap structure are unchanged. The governing rule is FRE 804(b)(3): if the declarant is unavailable, a statement is not excluded by the hearsay rule when a reasonable person in the declarant's position would have made it only if true because, when made, it was contrary to the declarant's pecuniary or proprietary interest, had a great tendency to invalidate the declarant's claim, or exposed the declarant to civil or criminal liability. Death makes a declarant unavailable under FRE 804(a)(4). Paul died, and his statement that he unclipped his own safety line before Daniel told him to would expose Paul to fault and undermine the damages claim against Daniel. The statement is therefore admissible as a statement against interest.

The distractor boundaries also match current federal evidence law. FRE 804(b)(2) requires a statement made while believing death to be imminent and concerning its cause or circumstances. The stem gives death and pain but no belief of imminent death. FRE 803(3) covers then-existing mental, emotional, or physical condition but excludes a statement of memory or belief offered to prove the fact remembered or believed, except for will-related matters. Paul's own-fault statement is being offered to prove fault, not his mental condition. FRE 803(2) requires a statement relating to a startling event or condition made while under the stress of excitement that it caused; the stem gives no such continuing-stress fact. Authority checked against the current Federal Rules of Evidence in 28 U.S.C. Appendix, Article VIII, including Rules 803 and 804: https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&path=%2Fprelim%40title28%2Ftitle28a%2Fnode232%2Farticle8. The 2024 amendment to FRE 804(b)(3)(B) concerns corroborating circumstances for criminal-case statements exposing the declarant to criminal liability and does not change this civil-liability statement-against-interest outcome.
