## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Lydia, who had no previous experience running a concert venue or hiring professional sound companies, bought a small private Christian concert hall. She signed a nine-month agreement with Stephen's sound company for the hall's sound-booth services. The agreement provided for "daily booth checks at $900 a week." During negotiations, Stephen knew that Lydia expected the sound company to check the booth seven days a week. At trial, clear evidence showed that in the professional sound-service trade, "daily booth checks" did not include Sunday checks. After Stephen's company skipped two Sundays in a row and said it would never perform Sunday checks, Lydia canceled the agreement. Stephen's company sued Lydia for breach of contract.

Is Stephen's company likely to succeed in its action?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A service company knows the new business owner understands "daily" to include Sundays, while the owner does not know the trade usage excluding Sundays. Whose meaning controls?

### 3. Final Answer Choices

A. Yes, because the professional sound-trade usage will control the meaning of "daily booth checks."

B. Yes, because the parol evidence rule prevents Lydia from proving the meaning she attached to "daily booth checks."

C. No, because Stephen's company knew the meaning Lydia attached to "daily booth checks," so Lydia's meaning controls.

D. No, because the parties attached materially different meanings to "daily booth checks," so no contract was formed.

### 4. Correct Answer

### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. The decisive fact is not that the sound-service trade has a usage. The decisive fact is the knowledge split at formation: Stephen knew Lydia understood "daily booth checks" to include Sunday checks, and Lydia did not know that Stephen or the trade used the phrase differently.

Gold Key: In a different-meanings problem, the innocent party's meaning controls when that party did not know of the other meaning and the other party knew the innocent party's meaning.

Silver Key: When the answer choices fight over "trade meaning" versus "private meaning," ask who knew about the mismatch before the contract was made. The party with knowledge of the mismatch cannot hide behind the trade usage.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because the stem gives clear evidence of a trade usage, and bar students are trained to respect commercial usage when interpreting contract language. The breaker is the knowledge split: Stephen knew Lydia's meaning, while Lydia did not know the trade meaning, so trade usage does not override Lydia's meaning here. Gold Key: a usage trap loses when one party knew the other's different meaning and the other party did not know the usage.

B is attractive because it sounds like a writing-control answer: the agreement says "daily booth checks," and Lydia wants negotiation evidence about what she meant. The breaker is that interpretation evidence is being used to explain the meaning of the written term, not to add a new inconsistent term.

D is attractive because "materially different meanings" is real contract vocabulary, and a misunderstanding can prevent mutual assent in some cases. The breaker is that the no-contract result fits the symmetric cases: neither side knows, or both sides know. Here, one side knew the other's meaning and the other did not, so the contract is interpreted according to the innocent party's meaning.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The credited outcome is unchanged: Stephen's company is unlikely to succeed because Lydia's meaning controls, making Sunday checks part of the agreement and making Stephen's refusal a breach.

The governing rule is Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 201(2): when parties attach different meanings to a term, the term is interpreted according to one party's meaning if that party did not know of the other meaning and the other party knew, or had reason to know, the first party's meaning. The ALI identifies the Restatement (Second) of Contracts as an authoritative statement of common-law contract principles, and the open Restatement Section 201 text states the controlling knowledge-asymmetry rule. Sources: https://www.ali.org/publications/restatement-law-second/contracts and https://advance.lexis.com/open/document/openwebdocview/-201-Whose-Meaning-Prevails/

Trade usage is a real interpretive input, but it does not save Stephen's company on these facts. Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 222 treats usage of trade as relevant when the parties knew or had reason to know of the usage; UCC Section 1-303 likewise treats usage of trade as relevant when it is in the trade in which the parties are engaged or of which they are or should be aware. Lydia was new to both the venue and sound-service businesses and did not know the trade usage; Stephen knew her contrary meaning. Source for UCC comparison: https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/1/1-303

The parol evidence distractor is also defeated by Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 214(c), which allows prior or contemporaneous negotiations to establish the meaning of a writing. Source: https://advance.lexis.com/open/document/openwebdocview/-214-Evidence-of-Prior-or-Contemporaneous-Agreements-and-Negotiations/
