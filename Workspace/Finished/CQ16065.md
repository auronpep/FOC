## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. Daniel's argument depends on reducing murder to voluntary manslaughter because he acted under intense emotion after Ruth's confession. Gold Key: heat-of-passion voluntary manslaughter has an objective adequacy screen; the provocation must be enough to cause an ordinary person in the situation to lose normal self-control. Silver Key: when the call asks for the prosecutor's best response to manslaughter mitigation, do not argue only that Daniel intended the harm. The stronger response attacks the adequacy of the provocation under the ordinary-person standard.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because repeated punches to the face and head strongly suggest Daniel meant to cause serious bodily harm. The breaker is that voluntary manslaughter can still involve intent to kill or intent to cause serious bodily harm; mitigation turns on legally adequate provocation, not on the absence of intent.

C is attractive for the same reason in a more severe form. The prosecutor may be able to argue intent to kill, but that does not directly answer Daniel's claim that the intentional killing should be mitigated by heat of passion. The best response is that an ordinary person would not have lost self-control to that degree.

D is attractive because old adultery examples often involve catching a spouse in the act. The breaker is that the prosecutor does not need to show the precise in-the-act fact to defeat manslaughter; the broader and stronger response is that the provocation was objectively inadequate for a person of ordinary temperament.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: voluntary manslaughter mitigation may reduce an otherwise murderous intentional killing when the defendant acts in heat of passion or extreme emotional disturbance, but the mitigation requires adequate provocation measured by an objective ordinary-person or reasonable-explanation standard. The most effective prosecution response is therefore that a person of ordinary temperament in Daniel's position would not have lost normal self-control.

The authority floor is real and current. Federal manslaughter law, 18 U.S.C. section 1112, describes voluntary manslaughter as an unlawful killing without malice upon a sudden quarrel or heat of passion. Model Penal Code section 210.3 treats a homicide that would otherwise be murder as manslaughter when it is committed under extreme mental or emotional disturbance for which there is a reasonable explanation or excuse. Cornell's Wex summary likewise describes voluntary manslaughter as intentional killing in heat of passion in response to adequate provocation and notes the MPC's reasonable-explanation framing. CALCRIM No. 570, used here only as a concrete modern jury-instruction illustration of the ordinary-person screen, asks whether a person of average disposition in the same situation would have reacted from passion rather than judgment.

Because intent to kill or inflict serious bodily injury can exist in a voluntary manslaughter case, arguments A and C do not best defeat the mitigation claim. Because adultery-related provocation is not reduced to a single in-the-act formula in the source rule, D is too narrow. B goes directly to the decisive issue: objective adequacy of the provocation.

Sources checked: https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/manslaughter, https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/crime_of_passion, https://codes.findlaw.com/us/title-18-crimes-and-criminal-procedure/18-usc-sect-1112/, and https://www.justia.com/criminal/docs/calcrim/500/570/.
