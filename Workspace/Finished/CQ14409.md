### 1. Final question

Lydia owned a private, for-profit Christian family camp called Mustard Seed Adventures. For an agreed price of $8 million, Barnabas Construction, a company specializing in large themed projects and excavation, contracted to design and build on Lydia’s lakeside parcel a Noah’s-Ark-shaped indoor climbing hall. Because sinkholes were common in the area, a local safety ordinance required the hall’s foundation and underground utilities to be excavated at least 18 feet.

Barnabas began digging and, at 11 feet, encountered a continuous shelf of limestone. When the contract was made, neither Lydia nor Barnabas knew of the limestone, and neither had hired a geotechnical expert to test for subsurface rock. Barnabas accurately claimed that removing enough limestone to build as planned would cost an additional $1.2 million and would probably leave it with a net loss of $800,000 on the job. Barnabas refused to continue unless Lydia promised to pay an additional $1 million for the completed hall.

If Lydia refuses and sues Barnabas for breach of contract, which of the following will the court probably decide?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Lydia should prevail because Barnabas was the specialist, accepted a fixed price, knew deep excavation was required, and skipped available subsurface testing. **Gold Key:** risk beats surprise. A contractor who takes a fixed-price excavation job while treating limited site knowledge as enough usually carries the unknown-site-condition risk. **Silver Key:** read the answer array as outcome plus reason. ### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** The mutual-mistake answer sells the strongest trap: both sides lacked the same subsurface fact. **Gold Key:** mutual mistake does not unlock avoidance when the affected party bears the risk of the mistake. Here, Barnabas was the excavation specialist, knew the job required deep digging, and did not test.

**C.** This answer grabs a true fact: Barnabas did not actually know about the limestone. **Silver Key:** test the because-clause. Lack of actual knowledge is not the reason Lydia wins; the risk-allocation fact is that Barnabas assumed the risk by pricing and taking the excavation job without testing.

**D.** The impracticability answer sells the added-cost story. The breaker is range. A fixed-price construction contract is built to absorb ordinary cost increases, and this added cost is not enough on these facts to become severe impracticability.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

The original tested rule and credited legal outcome are preserved: the owner prevails because the professional builder/excavator bore the risk of the unknown subsurface condition, so neither mutual mistake nor impracticability excuses refusal to perform. The uploaded source row states the original official key as C and explains that the builder specialized in excavation, had an opportunity to investigate, knew deep excavation was required, failed to test, and therefore assumed the risk.
