### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** Diminished capacity, where a jurisdiction recognizes it, is a partial doctrine — a rule of evidence, not a complete defense. It permits the defendant to introduce evidence of a mental abnormality to show that he could not actually form a *specific* mental state the charged crime requires. First-degree murder requires **premeditation and deliberation** (a heightened, specific-intent mental state). A documented neurocognitive disorder that impairs the ability to plan ahead goes directly to that element. If the jury accepts the evidence, the prosecution may fail to prove first-degree murder; Stephen can still be convicted of a lesser-included homicide (e.g., second-degree murder or manslaughter) because he *did* know the nature of the act and *did* know it was wrong — so the actus reus and the basic mens rea are intact.

**Silver Key:** *When a stem flags "the jurisdiction recognizes diminished capacity," that single sentence forces the answer into the partial-reduction lane — never a complete acquittal, never "no effect."* The flag is the question.

**Why B (insanity/acquittal) is wrong:** Insanity is governed by the M'Naghten test (or a modern equivalent), which requires that the defendant *did not know the nature and quality of the act* or *did not know the act was wrong* because of a disease of the mind. Stephen admitted he *knew* he was swinging at a person *and* knew it was wrong. M'Naghten fails; the choice's "acquittal by reason of insanity" is foreclosed. The fact that Stephen has a mental defect does not convert a failed M'Naghten claim into a complete acquittal.

**Why D (no effect) is wrong:** The stem expressly says the jurisdiction recognizes diminished capacity. If the jurisdiction recognizes the doctrine *and* the defendant has admissible mental-state evidence connecting to a specific-intent element, the evidence must do *something*. "Knew it was wrong" forecloses the *insanity* defense; it does not foreclose the *diminished-capacity* doctrine, which targets a different element (premeditation) and asks a different question (could the defendant actually plan ahead, not whether he could tell right from wrong).

**Why A (involuntary intoxication) is wrong:** Involuntary intoxication is a defense that arises from the *unknowing or coerced ingestion of an intoxicant* (a drug or alcohol). Dementia is a documented neurocognitive disorder, not an ingested substance. The choice mislabels the cause and applies the wrong doctrine.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations (one per distractor)

#### B (was original A — acquittal by reason of insanity)

This is the **dominant trap**. It is engineered to seduce a student who has internalized the bare phrase "mental disease or defect" but has not separated the **insanity** doctrine from the **diminished capacity** doctrine. The cognitive move the choice sells is: *"Stephen has a documented mental disorder → mental disorder = insanity → insanity = complete acquittal."* The move is fluent and sounds lawyerly, but it elides the M'Naghten test. **Gold Key:** *Insanity and diminished capacity are distinct doctrines. Insanity is a complete affirmative defense that requires M'Naghten's "did not know nature or wrong" failure. Diminished capacity is a partial rule of evidence that may negate a specific-intent element.* Because Stephen *knew* he was swinging at a person *and knew it was wrong*, M'Naghten fails on its face, and a complete acquittal is unreachable on these facts regardless of how severe the dementia is.

#### D (was original C — no possible effect)

The cognitive move this choice sells is: *"Stephen knew it was wrong, so the M'Naghten right-wrong branch of the insanity test is satisfied; the evidence is therefore legally irrelevant."* That collapses a *partial* defense (diminished capacity) into a *complete* one (insanity) by treating "knew it was wrong" as a global bar on all mental-state evidence. The breaker: the stem itself signals that diminished capacity is recognized in this jurisdiction. Once that flag is in the stem, the evidence must do *something* — namely, attack the *premeditation* element. The "knew it was wrong" fact is what defeats the insanity trap, not what neutralizes the diminished-capacity claim.

#### A (was original D — involuntary intoxication)

The cognitive move this choice sells is: *"Stephen was impaired → impairment by a substance is the textbook involuntary-intoxication pattern."* The move is the right shape (mental impairment + non-volitional cause), but it fits the wrong legal label. **Involuntary intoxication** is the doctrine that applies when an intoxicant is unknowingly or coercively ingested (spiked drink, prescribed drug taken without knowledge of its effects, etc.). A documented neurocognitive disorder is not an ingested substance. The choice relabels a medical condition as an intoxication — a categorical mismatch, not a factual one.

### 9. Full black-letter verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested rule.** Where a jurisdiction recognizes diminished capacity, evidence of a mental disease, defect, or disorder that is insufficient to establish legal insanity (M'Naghten) may nonetheless be introduced to show that the defendant was incapable of forming the specific mental state required for the charged offense. First-degree murder is a *specific-intent / heightened-intent* crime requiring premeditation and deliberation. If the mental-state evidence negates premeditation, the prosecution fails to prove first-degree murder; the defendant may be convicted of a lesser-included homicide.

**Authority floor.**

- *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (H.L. 1843) — the right-wrong / nature-of-act test for legal insanity; a defendant who knows the nature of the act and knows it is wrong is not legally insane under the M'Naghten rule.
- Model Penal Code § 2.01 (purpose, knowledge, recklessness, negligence) and § 4.01 (mental illness/disability relevant to whether the actor had the culpability required for the offense) — modern codification that mental-condition evidence is relevant to the *mens rea* element, not only to a freestanding "insanity" affirmative defense.
- *People v. White*, 117 Cal. App. 3d 270 (1981) (the "Twinkie defense" case) — the historical vehicle for diminished-capacity evidence negating premeditation; the doctrine was rolled back legislatively in California in 1982 but survives in many state and federal variants as a rule of evidence.
- Insanity Defense Reform Act, 18 U.S.C. § 17 (1984) — abolished the federal diminished-capacity defense, but most states have not followed; the MBE frequently tests the doctrine as it survives in a "the jurisdiction recognizes" stem.
