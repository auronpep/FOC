## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule:** A person has no reasonable expectation of privacy in garbage left at the curb (or roadside) for collection. The Fourth Amendment does not protect trash that has been placed in an area accessible to the public for collection. No warrant, consent, or individualized suspicion is required for police to search such garbage.

**Authority:**
- **California v. Greenwood, 486 U.S. 35 (1988):** The Supreme Court held 6-2 that the Fourth Amendment does not prohibit the warrantless search and seizure of garbage left for collection outside the curtilage of a home. The Court reasoned that (1) garbage at the curb is readily accessible to animals, children, scavengers, and other members of the public, and (2) the homeowner placed the garbage out expressly for a third party to take it away — conduct that constitutes abandonment of any expectation of privacy. The Court explicitly rejected the argument that opaque bags preserve privacy.
- **Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 473 (1967):** The "reasonable expectation of privacy" test — the Fourth Amendment protects people, not places. The relevant inquiry is whether the individual manifested a subjective expectation of privacy that society is prepared to recognize as reasonable. No reasonable society would recognize an expectation of privacy in trash left at the curb.
- **Abandonment doctrine:** Once personal property is abandoned (including garbage placed for collection), the owner loses Fourth Amendment protection in it. See also Abel v. United States, 362 U.S. 217 (1960) (abandoned property in a hotel wastebasket).

**Verification against source:** The source question's credited answer (B → "Admit — no reasonable expectation of privacy in curbside garbage") is fully consistent with California v. Greenwood.
