### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**A** is correct. A Section 1292(b) appeal is **not** appealable as of right. The statute creates a **discretionary, two-step route**: (1) the district court must **certify** that the order involves a controlling question of law as to which there is a substantial ground for difference of opinion, and that an immediate appeal may materially advance the ultimate termination of the litigation; **and** (2) the court of appeals must then **grant permission** to take the appeal. Neither step happened here. The district judge did not certify, and the court of appeals did not grant permission. Hannah's "important issue" footnote is a colloquial comment, not a certification, and a bare notice of appeal does not invoke the §1292(b) route. **Silver Key:** when a party files an immediate notice of appeal from a non-final order, check BOTH the certification and the appellate-permission steps before concluding the appeal is available. The default is no interlocutory appeal as of right.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. Yes, because important questions are automatically appealable.**

This is the dominant trap. The "importance" hook in the footnote looks like the §1292(b) standard, but a judge's footnote is not certification, and "importance" alone is not the statutory test. Section 1292(b) requires an express certification by the district court **and** a grant of permission by the court of appeals; a private party's importance argument is rhetoric, not a rule. **Silver Key:** when the call invokes §1292(b), scan for BOTH the district-court certification AND the court-of-appeals permission. A denial of either step is fatal to the immediate appeal.

**C. No, because interlocutory appeal is impossible in federal court.**

This is the overclaim trap. Interlocutory appeal in federal court is not impossible; it is available through a small set of permission-based routes — Section 1292(a) (injunctions), Section 1292(b) (certified question), Rule 23(f) (class certification), mandamus, and the collateral-order doctrine. The "impossible in federal court" framing is a flat misstatement. The breaker is that interlocutory review **does** exist — it just requires the statutory or rule-based hook, not a bare notice of appeal.

**D. Yes, because district-court certification is unnecessary.**

This is the false-rule trap. Section 1292(b) expressly conditions immediate appeal on the district court's certification that the statutory criteria are met. Eliminating the certification step rewrites the statute. The breaker is the text of §1292(b) itself: a §1292(b) appeal does not exist without a certification order from the district court.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 19337, a Civil Procedure item involving a district judge's denial of a motion with a footnote stating that the issue is "important," the absence of any certification order, and a party's immediate notice of appeal claiming a Section 1292(b) appeal as of right. The official key is C: "No, because Section 1292(b) requires proper certification and appellate permission."

The default rule of federal appellate jurisdiction is the final-judgment rule of 28 U.S.C. § 1291: a party may appeal only from a "final decision" of the district court. A denial of a motion in an ongoing case is interlocutory; it is not a final decision. Absent a specific statutory or rule-based exception, immediate appellate review is unavailable.

Section 1292(b) supplies one such exception, and it is narrow and two-step. The statute provides that a district court may certify an order for interlocutory appeal only if the order "involves a controlling question of law as to which there is substantial ground for difference of opinion" and that "an immediate appeal from the order may materially advance the ultimate termination of the litigation." 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b). Even when the district court enters such a certification, the court of appeals has discretion whether to permit the appeal. The two steps are independent: the district court cannot certify sua sponte for the court of appeals, and the court of appeals cannot grant permission absent a district-court certification. A bare footnote expressing that the issue is "important" is not a certification order; it is a colloquial comment. The losing party cannot manufacture appellate jurisdiction by filing a notice of appeal and arguing that the underlying question is important.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: Hannah must obtain BOTH a district-court certification AND a court-of-appeals grant of permission before the court of appeals can hear her interlocutory appeal. Authority: 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b); 28 U.S.C. § 1291 (final-judgment default); see generally *Wisconsin Knife Works v. National Metal Crafters*, 781 F.2d 1280 (7th Cir. 1986) (interlocutory appeal under §1292(b) is permissive on both ends).

## program_frame

This is a Civil Procedure question that turns on the appellate route from an interlocutory order under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b). The default federal rule is the final-judgment rule of 28 U.S.C. § 1291; denials of motions in ongoing cases are not final and therefore not appealable as of right. Section 1292(b) carves out a narrow, **two-step, permission-based** exception.
