## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Stephen works part-time at a private Christian conference center. Before an evening apologetics seminar, his manager tells him to carry a boxed portable projector from the media room to the chapel lectern for a waiting speaker. While walking through the hallway, Stephen slips the projector case under his coat, walks out a side door, and leaves with it, intending to keep it.

What is the best common-law theft offense?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

An employee is allowed to carry the owner's property only from one place to another for the owner, but instead hides it and leaves with it; what common-law theft offense fits?

### 3. Final Answer Choices

A. Embezzlement, because every employee who converts workplace property embezzles it.

B. No theft offense, because Stephen touched the projector case with permission.

C. Larceny, because Stephen had only custody and trespassorily took property still possessed by his employer.

D. False pretenses, because Stephen implicitly deceived the manager by leaving.

### 5. Correct Answer

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Stephen had permission to carry the projector case for the center, but only as a limited handler. That gave him custody, not possession. Gold Key: at common law, an employee with only custody of the employer's property commits larceny if he converts it, because possession remains with the employer. Silver Key: sort theft crimes by how the defendant first got the property; custody-only handling points to larceny, lawful possession points to embezzlement, and title obtained by a lie points to false pretenses.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is the dominant trap because the word "employee" makes embezzlement sound automatic. Gold Key: employment is not enough; embezzlement requires lawful possession, and Stephen had only limited custody while carrying the case for his employer.

B uses the true fact that Stephen was allowed to touch and carry the case, then stretches it past its limit. Silver Key: ask what permission covered. It covered delivery to the lectern, not hiding the case under his coat and leaving with it.

D borrows a neighboring property-crime label. False pretenses requires obtaining title through a false representation; Stephen did not obtain title by a lie. He converted property the center still possessed.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: a servant or employee who has only custody of an employer's property and converts it may commit larceny because possession remains with the employer. Common-law larceny requires a trespassory taking and carrying away of another's personal property with intent to permanently deprive; the Department of Justice Criminal Resource Manual section 1006 states the larceny elements as wrongful taking and carrying away, absence of consent, and intent to deprive: https://www.justice.gov/archives/usam/criminal-resource-manual-1006-larceny. Embezzlement differs because the property is entrusted to, or lawfully comes into the hands of, the defendant; DOJ Criminal Resource Manual section 1005 quotes Moore v. United States, 160 U.S. 268 (1895), on that distinction: https://www.justice.gov/archives/usam/criminal-resource-manual-1005-embezzlement. State v. Bailey, 25 N.C. App. 412 (1975), citing State v. McDonald, 133 N.C. 680 (1903), states the classic line: larceny requires a trespassory taking, while embezzlement covers property lawfully in the offender's possession. Stephen was merely carrying the projector case for delivery inside the center; he did not receive lawful possession for his own independent control. His concealment and departure were trespassory. Larceny is the best common-law theft offense.
