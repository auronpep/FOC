1. Final question — the revised question stem + call.

Esther was suspected of participating in a bank robbery. Two days later she checked into a downtown hotel. When Esther left her room for the evening, the hotel manager opened the hotel-room door so a federal officer could enter and look inside. The officer found no stolen money but saw Esther's diary open on the bed; the diary contained a confession of involvement in the robbery. Esther was charged in federal court and moved to suppress the diary. Should the court suppress the diary?

7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register).

The Fourth Amendment protects a hotel guest's reasonable expectation of privacy in the guest room. A warrantless entry by law enforcement is presumptively unreasonable absent either a warrant, valid consent, or exigent circumstances. A hotel manager generally lacks authority to consent to a guest's private room (Stoner v. California), and no exigency is present here. Because the officer entered and observed the diary without a warrant and without valid consent or exigent circumstances, the diary should be suppressed under the exclusionary rule. See Minnesota v. Olson (recognizing expectation of privacy in a temporary guest's room) and Stoner v. California (manager lacks authority to consent).

8. Full wrong-answer explanations — one per distractor.

B (Self-incrimination): Attractive because the diary contains a confession, but the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination protects against compelled testimonial communication, not the voluntary statements found in personal writings observed during an unlawful search. The suppression issue is Fourth Amendment, not Fifth.

C (Manager authority): Attractive to students who conflate hotel staff duties with consent authority. Stoner v. California holds that hotel employees do not have the authority to consent to entry of a guest's room, so the manager's opening the door did not validate the officer's warrantless entry.

D (Apparent authority): Attractive where students focus on officer reliability rather than legal doctrine. Illinois v. Rodriguez allows warrantless entry based on reasonable belief in third-party authority, but that doctrine presumes objectively reasonable belief; here, Stoner limits the legitimacy of a manager's authority and the officer's reliance is not reasonable as a legal matter when hotel staff lack actual authority to consent.

9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer.

Rule: The Fourth Amendment requires a warrant to enter and search a dwelling absent valid consent or exigent circumstances. A hotel guest has a protected expectation of privacy in the guest room (Minnesota v. Olson, 495 U.S. 91 (1990)). Consent by a third party is valid only when that third party has actual authority over the premises (Stoner v. California, 376 U.S. 483 (1964)); reliance on apparent authority can sometimes validate a warrantless entry (Illinois v. Rodriguez, 497 U.S. 177 (1990)), but where the third party lacks authority as a matter of law and no exigency exists, suppression is required. Applying these authorities, the officer's warrantless entry based on the manager's opening the door produced the diary through an unconstitutional search; suppression is proper.
