## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: the Fourth Amendment protects the home and its curtilage, but not open fields. To decide whether an area is curtilage, courts apply the Dunn factors: proximity to the home, whether the area is within an enclosure surrounding the home, the nature of the area's use, and steps taken to protect it from observation.

United States v. Dunn, 480 U.S. 294 (1987), held that a barn about 50 yards from the fence around the house, outside the enclosure, not shown to be used for intimate home activities, and insufficiently shielded from view was outside the curtilage. Oliver v. United States, 466 U.S. 170 (1984), supports the open-fields doctrine. Florida v. Jardines, 569 U.S. 1 (2013), confirms that curtilage around the home gets strong protection, but it does not convert a non-curtilage outbuilding into the home. Sources checked: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/480/294/, https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/466/170/, and https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/569/1/.

Peter's shop is 75 yards from the farmhouse, outside the fence enclosing the farmhouse and prayer garden, oriented away from the house, used for illegal production rather than home life, and not shielded from outside view. Under Dunn, it is outside the curtilage. The agents did not enter the shop; they looked through an exposed window from outside. The motion to suppress should be denied.
