### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is the answer because courts may not decide whether a religious belief is reasonable, sensible, or persuasive. Gold Key: free exercise protects sincere religious belief without asking whether judges think the belief makes sense. The court can ask whether the belief is sincerely held, and it can analyze the government interest, but it cannot grade the belief's reasonableness.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells a clean-sounding no-inquiry move: if courts cannot judge religious truth, maybe they also cannot look at whether the practice has roots. The breaker is that history or tradition can be considered as evidence bearing on sincerity or religious character; it is not a judicial ruling that the belief is reasonable.

B attracts students who remember that courts stay out of religious truth. The breaker is that sincerity is different from reasonableness; courts may ask whether Daniel honestly holds the belief he asserts.

D sounds too demanding because modern free exercise doctrine does not make strict scrutiny necessary for every neutral, generally applicable law. The breaker is the negative call: a court may still consider whether the state has a compelling interest when constitutionality is being decided, especially if heightened scrutiny is triggered.

### 9. Black-Letter Verification And Legal Reasoning

The First Amendment's Free Exercise Clause bars government from deciding the truth, validity, or reasonableness of religious beliefs. United States v. Ballard, 322 U.S. 78 (1944), is the core authority for the no-truth/no-validity principle; Thomas v. Review Board, 450 U.S. 707 (1981), confirms that religious beliefs need not be logical, consistent, or comprehensible to others. Courts may, however, consider sincerity. Free exercise scrutiny can also consider the government's interest, including compelling-interest analysis when a law is not neutral or generally applicable under Employment Division v. Smith, 494 U.S. 872 (1990), and Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah, 508 U.S. 520 (1993). The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: reasonableness of the religious belief is the forbidden inquiry.
