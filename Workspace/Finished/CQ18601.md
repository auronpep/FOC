### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. The officer asked one narrow question aimed at finding a dangerous device in a crowded auditorium. Gold Key: the public-safety exception permits limited unwarned questioning when officers reasonably need answers to protect the public or themselves from an immediate danger. Silver Key: read the question's scope, not just the custody status. The officer asked where the device was, not for a narrative confession or later investigative details.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells a too-small version of the exception. The original Supreme Court case involved a gun, but the Gold Key turns on immediate public safety, not a firearm-only category. A hidden incendiary device in a crowded room creates the same kind of urgent public danger.

C sells the right emergency instinct but overextends it. A limited safety question can come before warnings, but that does not waive every later Miranda right or authorize ordinary investigative questioning without warnings.

D sells the dominant Miranda shortcut. Custody and interrogation normally require warnings, but "always" breaks the answer because the public-safety exception covers narrow questions reasonably prompted by immediate danger.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: Miranda warnings are generally required for custodial interrogation, but there is a narrow public-safety exception for questions reasonably prompted by a need to protect officers or the public from immediate danger.

New York v. Quarles, 467 U.S. 649 (1984), recognized the public-safety exception and admitted an unwarned answer to a question about the location of a dangerous weapon in a public place. Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), supplies the ordinary custodial-interrogation warning rule that Quarles treats as limited by emergency public-safety needs. Sources checked: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/467/649 and https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/384/436/.

Lydia was in custody, and the officer asked a question before warnings, so the Miranda issue is real. But the question was limited to locating a dangerous device in a packed public auditorium, immediately after reports that Lydia placed it there. That is a public-safety question, not ordinary investigative interrogation. The answer is admissible despite the lack of prior warnings.
