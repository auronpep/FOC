### 5. Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** Under the pre-existing duty rule, a party's promise to do something that party is already legally obligated to perform under an existing contract does not constitute valid consideration for a new promise. Here, the men were already contractually bound to complete the three-week harvest at $200 per week. Their promise to finish the final week — work they were already required to do — is not new consideration for Peter's promise to pay $500 per week. The pre-existing duty rule exists precisely to prevent the kind of coercive mid-performance renegotiation that occurred here: the men exploited Peter's time-sensitive inability to find replacements to extract a higher price for work they had already agreed to do. Peter's written agreement to the increase is unenforceable for lack of consideration, and he owes only the original contract rate.

**Gold Key:** Under the pre-existing duty rule (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 73), performance of a duty already owed under an existing contract is not valid consideration for a new promise. The rule bars enforcement of mid-performance modifications extracted by the threat of breach, even if the modification is in writing and even if the promisor was under economic pressure. The sole common-law exceptions — additional duties, unforeseen difficulties, or third-party promises — do not apply here because the men promised nothing beyond what they were already bound to do.

### 6. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is attractive because** contract law does permit parties to modify agreements, and students may assume that a written modification reflects a legitimate change in the deal. The language "circumstances change" sounds like a neutral, fair principle. **The breaker:** nothing about the circumstances changed. The men faced the same harvest work they had always agreed to do. The pre-existing duty rule specifically blocks renegotiation where one party merely threatens to breach unless paid more for the same performance. No new consideration supports the modification.

**C is attractive because** promissory estoppel is a well-known doctrine that enforces promises when the promisee reasonably relies on them to their detriment. The men did continue working, and Peter's promise was in writing — facts that look like reliance. **The breaker:** promissory estoppel does not override the pre-existing duty rule in this context. The men's "reliance" (finishing the job) was performance they were already contractually obligated to provide. Courts applying the pre-existing duty rule do not allow the reliance argument to bootstrap an unenforceable modification. **Gold Key:** The pre-existing duty rule bars enforcement even when the modification is in writing and the promisee relies by performing exactly what the original contract required.

**D is attractive because** Peter was clearly under economic pressure — the crop was ready to ship and he had no time to find replacements. A student who spots the duress may select this answer as the "safest" reason. **The breaker:** while economic duress may be factually present, it is not the doctrinal rule that decides this question. Duress makes a contract voidable, but the pre-existing duty rule goes further — it means there was never an enforceable modification in the first place because consideration was lacking. On the MBE, when both doctrines are answerable, the pre-existing duty rule is the more precise and complete answer because it attacks the formation of the modification itself, not merely its voluntariness.

### 7. Black-Letter Legal Verification

**Governing rule:** The pre-existing duty rule, Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 73: "Performance of a legal duty owed to a promisor which is neither doubtful nor the subject of honest dispute is not consideration."

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 73 (1981). The rule is a standard common-law doctrine applied in virtually all U.S. jurisdictions for service contracts and other non-UCC modifications. *See also* Alaska Packers' Ass'n v. Domenico, 117 F. 99 (9th Cir. 1902) (ship workers' mid-voyage demand for higher wages unenforceable under pre-existing duty rule); Austin Instrument, Inc. v. Loral Corp., 272 N.E.2d 533 (N.Y. 1971) (distinguishing duress from pre-existing duty analysis).

**Application:** The men were contractually obligated to work all three weeks at $200/week. Their promise to work the final week was performance of a pre-existing legal duty. No new consideration supports Peter's promise to pay more. The modification is unenforceable. The legal issue (pre-existing duty), the tested rule, and the credited outcome (employer wins, modification unenforceable) are unchanged from the original.

**Doctrinal currency check:** The pre-existing duty rule remains good law. The UCC § 2-209(1) abrogates the rule for sales of goods, but this is a service contract governed by common law. No hotlist issue.
