### 1. Final question

A grand jury indicted Lydia on Monday for burglarizing a private Christian bookstore's hymnal archive. Two days later, before Lydia's first court appearance and before any lawyer had been hired or appointed, a detective came to Lydia's apartment and asked her about the archive burglary. Lydia was not under arrest and was free to end the conversation. The detective gave no warnings. ### 7. Full right-answer explanation

A is correct. The indictment is the trigger fact. Once the grand jury returned the indictment, the government's role had shifted from investigation to formal accusation on that charged offense. The detective then directly asked Lydia about that same archive burglary and elicited an incriminating statement without counsel present and without a knowing waiver. Gold Key: after formal adversarial proceedings begin, the Sixth Amendment bars deliberate elicitation about the charged offense unless counsel is present or the accused knowingly waives that right. Silver Key: do not start with custody. In a post-indictment question, first ask whether the statement was deliberately elicited about the charged offense.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

B is the dominant trap because "not in custody" is the familiar Miranda move. The breaker is that the question is not solved by Miranda custody; the indictment makes the Sixth Amendment counsel rule active, and that rule can apply outside custody.

C is attractive because no lawyer had appeared in the story yet. The breaker is the fabricated requirement: the Sixth Amendment right attaches when formal proceedings begin, not when the defendant hires or receives a lawyer.

D is attractive because Lydia answered willingly. Gold Key: willingness is not the same thing as a knowing waiver of the attached Sixth Amendment counsel right; without warnings or some other proof that Lydia knew and relinquished the right, voluntariness alone does not validate the statement.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, trap structure, and credited outcome are unchanged. The Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches when adversarial judicial criminal proceedings begin, including by indictment. Once attached, the government may not deliberately elicit statements from the accused about the charged offense in the absence of counsel unless the accused knowingly and voluntarily waives the right.

Authority: In Massiah v. United States, 377 U.S. 201 (1964), the Supreme Court held that statements deliberately elicited from an indicted defendant in counsel's absence could not be used against him at trial: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/377/201/. Rothgery v. Gillespie County, 554 U.S. 191 (2008), confirms that adversary judicial proceedings trigger attachment of the Sixth Amendment right to counsel: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep554/usrep554191/usrep554191.pdf. Patterson v. Illinois, 487 U.S. 285 (1988), permits a post-indictment waiver when the accused is adequately informed and knowingly waives; that supports suppression here because no warnings or other knowing waiver facts were supplied: https://www.oyez.org/cases/1987/86-7059.

Here, Lydia had already been indicted for the archive burglary. The detective was a government actor and asked her about that same charged burglary. Her statement identifying where the hymnals were hidden was deliberately elicited. Custody was unnecessary for the Sixth Amendment claim, and the absence of appointed or retained counsel did not delay attachment. Because the stem gives no warning or knowing waiver, the statement should be suppressed.
