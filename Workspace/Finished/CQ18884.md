## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

After a dawn cleanup following a city-sponsored Christian youth rally at a convention center, Naomi saw a hooded thief grab a volunteer's backpack for about three seconds from the far end of a dim loading hallway. Seven weeks later, police showed Naomi one phone image and said, "This is the person we arrested." Naomi paused for several moments, then identified the person in the image. The defendant moves to suppress the identification.

Should the identification be suppressed?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

Should a court suppress a police-arranged single-image identification when the procedure was suggestive and the reliability factors are weak?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. The police arranged the suggestive circumstance by showing Naomi a single image and effectively vouching for it with the statement that this was the person arrested. The reliability facts are also weak: Naomi saw the thief only briefly, from far away, in dim conditions, with the thief hooded, and seven weeks passed before the identification. Her pause before identifying adds another reliability weakness. Gold Key: a police-arranged suggestive identification is not automatically excluded, but it must be suppressed when the totality of the circumstances creates a substantial likelihood of misidentification. Silver Key: do not stop at "single image"; run the reliability factors.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells the jury-only instinct. Juries ordinarily weigh credibility, but due process requires pretrial screening when police arrange suggestive identification circumstances. If the reliability factors are too weak, the judge suppresses the identification before the jury hears it.

B sells a true concern with an overbroad remedy. Single-image displays are highly suggestive, but they are not automatically excluded. The reason this identification is suppressed is the combination of police-arranged suggestiveness and weak reliability.

D sells the event as if the witness's final answer ends the analysis. Naomi did identify the person, but the question is whether that identification is reliable enough after a suggestive police procedure. Her hesitation and the poor viewing facts undermine reliability.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: due process bars an eyewitness identification when police arranged an unnecessarily suggestive procedure and, under the totality of the circumstances, the reliability factors are too weak to avoid a substantial likelihood of misidentification. The item is not a Sixth Amendment counsel-at-lineup question and not a Fifth Amendment confession question; it belongs under Fourteenth Amendment identifications.

Neil v. Biggers, 409 U.S. 188 (1972), supplies the reliability factors: opportunity to view, degree of attention, accuracy of prior description, certainty at confrontation, and time between crime and confrontation. Manson v. Brathwaite, 432 U.S. 98 (1977), rejects automatic exclusion and makes reliability under the totality of the circumstances the controlling admissibility inquiry. Perry v. New Hampshire, 565 U.S. 228 (2012), confirms that the due-process screening rule applies when law enforcement arranged the suggestive circumstances. Sources checked: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/409/188/, https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/432/98/, and https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/10-8974.

Naomi's facts are weak under the Biggers/Manson reliability screen. She had only a three-second view, from a distance, in dim lighting, of a hooded person; seven weeks passed; and she paused before making the identification. The police-created single-image procedure and statement, "This is the person we arrested," add strong suggestiveness. Because weak reliability does not outweigh the corrupting effect of the procedure, suppression is warranted.
