## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

The objection should be sustained. At common law, the attorney-client privilege protects confidential communications made by a person to an attorney for the purpose of seeking legal advice. The general rule is that the presence of a third party during such a communication creates an inference that the communication was not intended to be confidential, which defeats the privilege. However, that inference does not arise when the third party's presence is reasonably necessary to facilitate the attorney-client relationship or to protect the client's interests.

Here, the court appointed Hannah as Nora's legal guardian specifically for purposes of this litigation, and state law required her joinder as a co-defendant. Hannah's presence at the consultation was necessary so that she could ensure Nora's legal rights were protected during the meeting with counsel. Because her presence was necessary, the inference of non-confidentiality does not apply, and the communication remains privileged.

**Gold Key:** When a third party's presence is *necessary* to the client's legal representation — such as a court-appointed guardian for a minor — the ordinary inference of non-confidentiality from third-party presence does not apply, and privilege is preserved.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**(A) Yes, because all communications made by a client to an attorney while seeking legal advice are privileged.**

*What makes it attractive:* This states the broadest version of the privilege rule — the version students memorize first. It sounds like a correct statement of the general principle, and most communications to an attorney *are* privileged.

*The breaker:* The word "all" is the tell. The privilege covers only *confidential* communications. A communication made in the presence of an unnecessary third person is not confidential and therefore not privileged. The "all" framing overclaims and ignores the confidentiality requirement. This is a **tiered_absolute** mold — it takes a real rule and removes its limiting conditions.

**(C) No, because Nora and Hannah are co-defendants in the action.**

*What makes it attractive:* Students learn that when multiple clients consult an attorney together about a matter of common interest, the privilege does not apply between them in subsequent litigation. The co-defendant fact pattern triggers that association.

*The breaker:* The joint-client exception applies only when one co-client seeks to introduce the communication *against the other*. Here, the communication is being offered by a *third party* (Paula) against *both* Nora and Hannah. The mere fact that Nora and Hannah are co-defendants does not, by itself, destroy the privilege against an outside party. This is a **half_truth** mold — the joint-client rule is real but does not apply in this posture.

**(D) No, because David never agreed to represent Nora and Hannah.**

*What makes it attractive:* Students may associate the attorney-client relationship with a formal retainer or contract. If the attorney declined representation, it seems logical that no privilege could attach.

*The breaker:* The privilege attaches to the *consultation for legal advice*, not to the formation of an ongoing retainer. A person who consults an attorney seeking legal advice — even if the attorney ultimately declines the case — makes a privileged communication during that consultation. No agreement to represent is required. This is a **fabricated_rule** mold — it invents a requirement that does not exist.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule:** The common-law attorney-client privilege protects confidential communications between a client and an attorney made for the purpose of seeking or providing legal advice. *See* 8 J. Wigmore, Evidence § 2292 (McNaughton rev. 1961); *Upjohn Co. v. United States*, 449 U.S. 383, 389 (1981) (privilege protects confidential communications made to counsel to obtain legal advice); *In re Lindsey*, 158 F.3d 1263, 1268 (D.C. Cir. 1998) (privilege belongs to the client).

**Third-party presence rule:** The presence of a third party during an attorney-client communication generally creates a presumption that the communication was not confidential, defeating the privilege. *However*, that presumption does not apply when the third party's presence is reasonably necessary to facilitate the representation. *See* 8 J. Wigmore, Evidence § 2311 (third-party presence waives privilege unless presence is necessary to the communication); *United States v. Kovel*, 296 F.2d 918, 921 (2d Cir. 1961) (presence of non-lawyer assistant does not waive privilege when necessary to the representation); *In re Grand Jury Proceedings*, 727 F.2d 1352, 1356 (4th Cir. 1984) (necessary third parties do not destroy confidentiality).

**Guardian-specific application:** When a guardian is appointed for a minor for purposes of litigation, the guardian's presence at attorney-client consultations is necessary to protect the ward's interests, and the privilege is preserved. *See* *Matter of Jacqueline F.*, 47 N.Y.2d 215, 417 N.Y.S.2d 884 (N.Y. 1979) (discussing privilege considerations when guardian is involved in minor's legal matters); Restatement (Third) of the Law Governing Lawyers § 73 cmt. e (2000) (presence of a person necessary to the client's communication with counsel does not waive privilege).

**Verification of credited outcome:** The original key (A) is correct. The father's/mother's presence as court-appointed guardian was necessary to protect the minor's legal interests, so the inference of non-confidentiality does not apply. The privilege is preserved. The credited outcome is **unchanged**.

#### Call and Prediction

The call is a binary yes/no: should the objection be sustained? The student should predict "Yes" because the guardian's presence was court-ordered and necessary to protect the minor's interests. The key is to identify *why* the "Yes" answer is correct — not just that privilege generally exists, but that the guardian's necessary presence defeats the third-party waiver inference.

#### Trigger Facts

1. **Nora is 16** — minor status triggers guardian appointment requirement
2. **Court appointed Hannah as guardian for the litigation** — establishes the guardian's necessary role
3. **State law required Hannah's joinder as co-defendant** — reinforces necessity
4. **Hannah present during Nora's admission to David** — third-party presence during attorney-client communication
5. **David declined representation** — tests whether retainer is required for privilege
6. **Paula called David as witness** — tests whether privilege survives against third-party subpoena

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice (A): "Yes, because all communications made by a client to an attorney while seeking legal advice are privileged."**

1. **C3 signal:** The word "all" is an absolute. Privilege requires *confidentiality*. A communication in front of an unnecessary third person is not confidential.
2. **Student label:** Overbroad privilege claim — "all communications"
3. **True/responsive version:** "Confidential communications made by a client to an attorney for the purpose of seeking legal advice are privileged." (Add the confidentiality qualifier.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The privilege protects only confidential communications. 8 J. Wigmore, Evidence § 2292. Third-party presence creates an inference of non-confidentiality. *Upjohn Co. v. United States*, 449 U.S. 383, 389 (1981).

**Choice (B): "No, because David never agreed to represent Nora and Hannah."**

1. **C3 signal:** The choice invents a requirement — agreement to represent — that the law does not impose on privilege.
2. **Student label:** Fabricated retainer requirement
3. **True/responsive version:** "No, because the communication was not made in confidence." (A real reason — but the facts show it *was* confidential since the guardian's presence was necessary.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The privilege attaches to the consultation for legal advice, not to a retainer agreement. *In re Lindsey*, 158 F.3d 1263, 1268 (D.C. Cir. 1998).

**Choice (C): "Yes, because the court's appointment of Hannah as Nora's legal guardian made Hannah's presence at the consultation necessary."**

1. **C3 signal:** The court appointment + state law joinder make the guardian's presence necessary, defeating the inference of non-confidentiality.
2. **Student label:** Correct — guardian's presence was necessary
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice is already true and responsive.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Third-party presence does not waive privilege when necessary to the representation. 8 J. Wigmore, Evidence § 2311; *United States v. Kovel*, 296 F.2d 918, 921 (2d Cir. 1961).

**Choice (D): "No, because Nora and Hannah are co-defendants in the action."**

1. **C3 signal:** The joint-client exception applies only when one co-client offers the communication *against the other*. Here, the evidence is being offered by an outside party (Paula) against *both* Nora and Hannah.
2. **Student label:** Joint-defendant confusion — rule applies only between co-clients
3. **True/responsive version:** "No, because the joint-client exception does not apply when the communication is offered by a third party against both co-clients." (This is legally accurate but does not support the "No" answer here — the privilege *does* apply against Paula.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The joint-client exception to privilege applies only in litigation between the co-clients themselves, not against outside parties. *In re Grand Jury Proceedings*, 727 F.2d 1352, 1356 (4th Cir. 1984).
