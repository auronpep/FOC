### 1. Final Question

Daniel was convicted of a drug offense in state court and sentenced to 15 years. The trial judge, not the jury, found the quantity of drugs by a preponderance of the evidence and applied a mandatory sentencing enhancement. In 2019, Daniel filed a federal habeas corpus petition challenging his conviction on other grounds, and the petition was denied on the merits. In 2025, the Supreme Court announces a new rule of constitutional law holding that any fact that increases a defendant's sentence beyond the statutory maximum must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Daniel's sentence was enhanced based on the judge's finding of drug quantity. Daniel now seeks to file a second federal habeas petition raising the new jury-trial rule. The court of appeals must decide whether to authorize the successive petition.

Under AEDPA, may Daniel file the successive petition?

**A.** No, because having litigated one habeas petition to final judgment, Daniel is barred from ever filing a second habeas petition on any ground.

**B.** Yes, if the Supreme Court has made the new jury-trial rule retroactive to cases on collateral review, because that is one of the two exceptions permitting a successive petition under AEDPA.

**C.** Yes, because a new Supreme Court decision always entitles a prisoner to reopen a previously decided habeas petition regardless of whether the Court declared the rule retroactive.

**D.** No, because Daniel waived the jury-trial argument by failing to raise it in the 2019 habeas petition, and AEDPA's abuse-of-writ doctrine bars claims that could have been raised in prior petitions.

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**B is correct.** Under 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(2)(A), AEDPA's general bar on successive habeas petitions has an exception: a second petition may proceed if the claim "relies on a new rule of constitutional law, made retroactive to cases on collateral review by the Supreme Court, that was previously unavailable." Daniel's claim is based on a jury-trial rule announced after his 2019 petition was denied — it was previously unavailable. But the exception only applies if the Supreme Court has made the rule retroactive on collateral review. Under *Teague v. Lane*, new procedural rules are generally not retroactive unless they are "watershed" rules of criminal procedure or substantive rules. The prisoner must also obtain authorization from the court of appeals before filing. If both conditions are met, the successive petition may proceed.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIMPRO-HABEAS-01):** *Under AEDPA § 2244(b)(2)(A), a successive habeas petition is permitted only if (1) the claim relies on a new rule of constitutional law that was previously unavailable, AND (2) the Supreme Court has made that rule retroactive to cases on collateral review. A new Supreme Court decision does not automatically apply retroactively on habeas — the Court must declare or demonstrate retroactivity.*

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. "No, because having litigated one habeas petition to final judgment, Daniel is barred from ever filing a second habeas petition on any ground."**
This is an overclaim. It states an absolute bar that doesn't exist. AEDPA § 2244(b) creates a default rule against successive petitions, but it provides two explicit exceptions: (1) a new constitutional rule made retroactive by the Supreme Court, and (2) newly discovered facts establishing actual innocence by clear and convincing evidence. The word "ever" is the tell — AEDPA does not impose a permanent, categorical bar.

**C. "Yes, because a new Supreme Court decision always entitles a prisoner to reopen a previously decided habeas petition regardless of whether the Court declared the rule retroactive."**
This is the dominant trap. It feels right — a new Supreme Court constitutional rule should help everyone, shouldn't it? But AEDPA and *Teague v. Lane* say otherwise. A new rule only supports a successive petition if the Supreme Court has made it retroactive on collateral review. The word "always" is the structural tell: it's an overclaim that ignores the retroactivity condition. Students who pick this are applying fairness intuition instead of the statute.

> **Gold Key callout (GK-CRIMPRO-HABEAS-01):** A new Supreme Court decision does NOT automatically apply retroactively on habeas. The Court must declare or demonstrate retroactivity. The Gold Key that kills this trap is knowing that retroactivity is a separate requirement, not automatic.

**D. "No, because Daniel waived the jury-trial argument by failing to raise it in the 2019 habeas petition, and AEDPA's abuse-of-writ doctrine bars claims that could have been raised in prior petitions."**
This is a distortion. The abuse-of-writ doctrine (codified in § 2244(b)(1)) bars claims that *were raised* in a prior petition. The related principle bars claims that *could have been raised* but were omitted. But Daniel couldn't have raised this claim in 2019 — the constitutional rule didn't exist yet. The "could have been raised" bar addresses claims that were available at the time of the first petition, not claims based on post-petition legal developments. The choice sounds right if you miss the timeline.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing doctrine:** AEDPA successive-habeas gateway, 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(2)(A); retroactivity under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989).

**Rule:** Under AEDPA, a second or successive habeas petition is barred unless the petitioner shows that the claim: (1) relies on a new rule of constitutional law that was previously unavailable, AND (2) that the Supreme Court has made retroactive to cases on collateral review. 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(2)(A). The petitioner must first obtain authorization from the court of appeals. 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(3). Under *Teague v. Lane*, new constitutional rules of criminal procedure generally do not apply retroactively on collateral review unless they are (a) substantive rules or (b) watershed rules of criminal procedure. The Supreme Court has rarely found rules satisfying the watershed exception.

**Application:** Daniel's claim is based on a jury-trial sentencing rule announced in 2025, after his 2019 petition was denied. The rule was previously unavailable. The exception applies only if the Supreme Court has made the rule retroactive on collateral review. If so, and if the court of appeals authorizes the petition, it may proceed.

**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(2)(A); *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989); *see also* Wex Legal Encyclopedia, "Habeas Corpus" (Cornell LII).

The core tension: a new Supreme Court constitutional rule is announced after a prisoner's first habeas petition was denied. Does the prisoner get a second chance? The C3 move is to spot the two-part exception: new rule + SCOTUS-made retroactive.

#### Student Access Test

A student who has completed the C3 lessons on habeas corpus and AEDPA should be able to:
1. Recall that AEDPA generally bars successive habeas petitions.
2. Identify the two exceptions: (a) new constitutional rule made retroactive by SCOTUS, (b) newly discovered facts establishing innocence.

4. Eliminate the other three choices using visible structural defects.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Daniel was convicted of a drug offense in state court and sentenced to 15 years. The trial judge, not the jury, found the quantity of drugs by a preponderance of the evidence and applied a mandatory sentencing enhancement. In 2019, Daniel filed a federal habeas corpus petition challenging his conviction on other grounds, and the petition was denied on the merits. In 2025, the Supreme Court announces a new rule of constitutional law holding that any fact that increases a defendant's sentence beyond the statutory maximum must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Daniel's sentence was enhanced based on the judge's finding of drug quantity. Daniel now seeks to file a second federal habeas petition raising the new jury-trial rule. The court of appeals must decide whether to authorize the successive petition.

**Call:** Under AEDPA, may Daniel file the successive petition?

**Choices:**
- **A.** No, because having litigated one habeas petition to final judgment, Daniel is barred from ever filing a second habeas petition on any ground.
- **B.** Yes, if the Supreme Court has made the new jury-trial rule retroactive to cases on collateral review, because that is one of the two exceptions permitting a successive petition under AEDPA.
- **C.** Yes, because a new Supreme Court decision always entitles a prisoner to reopen a previously decided habeas petition regardless of whether the Court declared the rule retroactive.
- **D.** No, because Daniel waived the jury-trial argument by failing to raise it in the 2019 habeas petition, and AEDPA's abuse-of-writ doctrine bars claims that could have been raised in prior petitions.
