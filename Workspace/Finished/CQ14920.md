### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** The farmer's warranty deed conveyed a fee simple determinable to the Bible college — the durational language "so long as the premises are used for religious instruction" creates a fee simple determinable, not a fee simple absolute. When a grantor conveys a fee simple determinable, the grantor automatically retains a possibility of reverter. That future interest is an outstanding interest in a third party (the farmer) that a prudent buyer would not accept without fear of litigation. Under the marketable title doctrine, a title subject to an outstanding future interest held by a third party is unmarketable. The developer is therefore justified in refusing to close, and the college cannot obtain specific performance.

**Gold Key:** A fee simple determinable automatically creates a possibility of reverter in the grantor. Durational language — "so long as," "while," "during," "until" — signals a fee simple determinable. The possibility of reverter is a future interest that makes the grantee's title unmarketable because a reasonable buyer would not purchase property subject to an automatic reversion.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect.** This is the dominant trap. It reaches the right conclusion (the college will not prevail) but for the wrong reason. The provision in the deed is not a restrictive covenant — it is a limitation that creates a fee simple determinable with a possibility of reverter in the grantor. A restrictive covenant is a promise about land use that runs with the land between private parties; here, the farmer retained a future interest, not a covenant. The title is unmarketable because of the outstanding possibility of reverter, not because of a restrictive covenant. Students who pick this answer confuse two distinct doctrines that happen to point to the same outcome. **Gold Key:** The possibility of reverter — not a restrictive covenant — is what makes this title unmarketable.

**C is incorrect.** This answer misapplies the doctrine of derogation from grant. The derogation doctrine prevents a grantor from undermining the estate conveyed by the same instrument — but here, the farmer conveyed a fee simple determinable, not a fee simple absolute. The possibility of reverter is not a separate act that "derogates" from the grant; it is an inherent feature of the estate conveyed. The warranty deed's covenants warrant against defects *at the time of conveyance*, but the deed itself created the determinable estate. The farmer didn't break his warranty; he conveyed exactly what the deed said — a fee simple determinable.

**D is incorrect.** This answer confuses the right to sell with the obligation to deliver marketable title. The college can sell the land, and the proceeds might well be used for religious purposes, but that has nothing to do with whether the title is marketable. Marketable title asks whether a prudent buyer would accept the title — not whether the seller can use the money. The charitable-trust concept is irrelevant to the marketability analysis.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** Marketable title is a title free from reasonable doubt — a title that a prudent, reasonable buyer would accept. *See* LII Wex, "Marketable Title"; Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 6.1. A title is unmarketable if there are outstanding interests held by third parties that could give rise to litigation, including future interests such as possibilities of reverter and rights of entry. *See* PastPaperHero, "Real estate contracts – Marketability of Title" ("Old possibilities of reverter or rights of entry still held by the grantor's successors ... can impair marketability").

**Fee Simple Determinable:** A fee simple determinable is created by durational language ("so long as," "while," "during," "until"). It automatically terminates upon the occurrence of the stated condition, and the property reverts to the grantor. The grantor's retained interest is called a possibility of reverter. *See* LII Wex, "Possibility of a reverter" ("A fee simple determinable and corresponding possibility of reverter are created by grants that use durational language, such as 'until' or 'as long as'").

**Application:** The deed's granting clause — "so long as the premises are used for religious instruction" — is classic durational language creating a fee simple determinable. The farmer retained a possibility of reverter. That outstanding future interest makes the college's title unmarketable because the farmer (or the farmer's successors) could reclaim the property if the condition is breached. The developer, as a reasonable buyer, would not accept title subject to this risk. The college cannot compel specific performance because it cannot deliver marketable title.

**Verification:** The tested rule (marketable title / future interests), legal issue (fee simple determinable / possibility of reverter), and credited outcome (title unmarketable; college does not prevail) are unchanged from the original.

**Authority:** LII Wex, Cornell Law School — "Possibility of a reverter" (https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/possibility_of_a_reverter); PastPaperHero — "Real estate contracts – Marketability of Title" (https://www.pastpaperhero.com/resources/ncbe-mbe-real-estate-contracts-marketability-of-title).
