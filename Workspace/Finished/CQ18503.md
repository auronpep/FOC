## Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**B is correct.** When the defendant offers a stipulation that fully satisfies the status element (here, that the defendant has a qualifying prior felony conviction), the probative value of the name, nature, or sentence details in the prior judgment drops sharply because those details are no longer necessary to prove the element. Under FRE 403, the court must balance the remaining probative value of the details against the danger of unfair prejudice. The name and details of a violent prior offense carry a serious risk that the jury will use them as propensity or bad-character evidence rather than for the narrow status purpose. When the risk of unfair prejudice substantially outweighs the now-reduced probative value, the court may (and here should) exclude the details and require the less prejudicial means of proof supplied by the stipulation.

**Gold Key:** When a defendant offers to stipulate to a status element, the probative value of alternative, more dramatic or detailed proof of that same element is reduced; the court must factor that reduced probative value into the FRE 403 balance and may require the less prejudicial form of proof.

## Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is the dominant trap.** It sells the attractive but incorrect idea that the prosecutor has an absolute right to prove every element "in the most dramatic way" and that the court cannot force a less prejudicial alternative when one is available. The breaker is the 403 balancing test itself (as interpreted in Old Chief): once a stipulation supplies the same probative value for the status element, the court must discount the probative value of the more prejudicial alternative and exclude it if the remaining value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice. **Gold Key** (the stipulation reduces probative need) directly defeats this choice.

**C is attractive because** it correctly senses that the details risk bad-character use, but it misstates the proper purpose and the remedy. The government is not offering the judgment "only to prove bad character"; it is offering it to prove the status element. The proper response is not to admit it for a forbidden purpose but to exclude the prejudicial details when a stipulation removes the need for them. This choice therefore fails both responsiveness and accuracy.

**D is attractive to students** who over-generalize the prejudice risk into a total ban. It is false because the fact of a qualifying conviction remains relevant and admissible to prove the status element; only the unnecessary name and details are subject to exclusion under 403 once a stipulation is offered. The choice fails the most basic relevance premise.

## Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule is FRE 403 discretionary exclusion of relevant evidence when its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice (or other listed risks). The specific application here is the Supreme Court's holding in *Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172 (1997).

In *Old Chief*, the defendant was charged with being a felon in possession of a firearm under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). He offered to stipulate that he had a prior felony conviction satisfying the status element. The prosecutor refused and introduced the full judgment record, which named the prior offense (assault resulting in serious bodily injury) and the sentence. The Supreme Court held that the district court abused its discretion under Rule 403. The name or nature of the prior offense carried a risk of unfair prejudice (luring the jury into propensity or bad-character reasoning). Because the stipulation provided alternative, relevant, admissible, and seemingly conclusive evidence of the prior conviction element, the probative value of the full record was reduced. When the risk of unfair prejudice substantially outweighed the discounted probative value, exclusion of the details was required.

The credited answer B is therefore correct under governing authority. The outcome, the rule, and the legal issue are identical to the source question. Authority: FRE 403; *Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172 (1997). No doctrinal-currency hotlist item (e.g., 2023–2024 amendments or cases) supersedes this principle for status-element stipulations.
