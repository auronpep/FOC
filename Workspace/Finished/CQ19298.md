### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Abuse of process targets the misuse of legal process *after it issues* for an ulterior purpose. Here, the teacher obtained a valid writ of attachment for equipment tied to the contract dispute — that was legitimate process. But then she used the resulting levy as leverage to coerce the tuner into surrendering a vintage guitar that was unrelated to the contract claim. That is a classic ulterior-purpose misuse: the process (the levy) was used to extract something the teacher had no legal right to demand. The underlying contract claim had probable cause and was still pending, but that does not immunize post-issuance misuse. The tuner is likely to prevail.

**Gold Key:** Abuse of process ≠ malicious prosecution. Malicious prosecution polices the *initiation* of groundless proceedings (and requires favorable termination). Abuse of process polices the *post-issuance misuse* of otherwise-valid process for an ulterior purpose. Probable cause to file is not a defense.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

The student who picks this one is merging two distinct torts. **Gold Key:** favorable termination is a malicious-prosecution element, not an abuse-of-process element.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Rule:** Abuse of process is a common-law tort that targets the misuse of legal process after it has issued, for an ulterior purpose not justified by the underlying action. The elements are: (1) a use of process (civil or criminal) that is improper, perverted, or irregular; (2) an ulterior motive or purpose — i.e., a purpose other than the one the process was designed to serve; and (3) resulting harm. *See* Restatement (Second) of Torts § 682 (1977); Wex, Cornell LII, "Abuse of Process."

**Distinction from malicious prosecution:** Malicious prosecution requires (a) initiation of proceedings (b) without probable cause (c) with malice (d) that terminate in the plaintiff's favor. Abuse of process has no favorable-termination requirement and does not require lack of probable cause. It polices post-issuance conduct. *See* Fleming, *The Law of Torts* (7th ed.) at 579–80; Restatement (Second) of Torts § 653 et seq. (malicious prosecution) vs. § 682 (abuse of process).

**Application:** The teacher's lawsuit had probable cause. The writ of attachment was validly obtained for equipment tied to the contract dispute. But the teacher's demand — surrender an unrelated vintage guitar or the levy stays — is a textbook ulterior-purpose misuse. The levy was process; using it to extract unrelated property is not what a prejudgment attachment is designed to accomplish. The tuner can prevail on abuse of process even though the underlying claim is still pending and had probable cause.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 682 (1977); Wex Legal Dictionary, Cornell LII (law.cornell.edu/wex/abuse_of_process); Fleming, *The Law of Torts* (7th ed., Law Book Co Ltd, Sydney, 1987) at 579–80.
