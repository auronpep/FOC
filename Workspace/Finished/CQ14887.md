## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**B is correct.** Martha's own prior wrist condition is a matter within her personal knowledge. If she had no wrist problems before the display wall collapsed and she claims a wrist injury after the collapse, that testimony has some tendency to make causation more probable. The evidence does not have to prove causation by itself to be admissible.

**Gold Key:** A lay witness may testify from personal knowledge about her own prior physical condition, and a fact that makes a consequential issue more or less probable is relevant.

**Silver Key:** On an admissibility call, do not ask whether the testimony wins the causation issue alone. Ask whether it moves the probability needle and fits the witness's personal knowledge.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A.** This choice sells the competing-cause instinct. A later workplace incident might matter to the jury's assessment of causation, but it does not make Martha's prior-condition testimony inadmissible. **Silver Key:** weight and admissibility are different calls; an alternative explanation usually gives the opponent a cross-examination point, not an exclusion rule.

**C.** This is the dominant trap. The answer reaches the correct result but gives the wrong reason: parties are still governed by the lay opinion rule and the personal-knowledge requirement. **Gold Key:** the testimony comes in because it is based on Martha's own perception and is relevant to causation, not because parties are exempt from Rule 701.

**D.** This choice over-demands expert status. Martha does not need medical expertise to say she had never had wrist problems before the incident. The expert line would matter if she tried to give a medical diagnosis or specialized causation opinion; this testimony is about her own prior condition.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are preserved. The original item asks whether an injured plaintiff may testify that she had no prior problems with the affected body part. The transformed item asks the same thing with a new accident setting and a different body part. The credited answer remains admissibility because the testimony is relevant and based on personal knowledge.

The authority floor is satisfied by the Federal Rules of Evidence. FRE 401 defines relevance in terms of any tendency to make a consequential fact more or less probable, and FRE 402 makes relevant evidence admissible unless a contrary authority excludes it. FRE 602 requires personal knowledge, which may be shown by the witness's own testimony. FRE 701 permits nonexpert opinion testimony when it is rationally based on the witness's perception, helpful to understanding testimony or determining a fact in issue, and not based on specialized knowledge. The current U.S. Courts Federal Rules of Evidence PDF reflects the 2024 amendments and includes these rules. [1]

Those authorities confirm the credited answer. Martha's testimony that she had no prior wrist problems is not a specialized medical causation opinion. It is a firsthand statement about her own condition before the incident. Because that fact tends to support the claim that the collapse caused the injury, it is relevant. Possible later causes affect the weight the jury gives the testimony, not its threshold admissibility. The supplied official key does not conflict with current Evidence doctrine.

**RC-EVIDENCE-PRIOR-CONDITION-WRIST-01 - Prior condition: relevance plus personal knowledge**

Signal: a plaintiff wants to testify she had no symptoms before the incident.

Student move: separate threshold relevance from final causation proof.

Tiny rule: no-prior-problem testimony can be admitted when it makes causation more probable and rests on personal knowledge.

Trap: rejecting the testimony because another cause is possible or because the witness is not an expert.

Confidence: `ANCHOR_ASSISTED`.

## Gold Key(s)

**GK-EVIDENCE-PRIOR-CONDITION-01**

A lay witness may testify from personal knowledge about her own prior physical condition, and a fact that makes a consequential issue more or less probable is relevant.

Type: `rule`.

Trigger: an injured party wants to testify that before the event she had no problems with the affected body part.

Authority: FRE 401; FRE 402; FRE 602; FRE 701.

## Silver Key(s)

**SK-EVIDENCE-WEIGHT-VS-ADMISSIBILITY-01**

On an admissibility call, do not ask whether the testimony proves causation by itself; ask whether it moves probability and fits the witness's personal knowledge.

Type: `clash`.

Trigger: a distractor says the evidence is inadmissible because another cause could explain the injury.
