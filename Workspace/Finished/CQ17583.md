### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The preclusive effect of the earlier federal securities judgment is governed by **federal common law**. The earlier judgment was a federal-question judgment — it rested on a federal statutory cause of action (the federal securities laws) decided in federal court. The Supreme Court has long held that the preclusive effect of a federal-court judgment on a federal question is determined by federal preclusion principles, not by the preclusion law of the state in which the federal court sits. **Gold Key:** the preclusion law that governs a federal-court judgment tracks the source of the substantive claim that produced the judgment: federal-question judgments get federal common-law preclusion; state-law judgments get the preclusion law of the rendering state. **Silver Key:** a federal court sitting in diversity is not automatically the right source — the question is the source of the *prior judgment*, not the source of the *forum*.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. The law of the forum state automatically, because all preclusion is state law.**

This is the dominant trap. The student half-remembers the *Erie* line of cases and assumes that preclusion is always a state-law question. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: the Erie rule governs the substantive content of the federal-court's *decision* when the case is brought in diversity under state-created law; it does not control the *preclusive effect* of a judgment already entered on a federal question. Federal-question judgments get federal common-law preclusion. **Silver Key:** the test is the source of the prior judgment, not the Erie posture of either case.

**C. The law of whichever state Mary prefers.**

This is the plaintiff-control wrong-frame. The student conflates preclusion law with choice-of-law or forum-selection principles. The breaker is structural: preclusion law is not a menu a plaintiff may pick from; it is fixed by the source of the prior judgment. Mary's preference has no role.

**D. No law applies until the same judge hears both cases.**

This is the wrong-court wrong-frame. The student invents a same-judge requirement that does not exist. The breaker is structural: preclusion applies across different judges and different courts; the prior judgment's preclusive effect does not depend on judge identity. Both cases are federal — the second federal court must decide what preclusion law to apply, and the answer is federal common law.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17583, a Civil Procedure item involving a federal-question judgment, a second federal action arising from the same transaction, and the question of which preclusion law governs. The official key is A: federal common law, because the earlier judgment was a federal-question judgment.

For lawyer confirmation: the Supreme Court has consistently held that the preclusive effect of a federal-court judgment on a federal cause of action is determined by federal preclusion principles, not by state law. See *Semtek International Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp.*, 531 U.S. 497 (2001) (federal common law governs the preclusive effect of a federal-court judgment on a federal claim; state law may provide a default rule where federal common law is silent, but federal law controls). The general federal common-law framework, applied in *Semtek*, also references the 18th-century English rule operative at the time of *Semtek*'s formulation. For diversity judgments (federal court sitting in diversity on a state-law claim), the preclusion law is that of the state in which the federal court sits — but the second action here is also federal, and the prior judgment was a federal-question judgment, so the diversity rule does not apply. The transformed outcome is identical to the original: federal common law governs the preclusive effect of the earlier federal securities judgment.

The doctrinal distinction the question is testing is one of the most-tested Civil Procedure distinctions on the bar: (i) federal-question judgments get federal common-law preclusion (the rule here), and (ii) state-court judgments get the preclusion law of the rendering state (the rule in the related companion question, QID 17704). The same framework underlies *Semtek*'s holding and its adoption of a uniform federal common-law rule for the preclusive effect of federal-court judgments.

## distilled_core_question

A plaintiff lost a federal-question case in federal court after a final judgment on the merits, then filed a second federal action against the same defendant on a different federal claim arising from the same transaction. ## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. Federal common law, because the earlier judgment was a federal-question judgment.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The earlier judgment was a federal-question judgment. Federal-question judgments get federal common-law preclusion.
2. **Student label:** Residual answer (federal common law on federal-question judgment).
3. **True/responsive version:** "Federal common law, because the prior judgment was a federal-question judgment."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** *Semtek International Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp.*, 531 U.S. 497 (2001): federal common law governs the preclusive effect of a federal-court judgment on a federal claim. The same framework was operative in the 18th-century English rule, which *Semtek* referenced as the default common-law rule. Federal-question judgments get federal common-law preclusion; state-law judgments get the preclusion law of the rendering state.

### B. The law of the forum state automatically, because all preclusion is state law.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The student imports Erie into a federal-question preclusion question. The Gold Key handles the axis: Erie does not control federal-question preclusion.
2. **Student label:** Erie-fallacy (all preclusion is state law).
3. **True/responsive version:** "State preclusion law controls when the prior judgment was a state-court judgment or when the second action is in federal court on a state-law diversity claim. Here the prior judgment was a federal-question judgment, so state preclusion law does not control."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** *Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938) governs the substantive content of a federal court's decision in diversity on a state-law claim. It does not control the preclusive effect of a federal-question judgment. The Erie-fallacy trap is the dominant wrong answer in this item.

### C. The law of whichever state Mary prefers.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Preclusion law is fixed by the source of the prior judgment, not by the plaintiff's preference. This is a wrong-frame answer.
2. **Student label:** Wrong-frame (plaintiff controls preclusion law).
3. **True/responsive version:** "Mary does not select the preclusion law by preference. The source of the prior judgment controls."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Preclusion law is not a menu a plaintiff may pick from; the source of the prior judgment controls the preclusion law that governs.

### D. No law applies until the same judge hears both cases.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Preclusion applies across judges and courts. The prior judgment's preclusive effect does not depend on judge identity. This is a wrong-frame answer.
2. **Student label:** Wrong-frame (no law applies until same judge).
3. **True/responsive version:** "Preclusion does not require the same judge. The second federal court must apply the preclusion law fixed by the source of the prior judgment."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Preclusion does not require the same judge. The second federal court is bound to apply the preclusion law fixed by the source of the prior judgment.
