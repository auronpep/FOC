### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

D is correct. The statute punishes a person who knowingly sells an intoxicating substance to a person under 21. Daniel knew he was selling the cider, but his strongest defense is that he did not know Esther was under 21. She looked about 25 and showed an altered ID stating that she was 22. If Daniel reasonably believed that age fact, the prosecution cannot prove the required knowledge as to the buyer's under-21 status.

Gold Key: When a statute uses "knowingly" for a sale to a protected class, a reasonable mistake about the protected-class fact can negate the required mens rea; uncertainty about what the statute means usually cannot.

Silver Key: Separate the two mistakes before choosing. The age mistake is a fact mistake that attacks the knowledge element; the cider-definition mistake is a law mistake about what the statute covers.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because it grabs the word "intoxicating" and treats actual drinking as the event that makes the item legally count. The breaker is the statute's act line: it punishes the knowing sale of an intoxicating substance to an underage buyer, not the buyer's later consumption or intoxication. A sealed 7% alcohol cider does not stop being an intoxicating substance because Esther never opened it.

B is attractive because the officer's timing feels unfair. The breaker is inducement. The officer did not persuade, pressure, encourage, or supply the fake ID; he merely watched Daniel complete the sale before intervening. Entrapment requires government inducement plus lack of predisposition under the common federal framing, and passive observation is not inducement.

C is the dominant trap. It is attractive because Daniel really was uncertain, and a student may treat any honest uncertainty as a mens rea defense. The breaker is the target of the uncertainty. Daniel was unsure about the statute's meaning, not about a historical fact. Gold Key: a mistake about what the statute covers is ordinarily ignorance of law, while the age-ID mistake is the fact mistake that can negate knowledge.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The credited answer preserves the original rule, legal issue, and outcome. The statute is not framed as strict liability because it uses "knowingly." Where a culpability term applies to material elements, the prosecution must prove the required mental state for those elements. Model Penal Code section 2.02 supplies the general culpability framework, and section 2.04(1)(a) recognizes mistake as a defense when the mistake negatives the required purpose, knowledge, belief, recklessness, or negligence for a material element. Daniel's reasonable belief that Esther was over 21 therefore attacks the required knowledge that the buyer was under 21.

Daniel's uncertainty about whether fermented pear cider counts as an "intoxicating substance" is different. That is a question about the statute's legal coverage. The ordinary rule is that ignorance of law or mistake of law is not a defense. Cheek v. United States, 498 U.S. 192 (1991), states the deeply rooted general rule, while Bryan v. United States, 524 U.S. 184 (1998), applies the traditional no-ignorance-of-law principle outside special willfulness contexts. Nothing in this problem creates a special statutory exception like the tax-willfulness issue in Cheek.

The officer's conduct does not create entrapment. Under Mathews v. United States, 485 U.S. 58 (1988), and Jacobson v. United States, 503 U.S. 540 (1992), federal entrapment analysis requires government inducement and lack of predisposition. The officer here did not induce the sale; he simply waited to observe it.

## wrong_answer_recovery_paths

A recovery: Return to the verb in the statute. If the verb is "sells," later drinking is not the element.

B recovery: For entrapment, look for government action before the crime that creates the crime. Waiting afterward is not enough.

C recovery: Ask whether the defendant is wrong about the world or wrong about the law's meaning. Only the first one attacks knowledge here.
