### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

California's strongest response is **A**. Article IV, Section 2, of the Constitution (the "Comity Clause") protects only the "Citizens of the several States" — that is, natural persons who are citizens of a state other than the forum state. A corporation, even one incorporated in another state, is not a "citizen" for Article IV P&I purposes; corporations must look to other doctrines (e.g., the dormant Commerce Clause, the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, or Equal Protection) to challenge discriminatory state regulation. **Gold Key:** the Article IV P&I plaintiff threshold is a person-class threshold, not a burdens-on-commerce threshold — and a corporation is not in the protected class at all.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. Article IV Privileges and Immunities protects corporations whenever they distribute across state lines.**

This is the dominant trap. The cognitive move it sells is the bridge from "the regulation burdens interstate commerce" to "Article IV must apply." That is the wrong bridge. **Gold Key:** the bridge is the *person-class* threshold — natural-person citizen of another state — and a corporation never satisfies it. Doctrines that do address this kind of burden (dormant Commerce Clause, etc.) are not before the court because Hymnsong pleaded only an Article IV P&I claim.

**C. Hymnsong wins automatically because the higher fee is a fundamental burden on out-of-state commerce.**

This distractor sells a tiered-absolute outcome: it claims automatic victory on the merits of a substantive "fundamental burden" theory. The breaker is the person-class threshold — Hymnsong loses before the merits are even reached, so there is no "automatic win" theory to apply. The substantive "fundamental burden" language also misnames the dormant-Commerce-Clause framework, which Hymnsong has not pleaded.

**D. Article IV Privileges and Immunities applies only to criminal defendants.**

This distractor overclaims the criminal-only frame. Article IV P&I protects civil economic rights of natural citizen-plaintiffs (e.g., the right to practice a livelihood, the right to own property) as well as rights historically tied to criminal procedure. The dominant misconception this distractor probes is "Article IV = criminal-procedure clause" — that is the Sixth Amendment's role, not Article IV's.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18586, a Constitutional Law item involving an out-of-state corporation challenging a state's higher filing fee, suing only under Article IV Privileges and Immunities. The official key is B: "The corporation is not a citizen for Article IV Privileges and Immunities purposes."

The Article IV Privileges and Immunities Clause provides: "The Citizens of each State shall be entitled to all Privileges and Immunities of Citizens in the several States." The Supreme Court has long held that the protections of Article IV extend only to *natural persons* who are citizens of a state, not to artificial entities such as corporations. In *Paul v. Virginia*, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) 168 (1869), the Court held that a corporation is "not a citizen" within the meaning of Article IV, but rather an "artificial being" whose right to do business in another state is governed by the *dormant Commerce Clause* and state corporate-franchise rules. The Court reaffirmed this person-class limitation in later cases addressing corporations and P&I.

The treatise treatment is consistent: *Corfield v. Coryell*, 6 F. Cas. 546 (C.C.E.D. Pa. 1823) (Washington, J.) catalogued fundamental privileges as belonging to "citizens," and the modern Court has continued to limit the clause to natural-person plaintiffs. As a result, when an out-of-state *corporation* is charged a higher fee than in-state corporations, Article IV P&I does not reach the claim; the corporation must bring a dormant Commerce Clause challenge, an Equal Protection claim, or (in some settings) rely on a specific federal statutory protection.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: Hymnsong (a Tennessee corporation) cannot bring an Article IV P&I claim against California because it is not a citizen for Article IV purposes. California's strongest response is exactly the person-class objection.
