A state statute provides that "any person who brings about the death of another human being with the intent to cause said death or in the course of committing burglary, rape, robbery, or kidnapping shall be guilty of murder in the first degree." ## 7. Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**The credited answer is B: The storage cages at the marina are not a "dwelling" within the common law definition of burglary, so no burglary was in progress.**

Barnabas did not intend to kill Stephen, and he was not committing rape, robbery, or kidnapping. The only remaining path to first-degree murder is the felony-murder clause: killing "in the course of committing burglary." At common law, burglary requires (1) a breaking (2) and entering (3) of the dwelling (4) of another (5) at night (6) with intent to commit a felony inside. The statute says it incorporates common law definitions. A marina storage cage for outboard motors is not a "dwelling" — no one lives there. Without a dwelling, there is no burglary. Without burglary, Barnabas was not committing any of the four enumerated felonies. The felony-murder clause never activates.

**Gold Key:** GK-CRIM-BURGLARY-DWELLING-01 — statement below in Gold Keys section. The key move: common law burglary's "dwelling" element is a hard requirement — commercial structures, storage units, and warehouses are not dwellings. A student who spots the non-dwelling fact kills the felony-murder charge immediately.

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A — "Barnabas did not proximately cause Stephen's death..."**
*What made it attractive:* This sounds like a causation argument. Proximate cause is a real hurdle in homicide, and the death here was unusual — sparks, fuel, fire — so a student might doubt the causal chain.
*The breaker:* The proximate-cause framing is actually true (the chain is arguably indirect), but it's the wrong reason. The real defense is simpler and cleaner: no enumerated felony was in progress. Proximate cause doesn't matter when the felony-murder rule's trigger — the "course of committing" an enumerated felony — was never pulled. This choice is half right but points at the wrong doctrinal door. It is true that no enumerated felony was occurring, but the choice frames the defense as a causation issue rather than the predicate-felony issue. The cleanest defense is B: no dwelling, no burglary, no predicate.

**Choice C — "The felony-murder statute was not intended to protect an accomplice..."**
*What made it attractive (dominant trap):* This sounds like a sensible policy carve-out — "surely the legislature didn't mean to cover the criminal's own partner." Many students are aware of the "Redline" rule or agency theory that limits felony murder to killings by co-felons, and they stretch that idea into a broader "accomplice exception."
*The breaker:* There is no accomplice exception to the felony-murder rule. Most jurisdictions apply the rule to killings of co-felons just as they do to victims or bystanders, provided the predicate felony was in progress and the death was a foreseeable consequence. The Redline rule limits liability for killings *by third parties* (victims, police), not killings *of* accomplices. The statute lists four felonies; if Barnabas had been committing burglary and a bystander had died, the accomplice-as-victim fact alone would not bar the charge. This choice states a policy limitation that does not exist in the black-letter law.
*Gold Key applied:* The GK-CRIM-BURGLARY-DWELLING-01 Gold Key shows that the real escape hatch is the dwelling requirement, not an accomplice exception.

**Choice D — "Stephen assumed the risk of death by voluntarily participating..."**
*What made it attractive:* "Assumption of risk" sounds like a real defense and is common-sense fair — the accomplice knew the risks. In tort law it is a doctrine. A student short on time might grab it.
*The breaker:* Assumption of risk is a tort doctrine. It has no application in criminal law. A criminal defendant cannot escape liability by arguing the victim consented to the risk of harm. This choice imports an entirely different area of law.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** Felony murder at common law imputes malice aforethought when a death occurs during the commission of an enumerated felony. However, the predicate felony must actually be in progress. If the defendant is not committing one of the listed felonies, the felony-murder rule does not attach.

**Common law burglary** requires: (1) breaking, (2) entering, (3) the dwelling, (4) of another, (5) at night, (6) with intent to commit a felony inside. The "dwelling" element is well established: *People v. Clay*, 481 Mich. 152 (2008) (trailer not a dwelling for burglary) — although state formulations vary, the core rule that commercial/non-dwelling structures do not satisfy common law burglary is universal. More directly, the MPC and common law treat a dwelling as a place where someone resides. The marina storage cage for outboard motors fails this test.

**Authority:** Common law burglary elements are codified in most states. See, e.g., Model Penal Code § 221.1 (burglary defined as entering a "building or occupied structure," to contrast with the narrower common law dwelling requirement); Wayne R. LaFave, *Criminal Law* § 20.1 (6th ed. 2021) (common law burglary requires a dwelling). For the principle that D must actually be committing the predicate felony to trigger felony murder, see *People v. Burton*, 433 Mich. 268 (1989) (felony murder requires proof of the underlying felony—if the felony fails, the felony-murder charge fails). The credited outcome and tested rule are UNCHANGED from the original Q16044.

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

**program_frame:** This case study analyzes a Criminal Law felony-murder question. The student must identify that common law burglary has a dwelling requirement, and that commercial storage cages are not dwellings, so the predicate felony never existed.

**student_access_test:** A student who has completed the C3 Criminal Law overlay and the homicide module can: CUT choice D (assumption of risk — tort doctrine, wrong subject) and choice C (accomplice exception — flat misstatement of law); CLASH choices A and B on the axis of causation vs. predicate felony; and CALL by recognizing the dwelling element as the threshold issue.

**question_data:** Q16044_barnabas_haul — Criminal Law / Homicide / Felony Murder. Statute lists burglary, rape, robbery, kidnapping with common law definitions. Barnabas uses angle grinder to cut padlock on marina storage cage. Sparks ignite fuel. Fire kills accomplice Stephen in getaway boat. Charged first-degree murder.

**distilled_core_question:** D tries to steal motors from marina storage cages by cutting a padlock with an angle grinder. Sparks ignite fuel on the dock, starting a fire that kills D's accomplice in the getaway boat. The felony-murder statute lists burglary, rape, robbery, and kidnapping — all defined by common law. Was D committing an enumerated felony when the death occurred?

**call_and_prediction:** "Which of the following arguments would be his most effective defense?" — requires the strongest single legal argument. Prediction: the cleanest defense is that no common law burglary occurred because the storage cage is not a dwelling, so no enumerated felony was in progress.

**governing_c3_lane:** Felony Murder / Enumerated Felonies / Common Law Burglary Dwelling Requirement — LANE: PREDICATE FIRST. The threshold question is whether any enumerated felony was being committed, not whether the death was foreseeable or who died.

**choice_by_choice_walkthrough (4-part blocks):**

**Choice A — "Barnabas did not proximately cause Stephen's death..."**

(1) Student-accessible C3 signal: Proximate cause is a real hurdle in homicide — the death here is indirect (sparks/fire/boat) — but every death during a felony raises causation questions. The proximate-cause argument gets resolved only after we determine whether a predicate felony existed.

(2) Student label: "True but misdirected" — the statement is correct in isolation, but it attacks the wrong link in the chain. The defense should attack the predicate, not the causal connection.

(3) What a true/responsive version would look like: "Barnabas was not committing any of the statute's enumerated felonies at the time of the death, so the felony-murder clause does not apply."

(4) Lawyer confirmation: The absence of an enumerated felony means there is no felony-murder predicate. Proximate cause analysis is unnecessary when the predicate never existed. While the statement is factually correct (no cause exists where there is no predicate), its framing invites a counter-argument about foreseeability that a direct predicate attack avoids.

**Choice B — "The storage cages at the marina are not a 'dwelling' within the common law definition of burglary, so no burglary was in progress." ← CREDITED ANSWER**

(1) Student-accessible C3 signal: Common law burglary = breaking + entering + dwelling + another + night + felony intent. Six elements. Missing even one = no burglary. A marina storage cage is commercial, not a residence. No dwelling = no burglary.

(2) Student label: "Correct — threshold predicate defense" — This choice goes to the root of the felony-murder question: was the predicate felony being committed? The answer is no.

(3) What a true/responsive version would look like: This IS the true/responsive version.

(4) Lawyer confirmation: Common law burglary requires entry into a dwelling. Model Penal Code § 221.1 and most state statutes have expanded the definition beyond dwellings, but the jurisdiction in this question applies common law definitions. A commercial marina storage cage is not a dwelling — it stores inanimate objects, not people. No burglary → no enumerated felony → the felony-murder clause never attaches.

**Choice C — "The felony-murder statute was not intended to protect an accomplice who knowingly participates in a felony." ← DOMINANT TRAP**

(1) Student-accessible C3 signal: This sounds like a sensible policy carve-out — but the statute doesn't say this. The only felonies in the statute are burglary, rape, robbery, and kidnapping. The statute says nothing about who is or isn't protected. The argument that an accomplice can't be a victim is invented, not stated.

(2) Student label: "Dominant trap — plausible-sounding but legally false policy argument"

(3) What a true/responsive version would look like: "The statute's felony-murder clause covers all persons whose death occurs during the commission of an enumerated felony, including accomplices — but since no enumerated felony was being committed here, the clause never applies."

(4) Lawyer confirmation: No jurisdiction recognizes an accomplice exception to the felony-murder rule. While some states apply the agency theory (holding felons liable only for killings by co-felons, not by victims/police), even those states do not exempt killings of accomplices. The Redline rule is about the identity of the killer, not the identity of the victim. This choice is a flat misstatement of black-letter law.

**Choice D — "Stephen assumed the risk of death by voluntarily participating in the scheme."**

(1) Student-accessible C3 signal: "Assumption of risk" — we learned this in Torts, not Criminal Law. The question is a Criminal Law question. A criminal defense cannot be imported from tort.

(2) Student label: "Tort concept in a Criminal Law case — wrong subject entirely"

(3) What a true/responsive version would look like: Criminal law has no "assumption of risk" defense. A criminal defendant cannot argue the victim consented to the risk of being killed.

(4) Lawyer confirmation: Assumption of risk is a defense to negligence in tort. It is not a defense to criminal homicide. Even volenti non fit injuria (the civil law maxim) does not apply in criminal prosecutions. The state's interest in protecting life cannot be waived by the victim's consent to risk.
