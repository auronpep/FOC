### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** The key is the offered-for purpose. Daniel says the crossbow discharge was accidental. Stephen’s testimony about prior violent confrontations with Peter is not being used merely to say Daniel is a violent person. It is being used to make intent and absence of accident more likely.

**Gold Key:** Prior bad acts are barred for propensity, but they may be admitted for a non-propensity purpose such as intent or absence of mistake/accident when that purpose is genuinely in dispute.

**Silver Key:** In a character-evidence array, lock the purpose first. “Admissible for intent” and “admissible because he is violent” are not the same answer.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells a foundation worry: Stephen did not know who started the earlier arguments. That side fact does not defeat the use of what Stephen personally observed — Daniel striking Peter — for the live purpose of intent or absence of accident.

**C.** This choice reaches an admissible-sounding result but gives the forbidden reason. **Gold Key:** the prior acts cannot be used to show Daniel is a violent person and therefore acted violently this time. That is propensity.

**D.** This is the dominant trap. It correctly hears the danger of prior-act evidence, but stops too early. **Silver Key:** the array is not asking “bad act or no bad act” in the abstract; it is asking what the act is offered to prove. Here the offered purpose is intent or absence of accident.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row: prior acts against the same victim are admissible when offered for intent or absence of mistake/accident after the defendant claims accident, but not when offered for violent-character propensity. The uploaded row states that the prior fights and beatings were admissible to establish intent or absence of mistake because the defense was accidental killing, and it also states that violent-character reasoning is not a permissible use. 

The authority floor is satisfied. Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b)(1) bars other-act evidence when used to prove character and action in conformity; Rule 404(b)(2) permits other-act evidence for another purpose, including intent, knowledge, absence of mistake, or lack of accident. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) Rule 403 still supplies the limiting balance if unfair prejudice substantially outweighs probative value. ([Legal Information Institute][2]) The U.S. Courts current-rules page confirms the Federal Rules of Evidence govern admission and exclusion in federal proceedings and notes that the Evidence Rules were last amended in 2024. ([United States Courts][3])
