## 6. Right-Answer Explanation (C)
At common law, the "breaking" element of burglary does not require physical force; it can be satisfied by "constructive breaking," which includes gaining entry through fraud, threats, or conspiracy. Silas used a fabricated "Diocesan Safety Inspector" ruse to deceive Sister Martha into opening the door. Because her consent was obtained via fraud, it is legally invalid, and the entry is treated as a breaking. Since the other elements are present (entry, dwelling house of another, nighttime, and intent to commit a felony [robbery]), Silas is guilty of burglary.

## 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations
- **(A):** This is incorrect because it overlooks the doctrine of constructive breaking. While physical force was absent, the use of fraud to induce the occupant to open the door fulfills the breaking element.
- **(B):** This is incorrect because the location of the entry (door vs. window) is irrelevant to the legal definition of a "breaking." A front door can be broken constructively just as a side window can be broken physically.
- **(D):** This is the dominant trap for students confusing merger. Burglary is a separate crime from the intended felony (robbery); Silas can be (and usually is) charged with both. Furthermore, "subsuming" a lesser charge is a matter of sentencing/merger, but does not prevent the defendant from being "guilty" of the burglary itself.

## 8. Black-Letter Verification
- **Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 892B (Consent induced by fraud); common-law precedents (e.g., *Nicholls v. State*, 68 Wis. 416 (1887)).
- **Rule:** Entry obtained by fraud or trickery constitutes a constructive breaking for the purposes of common-law burglary.
