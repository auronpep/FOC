## 6. Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** A traffic stop is a seizure. The stop's "mission" includes checking papers, writing the ticket or warning, and returning documents. Once the mission is complete, the seizure must end. Extending the stop for a drug dog sniff requires either (a) independent reasonable suspicion of drug activity, or (b) the driver's consent. Neither existed here. The prolongation was an unlawful seizure → suppress.

Deborah was stopped for a cracked tail lamp. The officer checked her papers, wrote a warning, and gave everything back. Mission done. At that point, Deborah was free to leave. Instead, the officer made her wait twelve more minutes for a drug dog — no reasonable suspicion, no consent. That's an unlawful extension of a completed stop. Dog alerts, narcotics found → suppress.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule:** A traffic stop is a seizure under the Fourth Amendment. The stop may last no longer than is necessary to effectuate the stop's purpose (checking license/registration, writing a ticket or warning, returning documents). Once the mission is complete, further detention constitutes a new seizure that requires either (a) independent reasonable suspicion of criminal activity, or (b) the driver's consent. A drug dog sniff conducted after the traffic mission is complete, without reasonable suspicion or consent, violates the Fourth Amendment.

**Authority:**
- **Rodriguez v. United States, 575 U.S. 348 (2015):** The Supreme Court held 6-3 that police may not extend an otherwise-completed traffic stop to conduct a dog sniff absent independent reasonable suspicion. The Court distinguished between the traffic stop's mission (addressing the traffic violation) and unrelated investigations (drug detection). The dog sniff is not part of the traffic mission. Prolonging the stop to conduct it is an unreasonable seizure.
- **Illinois v. Caballes, 543 U.S. 405 (2005):** A dog sniff of a car's exterior during a lawful traffic stop is not a "search" under the Fourth Amendment because it only reveals the presence of contraband and does not expose non-contraband items. This case is relevant but addresses a different question (search vs. seizure).
- **Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968):** The foundational case for seizure duration — a seizure must be temporary and last no longer than necessary to effectuate its purpose.

**Verification against source:** The source question's credited answer (A → "Yes, suppress — officer prolonged completed stop without RS") is fully consistent with *Rodriguez v. United States*. The legal substance (completed mission + prolongation without RS = unlawful seizure), the correct outcome (suppress), and all distractor mechanics are UNCHANGED.
