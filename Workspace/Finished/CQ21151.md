## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

**Stem:** Lydia is a merchant in Laodicea who sells imported fabric and dyes. She is arraigned on a charge of commercial fraud for misrepresenting the origin of her goods. Counsel is appointed to represent her. Three days later, a revenue officer from the city magistrate's office visits Lydia's shop. The officer tells Lydia he wants "her side of the fraud" and asks detailed questions about her supplier relationships and invoice records. The officer gives no warnings and never mentions that Lydia has a lawyer. Lydia admits she knowingly sold goods with false origin labels. She was not in custody during the conversation.

**Call:** Should Lydia's fraud statement be suppressed?

### 7. Right-Answer Explanation

**B is correct.** The Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches when adversary judicial proceedings begin — here, at arraignment. *Massiah v. United States*, 377 U.S. 201 (1964). Once attached, the government cannot **deliberately elicit** incriminating statements about the charged offense from the defendant without counsel present. The revenue officer did exactly that: he came to Lydia's shop, asked detailed questions about the charged fraud, and obtained admissions. He gave no warnings and never mentioned counsel. For the statement to be admissible, the prosecution would need to show Lydia made a **knowing and intelligent waiver** of her Sixth Amendment right — but a voluntary statement to an officer who never mentions counsel is not a waiver. *See Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938). The statement should be suppressed.

**Gold Key:** The Sixth Amendment right to counsel is NOT the same as the Miranda right. Miranda requires *custody + interrogation* and is triggered by the Fifth Amendment. The Sixth Amendment right attaches when *adversarial proceedings begin* (arraignment, indictment) and is triggered by *deliberate elicitation* about the charged offense — even without custody. When the stem says "arraigned" and "counsel appointed," lock onto the Sixth Amendment, not Miranda.

### 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original D):** This choice says the statement is suppressible because the officer didn't "Mirandize" Lydia — but that is not the basis for suppression here. It misidentifies the legal theory (Miranda/versus Massiah). The statement is suppressible under the Sixth Amendment deliberate-elicitation rule, not the Miranda custodial-interrogation rule.

**C (original B — DOMINANT TRAP):** This choice says "no, because not in custody" — and it is true that Lydia was not in custody. But custody is a *Miranda* requirement, not a *Sixth Amendment* requirement. The Massiah rule applies once adversary proceedings begin, regardless of whether the defendant is in custody. The stem's "not in custody" fact is designed to lead students away from the correct doctrinal basis. Many students correctly sense that suppression is warranted but then pick the wrong reason — "not in custody" is the trap because it sounds like it defeats suppression when it actually doesn't apply to the Sixth Amendment analysis.

**D (original C):** This choice says "no, because Lydia voluntarily answered" — and her statements were indeed voluntary in the Fifth Amendment sense (no coercion). But voluntariness alone does not constitute a **knowing and intelligent waiver** of the Sixth Amendment right to counsel. The officer never told Lydia she had a lawyer, never asked if she wanted to talk to counsel first, and never obtained any waiver. A voluntary statement without a valid waiver is suppressible under Massiah.

### 9. Black-Letter Verification

**Authority.** The Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches upon the commencement of adversary judicial proceedings (arraignment, indictment, preliminary hearing, information). *Massiah v. United States*, 377 U.S. 201 (1964). Once attached, the government may not deliberately elicit incriminating statements from the accused in the absence of counsel. *Id.* Deliberate elicitation occurs when the government, through its agents, acts with the purpose of drawing out incriminating information. *See also Maine v. Moulton*, 474 U.S. 159 (1985). A waiver of the Sixth Amendment right must be knowing and intelligent. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938). Mere voluntary cooperation with questioning does not constitute a waiver.
