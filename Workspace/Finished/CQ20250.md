Naomi received valid warnings, waived them, was questioned for a limited time, was allowed breaks, and was not threatened or promised anything. **Gold Key:** police deception is relevant to voluntariness, but a noncoercive false-evidence claim by itself does not automatically overbear a suspect's will. **Silver Key:** when the answer choices fight over absolutes, keep the middle rule: deception counts, but it does not decide the issue alone.

**C.** This answer is attractive because it reaches the same no-result as the credited answer. The breaker is its never-language. Deception can be considered in the totality; it simply is not dispositive on these facts.

**D.** This answer is the dominant trap because it treats a police lie as a due-process automatic loss. **Gold Key:** a false-evidence statement is relevant, but due process does not suppress every confession that follows a lie unless the totality shows coercion that overbore the suspect's will.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are preserved. The question still asks whether a false police statement about evidence, after valid Miranda warnings and waiver, automatically makes a confession involuntary when the interrogation was limited and included no threats or promises.

The governing layer is the Due Process voluntariness rule for confessions, applied through the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. In **Frazier v. Cupp, 394 U.S. 731 (1969)**, the Supreme Court treated an officer's false statement that an accomplice had confessed as relevant but insufficient, under the totality, to make an otherwise voluntary confession inadmissible. Source checked: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/394/731/. In **Colorado v. Connelly, 479 U.S. 157 (1986)**, the Court stated that coercive police activity is a necessary predicate to finding a confession involuntary under the Due Process Clause. Source checked: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/479/157/. **Moran v. Burbine, 475 U.S. 412 (1986)** also supports separating a valid Miranda waiver from later claims that police conduct withheld or distorted information unless the defendant's own waiver was constitutionally defective. Source checked: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/475/412/.

Those authorities support the supplied key. Ordinary deception about evidence is a factor in the totality, but it is not an automatic due-process violation and does not automatically undo a valid Miranda waiver. With valid warnings, a waiver, short questioning, breaks, and no threats or promises, the credited no-answer remains correct.
