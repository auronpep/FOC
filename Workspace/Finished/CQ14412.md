### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Lydia is trying to enforce Stephen’s promise not to sue. The only thing Lydia gave for that promise was a promise to pay the same fixed, undisputed fee she already owed after the due date had passed. That is not new consideration for Stephen’s no-suit promise.

**Gold Key:** Same fixed debt later is not new consideration.

**Silver Key:** Track which promise needs support. Here, Lydia needs Stephen’s no-suit promise to be enforceable. Her old duty to pay the fee does not support it.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells the idea that postponing payment of a past-due bill is automatically enforceable as a settlement move. The breaker is the Gold Key: a promise to pay an already-due fixed debt later needs new consideration to bind the creditor’s no-suit promise.

**C.** This is the dominant trap. It uses the word “bargained” to make the exchange feel complete, but it proves the wrong thing. Lydia may have bargained for Stephen’s forbearance, but the motion depends on whether Lydia gave new consideration for Stephen’s promise not to sue. She did not.

**D.** This choice correctly senses a consideration problem but points it at the wrong promise. Stephen’s promise to forbear from suing can support Lydia’s promise to pay later. The missing support is the other direction: Lydia gave no new consideration for Stephen’s promise not to sue.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The source row identifies QID 14412, Contracts, Formation of Contracts, original key A, and the original explanation that the creditor’s promise not to sue was unsupported because the debt was liquidated and the debtor promised only what was already owed. 

The governing rule is the common-law pre-existing duty rule. Cornell’s Wex explains that consideration is needed for an enforceable contract and that a party already under a duty gives no consideration for a modification by promising only the existing duty. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 73 states the same core rule: performance of a legal duty owed to the promisor, when neither doubtful nor honestly disputed, is not consideration. ([Open Casebook][2]) Foakes v. Beer is the classic common-law debt case: a debtor’s promise to pay the same debt later, or by installments, was not good consideration for the creditor’s promise not to proceed. ([IPSA LOQUITUR][3])

The transform preserves the legal issue and outcome. Lydia’s fee is fixed, undisputed, and already due. Her later-payment promise adds nothing new. Stephen’s no-suit promise is unenforceable. The motion should be denied.
