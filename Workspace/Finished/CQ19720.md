## Final Question

At a weekend Bible memory tournament, Daniel was irritated because Lydia had corrected his score sheet. Later, while Lydia was quietly stacking hymnals backstage with her back turned, Daniel stepped behind her and snapped a rubber praise-band bracelet hard against her upper arm. Lydia did not see Daniel, hear him approach, or realize any contact was coming until the bracelet hit and left a red welt. Daniel argues that because Lydia never feared or anticipated the snap, the apprehension element of assault was missing and he therefore cannot be convicted of battery.

Is Daniel's argument that battery requires prior apprehension legally correct?

## Distilled Core Question

If a defendant intentionally causes harmful or offensive contact with a victim who had no warning before impact, is lack of prior apprehension a defense to battery?

**D.** This choice sells the core confusion directly by merging assault and battery into one apprehension-based offense. The breaker is the offense split. Assault requires apprehension; battery requires contact. Lydia's surprise makes assault harder, but it does not undo the completed contact.

## Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. Common-law battery requires an intentional application of force resulting in harmful or offensive contact with another person; prior apprehension by the victim is not an element. Assault is the separate apprehension offense.

Authority layer: In **United States v. Castleman**, 572 U.S. 157 (2014), the Supreme Court described common-law battery force as the degree of force supporting an offensive touching, relying on the common-law meaning discussed in **Johnson v. United States**, 559 U.S. 133 (2010), and Blackstone. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/12-1371. Castleman is not an MBE battery-elements statute, but it is reliable primary authority for the common-law battery contact concept. The source rule also matches the standard bar-exam distinction: assault turns on apprehension; battery turns on completed harmful or offensive contact.

Daniel intentionally caused the bracelet to strike Lydia. The contact was at least offensive and also caused a welt. Because the rule does not require Lydia to know the contact was coming, the lack of apprehension does not defeat battery. The official key is therefore validated.
