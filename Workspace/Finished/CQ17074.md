## 1. Final Question

Peter agreed to buy Esther's commercial espresso trailer for a private Christian writers' conference if the county issued a temporary mobile-vendor permit for the conference weekend. The written contract required Peter to submit the permit packet and pay the application fee. After finding a cheaper trailer, Peter decided he no longer wanted Esther's trailer and intentionally never submitted the permit packet. When the county issued no permit, Peter refused to buy, arguing that the permit condition had not occurred. Which result is most likely?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A buyer promised to apply for a third-party permit condition but intentionally never applied. Can the buyer rely on the missing permit to avoid buying?

## 3. Final Answer Choices

A. Peter wins because the county, not Peter, had final authority to issue the permit.

B. Peter cannot rely on the nonoccurrence of the permit condition if his failure to make the required good-faith application caused it.

C. Esther loses unless she proves the county certainly would have issued the permit.

D. Peter wins because an express permit condition that did not occur excuses his duty to buy.

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is the best answer. A condition tied to a government permit can matter, but a party cannot deliberately block the step he promised to take and then use the missing permit as his escape hatch. Gold Key: a party who materially contributes to a condition's nonoccurrence through nonperformance or bad-faith inaction cannot invoke that nonoccurrence against the other party. Peter promised to submit the permit packet and intentionally did not do it. Silver Key: separate final approval from the required first step; the county controlled the final permit, but Peter controlled the application.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the third-party-control move. The county had final authority over the permit, but Peter controlled the required application step and deliberately left it undone. Silver Key: final approval and first-step compliance are different control points.

C sells the litigation-burden move. Esther does not have to prove with certainty that the county would have issued the permit before Peter's prevention matters. The breaker is Peter's promised application step and intentional nonperformance.

D sells the absolute-condition shortcut. Express conditions usually must occur, but the prevention doctrine stops a party from relying on a condition's failure when that party helped cause the failure. Gold Key: a prevented condition is treated differently from an innocent nonoccurrence.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 245 states the core prevention rule: when a party's breach by nonperformance materially contributes to the nonoccurrence of a condition of that party's duty, the nonoccurrence is excused. Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 205 also supplies the general duty of good faith and fair dealing in contract performance and enforcement. Peter's duty to buy was conditioned on county permit approval, but the same contract required Peter to submit the permit packet. Because Peter intentionally failed to make the required application after changing his mind, he cannot use the missing permit as a defense if his bad-faith nonperformance caused the condition's nonoccurrence.

## 10. Why The Variation Preserves Rigor And Difficulty

The variation keeps the same legal architecture: a contract duty conditioned on third-party approval, a promisor obligated to pursue that approval, deliberate nonapplication, and an attempted defense based on the condition's nonoccurrence. It preserves the strongest trap that final approval belonged to a third party, while changing the asset, approval context, setting, cast, and fact rhythm.
