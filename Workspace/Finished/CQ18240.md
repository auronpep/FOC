### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The court should **not** dismiss for lack of the amount in controversy. The diversity statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1332, requires that the amount in controversy exceed $75,000, exclusive of interest and costs. A plaintiff who pleads damages in excess of that threshold in good faith controls the jurisdictional inquiry, and the suit will be dismissed for failure to satisfy the amount-in-controversy requirement only when it appears to a **legal certainty** that the plaintiff cannot recover more than the threshold. **Gold Key:** "legal certainty" means a fact that the law makes impossible on the face of the complaint — for example, a statutory cap, an admitted contract limitation, or a binding prior judgment — not a party's prediction of what discovery will reveal. Here, Naomi pleaded $90,000 in good faith, no statute caps her recovery below $75,000, and no contract clause limits her damages to $40,000 or less. Paul's argument that discovery will probably show only $40,000 is a factual prediction, not a legal bar, so the court cannot dismiss at the pleading stage.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**C. Yes, because likely recovery below $75,000 defeats jurisdiction.**

This is the dominant trap. The student confuses the **legal-certainty** test with a **probability-of-recovery** test. The amount-in-controversy requirement is jurisdictional, but the jurisdictional inquiry is governed by what the plaintiff pleaded in good faith and what the law (statutes and contracts) makes legally possible — not by what either party predicts discovery will eventually reveal. **Gold Key:** if no statute or contract caps recovery below the threshold, a good-faith allegation controls and the court will not dismiss for lack of amount in controversy. Paul's "discovery will probably show $40,000" argument is a fact prediction, not a legal bar, and it does not authorize dismissal at the pleading stage. The breaker is the legal-certainty test.

**D. Yes, because the defendant disputes damages.**

This is the "damages-dispute = no jurisdiction" trap. A defendant's contention that the plaintiff's damages are lower than pleaded does not itself defeat the amount in controversy. Diversity jurisdiction is tested by the good-faith pleading plus the absence of a legal cap, not by the parties' factual disagreement. The student should recognize that contested damages go to the merits, not to subject-matter jurisdiction. The breaker is the same Gold Key: jurisdiction is governed by the legal-certainty test, and a damages dispute is not a legal certainty.

**B. No, but only if the claim arises under federal law.**

This is the wrong-doctrine trap. The student imports federal-question jurisdiction into a pure amount-in-controversy problem. The presence or absence of a federal question is irrelevant when the suit rests on diversity of citizenship and the amount in controversy. The student who picks this conflates § 1331 (federal question) with § 1332 (diversity). The breaker is the call: the question asks about amount in controversy, not about federal-question jurisdiction; the analysis is whether the pleaded amount clears the $75,000 threshold by legal certainty, not whether a federal claim is pleaded.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18240, a Civil Procedure item on diversity amount in controversy. The official key is C: a good-faith amount allegation controls unless it appears to a legal certainty that the plaintiff cannot recover more than $75,000.

The governing statute is the diversity jurisdiction statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a). The amount-in-controversy requirement is satisfied when the plaintiff's good-faith pleading alleges damages in excess of $75,000, exclusive of interest and costs. The leading articulation of the legal-certainty test comes from the Supreme Court's decision in *St. Paul Mercury Indemnity Co. v. Red Cab Co.*, 303 U.S. 283 (1938), which held that a federal court will not dismiss for lack of amount in controversy unless it appears to a legal certainty that the plaintiff cannot recover more than the statutory threshold. The legal-certainty test is met only when something in the law itself — a statute, a contractual provision, a binding prior judgment — caps the plaintiff's maximum possible recovery below the threshold. A defendant's prediction that discovery will reduce the actual recovery is a factual matter that does not authorize dismissal at the pleading stage; if discovery later bears out the prediction, the case may be dismissed for lack of proof on the merits, but the jurisdictional defect is not present at the threshold.

The probability-vs-certainty distinction is the central trap. Students who read "discovery will probably show $40,000" as a jurisdictional defect misapply the legal-certainty test by substituting factual probability for legal impossibility. The correct move is to identify what the law (statute + contract) makes legally possible — here, there is no cap below $75,000, so the legal ceiling is at least $90,000.

The damages-dispute distractor is the second classic trap: even an honest contention that the plaintiff will not recover the pleaded amount does not defeat jurisdiction, because the jurisdictional inquiry is governed by the pleading and the legal ceiling, not by the parties' factual disagreement.

The federal-question distractor is wrong-doctrine: federal-question jurisdiction under § 1331 has no amount-in-controversy requirement at all, so the call's "arises under federal law" condition is irrelevant to a diversity amount question.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: the court should not dismiss for lack of the amount in controversy.

### 2. The 17-section student case study

## program_frame

This is a Civil Procedure question on the diversity amount-in-controversy requirement. The parties are completely diverse; the plaintiff pleaded $90,000 in compensatory damages; the defendant argues discovery will probably show $40,000; no statute or contract caps recovery below $75,000. The call is whether the court will dismiss for lack of the amount in controversy. The central pivot is the legal-certainty test: a good-faith pleading controls unless the law (statute or contract) makes it legally impossible to recover more than the threshold.

## student_access_test

A student without the Gold Key sits in `anchor_assisted` territory on the probability-vs-certainty pivot, but the answer is settled by the *St. Paul Mercury* doctrine.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. No, because it is not legally certain on these facts that the plaintiff cannot recover more than $75,000.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the residual answer. After the wrong-doctrine and damages-dispute distractors are cut, the legal-certainty vs probability axis is the only axis left. The Gold Key resolves to legal certainty: no statute or contract caps recovery below the threshold, so the good-faith pleading controls.
2. **Student label:** Residual answer (legal-certainty test).
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Under *St. Paul Mercury Indemnity Co. v. Red Cab Co.*, 303 U.S. 283 (1938), a federal court will not dismiss for lack of amount in controversy unless it appears to a legal certainty that the plaintiff cannot recover more than the statutory threshold. Naomi pleaded $90,000 in good faith, no statute caps recovery below $75,000, and no contract clause limits her damages to $40,000 or less. The court should not dismiss.

### B. No, but only if the claim arises under federal law.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Wrong-doctrine. The student imports federal-question jurisdiction into a pure amount-in-controversy problem. Federal-question jurisdiction is not in play when the suit is grounded in diversity of citizenship and the call is the amount question.
2. **Student label:** Wrong-doctrine (federal-question import).
3. **True/responsive version:** "No, but only if the claim arises under federal law" MIGHT be true if the call were about federal-question jurisdiction, where the answer is yes-by-definition (no amount requirement). The call is the amount question, not federal-question jurisdiction.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Federal-question jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 has no amount-in-controversy requirement at all. The call asks about amount in controversy, not about federal-question jurisdiction; the analysis is whether the pleaded amount clears the $75,000 threshold by legal certainty, not whether a federal claim is pleaded.

### C. Yes, because likely recovery below $75,000 defeats jurisdiction.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the probability-vs-certainty dominant trap. The student confuses the legal-certainty test with a probability-of-recovery test. The Gold Key tells them: "legal certainty" means a fact that the law makes impossible, not a party's prediction.
2. **Student label:** Probability-vs-certainty (dominant trap).
3. **True/responsive version:** "Yes, because likely recovery below $75,000 defeats jurisdiction" MIGHT be true if the court used a probability-of-recovery test. The court uses a legal-certainty test; a probability of low recovery is not a legal bar.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Under *St. Paul Mercury*, a defendant's prediction that discovery will reduce actual recovery does not authorize dismissal at the pleading stage. The probability-vs-certainty distinction is the central trap.

### D. Yes, because the defendant disputes damages.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Wrong-doctrine / misfit. The student treats the defendant's factual contention that damages are lower than pleaded as itself defeating the amount in controversy. The damages dispute goes to the merits, not to subject-matter jurisdiction. The Silver Key tells the student: a damages dispute is not a jurisdictional defect.
2. **Student label:** Damages-dispute (no jurisdiction).
3. **True/responsive version:** "Yes, because the defendant disputes damages" MIGHT be true if a damages dispute itself defeated the amount in controversy. It does not; the jurisdictional inquiry is governed by the good-faith pleading and the absence of a legal cap, not by the parties' factual disagreement.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Diversity jurisdiction is tested by the good-faith pleading plus the absence of a legal cap, not by the parties' factual disagreement. Contested damages go to the merits; the jurisdictional defect is not present at the threshold.
