### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** When Naomi delivered the deed to Peter at closing, title to the vineyard passed to Peter at that moment. Recording is not required for a deed to be valid between the parties — it is only required to protect against subsequent BFPs. The creditor's judgment lien statute says a lien attaches to property "then owned or subsequently acquired" by the judgment debtor. At the time the creditor filed the judgment, Naomi no longer owned the vineyard — she had already conveyed it to Peter. Therefore, the judgment lien never attached to the vineyard.

Moreover, even if the lien could somehow reach the vineyard, the creditor cannot claim protection under the notice recording statute because the creditor is **not a bona fide purchaser**. A BFP must pay value for the interest acquired. The creditor obtained the judgment by operation of a lawsuit, not by purchasing anything for value. Since the creditor is not a BFP, the recording statute — which only protects subsequent purchasers for value without notice — is irrelevant to the creditor's claim.

Peter holds good title, and Lydia must close.

**Gold Key:** A judgment creditor is not a "purchaser for value" and therefore cannot qualify as a bona fide purchaser under any type of recording statute. The recording act protects only those who pay value — not those who acquire liens through litigation.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect** (dominant trap). This choice correctly observes that the judgment was obtained before Peter recorded, but it assumes that this timing matters under the recording act. It does not — because the creditor is not a BFP. The recording statute only protects "subsequent purchasers for value and without notice." A judgment creditor is not a purchaser at all, let alone one who paid value. The relevant timing is not recording vs. judgment — it is whether the debtor still owned the property when the lien was filed. Here, Naomi had already conveyed to Peter, so the lien never attached. **Gold Key:** A judgment creditor is not a "purchaser for value" and therefore cannot qualify as a bona fide purchaser under any type of recording statute.

**B is incorrect.** This choice invents a "threat of litigation" that would make title unmarketable. But the creditor's judgment lien never attached to the vineyard because Naomi no longer owned it when the judgment was filed. There is no cloud on Peter's title from this judgment. The risk of litigation, if any, is between the creditor and Naomi — not between the creditor and Peter as owner of the vineyard.

**D is incorrect.** While Peter does win, the reasoning in this choice is flawed. It focuses on the fact that Peter recorded before the creditor filed the judgment. But under a **notice** statute (not a race-notice statute), the key question is not who recorded first — it is whether the subsequent party is a BFP without notice. The creditor is not a BFP regardless of recording order. The correct reasoning is that title passed to Peter by delivery of the deed before the judgment was filed, so the lien never attached. Recording timing is a red herring under a pure notice statute.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Rule 1 — Delivery of deed transfers title.** A deed is effective to transfer title upon delivery and acceptance. Recording is not required for validity between the grantor and grantee. *See* Melendrez v. D & I Investment, Inc. (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 1238, 1251 (elements of bona fide purchase require payment of value, in good faith, and without notice); In re Marriage of Cloney (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 429, 437 (BFP without notice takes property free of unknown interests).

**Rule 2 — Judgment lien attaches only to property owned by the debtor at filing (or subsequently acquired).** The jurisdiction's statute expressly limits the lien to property "then owned or subsequently acquired" by the judgment debtor. Once Naomi delivered the deed to Peter, she no longer owned the vineyard. The creditor's judgment, filed afterward, therefore did not create a lien on the vineyard. *See* Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 697.340(a) (judgment lien attaches to real property interests of the debtor in the county where recorded); Nolo, "Judgment Liens in New Mexico" ("A properly docketed and filed judgment attaches a lien to real estate the judgment debtor presently owns").

**Rule 3 — A judgment creditor is not a bona fide purchaser for value.** A BFP must acquire the interest "for value" — meaning the person must pay consideration. A judgment creditor acquires a lien by operation of law, not by purchase. The creditor gave no value for the lien. Therefore, the creditor cannot claim protection under any recording statute (notice, race-notice, or race). *See* Texas Prop. Code § 13.001(a) (conveyance void as to "creditor" or "subsequent purchaser for valuable consideration without notice" — treating creditors and purchasers as distinct categories); Madison v. Gordon, 39 S.W.3d 604 (Tex. 2001) (BFP must pay valuable consideration).

**Rule 4 — Notice recording statute.** The jurisdiction's recording act provides: "Unless the same be recorded according to law, no conveyance shall be good against subsequent purchasers for value and without notice." This is a **notice statute** — an unrecorded conveyance is void only against a subsequent BFP without notice. Since the creditor is not a purchaser for value, the recording statute does not protect the creditor regardless of whether Peter recorded.

**Conclusion:** Peter received title by delivery of the deed before the judgment was filed. The judgment lien never attached to the vineyard. The creditor is not a BFP and gets no protection from the recording act. Peter holds good title. The credited answer (C) is correct, and the legal issue, rule, and outcome are unchanged from the original.
