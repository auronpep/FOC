## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Paul is a dealer who buys and sells rare Bible atlases. On March 1, Paul obtained from Daniel a particularly scarce 16th-century Bible atlas page in exchange for $800 and Paul's signed, written promise to re-deliver to Daniel "not later than November 30 this year" a comparable specimen of the same kind of page, without further charge to Daniel. On March 2, Paul sold that page to a collector as the centerpiece of a complete set. On September 1, the market price for rare Bible atlas pages began a rapid, sustained rise. On September 14, Daniel wrote Paul asking for assurance that Paul would timely meet his replacement commitment. Paul replied, "Given the surprising market, it seems unfair that I should have to replace your page within the next few months." After receiving Paul's message on September 16, Daniel called Paul, who said, "I absolutely will not replace your page until the market drops far below its present level." Daniel then sued Paul on October 10 for the market value of a comparable replacement page as promised by Paul in March. The trial began on November 15. If Paul moves to dismiss Daniel's complaint, which of the following is Daniel's best argument in opposing the motion?

**Answer A:** Although the doctrine of anticipatory breach is not applicable under the prevailing view if, at the time of repudiation, the repudiatee owes the repudiator no remaining duty of performance, the doctrine applies in this case because Daniel, the repudiatee, remains potentially liable under an implied warranty that the page advanced to Paul was genuine.

**Answer B:** Daniel's implied duty of good faith and fair dealing in enforcement of the contract required him to mitigate his losses on the rising market by suing promptly, as he did, after becoming reasonably apprehensive of a prospective breach by Paul.

**Answer C:** Anticipatory repudiation, as a deliberate disruption without legal excuse of an ongoing contractual relationship between the parties, may be treated by the repudiatee at his election as a present tort, actionable at once.

**Answer D:** When either party to a sale-of-goods contract repudiates with respect to a performance not yet due, the loss of which will substantially impair the value of the contract to the other, the aggrieved party may in good faith resort to any appropriate remedy for breach.

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** UCC § 2-610 — When either party to a contract for sale repudiates with respect to a performance not yet due, the loss of which will substantially impair the value of the contract to the other, the aggrieved party may resort to any remedy for breach. The aggrieved party does not have to wait until the performance date to sue.

Paul's September 16 statement — "I absolutely will not replace your page until the market drops far below its present level" — is an unequivocal repudiation of his contractual duty to return a comparable page by November 30. The repudiation concerns a performance not yet due (November 30 had not arrived), and losing that performance would substantially impair the value of the contract to Daniel, who gave up a scarce page with no guarantee of return. Under UCC § 2-610, Daniel was entitled to treat the repudiation as an immediate breach and resort to any appropriate remedy — including suing for damages — without waiting for the November 30 performance date to pass. Paul's motion to dismiss should be denied.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original B):** This choice is attractive because it correctly states the general rule that anticipatory breach requires the repudiatee to have no remaining duties, and it tries to manufacture a remaining duty (implied warranty of genuineness). But the implied warranty of genuineness runs from seller to buyer in a sale — here Daniel is the bailor/seller who transferred the page *to* Paul. Daniel made no warranty about the page's genuineness, and even if he did, that warranty would not constitute a "remaining duty of performance" that triggers anticipatory-breach doctrine. The doctrine's applicability here does not depend on any warranty issue. This is a **bait_doctrine** — it name-drops a real UCC concept in the wrong context.

**B (original A):** This choice tempts students who know that the duty to mitigate is important in contract law. But the duty to mitigate arises only *after* a breach has occurred — it does not require the injured party to sue promptly out of "apprehension" of a prospective breach. Here, Paul's repudiation *is* the breach (by operation of UCC § 2-610), and Daniel sued promptly after it. The framing that Daniel was required to mitigate "on the rising market by suing promptly" misstates when the mitigation duty attaches. This is a **misfit** — the mitigation doctrine is real but not responsive to the call.

**C (original D) — DOMINANT TRAP:** This is the most attractive wrong answer. It sounds sophisticated: "deliberate disruption without legal excuse of an ongoing contractual relationship" accurately describes what Paul did, and "actionable at once" reaches the right conclusion (Daniel can sue now). But it reaches that conclusion through the wrong body of law. Anticipatory repudiation is a *contract* doctrine, not a tort. The non-repudiating party's remedy is contract damages, not a tort action. Students who are drawn to the moral framing — Paul "deliberately disrupted" the relationship — are pulled toward tort language. The half-truth is that repudiation *is* a wrong and *is* actionable at once, but the "present tort" framing is legally incorrect. **Gold Key (UCC § 2-610):** The remedy flows from the UCC, not from tort law.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing Authority:** UCC § 2-610 (Anticipatory Repudiation).

**Text of UCC § 2-610:** "When either party repudiates the contract with respect to a performance not yet due the loss of which will substantially impair the value of the contract to the other, the aggrieved party may (a) for a commercially reasonable time await performance by the repudiating party; or (b) resort to any remedy for breach (Section 2-703 or Section 2-711), even though he has notified the repudiating party that he would await the latter's performance and has urged retraction; and (c) in either case suspend his own performance or proceed in accordance with the provisions of this Article on the seller's right to identify goods to the contract notwithstanding breach or to salvage unfinished goods (Section 2-704)."

**Application:** Paul's signed, written promise to re-deliver a comparable page by November 30 created a contractual obligation. His September 16 telephone statement — "I absolutely will not replace your page until the market drops far below its present level" — is an unambiguous, unequivocal repudiation of that obligation. The performance (return of a comparable page) was not yet due. The loss of that performance would substantially impair the value of the contract to Daniel, who parted with a scarce, valuable page in exchange for Paul's promise. Under § 2-610(b), Daniel was entitled to "resort to any remedy for breach" immediately, without waiting for November 30.

**Confirmed:** The tested rule (UCC § 2-610 anticipatory repudiation), the legal issue (whether the aggrieved party may sue before the performance date), and the credited outcome (yes, the aggrieved party may resort to any remedy) are all unchanged from the original.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Paul is a dealer who buys and sells rare Bible atlases. On March 1, Paul obtained from Daniel a scarce 16th-century Bible atlas page in exchange for $800 and Paul's signed, written promise to re-deliver to Daniel "not later than November 30 this year" a comparable specimen, without further charge. On March 2, Paul sold the page to a collector. On September 1, the market price for rare Bible atlas pages began a rapid, sustained rise. On September 14, Daniel wrote Paul asking for assurance. Paul replied, "Given the surprising market, it seems unfair that I should have to replace your page within the next few months." On September 16, Daniel called Paul, who said, "I absolutely will not replace your page until the market drops far below its present level." Daniel sued on October 10. Trial began November 15.

**Call:** If Paul moves to dismiss Daniel's complaint, which of the following is Daniel's best argument in opposing the motion?

**Choices:**
- **A:** Implied warranty of genuineness argument
- **B:** Duty to mitigate / good faith argument
- **C:** Tort-frame argument (present tort, actionable at once)
- **D:** UCC § 2-610 argument (aggrieved party may resort to any remedy)

#### Distilled Core Question

A dealer promised to return a comparable rare page by a date certain, then unequivocally refused before the date arrived. The owner sued early. Can the dealer get the case dismissed?

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks for Daniel's *best argument* against Paul's motion to dismiss. Daniel needs a rule that lets him sue *now*, before the performance date. **Prediction:** The correct answer will state that anticipatory repudiation allows the aggrieved party to treat the repudiation as an immediate breach.

3. **Performance not yet due (November 30)** — the repudiation occurs before the performance date, triggering anticipatory repudiation analysis.
4. **Rapid market rise** — explains Paul's motive but is legally irrelevant to whether repudiation occurred.
