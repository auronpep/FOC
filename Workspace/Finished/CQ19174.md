## 1. Final question

Naomi hired Barnabas Blasting LLC to remove a granite ledge on her privately owned retreat property. Before the blast, the company roped off a 300-foot exclusion area and posted signs stating, "Blasting today. Flying rock hazard. Stay out." Peter, who wanted a close-up photo for a Bible-camp brochure, read the signs, ducked under the rope, and stood near the ledge. A flying stone struck Peter.

In Peter's strict-liability suit against the blasting company, what is the company's best defense?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A plaintiff reads warnings about the particular risk of an abnormally dangerous activity, enters the danger zone anyway, and is hurt by that risk. What is the best strict-liability defense?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.**

Strict liability for an abnormally dangerous activity does not turn on whether the defendant acted carefully. The defendant's best defense is that Peter read the flying-rock warning, crossed the exclusion rope, and stood near the blast anyway. That is knowing and voluntary exposure to the very abnormal risk that injured him.

**Gold Key:** For abnormally dangerous activity strict liability, due care does not defeat liability; assumption of the particular abnormal risk is the defense.

**Silver Key:** When warning facts appear in a strict-liability defense question, ask which side of the warning matters. Warnings do not erase strict liability by proving care, but they can prove the plaintiff knew the exact risk and chose to face it.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

**Authority floor:** Restatement (Second) of Torts sections 519, 520, 523, and 524. Supporting research source for the abnormally dangerous activity framework: Cornell LII Wex, https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/abnormally_dangerous_activity.

Restatement (Second) of Torts section 519 imposes strict liability for harm caused by an abnormally dangerous activity, limited to the kind of harm that makes the activity abnormally dangerous. Section 520 lists the abnormal-danger factors, including the high degree of risk, likelihood of great harm, inability to eliminate the risk by reasonable care, uncommon usage, inappropriate location, and community value. That confirms why due care and public usefulness are not complete defenses once strict liability is in play.

Restatement (Second) of Torts section 523 recognizes assumption of risk as a defense when the plaintiff knowingly and voluntarily encounters the risk created by the abnormally dangerous activity. Section 524 distinguishes ordinary contributory negligence from knowing exposure to the risk. Peter read a warning about flying rock, crossed the exclusion rope, and was struck by flying rock. The same particular risk warned against caused the harm. The credited outcome is therefore unchanged from the source: the best defense is knowing and voluntary assumption of the particular abnormal risk.
