## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** *When two or more tortfeasors each contribute to a single, indivisible harm, and the plaintiff's medical evidence cannot apportion responsibility between them, the burden of that impossibility falls on the wrongdoers rather than the victim. Both defendants are jointly and severally liable for the full extent of the harm.*

This is the core policy-driven rule for joint and several liability with indivisible injuries. The question turns entirely on cause-in-fact: each defendant says, "You can't prove how much I caused." Choice **C** answers that directly — the law places the burden of non-apportionment on the wrongdoers. The injury is treated as indivisible, and the plaintiff recovers against either or both for the full amount.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (originally B) — ISSUE_SENSE / misfit.** This choice sounds responsible and legally literate: the defendants clearly did breach a duty of reasonable care (texting while driving). That is the trap's attractiveness — it states a true and important element of negligence. But the directed verdict motion is not challenging duty or breach. It is challenging *cause-in-fact*: each defendant claims the jury cannot determine how much harm that defendant caused. Stating that the defendants breached their duty does nothing to counter the apportionment argument. The element of duty has already been established; it is not the failing point.

**B (originally C) — EAR_FALSITY / backwards — DOMINANT TRAP.** This is the most attractive wrong answer because it uses powerful causal language ("each was the proximate cause in fact of all damages") that sounds like exactly what the plaintiff needs to say. But it is a legal blunder. "Proximate cause" and "cause-in-fact" are distinct legal elements. Cause-in-fact is the "but-for" or "substantial factor" inquiry — the very thing the plaintiff cannot establish for each defendant individually. Proximate cause (legal cause) is never even reached unless cause-in-fact is first satisfied. By saying "each was the proximate cause in fact of *all* damages," this choice conflates two separate doctrines and, if credited, would mean the plaintiff has answered a question she hasn't answered at all. The Gold Key tells you why this trap fails: the correct response is not to *assert* each caused everything — it is to explain *why the law does not require the plaintiff to separate their contributions*.

**D (originally D) — ISSUE_SENSE / wrong_element.** This choice correctly identifies the issue (joint and several liability for aggravation of a preexisting condition) but then merely restates the conclusion the question already asks for. The call asks: "The plaintiff's best argument [for joint and several liability] is that the defendants are jointly and severally liable because…" and this answer replies: "…because the defendants are joint tortfeasors who aggravated the preexisting condition." That is circular — it gives the *label* without the *reason*. The law does not impose joint and several liability merely because two people contributed to harm. It depends on whether the harm is divisible or indivisible, and that is precisely what Choice C addresses while D does not.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule:** At common law, when two or more tortfeasors cause a single, indivisible injury to the plaintiff, and it is impossible to apportion the harm between them, each tortfeasor is jointly and severally liable for the entire harm. The burden of the impossibility of apportionment falls on the defendants, not on the innocent plaintiff. This is a foundational principle of joint and several liability for concurrent tortfeasors causing an indivisible injury.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 875, 879 (apportionment of damages; where harm is indivisible, each tortfeasor is liable for the whole); Restatement (Third) of Torts: Apportionment of Liability § 26 cmt. g (noting the common-law rule that the burden of uncertainty in apportionment falls on the tortfeasor). See also *Summers v. Tice* (1948) 33 Cal.2d 80 (burden-shifting rationale applied where two defendants acted tortiously and plaintiff cannot identify which caused the harm, though the classic joint-and-several-liability indivisible-injury rule in § 875 is broader and covers sequential as well as simultaneous acts). Prosser & Keeton on Torts, § 52 (indivisible-injury doctrine).

#### Distilled Core Question

Two negligent acts worsen the same chronic condition. The doctor cannot apportion how much each caused. When defendants move for directed verdict on apportionment grounds, what is the plaintiff's best counterargument?

---

#### Call and Prediction

**Call type:** "Best argument… because" — asks for the legal reason supporting joint and several liability.

**What the call demands:** An answer that explains *why* the plaintiff can hold both defendants liable for the full harm despite being unable to prove each one's individual share.

**Prediction before looking at choices:** The answer will say something about the burden of apportionment falling on the defendants, or the harm being indivisible, or the law not requiring the plaintiff to separate the contributions.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "the defendants breached a duty of reasonable care that each of them owed to Hannah."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The directed verdict motion does not challenge duty or breach. It challenges cause-in-fact. Stating that the defendants breached their duty is true but does not answer the problem raised.
2. **Student label:** True but not the issue.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice that addresses why the plaintiff can recover despite being unable to apportion harm between the two defendants.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Breach of duty is established on these facts (texting while driving = negligence). The directed verdict motion challenges only cause-in-fact/apportionment. This choice is ISSUE_SENSE — misfit.

**Choice B: "each defendant was the proximate cause in fact of all of Hannah's damages."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This sounds like exactly what the plaintiff needs to say — but "proximate cause in fact" is a legal blunder. Proximate cause and cause-in-fact are two different things. And saying each defendant caused *all* the damages is factually implausible (the first crash didn't cause the second crash's contribution).
2. **Student label:** Sounds powerful but confuses two different legal terms.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice that correctly identifies why the law does not require the plaintiff to separate the defendants' contributions.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** This is EAR_FALSITY — backwards. It conflates proximate cause (legal cause) with cause-in-fact (but-for/substantial factor). Proximate cause is never reached unless cause-in-fact is first established. This is the **dominant trap**.

**Gold Key:** The correct response is not to assert that each defendant caused everything — it is to invoke the rule that when harm is indivisible and apportionment is impossible, the burden falls on the wrongdoers. Choice B is the trap that ignores this Gold Key.

**Choice C: "the wrongdoers, rather than their innocent victim, should bear the burden of the impossibility of apportioning harm between them."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This directly addresses the apportionment problem. The doctor can't split the harm — so who bears that uncertainty? This answer says: the wrongdoers do.
2. **Student label:** The burden of impossibility falls on the wrongdoers.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true and responsive version. It gives the legal reason why joint and several liability applies despite the apportionment problem.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** This is the correct answer. Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 875, 879. The indivisible-harm doctrine places the burden of non-apportionment on the tortfeasors.

**Choice D: "the defendants are joint tortfeasors who each aggravated the plaintiff's preexisting condition."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This restates the conclusion. The call asks *why* the defendants are jointly and severally liable, and this answer says *because they are joint tortfeasors*. That is circular.
2. **Student label:** Circular — restates the conclusion without the reason.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice that explains *why* being joint tortfeasors leads to joint and several liability — specifically, because the harm is indivisible.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Being joint tortfeasors does not automatically trigger joint and several liability. The key is whether the harm is divisible. This choice is ISSUE_SENSE — wrong_element (restates the conclusion).
