## 1. Final question

Luke, a skilled boatbuilder, sues Cornelius for the unpaid balance on a contract to refit Cornelius's commercial fishing vessel, including substantial hull repairs and replacement rigging. Cornelius has a related claim that the refit work was defective in ways that render the vessel unseaworthy and dangerous to operate. Full relief on that defective-refit claim, however, requires joining the naval architect who prepared the detailed specifications for the work. ## 6. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** Under the plain language of the governing rule, a claim that otherwise arises from the same transaction or occurrence is not compulsory if adjudicating it would require the presence of third parties over whom the court cannot acquire jurisdiction.

**Silver Key:** When the stem supplies a concrete reason why joinder of a necessary additional party is jurisdictionally impossible, match that exact fact against the exception clause in the rule rather than stopping after identifying "same transaction."

The stem states that full relief requires joining the naval architect and that the architect is beyond the court's personal jurisdiction. That fact directly triggers the statutory exception. Therefore the related claim is not compulsory.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A (original D mechanic).** This choice is attractive because students sometimes assume that any claim labeled "defective work" or "construction defect" automatically falls into the permissive category. That is not the law. Related claims can be compulsory unless an exception applies. The rule itself supplies the exception here; the category label does not control.

**B (original A mechanic — DOMINANT TRAP).** This is the classic trap: the student correctly spots that the claim arises from the same vessel refit project (the "transaction or occurrence") and stops. The rule is an "and" rule, not an "or" rule. The same-transaction finding is necessary but not sufficient once the stem supplies the unavailable-party fact.

**D (original C mechanic).** This choice sells the overbroad idea that counterclaims are somehow exempt from ordinary jurisdictional limits on party joinder. The rule expressly accounts for the situation in which the court cannot reach a necessary additional party; it does not pretend that personal jurisdiction magically appears for counterclaim purposes.

## 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested black-letter rule is unchanged from the source item: FRCP 13(a) defines a compulsory counterclaim as one that "arises out of the same transaction or occurrence that is the subject matter of the opposing party's claim **and does not require for its adjudication the presence of third parties of whom the court cannot acquire jurisdiction**."

The credited outcome is also unchanged: when the stem supplies the unavailable-party fact, the claim is not compulsory.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):** Fed. R. Civ. P. 13(a). The exception language has been part of the rule since its adoption and remains in the current text. No leading case is required; the rule text itself is dispositive. Research (Cornell LII, court rules repositories, and standard treatises) confirms the exception operates exactly as described in the source explanation. The official key B (now C after shuffle) is correct; no key disagreement exists.
