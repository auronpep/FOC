## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**B is correct.** The deputies found Stephen's stole in crop rows, not in Lydia's house or the immediate home area. The Fourth Amendment does not protect open fields, even when the land is private and fenced. Because Lydia had no reasonable expectation of privacy in those crop rows, the warrantless entry into the field does not support suppression of the stole.

**Gold Key:** Open fields are not Fourth Amendment protected areas. A fence, chain, private ownership, or a trespass label does not turn crop fields into the home or its curtilage.

**Silver Key:** Lock the evidence item and the time it was found. The motion targets the stole, and the stole was found before the later threat and confession; do not let later interrogation facts control the earlier field discovery.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A.** This choice sells the emotional weight of the later threat. The deputy's threat sounds coercive, so it invites a student to treat the entire episode as one due-process problem. The breaker is timing and target: Lydia moved to suppress the stole, and the deputies found the stole before the threat and confession. **Silver Key:** lock the evidence item and the time it was found before choosing a suppression theory.

**C.** This choice sells a familiar property instinct: if the stole belonged to Stephen, Lydia should not be able to complain about its seizure. The breaker is that Fourth Amendment standing turns on Lydia's reasonable expectation of privacy in the place searched, not on whether she owned the object found. Ownership of the stole is not the decisive element.

**D.** This is the dominant trap. It treats "no warrant" as automatically fatal because the deputies cut a fence and entered private rural land. **Gold Key:** open fields are not protected by the Fourth Amendment, even when fenced or privately owned. If the place searched is an open field rather than a home, effect, or curtilage, the missing warrant does not require suppression.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule is the Fourth Amendment open-fields doctrine. The Fourth Amendment protects persons, houses, papers, effects, and the home's curtilage, but it does not create a reasonable expectation of privacy in open fields. That rule controls even when officers enter private land, cross a fence, or trespass in the ordinary property-law sense.

Authority in the lawyer-confirmation layer:

- U.S. Const. amend. IV.
- *Hester v. United States*, 265 U.S. 57 (1924), recognizes that Fourth Amendment protection does not extend to open fields. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/265/57/
- *Oliver v. United States*, 466 U.S. 170 (1984), reaffirms the open-fields doctrine and applies it despite private land, secluded fields, and signs or barriers. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/466/170/
- *United States v. Dunn*, 480 U.S. 294 (1987), distinguishes curtilage from open fields and gives the curtilage factors: proximity to the home, enclosure, use, and steps taken against observation. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/480/294/
- Congressional Constitution Annotated summarizes the same rule and notes that fenced fields remain within the open-fields doctrine. Source: https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/amdt4-2-5/ALDE_00013719/
