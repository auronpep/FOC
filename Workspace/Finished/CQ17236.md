### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Daniel cannot rely on the signed consent form. A patient may revoke consent at any time before the contact occurs; the revocation need not be in any particular form. Once Hannah clearly told Daniel "I changed my mind. Do not inject me," her earlier written consent no longer authorized the contact, and the injection that followed was an unauthorized intentional contact — battery. **Gold Key:** a signed consent form is a license that the patient can revoke at any time before the act; revocation does not require written notice or any particular magic words. **Silver Key:** battery does not require serious injury; an unauthorized *offensive or harmful* contact is enough.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Yes, because written consent can never be withdrawn.**

This is the dominant trap. It treats a signed form as a contract-like instrument that locks the patient in once signed. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: a consent to a non-trivial touching is a present license that the patient may revoke at any time before the contact occurs, regardless of the form. The "never withdrawn" formulation is a flat misstatement of the law.

**B. No, because consent is never a defense to battery.**

This is the mirror-image trap. The student overcorrects, hears "revoked before contact" and concludes that consent never matters. The breaker is the foundational rule: consent is a defense to battery when it is valid, voluntary, and unrevoked. Hannah's revocation made the consent invalid at the moment of contact — not because consent is never a defense, but because this consent was no longer operative at the moment of contact.

**D. Yes, because the injection was minor.**

This is the "no harm, no foul" trap. The student maps battery onto a serious-injury requirement (the everyday-negligence frame). The breaker is the **Silver Key**: battery requires only an unauthorized *offensive or harmful* contact; the contact itself can be minor, cosmetic, even trivial in physical terms. A botox-style injection without consent is a battery even though it causes no lasting damage.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17236, a Torts item involving a signed consent form for a minor cosmetic injection, a clear pre-injection revocation, and a doctor who proceeded with the injection anyway. The official key is A: no, the patient revoked consent before the contact.

Battery is the intentional causation of harmful or offensive contact with another person. Consent is a defense to battery when it is voluntarily given, by a person with capacity, and not exceeded. A consent to medical treatment may be revoked at any time before the contact occurs, regardless of any earlier written form. The Restatement (Third) of Torts: Intentional Infliction of Economic Harm is not the right reference; the canonical statement is in the Restatement (Second) of Torts § 892A (consent is effective when given, and may be limited or withdrawn before the conduct in question occurs); Prosser & Keeton, *Torts* § 18 (consent is a defense to intentional torts including battery, but a license may be revoked at any time before the act). The leading case is *Mohr v. Williams*, 95 Minn. 261, 104 N.W. 12 (1905), in which the Minnesota Supreme Court held that a surgeon who performed a different operation than the one consented to committed an actionable battery; the broader principle — that the consent authorizes only the scope of the touching the patient agreed to, and may be revoked before the contact — is reflected in the modern doctrine of informed consent and the related medical-battery cases.

The contact here — a minor cosmetic injection — is the kind of "offensive" touching that fits within the Restatement definition. Hannah's pre-injection revocation was clear and contemporaneous. Daniel's decision to proceed with the injection was intentional. The contact was unauthorized at the moment it occurred. Battery is satisfied.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: the signed form does not authorize the contact after a clear revocation; the doctor is liable for battery.
