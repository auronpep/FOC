## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** **Gold Key:** An oral promise to pay another person’s debt is usually in the Statute of Frauds. The main-purpose exception removes the promise from the writing requirement only when the guarantor’s leading purpose is the guarantor’s own economic advantage.

Paul’s words create a secondary promise: if Lydia does not repay Barnabas, Paul will. The facts show the money went to Lydia for her puppet stage. Nothing in the stem gives Paul a personal economic stake. **Silver Key:** sort the defenses by whose promise they attack. The lawsuit is against Paul, so the best defense is the one attacking Paul’s oral guaranty, not Lydia’s loan terms.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**D.** The phrase “one year” activates a familiar Statute of Frauds bucket. The breaker is the lane: the responsive defense is the suretyship writing gate, not the neighboring one-year provision. **Silver Key:** when the call asks for Paul’s best defense, stay on Paul’s guaranty.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

The legal issue and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row: an oral collateral promise to answer for another’s debt is tested against the Statute of Frauds and the main-purpose exception. The supplied outline reference lists **54030400** as `Defenses to Formation or Enforcement > Statute of Frauds`, so that code is used everywhere. 

Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 110 includes “a contract to answer for the duty of another” among contracts subject to the Statute of Frauds. ([Open Casebook][1]) Restatement § 112 defines the suretyship requirement: the promisee must be obligee of the other’s duty, the promisor must be surety, and the promisee must know or have reason to know of the suretyship relation. ([Matthew Miner][2]) Restatement § 116 states the main-purpose rule: the promise is not within the suretyship Statute of Frauds if the consideration was desired mainly for the promisor’s own economic advantage rather than to benefit the third person. ([Matthew Miner][3])

Paul’s promise is collateral. Barnabas is the promisee/obligee. Lydia is the principal debtor. Paul promised to pay only if Lydia did not. There is no signed writing by Paul and no fact showing Paul’s own economic advantage. The main-purpose exception does not apply. The oral guaranty is therefore unenforceable against Paul.

The dominant no-consideration trap is not a valid defense. Restatement § 71 states that bargained-for performance may be given to the promisor “or to some other person,” so Barnabas’s loan to Lydia can support Paul’s promise even though Paul did not personally receive the money. ([Open Casebook][4]) The interest-rate distractor also does not defeat the claim because Restatement § 33 asks whether terms are reasonably certain enough to determine breach and remedy, and Restatement § 204 permits a reasonable term to be supplied when an otherwise defined bargain omits an essential term. ([Open Casebook][5])

Research does not contradict the official key.
