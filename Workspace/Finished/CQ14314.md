Congressional committees heard testimony from present and former holders of permits issued by state boards that license operators of private Christian family campgrounds and wilderness retreat centers. According to the testimony, the boards had unfairly manipulated their disciplinary proceedings in order to revoke the permits of some operators as a means of protecting favored operators from competition. In response, Congress enacted a statute prescribing detailed procedural requirements for the disciplinary proceedings of all state boards that license operators of such Christian family campgrounds and retreat centers. The statute requires the state boards to provide permit holders with adequate notice and opportunity for an adjudicatory hearing in all disciplinary proceedings. The statute also prescribes membership criteria for the state boards, designed to ensure that the boards are likely to be neutral. ### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C** is correct. Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment authorizes Congress to enact legislation to prevent or remedy state violations of due process, including unfair licensing procedures that favor some operators over others. The statute seeks to prevent and/or remedy due process violations against permit holders (via favoritism and unfair disciplinary proceedings) and its requirements (notice, hearing, neutral board membership) are congruent with and proportional to that discrimination by the state boards. **Gold Key:** Section 5 allows Congress to enact congruent and proportional remedial legislation addressing state due process violations; it is not limited to tax-and-spend measures and does not require out-of-state discrimination or a physical taking. **Silver Key:** match the call ("best source of authority for this federal statute") to the precise enforcement power that fits the facts of state procedural unfairness, then cut the neighboring doctrines that sound close but require different facts.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. The general welfare clause of Article I, Section 8.**

This is the dominant trap because the call asks for congressional authority and "general welfare" sounds like a broad power to protect the public or licensees from unfair state boards. The breaker is the scope of the clause: it authorizes Congress to tax and spend for the general welfare, not to impose direct regulatory requirements on state licensing boards' disciplinary procedures. **Gold Key:** the general welfare clause does not give Congress a free-standing power to regulate state vocational or permitting processes.

**B. The privileges and immunities clause of Article IV, Section 2.**

This is attractive because the statute requires "neutral" board membership and the call is about authority to impose fairness rules. The breaker is the facts the clause requires: it prevents states from discriminating against citizens of other states in fundamental rights and essential activities. There are no facts here about out-of-state operators or discrimination on the basis of state citizenship. **Silver Key:** do not let a single attractive word ("neutral") pull you into the wrong neighboring doctrine when the stem shows no interstate-citizenship facts.

**D. The takings clause of the Fifth Amendment.**

This is attractive because the statute imposes new procedural burdens on state boards and "takings" sounds like a limit on government action affecting property interests in licenses. The breaker is the absence of any taking: the statute does not authorize the federal government to take anyone's property; it imposes procedural safeguards on state disciplinary proceedings. **Silver Key:** when the call is "source of authority for this federal statute," cut any choice whose doctrine describes a limit on government power rather than a grant of power to Congress.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row: Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment supplies the authority for this federal statute because the statute seeks to prevent and/or remedy due process violations by state licensing boards (favoritism and unfair disciplinary proceedings that revoke permits to protect favored operators) and the statute's requirements are congruent with and proportional to those violations.

Lawyer-confirmation authority: U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 5 ("The Congress shall have power to enforce, by appropriate legislation, the provisions of this article."). The governing standard for the scope of "appropriate legislation" under Section 5 is set forth in *City of Boerne v. Flores*, 521 U.S. 507 (1997): legislation is appropriate if it seeks to prevent or remedy actions by state or local governments that violate provisions of the Fourteenth Amendment and its requirements are congruent with and proportional to the constitutional violations it addresses. Procedural due process under the Fourteenth Amendment requires states to provide adequate notice and an opportunity to be heard before depriving a person of a protected property interest (such as a license or permit to operate a business). See *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319 (1976) (balancing test for what process is due); *Greene v. McElroy*, 360 U.S. 474 (1959) (procedural protections attach to government action affecting the right to follow a chosen profession).

The general welfare clause (U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 1) authorizes Congress to tax and spend for the general welfare but does not confer a general regulatory power. *United States v. Butler*, 297 U.S. 1 (1936) (the clause is not a source of power to regulate agricultural production through a tax-and-subsidy scheme that effectively controlled production). The Privileges and Immunities Clause of Article IV, § 2 protects state residents from discrimination by other states in fundamental rights and essential activities; it has no application where the statute addresses in-state favoritism without regard to state citizenship. The Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment prohibits the federal government from taking private property for public use without just compensation; the statute here imposes no taking.
