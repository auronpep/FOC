Lydia owned a small brick building in the city where she ran a Christian calligraphy studio. She borrowed $180,000 from First Harbor Bank and gave the bank a promissory note secured by a mortgage on the building. Lydia later defaulted on her obligation to First Harbor. First Harbor validly accelerated the debt and began nonjudicial foreclosure proceedings as permitted by the jurisdiction. Cedar received notice of the foreclosure sale but did not attend the sale or otherwise act. At the foreclosure sale, Ruth, who was not acting in collusion with Lydia, outbid all other bidders and received a deed to the building. Does Cedar continue to have a valid mortgage on the building?

7. **Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)**

**Gold Key:** A valid senior mortgage foreclosure cuts off junior interests whose holders received the required notice or were properly joined; a later noncollusive sale back to the original owner does not revive the destroyed junior lien.

**Silver Key:** In a because-style mortgage answer, do not stop at the yes/no result. The reason must identify the lien event that legally did the work.

Cedar had a junior mortgage. First Harbor had the senior mortgage, validly accelerated the debt, and validly foreclosed under the jurisdiction's nonjudicial foreclosure procedure. Cedar received notice of the sale but did not appear, bid, redeem, or otherwise protect its interest. Ruth was an arm's-length foreclosure buyer, not a straw purchaser for Lydia. That sale cut off Cedar's junior lien. Lydia later received whatever title Ruth could convey: title free of Cedar's already-destroyed lien. Choice B is therefore the true and responsive answer.

8. **Full wrong-answer explanations**

**A.** The attractive move is the title-reacquisition shortcut. Lydia did end up owning the building again, but title returning to the original owner is not enough to revive a junior lien that was already eliminated by a valid senior foreclosure through a noncollusive buyer.

**C.** The attractive move is a correct-result/wrong-reason shortcut. The answer reaches the right "no" outcome, but the due-on-sale clause did not eliminate Cedar's lien; the valid senior foreclosure with notice did. **Silver Key:** the because-clause matters.

**D.** The attractive move is the familiar-sounding title doctrine. Estoppel by deed deals with a grantor who purports to convey an estate the grantor does not yet have and later acquires it; it does not restore a junior mortgage after a noticed senior foreclosure. The **Gold Key** kills the trap: the senior foreclosure, not later title vocabulary, controls the junior lien's fate.

9. **Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer**

The tested rule is unchanged: a valid foreclosure of a senior mortgage terminates junior interests in the foreclosed real estate when the junior interest holders are properly joined or notified under applicable law. Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 7.1 states the governing mortgage-priority rule; § 4.9 addresses the special problem of acquisition by the equity-of-redemption holder and distinguishes a direct or collusive mortgagor purchase from a bona fide third-party purchase followed by a later sale back to the prior owner.

Applied here, First Harbor was senior, Cedar was junior, Cedar received notice, and Ruth bought at the foreclosure sale without collusion. Ruth therefore took title free of Cedar's junior mortgage lien, and Lydia later took the same free-and-clear title from Ruth. No researched authority contradicts the supplied key. D recovery: Match doctrine to posture. Estoppel by deed is a deed/title-acquisition doctrine, not the mortgage foreclosure priority rule.

## review_truth

A valid senior foreclosure with proper notice cuts off junior mortgage liens, and a later noncollusive reacquisition from a bona fide buyer does not revive them.
