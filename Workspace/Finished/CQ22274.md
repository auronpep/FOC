## 1. Final question — stem + call

A roadside produce market in a farming county was robbed by two people wearing matching curved ram's-horn masks. Several customers gave officers a clear description of the masks. Officers came to believe that a local woman, Hannah, and her friend Lydia had committed the robbery. The officers obtained valid arrest warrants for both Hannah and Lydia and drove to Hannah's farmhouse to arrest her. When they knocked, they heard hurried shuffling inside that led them to believe Hannah was trying to slip out the back. The officers announced themselves and stepped inside. During the arrest, one officer went into the back room to see whether Lydia was inside. The room held a tall standing wardrobe with its door closed. ## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**B is correct.** The officers were lawfully inside on valid arrest warrants and had already arrested Hannah. They knew a second robber, Lydia, was at large and might be in the farmhouse. That gives the reasonable, articulable suspicion that lets an arresting officer make a quick **protective sweep** of spaces big enough to hide a person — and a tall standing wardrobe is exactly such a space. Because the sweep was lawful, the two masks sitting in plain view inside the open wardrobe are admissible. **Gold Key:** search incident to arrest reaches only the arrestee's wingspan at the moment of arrest; to look elsewhere in the home for hidden accomplices you need the protective-sweep doctrine, and plain-view evidence found during a lawful sweep comes in. **Silver Key:** when two choices share the same yes/no conclusion, the credited one is the rationale whose factual trigger is actually present — a known accomplice plus a person-sized space is a sweep, not the first plausible "No."

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. No, because the back-room search was incident to the arrest.** This sells the instinct that any search around an arrest rides along on the arrest itself, and it has the comfort of reaching the right outcome (admit). The breaker: search incident to arrest reaches only the area within the arrestee's immediate control at the moment of arrest — Hannah was seized in the doorway after pulling the back-room door shut, so the wardrobe in the next room is outside her wingspan. The justification that actually fits is the protective sweep. **Gold Key:** wingspan-only for incident-to-arrest; use the protective-sweep doctrine to reach hidden-accomplice spaces.

**C. Yes, because the officers had no search warrant for the farmhouse.** This rides the familiar "no warrant, no search" reflex. The breaker: a warrant is the default, not an absolute — a valid protective sweep is one of the recognized exceptions, so the absence of a search warrant does not by itself force suppression.

**D. Yes, because the wardrobe door was closed.** This trades on the feeling that a shut door marks a private zone the police may not cross. The breaker: a closed, person-sized container is a perfectly valid place to look during a protective sweep precisely because a person could be hiding inside; the closed door does not shield it.

## 9. Black-letter verification + affirmative legal reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are UNCHANGED from QID 22274. Governing authority:

- **Protective sweep:** *Maryland v. Buie*, 494 U.S. 325 (1990) — incident to an in-home arrest, officers may, as a precautionary matter and without probable cause or a warrant, look in closets and other spaces immediately adjoining the place of arrest from which an attack could be launched; and, on reasonable articulable suspicion that the area harbors a dangerous individual, sweep a wider protective area limited to places where a person could hide. Evidence in plain view during a lawful sweep is admissible.
- **Wingspan limit on search incident to arrest:** *Chimel v. California*, 395 U.S. 752 (1969) — a search incident to arrest is limited to the arrestee's person and the area within his immediate control. This is why choice A fails: the wardrobe is beyond Hannah's reach at the point of arrest.
- **Plain view:** *Horton v. California*, 496 U.S. 128 (1990) — incriminating items observed from a lawful vantage point may be seized without a warrant.

Here the officers were lawfully present executing valid arrest warrants, knew of an at-large accomplice (reasonable suspicion), and confined the intrusion to a person-sized wardrobe in an adjoining room; the masks were in plain view. Credited answer = admit (not suppressed). No authority contradicts the official key.
