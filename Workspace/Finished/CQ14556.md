### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.**

**Gold Key:** Do not trade a bad arrest for a dismissed indictment. An unlawful arrest can support a targeted remedy, but it does not by itself immunize the defendant from prosecution.

**Silver Key:** This answer array is a two-defendant 2×2 matrix. Do not first ask whose arrest was worse. First ask whether the requested remedy—dismissal of the indictment—is available at all. Because that remedy is unavailable for either defendant, the “deny both” cell wins.

Daniel’s seizure abroad and Lydia’s warrantless home arrest may create serious Fourth Amendment or other remedial questions. But the motion in the call is a motion to dismiss the indictments. That is the wrong remedy. The indictments are not dismissed merely because the defendants were brought before the court through unlawful arrests.

### 9. Black-letter verification and legal reasoning

The credited outcome is legally sound. The Supreme Court has stated that an illegal arrest, without more, is not a bar to later prosecution and is not a defense to a valid conviction; the defendant is not himself a suppressible “fruit.” ([Justia Law][1]) Gerstein also states that illegal arrest or detention does not void a later conviction. ([Justia Law][2])

For Daniel’s cross-border seizure, the close authority is the Ker-Frisbie line as applied in *United States v. Alvarez-Machain*, where the Court held that a defendant abducted from Mexico did not thereby acquire a defense to the jurisdiction of U.S. courts and could be tried in federal court. ([Legal Information Institute][3])

For Lydia’s home arrest, *Payton v. New York* confirms that warrantless, nonconsensual entry into a suspect’s home to make a routine felony arrest violates the Fourth Amendment absent exigent circumstances. That supports why the trap is attractive, but it does not change the remedy asked for here. ([Justia Law][4])
