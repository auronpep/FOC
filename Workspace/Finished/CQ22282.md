Lydia operated a booth that sold handmade Scripture journals and hymnbook covers at local Christian festivals. She wanted to set up at a city-sponsored hymn festival in the public square, but she worried that the city licensing office would not approve her vendor permit in time. A statute in the jurisdiction provides that "[a]ny person who shall give or accept a fee not authorized by law as consideration for the act of any public employee is guilty of bribery, a felony. Any person who shall offer to commit a bribery is guilty of bribery in the second degree, a felony."

If the jurisdiction applies the common law definition of conspiracy, of which of the following crimes can the clerk properly be convicted?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. The clerk offered to accept an unauthorized payment in exchange for an act as a public employee, so the statute's second-degree bribery provision is satisfied even though Lydia arrived after the clerk had been fired. Gold Key: Wharton's Rule blocks a separate common-law conspiracy charge when the agreed crime necessarily requires the same two willing participants and no extra participant is involved. Silver Key: once the statute makes the clerk's offer its own crime, the answer-array fight is whether conspiracy can be added; the two-party bribery setup cuts off that add-on.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is the dominant trap. It sells the familiar idea that conspiracy is separate from the target offense and usually does not merge. The breaker is the Gold Key: Wharton's Rule is the special common-law exception for a necessarily two-party offense such as bribery when only the necessary two participants agreed.

C is attractive because the facts contain an agreement and the clerk was gone before any money changed hands. The breaker is that the statute separately punishes an offer to commit bribery, so the clerk's offer already completed second-degree bribery; the same two-party bribery setup does not supply a separate conspiracy.

D is attractive because it tries to preserve both inchoate labels while avoiding double punishment. The breaker is that the false choice is not "one inchoate crime or the other"; second-degree bribery is available, and common-law conspiracy is unavailable under Wharton's Rule.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested issue is unchanged: a public employee offered to accept an unauthorized payment for an official act, and the only conspiracy participants were the two people necessary for the bribery transaction. The hypothetical statute makes an offer to commit bribery second-degree bribery, so the clerk's offer is enough for that conviction.

For conspiracy, the jurisdiction applies common law. The Supreme Court's discussion in Iannelli v. United States, 420 U.S. 770 (1975), describes Wharton's Rule as an exception for offenses that necessarily require concerted action by the same participants, while also treating the doctrine as a judicial presumption absent contrary legislative intent. Authority URL: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/420/770. On the source facts and this variation, no third participant and no contrary legislative intent appears, so the common-law conspiracy conviction is blocked while the statutory second-degree bribery conviction remains.
