7. Full right-answer explanation

`C` is right because common-law larceny turns on the defendant's intent at the moment of taking, not on what happened to the property afterward. `Gold Key:` a trespassory taking is larceny only if the taker intends then and there to permanently deprive the owner, or at least to keep the property so long or use it in such a way that the law treats the deprivation as equivalent to permanent loss. Here the defendant planned to return the tuning fork in a week or two, so the required intent was missing when she slipped it into her tote bag. `Silver Key:` when the array splits between later consequences and the defendant's original mental state, lock onto the time stamp the crime uses. For larceny, that time stamp is the taking.

8. Full wrong-answer explanations

`A` sells the student who looks at the final result and forgets the element the crime tests. The choir director did lose the tuning fork for good, but that later permanent deprivation does not prove that the defendant intended permanent deprivation when she first took it. `Silver Key:` this item is about the defendant's mental state at caption and carrying away, not about the eventual outcome.

`B` sells the student who treats later theft like a causation problem and imports foreseeability into an offense that is already complete, or not complete, at the taking. The breaker is that larceny does not ask whether later loss was foreseeable; it asks whether the defendant had the required intent when she first took the property.

`D` sells the student who also chases the later theft, but in the opposite direction. The later theft does not excuse a completed larceny, yet that point does not help here because the right reason for acquittal is not superseding cause; it is the absence of intent to permanently deprive at the outset.

9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited outcome is unchanged from the source item: the defendant should not be convicted of common-law larceny. In `Morissette v. United States`, `342 U.S. 246` (1952), the Supreme Court explained that common-law stealing imports a felonious taking plus an intent to permanently deprive the owner of property, and quoted a lower court's statement that a joyride taken with intent to return the vehicle was "merely trespass; secret borrowing," not stealing. In `Harvey v. State`, `79 Nev. 408, 385 P.2d 761` (1963), the Nevada Supreme Court stated that to constitute larceny there must be, at the time of the taking, the specific intent to permanently deprive the owner of the property, and that a taking with the intention of returning the property will not amount to larceny. `People v. Avery`, `27 Cal. 4th 49` (2002), confirms the same common-law baseline while noting that some takings meant to be temporary can still count if the deprivation is effectively equivalent to permanent loss.

That baseline governs here. The defendant took a durable item and intended to return it in a week or two. Nothing in the facts suggests that the temporary taking would itself destroy the primary value of the tuning fork or make return improbable in the way common-law exceptions sometimes do. The later theft from her tote bag happened after the original taking and does not retroactively create the missing mens rea. Choice `A` focuses on eventual deprivation rather than intent at taking. Choices `B` and `D` import later-causation language that does not answer the larceny-intent issue. Authority: `Morissette v. United States`, `342 U.S. 246` (1952), Justia at `https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/342/246/`; `Harvey v. State`, `79 Nev. 408, 385 P.2d 761` (1963), Justia at `https://law.justia.com/cases/nevada/supreme-court/1962/4487-1.html`; `People v. Avery`, `27 Cal. 4th 49` (2002), Justia at `https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/27/49.html`.
