## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** Under the doctrine of temporary impracticability, a party's duty to perform is *suspended* during a period of impracticability but is not discharged unless performance after the impracticability ceases would be **materially more burdensome** than if there had been no interruption. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 269.

C is correct. If the replacement herder was the only qualified person Daniel could find, and that person demanded a contract for the remaining thirty-three days of the drive, then reinstating Ruth would require Daniel to pay two full salaries for the remainder of the tour — a result that is materially more burdensome than the original contract. Under Restatement § 269, Daniel's duty to perform under the Ruth contract is therefore *discharged* (not merely suspended). The temporary impracticability (Ruth's hospitalization) thus becomes a permanent excuse because the intervening commitment to the replacement makes re-performance materially more burdensome.

Impracticability requires that performance be rendered impracticable or materially more burdensome — not that someone else does the job better.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule — Restatement (Second) of Contracts:**

- **§ 261 (Discharge by Supervening Impracticability):** Where, after a contract is made, a party's performance is made impracticable without his fault by the occurrence of an event the non-occurrence of which was a basic assumption on which the contract was made, his duty to render that performance is discharged, unless the language or the circumstances indicate the contrary.

- **§ 262 (Death or Incapacity of Person Necessary for Performance):** If the existence of a particular person is necessary for the performance of a duty, his death or such incapacity as makes performance impracticable is an event the non-occurrence of which is a basic assumption on which the contract is made.

- **§ 269 (Temporary Impracticability or Frustration):** Impracticability of performance or frustration of purpose that is only temporary suspends the obligor's duty to perform while the impracticability or frustration exists but does not discharge his duty or prevent it from arising **unless his performance after the cessation of the impracticability or frustration would be materially more burdensome than had there been no impracticability or frustration.**

**Application:** Ruth's hospitalization is a temporary incapacity of a person necessary for performance (analogous to § 262). Under § 269, Daniel's duty to employ Ruth is *suspended* during her hospitalization. The critical question is whether that duty is *discharged* when she recovers. Under § 269, discharge occurs only if re-performance would be "materially more burdensome." If the only available replacement demanded a full-term contract for the remainder of the drive (choice C / original B), then reinstating Ruth would require Daniel to pay two people for the same work — a materially more burdensome result. The duty is therefore discharged.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 261, 262, 269 (American Law Institute, 1981). The "materially more burdensome" standard of § 269 is the key authority for the correct answer. See also the discussion in *Pacific Trading Co. v. Mouton Rice Milling Co.*, 184 F.2d 141 (8th Cir. 1950) (temporary impossibility merely postpones performance unless the delay materially impairs the value of the bargain).

**Verification:** The tested rule (impracticability / temporary impracticability), legal issue (whether the hiring party's duty is discharged), and credited outcome (the only-available-substitute fact pattern is the best defense) are all unchanged from the original.

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

#### Program Frame

This item tests the doctrine of **temporary impracticability** in contract law — specifically, the line between *suspension* of a contractual duty and *discharge* of that duty when a service provider becomes temporarily unable to perform. The governing rule is Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 269. This is a Core-difficulty Contracts item requiring the student to identify the single fact pattern that establishes the "materially more burdensome" standard for discharge.

#### Student Access Test

The C3 path is partially anchor-assisted. Choices A, B, and D can be eliminated through visible structural defects (wrong legal frame, backwards logic, colloquialism). Choice C requires knowledge of the § 269 "materially more burdensome" standard — a Gold Key doctrinal unlock. The item is rated ANCHOR_SOLVE: the C3 structural work gets the student to narrow to C, but the final confirmation requires the taught anchor.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Ruth agreed to work as a goat-herd for Daniel on his forty-day livestock drive through hill country. The contract was for the entire drive at a fixed wage. On the second day of the drive, Ruth was hospitalized with a severe allergic reaction to a bee sting. Daniel replaced her with an experienced herder he found in the nearest town. One week later, Ruth recovered and returned, but Daniel refused to let her resume her role for the remaining thirty-three days of the drive.

**Call:** In an action by Ruth against Daniel for breach of contract, which of the following, if proved, would be Daniel's best defense?

**Choices:**
- (A) Daniel offered to let Ruth assist with camp chores for the rest of the drive at her original wage, but Ruth declined.
- (B) Both Daniel and Ruth knew that Ruth had once been briefly hospitalized for a similar allergic reaction two years earlier.
- (C) The replacement herder was the only qualified person Daniel could locate, and the herder would accept nothing less than a contract covering the remainder of the forty-day drive.
- (D) The replacement herder, by wide agreement among the other drovers, was far more skilled with goats than Ruth had been.

#### Distilled Core Question

A service provider becomes temporarily unable to work due to illness, is replaced, and recovers — is the hiring party's duty to reinstate discharged if the only replacement demanded a full-term commitment?

#### Call and Prediction

**Call type:** "Best defense" — requires identifying the single choice that provides a legally sufficient affirmative defense to breach of contract.

**Prediction:** The correct answer will establish that Daniel's duty to employ Ruth was *discharged* (not merely suspended) because re-performance would be materially more burdensome. Look for the fact pattern that shows reinstating Ruth would impose a significantly greater burden than the original contract.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "Daniel offered to let Ruth assist with camp chores for the rest of the drive at her original wage, but Ruth declined."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice changes the legal question. The call asks whether Daniel has a *defense* to breach — i.e., whether his duty was discharged. This choice asks whether Ruth *mitigated her damages* by accepting an alternative offer. Those are two different legal inquiries. A declined offer of different work at the same wage does not discharge the original contractual duty.
2. **Student label:** The declined-offer trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice stating that the contract was terminated by mutual agreement, or that Ruth was offered and accepted the same role back but at a reduced wage.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Mitigation of damages is a separate doctrine from discharge by impracticability. An offer to employ in a different role does not establish that the original duty was excised. NOT_RESPONSIVE — ISSUE_SENSE (wrong_element).

**Choice B: "Both Daniel and Ruth knew that Ruth had once been briefly hospitalized for a similar allergic reaction two years earlier."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This fact *hurts* Daniel's case, not helps it. If Ruth's illness was foreseeable (because it had happened before and both parties knew), then the "non-occurrence of which was a basic assumption" element of § 261 is undermined. You can't claim an event was unforeseeable if you knew it had happened before.
2. **Student label:** The prior-knowledge inversion.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice stating that the allergic reaction was completely unprecedented and unforeseeable — that would strengthen, not weaken, the defense.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Foreseeability negates the basic-assumption element of impracticability. Prior knowledge of the same illness makes the event foreseeable. NOT_TRUE — EAR_FALSITY (backwards).

**Choice C: "The replacement herder was the only qualified person Daniel could locate, and the herder would accept nothing less than a contract covering the remainder of the forty-day drive."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** If this is true, reinstating Ruth means Daniel pays two people for the same job for 33 days. That's materially more burdensome than the original deal. Under the Gold Key rule (§ 269), temporary impracticability *discharges* the duty when re-performance would be materially more burdensome.
2. **Student label:** The correct answer.
3. **Gold Key:** Temporary impracticability suspends the duty but does NOT discharge it unless re-performance would be materially more burdensome. Paying two full salaries for one job is the paradigm case of material burden. Daniel's duty to Ruth is therefore discharged.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 269. The "materially more burdensome" standard is satisfied when the obligor would have to pay twice for the same service. This is the best — and only legally sufficient — defense among the choices.

**Choice D: "The replacement herder, by wide agreement among the other drovers, was far more skilled with goats than Ruth had been."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** "Better" is not a legal defense. The doctrine of impracticability asks whether performance is *impossible or materially more burdensome* — not whether someone else does the job better. This is a fairness/common-sense answer, not a legal one.
2. **Student label:** The quality-is-irrelevant trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice stating that the replacement was the *only* person available AND that hiring Ruth back would require paying two people — that would be a material burden argument.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Relative quality of performance is not a recognized basis for discharging contractual obligations. NOT_TRUE — EAR_DISTORTION (colloquialism).

#### Remediation Card

**Card ID:** REM-CONTRACTS-IMPRACT-01
**Title:** Temporary Impracticability: Suspension vs. Discharge
**Signal:** A service provider becomes temporarily unable to work mid-contract due to illness or injury.
**Student move:** Ask: "Would re-performance after recovery be *materially more burdensome* than if there had been no interruption?" If yes → duty is discharged. If no → duty is merely suspended.
**Tiny rule:** Temporary impracticability suspends duty; discharge requires material burden of re-performance.
**Trap:** Confusing suspension with discharge; thinking any temporary excuse permanently ends the contract.
**Confidence:** ANCHOR_ASSISTED

#### Gold Key(s)

**GK-CONTRACTS-IMPRACT-01**
**Statement:** Temporary impracticability (like a service provider's illness) suspends the other party's duty to perform but does NOT discharge it — unless performance after the impracticability ceases would be *materially more burdensome* than if there had been no interruption. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 269.
**Type:** rule
**Unlocks:** The distinction between suspension and discharge in temporary impracticability cases.
**Trigger:** A service provider becomes temporarily unable to work, is replaced, recovers, and the hiring party refuses to reinstate.
**Tested choice:** C
**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 269 (American Law Institute, 1981)
**Last-minute review:** "Temporary impracticability → suspended, not discharged → UNLESS materially more burdensome → paying two people for one job = material burden → DISCHARGED"

#### Wrong-Answer Recovery Paths

**If the student picked A (declined-offer trap):**
- Recovery: Remind the student that the call asks for a *defense to breach* — i.e., whether Daniel's duty was legally excused. A declined offer of different work goes to *mitigation of damages*, not to whether the duty existed in the first place. These are two separate legal questions. When you see a "best defense" call, focus on whether the duty was discharged, not on what happened after the breach.

**If the student picked B (prior-knowledge inversion):**
- Recovery: Ask: "Does prior knowledge of a risk make the risk more foreseeable or less?" More foreseeable. And if an event was foreseeable, it can't be the basis for an impracticability defense (because its non-occurrence wasn't a "basic assumption" of the contract). This fact *weakens* Daniel's case. When you see a fact that seems to help the defense, check whether it actually helps or hurts.

**If the student picked D (quality-is-irrelevant trap):**
- Recovery: Ask: "Is 'the replacement was better' a recognized legal defense to breach of contract?" No. The doctrine of impracticability asks whether performance is *impossible or materially more burdensome* — not whether someone else does the job better. Quality is irrelevant to the legal analysis. When you see a choice that sounds like common sense but doesn't match a legal doctrine, it's probably a trap.
