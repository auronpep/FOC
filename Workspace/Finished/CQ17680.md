### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Mary's strongest Equal Protection argument is that **the denial can fail rational basis review because it rests only on bare animus toward the group**. The Supreme Court has long recognized that even under deferential rational basis review, a government action must rest on some legitimate government interest. *Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432 (1985); *U.S. Department of Agriculture v. Moreno*, 413 U.S. 528 (1973). A bare desire to harm or exclude an unpopular group — animus — is not a legitimate government interest. *Romer v. Evans*, 517 U.S. 620 (1996) (striking down a state constitutional amendment that "impos[ed] a broad and undifferentiated disability on a single named group" under rational basis review). Here, the board's stated reason — keeping "that kind of Christian group" out — is bare animus, and the board gave no safety, zoning, fiscal, or land-use reason for the denial. That is the classic "rational basis with bite" pattern. **Gold Key:** animus toward a group is not a legitimate government interest, even under rational basis review; the discriminator can lose at rational basis without the court elevating the level of scrutiny. **Silver Key:** when the call asks for the strongest argument, look for the doctrinal move that wins on the actual record (here, animus on the face of the hearing record) rather than the move that escalates the level of scrutiny (strict scrutiny does not apply because the group is not a suspect or quasi-suspect class).

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. Strict scrutiny applies automatically because the group is unpopular.**

This is the dominant trap. The student half-remembers *Cleburne* and *Romer* and assumes the Court upgraded the level of scrutiny when animus is in play. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: the "rational basis with bite" cases do not convert the group into a suspect or quasi-suspect class; they apply rational basis review but conclude that animus is not a legitimate interest, so the law fails even on deferential review. The group can lose under rational basis without strict scrutiny ever being triggered. The student who picks B is reaching for a stronger argument that does not exist; the strongest argument on these facts is the rational-basis-with-bite argument, not a strict-scrutiny argument.

**C. The denial is valid because rational basis review means no review at all.**

This is the tiered-absolute wrong answer. The student treats rational basis review as rubber-stamp. The breaker is the **Silver Key**: rational basis review is deferential, but it still requires a legitimate government interest. Animus is not a legitimate interest. The denial cannot survive even deferential review when the only articulated reason is animus.

**D. The denial is valid if any resident dislikes the group.**

This is the colloquialism wrong answer. The student treats majority disfavor as the test. The breaker is structural: Equal Protection does not ask whether any resident dislikes the group; it asks whether the government action rests on a legitimate interest. Bare dislike by an individual resident is not a legitimate government interest, and the majority's bare dislike is not a legitimate interest either.
