### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Peter created the dangerous bleeding injury, and the clinic's careless delay was the kind of dependent medical response that predictably follows a serious wound. Gold Key: ordinary negligent medical treatment usually does not supersede the original attacker; only extraordinary, independent, or grossly improper treatment can break the chain. Silver Key: when the answer pair fights over "immediate cause" versus "foreseeable response," ask whether the later event grew out of the wound or arrived as a new independent danger. This delay grew out of the wound.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because "might have survived" sounds like it cancels cause. The breaker is that causation asks what the defendant's wound set in motion, not whether perfect treatment could have produced a better ending.

C is attractive because it reaches the right result with a broad-sounding safety rule. The breaker is the word "never": extraordinary or independent medical misconduct can affect causation.

D is the dominant trap because it focuses on the last human mistake before death. The breaker is the Gold Key: ordinary negligent treatment is a foreseeable response to a dangerous wound, so it does not replace the actor who created the need for treatment.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. Criminal result causation requires actual causation and a proximate-cause connection that is not too remote, accidental, or broken by a superseding cause. Model Penal Code section 2.03 frames the result-causation inquiry around whether the actual result is too remote or accidental to bear on liability. State homicide cases apply the same exam rule to medical treatment: ordinary negligent treatment of wounds is foreseeable and generally does not free the original attacker from causal responsibility. In State v. Perez-Cervantes, the Washington Supreme Court described the general rule that negligent wound treatment does not break causation unless treatment is grossly negligent or intentional malpractice. People v. Roberts likewise states that inadequate medical treatment ordinarily is no defense after a dangerous wound, while grossly improper treatment may matter only if it is the sole unforeseeable cause. People v. Saavedra-Rodriguez explains that simple negligent medical treatment is foreseeable and that improper treatment relieves the defendant only when it is grossly negligent and the initial wound would not likely have caused death absent the treatment.

Sources: Model Penal Code section 2.03; State v. Perez-Cervantes, https://law.justia.com/cases/washington/supreme-court/2000/66719-5-1.html; People v. Roberts, https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/2/271.html; People v. Saavedra-Rodriguez, https://law.justia.com/cases/colorado/supreme-court/1999/97sc543-0.html.
