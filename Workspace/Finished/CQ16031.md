### 6. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The correct answer is **C**.

**Silver Key:** When the array is `both / only / neither / only`, test each charged offense separately before trusting the combo answer. If each standalone offense fails a different element, the `both` choice falls with them and `neither` becomes live fast.

Paul is guilty of **neither** charged offense. He is not guilty of **embezzlement** because embezzlement starts with **lawful possession** or entrustment. Here, Paul got the $300 by fraud at the front end. He never intended to build the bookshelf, so his possession was not lawful when it began.

He is also not guilty of **larceny by trick**. **Gold Key:** Larceny by trick covers a fraud-induced transfer of **possession only**. When the victim pays money as part of a purchase or service price, the victim ordinarily means the recipient to **own the money**, so title passes. Martha paid $300 toward the contract price. She was not lending Paul the cash or asking him to hold it for her. Because she intended him to own that money as part of the bargain, larceny by trick is out.

That leaves `neither`.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. embezzlement and larceny by trick.**

This attracts the student who senses fraud everywhere and wants the broadest theft answer on the page. The problem is that the combination answer cannot survive if each underlying offense independently fails. **Silver Key:** separate the two crimes first. Embezzlement fails because possession was never lawful, and larceny by trick fails because Martha meant Paul to own the $300 as part of the price. So `both` collapses.

**B. embezzlement only.**

This sells the idea that Paul received money for a limited purpose and then misused it, which sounds like embezzlement at a glance. The breaker is the front-end possession element. **Gold Key:** embezzlement begins with lawful possession or entrustment, not possession obtained by a lie from the first moment. Paul got the cash by pretending he would perform a job he never intended to perform, so the lawful-possession requirement is missing.

**D. larceny by trick only.**

This is the dominant trap because the facts look like classic theft-by-deception: lie, take cash, disappear. But larceny by trick needs the victim to part with **possession only**, not title. **Gold Key:** when money is paid toward a contract price, the payer usually intends the recipient to own that money. Martha paid part of the price, so title to the cash passed. That points toward false pretenses or theft by deception territory, not larceny by trick.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited outcome is unchanged from the original source row: the right answer remains **neither**.

For **embezzlement**, the governing idea is lawful prior possession. Oklahoma's statute defines embezzlement as fraudulent appropriation of property "`legally obtained`." See **Okla. Stat. tit. 21, § 1451(A)**, https://law.justia.com/codes/oklahoma/title-21/section-21-1451v2/. The New Mexico embezzlement materials likewise state that the defendant must have been entrusted with **lawful possession** before conversion. See **N.M. Stat. § 30-16-8** note citing **State v. Kovach, 2006-NMCA-122**, https://law.justia.com/codes/new-mexico/chapter-30/article-16/section-30-16-8/. Because Paul procured the $300 by fraud from the outset, he did not begin with lawful possession, so embezzlement is not satisfied.

For **larceny by trick**, the controlling distinction is title versus possession. In **Ackerson v. United States, 185 F.2d 485 (8th Cir. 1950)**, the court explained that if the owner means to part only with possession, the fraud can amount to larceny, but if the owner parts with **title as well**, the offense is not larceny and instead fits the false-pretenses side of the line. See https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/185/485/50359/. Martha paid cash **toward the price** of the bookshelf job. The best reading is that she intended Paul to own that money as contract consideration, not merely hold it for temporary custody. So title passed, which defeats larceny by trick.

Bottom line: embezzlement fails because possession was not lawfully obtained, and larceny by trick fails because title to the money passed. The original tested rule, issue, and credited outcome all stay the same.
