### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**A is correct.** The general rule in strict liability is that ordinary contributory negligence — mere inattentiveness or failure to discover a risk — is *not* a defense. But there is a narrow, hard-tested exception: when the plaintiff **knows** of the danger, **appreciates** its magnitude, and **voluntarily and unreasonably** exposes himself to it anyway, the plaintiff has assumed the risk. That conduct bars recovery even against a strict liability defendant. Here, Timothy saw and understood the warning signs and the barriers. He appreciated the danger. He chose to duck under the barrier and enter the launch zone anyway. That was not mere inattention — it was a knowing, voluntary, and unreasonable decision. His assumption of risk defeats his claim.

**Gold Key:** "Even in strict liability, a plaintiff who knowingly, voluntarily, and unreasonably exposes himself to a known ultra-hazardous danger assumes the risk and is barred from recovery."

**C** is attractive because retrieving a pet feels reasonable — a student's moral common-sense instinct is to rescue the animal. But the legal standard is not "was Timothy's motivation sympathetic?" ### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** Under the Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 519–524, a defendant who engages in an abnormally dangerous activity is subject to strict liability for harm resulting from the activity, regardless of the care exercised. Blazing operations, fireworks displays, and similar ultra-hazardable activities satisfy the two-part test: (1) foreseeable risk of serious harm even with reasonable care, and (2) not a matter of common usage.

**Exception — Assumption of Risk:** Under Restatement (Second) of Torts § 523, assumption of the risk is a defense to strict liability. Specifically, a plaintiff who knows of the danger, appreciates its magnitude, and voluntarily and unreasonably chooses to encounter it is barred from recovery. This is distinct from ordinary contributory negligence (mere inattentiveness), which is *not* a defense in strict liability cases. The assumption-of-risk defense applies when the plaintiff's conduct goes beyond carelessness to a deliberate, informed choice to face the known danger.

**Application:** Paul's fireworks display is an abnormally dangerous activity — strict liability would ordinarily apply. However, Timothy saw and understood the warning signs and barriers, appreciated the danger, and voluntarily and unreasonably entered the launch zone. This constitutes assumption of the risk under § 523, which bars his recovery despite the strict liability framework.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 519, 520, 523 (1977). The Restatement framework is the predominant authority tested on the MBE for abnormally dangerous activities and their defenses.
