### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Daniel's Excessive Fines claim can proceed because the Excessive Fines Clause of the Eighth Amendment is incorporated against the states (and their political subdivisions) through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause. The Supreme Court held in *Timbs v. The county's motion to dismiss on a "federal-only" theory therefore fails; the claim is properly before the court on the merits, and the court will apply the incorporated Excessive Fines Clause to evaluate the gross disproportionality of the $400,000 forfeiture to a minor food-code placard violation. **Gold Key:** the Excessive Fines Clause applies to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause. **Silver Key:** lock the call to the best incorporation route, not to the merits of the proportionality test.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. The claim fails because the Excessive Fines Clause of the Eighth Amendment applies only to the federal government, not to states or their political subdivisions.**

This is the dominant trap and the classic "Bill of Rights constrains only the federal government" misconception. The student knows that some provisions were originally understood to apply only to the federal government and treats the Excessive Fines Clause as one of them. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: *Timbs v. Indiana* (2019) squarely held that the Excessive Fines Clause is incorporated through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause, so the county's "federal-only" theory fails on the incorporation question.

**B. The claim wins automatically because every forfeiture larger than the maximum statutory fine is unconstitutional, regardless of the violation.**

This is the "every large fine is unconstitutional" overclaim. The student has half-grasped that the Excessive Fines Clause is about disproportionality and converts it into a flat rule. The breaker is the call: the question asks about the best incorporation route, not the merits of the proportionality analysis. Even with incorporation established, the court still has to evaluate gross disproportionality; it does not automatically invalidate every forfeiture that exceeds the maximum civil fine.

**D. The claim belongs only under the procedural due process rules requiring notice and a hearing before a forfeiture is imposed.**

This is the "procedural due process" misdirection. The student has reduced the constitutional claim to a notice-and-hearing issue. The breaker is the doctrinal point: the Excessive Fines Clause is a substantive constraint on the size of the forfeiture, not a procedural constraint on the procedure for imposing it. Procedural due process addresses how the government takes a protected interest; substantive due process (and the incorporated Excessive Fines Clause) addresses the kind of interest the government can take. A claim that the forfeiture is grossly excessive is a substantive claim, not a procedural one.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17114, a Constitutional Law item involving a $500,000 city forfeiture for a minor municipal offense, in which the defendant argued the fine is excessive under the Eighth Amendment as applied to the city through the Fourteenth Amendment. The official key is B: the claim can proceed through Fourteenth Amendment incorporation.

The Incorporation Doctrine applies most provisions of the Bill of Rights to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause. Beginning with *Chicago, Burlington & Quincy Railroad Co. v. Chicago*, 166 U.S. 226 (1897), the Court has held that the Fourteenth Amendment makes provisions of the Bill of Rights applicable to the states against the predations of state power. The Court has, in successive cases, applied the "incorporation" framework to the Fifth Amendment's Just Compensation Clause (*Chicago, B. & Q. R. Co.*), the First Amendment's Free Speech and Free Exercise Clauses, the Second Amendment (*McDonald v. City of Chicago*, 561 U.S. 742 (2010)), and most other provisions. The framework asks whether a particular Bill of Rights guarantee is "fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty" or "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" such that "neither liberty nor justice would exist if [it] were sacrificed." *Timbs v. Indiana*, 586 U.S. 365, 375 (2019) (internal quotation marks omitted).

In *Timbs*, the Court squarely held that the Eighth Amendment's Excessive Fines Clause is incorporated against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause. The Court observed that the protection against excessive fines is "both fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty and deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition," and that American courts had, for centuries, treated the protection as binding on the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The result is that a state's imposition of an excessive fine violates the Fourteenth Amendment, and the incorporated Excessive Fines Clause is the proper doctrinal hook for the claim.

The court will therefore evaluate the forfeiture on the merits under the incorporated Excessive Fines Clause, applying the gross-disproportionality framework of *United States v. Bajakajian*, 524 U.S. 321 (1998), and its progeny. The disproportionality analysis is a separate question from incorporation; the question presented on these facts is the incorporation route, and the answer is that the claim can proceed through the Fourteenth Amendment.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original. The county's "federal-only" incorporation theory fails; the Excessive Fines Clause applies to the county through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause.
