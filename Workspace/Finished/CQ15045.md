### 5. Full Right-Answer Explanation (C)

**Gold Key:** A deed need not be physically handed to the grantee to effect a valid conveyance. Recording the deed creates a rebuttable presumption of delivery, and the grantee's acceptance is presumed unless the grantee expressly rejects the conveyance.

Naomi executed a valid deed and directed Peter to record it. Recording is the strongest objective evidence of the grantor's intent to deliver. The fact that Timothy never physically possessed the deed — and never even knew about it — is irrelevant. Timothy never rejected the conveyance, so acceptance is presumed. The conveyance was complete upon execution and recording, and Naomi cannot undo it.

### 6. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B (original A) — the dominant trap.** The surgery and fear of death make the inter vivos/causa mortis distinction feel like the key issue. A student who fixates on the "fearing she might not survive" facts may think the gift's classification as inter vivos (rather than causa mortis) somehow defeats it. But the distinction is irrelevant to whether the deed was delivered and accepted. An inter vivos gift of land by deed is perfectly valid — the delivery requirement is satisfied by recording, and the gift's inter vivos character is actually what *helps* the grantee's estate, not hurts it. **Gold Key:** The inter vivos vs. causa mortis classification matters for *when* a gift takes effect and whether it can be revoked, but it has no bearing on whether delivery occurred.

**D (original B).** The tax rolls showing Timothy's estate as owner might look like official confirmation of the conveyance, but listing on the tax rolls is not an essential element of a valid conveyance. The two essential elements are execution and delivery. Tax assessment records are administrative — they don't create or supply missing conveyance requirements. The conveyance stands or falls on its own legal merits, not on what the tax authority did afterward.

**A (original C).** The First Amendment's religious freedom provisions have no application here. The call asks why the conveyance stands — whether a devisee's disappointment implicates constitutional rights is a non sequitur. No one's religious freedom is at stake in a property dispute over whether a deed was delivered. This answer is pure noise.

### 7. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule:** For a conveyance of real property by deed to be valid, two elements are required: (1) execution (the deed must be signed by the grantor) and (2) delivery (the grantor must manifest an intent to make the deed presently operative). Physical transfer to the grantee is not required. Recording of a deed creates a rebuttable presumption of delivery. Acceptance by the grantee is presumed unless the grantee expressly rejects the conveyance. *See* 23 Am. Jur. 2d Deeds §§ 137–145; Restatement (Third) of Property: Wills and Other Donative Transfers § 6.3.

**Authority:** The recording presumption is widely recognized. *See, e.g.*, Midland Title, "Conveyances of Real Estate by Deed" ("Recordation of the deed in the land records is generally presumed to satisfy this [delivery] requirement"); South Dakota Law Review, "Delivery of Deeds in South Dakota — Intention of the Grantor," 1 S.D. L. Rev. 100 (1956) (discussing presumptions arising from recording).

**Verification:** The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original. The official key (D = delivery presumed from recording) is correct. The transform preserves the invariant layer exactly.
