# BarMatrix CQ19374 — Sailboat-Engine Dispute (Christian variation of Q19374)

## 1. Final question (revised stem + call)

Sarah owns a small sailboat brokerage. Six months ago she sold a 22-foot sailboat to Paul. Paul is now suing Sarah, claiming the boat's diesel engine had a cracked block that Sarah knew about before the sale. At trial, Paul's counsel asks the marina mechanic Barnabas: "Did Mark tell you that Sarah admitted the engine had a cracked block before the sale?" Before Barnabas answers, Sarah objects on hearsay grounds. Paul's counsel argues Barnabas should answer and the jury can decide whether the statement is reliable. How should the court proceed?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

In a federal trial, before a witness answers a question about an out-of-court statement, a party objects on hearsay grounds and the offering party argues the jury can decide reliability. How should the court proceed?

## 3. Final answer choices (A–D, post-shuffle)

- **A.** Require Barnabas to answer outside the jury's hearing if the court needs the answer to evaluate admissibility.
- **B.** Overrule the objection because all questions about admissions of defects are party-opponent statements.
- **C.** Allow the answer because reliability of hearsay is always for the jury.
- **D.** Allow the answer before the jury and then strike it if the judge later disagrees.

## 6. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**A is correct.** Admissibility of evidence is a preliminary question for the court, not the jury. **Federal Rule of Evidence 104(a)** places that gatekeeping role squarely on the trial judge: "The court must decide any preliminary question about whether a witness is qualified, a privilege exists, or evidence is admissible." Counsel's argument that the jury can "decide whether the statement is reliable" addresses weight, not admissibility — those are two different jobs. The judge decides whether the evidence comes in; the jury decides how much to credit it once it is in.

Once the objection is on the record, the judge must also take care not to let the witness's answer suggest inadmissible evidence to the jury. **Federal Rule of Evidence 103(d)** requires: "To the extent practicable, the court must conduct a jury trial so that inadmissible evidence is not suggested to the jury by any means." The mechanism for that, on a question like this, is to take the proffered answer outside the jury's hearing while the judge decides admissibility. If the answer is admissible, the witness is recalled before the jury and answers again. If not, the jury never hears it.

**Silver Key:** "Reliability for the jury" is a category error when the question is really "admissibility for the judge." A choice that conflates the two roles is collapsing FRE 104's gatekeeping into FRE 104(e)'s weight-and-credibility reservation. When counsel says "the jury can decide reliability," translate that to "I am asking the judge to abdicate" — and pick the choice that lets the judge do the gatekeeping job properly.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations (one per distractor)

### C — "Allow the answer because reliability of hearsay is always for the jury." (original A)

This is the **dominant trap** and the classic misconception this item is built to expose. It is attractive because counsel literally just said, "the jury can decide whether the statement is reliable," and the choice appears to honor that argument. The cognitive move it sells: hearsay reliability is a credibility question, and credibility is for the jury. That move confuses two different jobs. **Federal Rule of Evidence 104(a)** puts admissibility — including the threshold hearsay analysis — on the judge. The jury's reliability job only begins after the evidence is admitted. A student who picks this is reading the trial-level colloquialism "the jury decides" as if it answered the gatekeeping question. The breaker: admissibility and weight are distinct; the gate is the judge's, and FRE 103(d) forbids letting the jury hear inadmissible evidence just to strike it later.

### B — "Overrule the objection because all questions about admissions of defects are party-opponent statements." (original D)

This choice is attractive to a student who has correctly learned that an opposing party's statement offered against that party is non-hearsay (FRE 801(d)(2)), and who notices that Sarah's alleged admission would qualify. The cognitive move it sells: "This is an admission, so it comes in; overrule." That move is too fast. The proffered testimony is layered: the question is asking what Mark told Barnabas. Two out-of-court statements sit in the answer — Mark's act of speaking to Barnabas, and Sarah's alleged admission. Each layer needs its own hearsay pass; one layer being a party-opponent statement does not launder the other. A choice built on "all … are party-opponent" is a **tiered absolute** that ignores the multiple-statement-layers problem. The breaker: the question is not "is Sarah's alleged admission hearsay," it is "is the proffered testimony admissible in the form the question seeks to elicit." That question is for the judge to resolve — exactly what choice A accomplishes.

### D — "Allow the answer before the jury and then strike it if the judge later disagrees." (original C)

This choice is attractive because it sounds practical and the offer to "strike" seems to cure any harm. The cognitive move it sells: "Let the jury hear it; if it turns out inadmissible, just tell them to ignore it." That move directly contradicts **Federal Rule of Evidence 103(d)**, which requires the court, "to the extent practicable," to keep inadmissible evidence from being suggested to the jury in the first place. Striking after exposure does not un-ring the bell. The breaker: the rule is preventive, not corrective. When the court can hear the answer outside the jury's hearing, it must. The same witness can be recalled and answer before the jury only if the judge admits the evidence; the contrary approach is reversible error when the precaution was practicable.

## 8. Black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

**Governing rules:**

- **Federal Rule of Evidence 104(a) — Preliminary Questions (In General).** "The court must decide any preliminary question about whether a witness is qualified, a privilege exists, or evidence is admissible. In so deciding, the court is not bound by evidence rules, except those on privilege." (Advisory Committee note confirms this is the rule of general application; the judge acts as trier of fact on the preliminary conditions, including evaluating whether a hearsay statement possesses the required characteristics.)
- **Federal Rule of Evidence 103(d) — Preventing the Jury from Hearing Inadmissible Evidence.** "To the extent practicable, the court must conduct a jury trial so that inadmissible evidence is not suggested to the jury by any means."

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation only):** Cornell LII, "Rule 104. Preliminary Questions," https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_104; Cornell LII, "Rule 103. Rulings on Evidence," https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_103. The FRE statutory text and Advisory Committee notes — including the note for 104(a) placing the responsibility on the judge for these determinations (citing McCormick §53 and Morgan, *Basic Problems of Evidence* 45–50 (1962)) — confirm the rule.

**Verification of credited outcome against the original:** The original Q19374 key is B, which states the same rule — the court should prevent inadmissible evidence from being suggested to the jury when practicable, and may take the proffer outside the jury's hearing to evaluate admissibility. The cited rules (FRE 103(d), 104(a)) match the original's `lb_rule_reference`. The transform preserves the test, the issue, the trap structure, and the credited outcome. **No drift; the credited answer remains correct on the variation.**

**Why the credited answer is right under the rule:** Counsel's question is an offer of testimony with multiple out-of-court statement layers. Whether the answer is admissible is a preliminary question of admissibility committed to the court by FRE 104(a). The court must, "to the extent practicable," avoid letting the witness's answer suggest the proffered evidence to the jury (FRE 103(d)). The mechanism that satisfies both rules is to take the answer outside the jury's hearing while the judge rules. Choice A is the only option that does this. Choices C and D misallocate the gatekeeping role to the jury or to a curative strike. Choice B invents a per-statement shortcut that ignores the layered hearsay problem.

### 2. The 17-section student case study

## program_frame

This is a Roles-of-judge-and-jury item, not a hearsay-definition item. The hearsay objection is the trigger; the test is whether the student knows that admissibility is a preliminary question for the judge (FRE 104(a)) and that the court, when practicable, must keep inadmissible evidence from being suggested to the jury (FRE 103(d)). The dominant misconception this item is built to expose is the colloquialism that "the jury decides reliability."

## student_access_test

A smart 10-year-old who has completed only the C3 lessons, the Evidence overlay, and the taught tiny anchors can solve this item by name. The basic rule — "the judge decides whether evidence comes in, the jury decides how much to credit it once it's in" — is enough to kill the dominant trap (C) and to pick the credited answer (A). The cure-by-strike trap (D) requires the FRE 103(d) refinement but is also reachable from the basic rule: if the answer is "maybe inadmissible," letting the jury hear it and then striking it is a workaround, not a gatekeeping decision. The multi-layer hearsay trap (B) requires the student to notice that the question asks what Mark told Barnabas — two statement layers, two hearsay passes. **No outside legal doctrine is required for the student path.**

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### Choice A (correct)

- **(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** The only choice that lets the judge do the gatekeeping job the rule gives him (FRE 104) and, when practicable, keeps inadmissible evidence from being suggested to the jury (FRE 103(d)). The witness can be recalled and answer before the jury only if the judge admits the evidence; the contrary approach is reversible error.
- **(2) Student label:** Correct — court takes the proffered answer outside the jury's hearing to evaluate admissibility.
- **(3) What a true-and-responsive version would look like:** A true-and-responsive version names the gatekeeping role of the judge and the jury-protection mechanic. Choice A is exactly that.
- **(4) Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 104(a) ("The court must decide any preliminary question about whether a witness is qualified, a privilege exists, or evidence is admissible") and FRE 103(d) ("To the extent practicable, the court must conduct a jury trial so that inadmissible evidence is not suggested to the jury by any means"). The mechanism is to take the answer outside the jury's hearing while the judge rules. *Authority: Cornell LII, https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_103 and https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_104.*

### Choice B (distractor)

- **(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** "All" is a C3 red flag word. The proffered testimony has multiple statement layers (Mark told Barnabas that Sarah admitted …). One layer being a party-opponent statement does not launder the other.
- **(2) Student label:** Wrong — "all" is a tiered absolute; the question is not "is Sarah's alleged admission hearsay," it is "is the proffered testimony admissible in the form the question seeks to elicit."
- **(3) What a true-and-responsive version would look like:** A true-and-responsive version would address the layered-hearsay problem head-on, e.g., "The court should first determine whether the proffered testimony is admissible; even if Sarah's alleged admission is a party-opponent statement, Mark's act of speaking to Barnabas is itself a separate out-of-court statement that needs its own hearsay pass."
- **(4) Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 801(d)(2) defines a party-opponent statement as the party's own statement offered against that party. Multiple statement layers raise multiple hearsay issues. The "all" in choice B is an overclaim that ignores the layered structure of the proffered testimony.

### Choice C (distractor — DOMINANT TRAP)

- **(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice directly contradicts the call's premise (counsel argues the jury can decide reliability) by adopting that very argument. Reliability is a weight question, not an admissibility question. The judge decides whether the evidence comes in; the jury decides how much to credit it once it is in.
- **(2) Student label:** Wrong — the choice makes the jury the gatekeeper on the very question the rule gives to the judge.
- **(3) What a true-and-responsive version would look like:** A true-and-responsive version would say the court (not the jury) decides admissibility, and that "reliability" addresses weight, not admissibility. Choice A is that.
- **(4) Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 104(a) places admissibility on the court. The trial-level colloquialism "the jury decides reliability" addresses weight, not admissibility. **Silver Key (SK-EVIDENCE-JUDGE-VS-JURY-CATEGORY-01):** When counsel argues "the jury can decide reliability," hear that as a category error: admissibility is the court's gate, reliability is the jury's job only after evidence is admitted.

### Choice D (distractor)

- **(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice sounds practical ("we can always strike it later") but contradicts the preventive requirement of FRE 103(d). Strike-after-exposure is the wrong cure.
- **(2) Student label:** Wrong — striking after jury exposure does not satisfy FRE 103(d)'s preventive requirement.
- **(3) What a true-and-responsive version would look like:** A true-and-responsive version would acknowledge that the court should, when practicable, take the answer outside the jury's hearing in the first place, not let the jury hear it and strike it later.
- **(4) Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 103(d) is preventive, not corrective. Curative strikes after jury exposure do not undo the suggestion of inadmissible evidence.
