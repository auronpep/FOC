### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**D** is correct.

**Gold Key:** When an out-of-court letter is offered to prove the medical cause or condition it asserts, start with hearsay. The declarant’s unavailability matters only if the proponent is using an unavailability-dependent exception.

**Gold Key:** Rule 703 lets an expert rely on some inadmissible facts, but it does not make the underlying letter substantive evidence. A one-off physician letter also is not a business record unless it was made and kept as a regular practice.

**Silver Key:** Pin the purpose before naming an exception. Here the letter is offered to prove cause, so the question is substantive hearsay admissibility, not expert-basis background.

The letter is offered to prove the cause of Ruth’s laryngitis. That is a truth use. No hearsay exclusion or exception fits on these facts. The expert’s reliance does not itself admit the letter, and the one-off letter is not a business record.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sounds safe because it reaches the same exclusion result and uses a familiar hearsay word: unavailability. The breaker is the wrong gate. **Gold Key:** unavailability matters only for unavailability-dependent exceptions, and no such exception is the route here.

**B.** This is the dominant trap. It sells the expert-basis shortcut: the expert relied on the letter, so the jury should be able to receive the letter. **Silver Key:** pin the purpose. The letter is offered to prove medical cause, not merely to explain the expert’s basis. **Gold Key:** Rule 703 is not a hearsay exception.

**C.** This choice is attractive because a physician’s writing looks like a medical record. The breaker is regularity. **Gold Key:** the business-records exception requires a record made and kept in the regular course as a regular practice; an ad hoc physician letter does not qualify.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The source row supplies QID 14800, Evidence / Hearsay, pick rates, official key A, and the official reasoning that the letter is hearsay, not a business record, and not admissible merely because an expert relied on it. 

Federal Rule of Evidence 801(c) defines hearsay as an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted. The physician’s letter is offered to prove medical cause, so the truth-purpose hearsay gate is triggered. ([U.S. Code][1])

Federal Rule of Evidence 803(6) requires a record made at or near the time, kept in the course of a regularly conducted activity, and made as a regular practice. A one-off letter to explain a cancellation is not a regular-practice record on these facts. ([U.S. Code][1])

Federal Rule of Evidence 703 permits an expert to base an opinion on facts or data reasonably relied on by experts, even if the facts or data are not independently admissible. But otherwise inadmissible basis material may be disclosed to the jury only if its probative value in helping evaluate the opinion substantially outweighs prejudice. Rule 703 does not transform the underlying letter into substantive hearsay evidence. ([U.S. Code][2])

Doctrinal-currency hotlist check: the expert-testimony hotlist is noted. FRE 702 was amended in 2023, but this item does not turn on admitting the expert’s opinion under Rule 702; it turns on hearsay, Rule 803(6), and Rule 703 disclosure of basis material. ([U.S. Code][3])

## Gold Key(s)

**GK-EVIDENCE-RETREAT-LARYNGITIS-01**
