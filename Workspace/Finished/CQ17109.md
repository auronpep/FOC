## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam register)

**C is correct.** Deputy Timothy was summoned to the barn precisely to confront the brawl, and the brawl is what hurt him. The **firefighter's rule** (the professional-rescuer rule) bars a police officer or firefighter from recovering for an injury caused by the very peril he was employed and called to confront — even when that peril arose from the defendant's negligence. The rule is grounded in **duty**: the negligent party owes the summoned officer no duty to avoid creating, or to protect him from, the hazard that is his job to face. That makes C the cleanest, fully dispositive ground for dismissal.

**Gold Key:** The firefighter's rule is a *no-duty* rule, not a causation or premises-status rule. A landowner owes no duty to a police officer or firefighter injured by the very hazard he was summoned to confront, even if the landowner's negligence created it — and the officer is treated as neither invitee nor licensee.

**Silver Key:** When a "best argument" array gives several choices that all reach "defendant wins," do not pick on outcome — pick the single theory that is both legally correct **and** fully dispositive. Here two choices reach no-liability (A licensee, C firefighter's rule); only C states a rule that completely defeats the claim, so C wins the clash.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A — licensee no-duty (dominant trap).** This choice is seductive because it reaches the right destination — "no duty," defendant wins — and dresses it in a genuine premises-liability category every student has drilled. The breaker is that a summoned officer is *sui generis*: he is neither invitee nor licensee, because his entry depends on neither permission nor invitation and cannot be refused. Worse, even if he were a licensee, a licensee is still owed *some* duty (to warn of known, concealed dangers), so the licensee theory would leave a residual duty intact and would not fully support dismissal. **Gold Key:** the firefighter's rule — not premises status — governs, and it bars the claim outright where the licensee frame does not.

**B — no proximate cause as a matter of law.** This choice is attractive because causation is a real element of negligence and a court *can* dispose of cases on it. The breaker is the overclaim buried in "as a matter of law": a brawl injury to an officer dispatched to break up that brawl is, at minimum, a foreseeable consequence and a jury question — not something a court resolves against the plaintiff on the pleadings. Proximate cause is the wrong, weaker lever; the official analysis notes that if there is no *duty*, the proximate-cause question never even arises.

**D — rescuer cannot recover.** This choice sells the correct outcome through a tidy-sounding absolute: rescuers who get hurt can't sue the person they were rescuing. The breaker is that the absolute is backwards as to ordinary rescuers — the **rescue doctrine** generally *lets* a rescuer recover for injuries that were a foreseeable result of the defendant's negligence. What actually bars *this* plaintiff is not his status as a rescuer but his status as a *professional* emergency responder. The choice over-generalizes a professional-only bar into a rule about all rescuers.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Right-Answer Legal Reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule (unchanged from the original):** the firefighter's rule / professional-rescuer rule. A landowner or other negligent actor owes **no duty** to a firefighter or police officer who is injured by the very hazard he was summoned in his professional capacity to confront, even if the actor's negligence created that hazard.

**Why C is correct under the governing rule.** Deputy Timothy was dispatched to confront the brawl and was injured by the brawl. The rule's rationale is expressly one of *duty*: there is no duty owed by the negligent party to the officer as to the peril that is the officer's business to handle. This is the strongest dismissal ground because it defeats the claim at the duty element, before breach, causation, or damages are reached.

**Authority (real, kept out of the student layer):**
- *Krauth v. Geller*, 31 N.J. 270, 157 A.2d 129 (1960) — leading statement of the firefighter's rule; a firefighter "cannot complain of negligence in the creation of the very occasion for his engagement," and is classed as neither invitee nor licensee.
- *Berko v. Freda*, 93 N.J. 81, 459 A.2d 663 (1983) — extends the rule to **police officers**: it bars suit for ordinary negligence that creates the occasion for the officer's presence where he is injured.
- Restatement (Second) of Torts § 345 (the older premises framing — public officers treated as licensees — that the firefighter's-rule cases depart from), and the rescue-doctrine line (Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical & Emotional Harm § 32) from which professional rescuers are excluded. *Rule numbers/leading cases stated; treat case parentheticals as confirmed against the cited reporters; no fabricated holdings.*

**Currency check:** the firefighter's rule is not on the doctrinal-currency hotlist; it remains majority common law (some jurisdictions have narrowed or abolished it, but the bar-tested majority rule is the no-duty bar). No superseded-framing issue. The C3 residual (C) **agrees** with the official key (orig B). **Verdict: PASS.** No key disagreement; nothing in the transform moves the credited outcome.
