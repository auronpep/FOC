### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Daniel was present briefly, for a commercial purchase, with no prior relationship to Ruth's apartment, no belongings there, and no overnight or social-guest connection. Gold Key: Fourth Amendment standing is personal; the defendant needs a legitimate expectation of privacy in the place searched, and a short-term commercial visitor normally lacks that interest in another person's home. Silver Key: the home is protected, but the call asks whether this defendant can claim that protection.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells the simple presence instinct: Daniel was inside when the police entered, so the invasion feels personal. The breaker is that being present on the premises is not enough; the question is whether Daniel had a legitimate privacy interest in Ruth's apartment.

C sells the right outcome with an overbroad rule. Some nonowners, especially overnight guests, can have Fourth Amendment standing in another person's home, so the word "ever" makes the answer too absolute.

D sells the strongest home-protection instinct. Homes receive special Fourth Amendment protection, but the Gold Key kills the trap because Daniel must show his own legitimate privacy interest, not just point to the kind of place searched.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: Fourth Amendment suppression is personal, and a defendant must show a legitimate expectation of privacy in the place searched. A short-term commercial visitor in another person's apartment normally lacks that expectation, while an overnight guest may have one.

Rakas v. Illinois, 439 U.S. 128 (1978), frames the inquiry around the defendant's legitimate expectation of privacy rather than mere presence. Minnesota v. Olson, 495 U.S. 91 (1990), confirms that an overnight guest can have a legitimate expectation of privacy in a host's home. Minnesota v. Carter, 525 U.S. 83 (1998), controls this fact pattern: the Court denied Fourth Amendment protection to persons present in another's apartment for a relatively short time for a purely commercial drug transaction, with no previous connection to the householder. Sources checked: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep439/usrep439128/usrep439128.pdf, https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-supreme-court/495/91.html, and https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep525/usrep525083/usrep525083.pdf.

Daniel's facts match Carter, not Olson. He was a first-time short-term commercial visitor, brought no belongings, and planned to leave immediately after the sale. The unlawful entry may have violated Ruth's Fourth Amendment rights, but Daniel cannot suppress based only on his presence in her apartment.
