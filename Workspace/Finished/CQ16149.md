### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. The clean defense is that Peter did not commit robbery. Robbery requires a taking from the person or immediate presence of another by force or fear. Peter used no force, and Lydia was not afraid. She laughed, mocked the threat, and chose to hand over a dollar. If no robbery occurred, Timothy cannot be guilty as an accomplice to robbery. Gold Key: accomplice liability does not skip the underlying offense; for robbery, first confirm force or fear. Silver Key: before chasing the lookout's withdrawal story, test the principal crime at the root.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is the dominant trap. It sounds attractive because Timothy left before Peter walked out, so students may label the departure "withdrawal." The breaker is that panic flight after planning, driving, and serving as lookout is not the best defense here, and it distracts from the root defect: no robbery occurred at all. Gold Key: accomplice liability does not matter unless the charged offense was committed.

C sells a detail-control mistake. Timothy did not need to know Peter's exact words if Timothy intentionally aided the planned robbery. The problem is not Peter's wording; the problem is that Lydia was not put in fear and no force was used.

D sells a participation-location mistake. An accomplice need not enter the store to be liable for the target offense if he intentionally aids it. The fact that Peter alone went inside would not be Timothy's best defense if a robbery had occurred.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are preserved. The item remains a robbery/accomplice-liability question. The right answer remains the defense that no robbery occurred because the person from whom property was taken was not put in fear and no force was used.

Lawyer-confirmation layer: robbery is a theft-type taking from the person or immediate presence of another accomplished by force or fear. California Penal Code section 211 states the classic force-or-fear formulation: robbery is taking property from another's person or immediate presence against that person's will by force or fear. See https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=211. California Penal Code section 212 identifies the fear contemplated by the robbery statute as fear of unlawful injury to the robbed person, specified relatives, family members, or someone in the person's company. See https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=212. For the accomplice layer, California Penal Code section 31 states the traditional principal-liability concept that those who directly commit, aid and abet, advise, or encourage a crime are principals in the crime committed. See https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=31.

Those authorities confirm the grading logic. Peter may have committed another offense, such as attempted theft or attempted robbery, but the charged completed robbery fails because the taking was not accomplished by force or fear. Timothy's best robbery defense is therefore the absence of robbery itself, not his location, late panic, or ignorance of Peter's exact words.
