### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Gold Key: defense of property has a force ceiling; deadly force or force likely to cause serious bodily harm is not justified solely to protect unattended property. The teenager was stealing, but the property at risk was a printer in a trailer, and no person was threatened before the crossbow fired. Gold Key: a mechanical device cannot do more than Daniel would be privileged to do personally. Because Daniel could not personally shoot a nonviolent thief solely to protect the printer, he cannot set a crossbow to do it for him.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because absence makes the device seem like Daniel's only practical way to guard the trailer. The breaker is that absence does not raise the force ceiling; a trap device cannot use deadly or seriously dangerous force just because the owner is not there.

C is attractive because the teenager was committing theft. The breaker is that theft of property does not justify deadly or seriously dangerous force when no person is threatened.

D is attractive because it notices that the property is not a home. The breaker is that defense of property is not limited to homes; the problem is the amount of force, not the location label.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged. Model Penal Code section 3.06 limits force for protection of property and does not allow deadly force merely to protect property from theft. Model Penal Code section 3.06(5) specifically restricts devices designed to protect property: the device may not be designed to cause death or serious bodily injury, and its use must be reasonable under the ordinary force limits. Katko v. Briney, 183 N.W.2d 657 (Iowa 1971), is the leading spring-gun case in the defense-of-property family: the law values human safety over property protection and rejects serious spring-gun injury for unoccupied property. People v. Ceballos, 12 Cal. 3d 470 (1974), confirms the same criminal-law principle: a spring gun cannot be used where the defendant would not be privileged to use deadly force personally.
