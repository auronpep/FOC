### 7. Right-Answer Explanation

**Choice B is correct.**

Criminal liability for a failure to act requires a pre-existing legal duty. Without a legal duty, the omission cannot form the *actus reus* of any crime.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIMINAL-OMISSION-01):** The five recognized sources of a legal duty to act are: (1) statute, (2) special status relationship (parent-child, spouses), (3) contract, (4) voluntary assumption of care, and (5) creation of the peril. Barnabas and Lydia were strangers. The jurisdiction had no bystander-aid statute. Barnabas had not undertaken any care of Lydia and had not placed her on the ledge. His rope and climbing skills gave him a moral opportunity to help — not a legal obligation. Moral duty alone cannot supply the *actus reus* of a crime at common law.

**Silver Key (SK-CRIMINAL-OMISSION-01):** On an omission call, the threshold and only threshold question is whether a legal duty exists. When two No answers survive, lock the one that names the missing legal duty — not the one that names causation.

### 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A** draws on the intuition that Lydia's own risk-taking caused her death. She left the main trail on her own; her choice feels like the real cause. The superseding-cause frame offers a clean, legally-flavored reason to say No. But the causation inquiry is premature here. Causation analysis attaches only after the prosecution establishes a criminal *actus reus*, which for an omission requires a legal duty. Because Barnabas owed Lydia no duty, his failure to act cannot constitute an *actus reus* — and without that, there is no causal chain through which Barnabas's conduct runs. A is an answer to a question the law has not yet reached.

**Gold Key (tested_choice B):** The five duty sources confirm there is no legal duty here. Once you know that, causation becomes irrelevant — there is nothing to trace it through.

**Choice C** sells the moral logic that a person who walks past a dying stranger — rope in hand, skills intact — bears criminal responsibility. The combination of easy ability and deliberate refusal makes liability feel obvious. But criminal law imposes omission liability only when a legal duty to act exists. Physical ability and equipment are not legal duty sources. Barnabas had no statute requiring him to act, no status relationship with Lydia, no contract, no voluntary assumption of her care, and no role in creating her peril. His ability was morally significant and legally irrelevant.

**Gold Key (tested_choice C):** The ability-to-help fact fits none of the five recognized duty sources. Rope in hand ≠ statute, status relationship, contract, assumption of care, or creation of peril.

**Choice D** frames Barnabas's walk-away as legally equivalent to pushing Lydia from the ledge. The deliberateness of the choice makes it feel active rather than passive. Treating all deliberate inaction as equivalent to commission would extinguish that distinction and expose every capable bystander to homicide liability. Courts have refused this move consistently; the equivalence claim only holds when a legal duty to act already exists — which it does not here.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule:** Criminal liability for an omission requires a pre-existing legal duty imposed by law. Moral duty and physical ability do not supply that duty. The common law and the Model Penal Code both require a legally cognizable duty before an omission can constitute *actus reus*.

**Five recognized duty sources:** (1) Statute; (2) Special status relationship (parent-child, spouses, ship captain/crew); (3) Contract (lifeguard, nurse, babysitter); (4) Voluntary assumption of care with seclusion of the victim; (5) Creation of the peril.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer):**
- *People v. Beardsley*, 150 Mich. 206, 113 N.W. 1128, 1130 (Mich. 1907): "The law recognizes that under some circumstances the omission of a duty owed by one individual to another, where such omission results in the death of the one to whom the duty is owing, will make the other chargeable with manslaughter. … This rule of law is always based upon the proposition that the duty neglected must be a legal duty, and not a mere moral obligation."
- *Jones v. United States*, 308 F.2d 307, 310 (D.C. Cir. 1962): articulates four circumstances imposing a legal duty to act: statute, status relationship, contract, and voluntary undertaking that creates reliance or seclusion.
- MPC § 2.01(3): "Liability for the commission of an offense may not be based on an omission unaccompanied by action unless: (a) the omission is expressly made sufficient by the law defining the offense; or (b) a duty to perform the omitted act is otherwise imposed by law."

No authority contradicts the official key. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are UNCHANGED from original Q21460. bank_validation_verdict: PASS.
