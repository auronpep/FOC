### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is correct. Peter is not challenging a pure legal rule, a bench-trial fact finding, or the effect of an already-identified error. He is challenging the trial judge's decision to admit prior-accident evidence after weighing relevance and unfair prejudice in the trial context. Gold Key: evidentiary admissibility rulings, including relevance and Rule 403-style balancing decisions, are reviewed for abuse of discretion. Silver Key: first name the thing being reviewed; if it is the trial judge's gatekeeping call about whether evidence came in, move to abuse of discretion before thinking about harmless error.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because "irrelevant and unfairly prejudicial" sounds like a legal test, and legal questions are often reviewed from scratch. The breaker is the Gold Key: this is not an abstract legal rule appeal; it is an evidentiary admissibility ruling made in the context of the trial record, so abuse of discretion governs.

B is attractive because appeals often sort facts from law, and a negligence case can make students look for fact-review language. The breaker is that the appellate issue is not a trial judge's factual finding after a bench trial. The jury found for Mary, and Peter attacks an evidentiary ruling, not a Rule 52 fact finding.

C is attractive because evidence errors often lead students straight to outcome-impact language. Silver Key: harmless error comes after the court identifies an error and asks whether it affected substantial rights; it is not the standard used to decide whether admitting the evidence was erroneous in the first place.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested issue remains Civil Procedure, Appealability and Review, Scope of Review, with verified outline code `93110300`. The legal issue and credited outcome are unchanged: an appellate challenge to a trial court's admission of evidence on relevance and prejudice grounds is reviewed for abuse of discretion.

Federal Rules of Evidence 401 and 403 frame the trial judge's admissibility decision: relevance asks whether evidence tends to make a consequential fact more or less probable, and Rule 403 permits exclusion when probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice or related dangers. See Fed. R. Evid. 401, https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_401; Fed. R. Evid. 403, https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_403. The Supreme Court has stated that abuse of discretion is the ordinary standard for reviewing evidentiary rulings. See General Electric Co. v. Joiner, 522 U.S. 136 (1997), https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/96-188.

The distractors match the source mechanics. Clearly erroneous review applies to factual findings by a judge in a nonjury trial under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 52(a)(6), not to a jury-trial evidentiary ruling. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_52. Harmless error is an appellate consequence rule for errors that do not affect substantial rights, not the first-pass standard for reviewing whether evidence was properly admitted. See 28 U.S.C. 2111, https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/2111; Fed. R. Civ. P. 61, https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_61. No doctrinal-currency hotlist issue is triggered.
