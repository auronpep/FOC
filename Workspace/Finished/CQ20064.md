**Original key:** A

---

### 1. Black-Letter Rule Being Tested

The distinction between **abuse of process** and **malicious prosecution**:
- **Abuse of process** (Restatement (Second) of Torts § 682): The gist is the misuse of legal process *after it is issued*, for an improper collateral purpose. The process need be regularly issued; the wrong is the perversion of it to an end it was not designed to serve.
- **Malicious prosecution** (Restatement (Second) of Torts § 674): The gist is the *wrongful initiation* of proceedings without probable cause, with malice, and requiring favorable termination of the underlying action.

The tested rule: when valid process (here, a subpoena) is used after issuance to pressure an unrelated outcome (here, a property sale), the correct theory is abuse of process — not malicious prosecution.

### 9. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**Gold Key:** Abuse of process and malicious prosecution protect against different wrongs. Abuse of process targets what you *do with* valid process after you get it — using it as a lever to coerce something the process was never designed to achieve. Malicious prosecution targets the *start* of a case — dragging someone into court without probable cause. The fork: if the wrong is how process was used after issuance, the claim is abuse of process; if the wrong is starting a baseless case, the claim is malicious prosecution.

**C is correct.** Paul obtained a subpoena — valid legal process — in a legitimate replevin action. He then used that subpoena to demand Hannah's proprietary recipes from her supplier, not to advance the replevin case, but to coerce Hannah into transferring her parking lot deed. That is a classic improper collateral purpose: the subpoena was used as a lever to extract something entirely outside the scope of the underlying litigation. This is abuse of process.

### 10. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations (Diagnostic Register)

**A (dominant trap) — "Malicious prosecution, because every aggressive litigation tactic constitutes malicious prosecution."**

*What makes it attractive:* The word "malicious" feels right when someone is acting badly in litigation. Students hear "misuse of legal process" and jump to "malicious prosecution" because the names sound synonymous. The aggressive threat ("tell the town") reinforces the "malicious" label.

*The breaker:* Malicious prosecution requires the *wrongful initiation* of proceedings — no probable cause, malice, and favorable termination. Here, the replevin action was valid and properly initiated. The wrong is what Paul did *after* he got the subpoena, not how he started the case. **Gold Key:** Abuse of process = misuse of valid process after issuance for a collateral purpose. Malicious prosecution = starting a baseless case. The fork is initiation vs. post-issuance misuse.

**B — "Defamation, because subpoenas are always public statements of fact."**

*What makes it attractive:* The stem mentions "threatening to tell the town" — a reputational threat. Students fixate on the embarrassment angle and map it to defamation.

*The breaker:* A subpoena is a court order compelling production; it is not a public statement of fact published to third parties. The tort described is about *misusing legal process*, not about false statements harming reputation. The threat to "tell the town" is the *collateral purpose* used to coerce the deed transfer — it is the lever, not the tort itself.

**D — "No tort is possible because the original replevin action was valid."**

*What makes it attractive:* Students know that a valid underlying suit can block some claims (e.g., malicious prosecution requires lack of probable cause). They overgeneralize: valid suit = no tort at all.

*The breaker:* Abuse of process does *not* require that the underlying process be invalid. In fact, the tort *requires* regularly issued process — the wrong is the misuse of that valid process for an improper collateral purpose. The validity of the replevin action is precisely what makes this abuse of process, not a bar to it.

### 11. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Abuse of process** — Restatement (Second) of Torts § 682 (1977):
> "One who uses a legal process, whether criminal or civil, against another primarily to accomplish a purpose for which it is not designed, is subject to liability to the other for harm caused by the abuse of process."

Elements: (1) regularly issued legal process, (2) an intent to accomplish an improper/collateral purpose, (3) a definite act or threat in furtherance of that purpose, (4) damages. *See also* Restatement (Second) of Torts § 682, comment a; *Hawley v. Bapoo*, 2005 CanLII 36451 (Ont. S.C.) at ¶85–86 (summarizing Fleming's formulation); *Aztec Sound Corp. v. Western States Leasing Co.*, 510 P.2d 897 (Colo. Ct. App. 1973).

**Malicious prosecution** — Restatement (Second) of Torts § 674 (1977):
> "One who takes an active part in the initiation, continuation or procurement of civil proceedings against another is subject to liability to the other for malicious prosecution if... he acts without probable cause, and primarily for a purpose other than that of securing the proper adjudication of the claim..."

Elements: (1) initiation/continuation of proceedings, (2) without probable cause, (3) with malice/improper purpose, (4) termination in favor of the accused, (5) damages. *See also* *O'Keeffe's Inc. v. 400 Times Sq. Assoc., LLC*, 2020 NY Slip Op. 30208(U) (Comm. Div.) (abuse of process claim cannot be based on filing a lawsuit alone; the gist lies in misuse of process after issuance).

**Verification:** The credited answer (abuse of process) is correct under the governing rule. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original. The transform preserves the invariant layer. **Bank validation verdict: PASS.**

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

#### Program Frame

**Subject:** Torts — Other Torts
**Topic:** Abuse of Process vs. Malicious Prosecution
**Difficulty:** Core
**Verdict:** C3_SOLVE — clean CUT, no anchor dependency beyond the Gold Key distinction.

This item tests whether you can tell two "bad litigation behavior" torts apart. The fork: was the wrong *starting* a baseless case (malicious prosecution), or *misusing* valid process after you got it (abuse of process)?

#### Student Access Test

Every claim in this case study must be resolvable by a student who has completed C3 lessons, subject overlays, and taught tiny anchors / Gold Keys — without outside legal doctrine. If a choice requires attorney knowledge to eliminate, it is tagged accordingly.

**Result:** All four choices are resolvable via C3 structural analysis (absolute words, non-sequiturs, misfits). The Gold Key (abuse-of-process vs. malicious-prosecution distinction) is the taught tiny anchor that makes this item easy.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Hannah sells her commercial oven to Paul on an installment contract. Paul stops paying. Hannah files a valid replevin action to recover the oven. After the court issues a subpoena in that case, Paul uses the subpoena to demand Hannah's proprietary bread recipes from Hannah's bakery supplier, threatening to tell the town Hannah uses inferior ingredients unless Hannah transfers the deed to her parking lot to Paul. Hannah sues Paul for misuse of legal process.

**Call:** Which theory best fits these facts?

**Choices:**
- A: Malicious prosecution, because every aggressive litigation tactic constitutes malicious prosecution
- B: Defamation, because subpoenas are always public statements of fact
- C: Abuse of process, because valid process was used for an improper collateral purpose after issuance
- D: No tort is possible because the original replevin action was valid

#### Distilled Core Question

A party uses a validly issued subpoena to pressure an unrelated property transfer. What tort theory applies?

#### Call and Prediction

**Call:** "Which theory best fits these facts?" — This is a standard torts identification call. No negative stem, no "EXCEPT," no adjacent-call trap.

**Prediction:** Look for the tort that covers misuse of valid process after issuance. That's abuse of process.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "Malicious prosecution, because every aggressive litigation tactic constitutes malicious prosecution"**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The word "every" is an absolute. Absolutes are red flags on the MBE. Also, the stem says the replevin action was *valid* — malicious prosecution requires a *baseless* case.
2. **Student label:** Overclaim — "every" aggressive tactic is not malicious prosecution.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Malicious prosecution, because Paul initiated the replevin action without probable cause and with malice." (But the stem says the action was valid, so this can't be true.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Malicious prosecution requires wrongful initiation without probable cause, malice, and favorable termination. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 674. The replevin action was valid, so this element is missing.

**Choice B: "Defamation, because subpoenas are always public statements of fact"**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The word "always" is an absolute. Also, a subpoena is a court order — it's not a "statement of fact" published to the public.
2. **Student label:** Misfit — defamation is the wrong tort.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Defamation, because Paul published a false statement of fact to the town that harmed Hannah's reputation." (But the threat to "tell the town" is the collateral lever, not the tort itself.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Defamation requires a false statement published to a third party. A subpoena is a court-ordered demand for production, not a publication.

**Choice C: "Abuse of process, because valid process was used for an improper collateral purpose after issuance"**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Matches the rule: valid process (subpoena) + improper collateral purpose (coercing a parking lot deed transfer) after issuance = abuse of process.
2. **Student label:** Correct.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This is the correct answer.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 682. Valid subpoena used to coerce a parking lot deed transfer — a purpose the subpoena was not designed to serve.

**Choice D: "No tort is possible because the original replevin action was valid"**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Non-sequitur. The validity of the replevin action doesn't logically lead to "no tort is possible." In fact, abuse of process *requires* valid process.
2. **Student label:** Half-truth — valid suit doesn't bar all torts.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "No tort is possible because Paul's use of the subpoena was within the scope of the replevin action." (But the facts say he used it to demand recipes and coerce a deed transfer — clearly outside the scope.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Abuse of process *requires* regularly issued process. The validity of the replevin action is a prerequisite for, not a bar to, the claim.

#### Residual Answer

After cutting A (overclaim), B (misfit), and D (non-sequitur), **C** is the only choice that breaks no filter. C is the credited answer.
