### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is the best answer. The killing happened fast, but the decision to kill was formed before the shot. Stephen bought the weapon, learned Hannah's routine, hid beside the path, waited, and attacked from a position of advantage. Gold Key: premeditation is about prior reflection and formed intent, not just the number of seconds between seeing the victim and firing. Silver Key: read the planning timeline before the trigger-pull timeline.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells the heat-of-passion shortcut. The loud disagreement makes the story feel emotional, but the next-morning weapon purchase, route study, concealment, and waiting show cooling and planning. That is the opposite of a sudden heat-of-passion killing.

B sells the split-second-shot trap. It notices that Stephen fired immediately when Hannah appeared, but it measures premeditation at the wrong point. The Gold Key kills this trap: the law looks to whether the intent to kill was formed before the killing, and the ambush preparation supplies that proof.

C sells a false arithmetic test. One shot can be enough for first-degree murder if it is the planned fatal act. The Silver Key would steer off this answer by asking how the defendant got to the shot, not how many times he fired.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule is unchanged. A willful, deliberate, and premeditated killing is first-degree murder, and lying in wait is a classic first-degree murder fact pattern. The fatal act can occur quickly if the prior conduct shows reflection, planning, concealment, waiting, and a surprise attack. A prior argument alone does not reduce a planned next-day ambush to voluntary manslaughter, and firing once does not make a planned killing involuntary manslaughter.

Authority floor: 18 U.S.C. section 1111(a), https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&path=%2Fprelim%40title18%2Fpart1%2Fchapter51, defines first-degree murder to include murder perpetrated by lying in wait or by any willful, deliberate, malicious, and premeditated killing. CALCRIM No. 521, https://www.justia.com/criminal/docs/calcrim/500/521/, states that first-degree murder can be based on willful, deliberate, premeditated killing and that the test is the extent of reflection, not the length of time; it also describes lying in wait as concealment, waiting and watching, and a surprise attack from a position of advantage. The supplied key matches these principles.
