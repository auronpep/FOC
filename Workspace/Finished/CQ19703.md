### 1. Final Question

On April 10, a church deacon named Daniel owned a small fishing boat that he used for ministry trips along the coast. As a gesture of gratitude for years of faithful service, Daniel gave the boat to his friend Lydia, a widow in the congregation. Daniel signed a written deed of gift, and Lydia accepted the delivery of the deed that same day. Lydia was slow to visit the harbormaster's office, and her deed went unrecorded.

On May 3, having heard nothing from Lydia, Daniel sold the same boat to Paul, a local fisherman who had no idea Lydia held any interest in the boat. Paul paid fair market value. On May 20, Lydia finally recorded her deed at the harbormaster's office. On May 22, Paul recorded his deed. In July, Paul sold the boat to Timothy, who immediately recorded his deed.

A statute in the jurisdiction provides: "Every conveyance of real property which shall not be recorded is void as against any subsequent purchaser of the same property in good faith and for a valuable consideration whose conveyance shall be first duly recorded."

In litigation between Lydia and Timothy, who should be declared owner of the boat?

**A.** Paul, because Paul recorded his deed before conveying the boat to Timothy.

**B.** Timothy, because Lydia did not purchase the boat from Daniel for value.

**C.** Lydia, because any recording by Paul was outside the chain of title.

**D.** Lydia, because Paul recorded his deed after Lydia recorded her deed.

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** Under a race-notice recording act, the prior grantee does NOT need to have paid value — the value/good-faith requirement applies to the *subsequent* purchaser. A grantee by gift who records a valid deed has a fully enforceable interest; the recording statute's protection of a subsequent BFP only kicks in when that BFP beats the prior grantee to the recording office.

D is correct. Lydia received a valid deed from Daniel and recorded it on May 20. Paul, a BFP for value without notice, recorded on May 22 — two days after Lydia. Under the race-notice statute, an unrecorded conveyance is void only against a subsequent BFP "whose conveyance shall be **first** duly recorded." Because Paul did not record before Lydia, Paul never obtained the statutory protection, and Lydia's recorded deed has priority. When Paul later conveyed to Timothy, Paul could only convey whatever interest he had — which was nothing as against Lydia. Timothy, despite recording, fares no better.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap) is attractive because** it states an undisputed fact: Paul did record his deed before he sold to Timothy. A student scanning for "who recorded first" might anchor on the Paul-Timothy transfer rather than the Lydia-Paul priority fight that actually decides the case. **The breaker:** the call asks about litigation between Lydia and Timothy. The only relevant recording "race" is between Lydia and Paul — the BFP who claimed under Daniel. Paul's recording date relative to Timothy is legally invisible for purposes of the statute. `Gold Key:` The race-notice statute only asks whether the *subsequent BFP* recorded before the *prior grantee* — not whether the BFP recorded before *his own* downstream grantee.

**B is attractive because** the statute's text prominently mentions "good faith and for valuable consideration," pulling the student toward analyzing who paid value. The student reasons: "The statute protects purchasers for value, and Lydia was a gift recipient, so she loses." **The breaker:** The value requirement is a condition on the *subsequent* purchaser's protection, not on the prior grantee. A prior grantee who received the property as a gift prevails over a subsequent BFP if the prior grantee recorded first — which Lydia did. `Gold Key:` In a race-notice scheme, a prior donee-grantee who records first will always prevail over a subsequent BFP, because the BFP was not "first duly recorded."

**C is attractive because** "outside the chain of title" sounds like a sophisticated recording-act concept, and students have learned that a grantor who has already conveyed has nothing left to grant. The student might reason: "After Daniel gave the boat to Lydia, Daniel had nothing left to sell to Paul, so Paul's deed is outside the chain." **The breaker:** Chain-of-title exclusion only applies when the recording would not be discoverable by a reasonable title search. Here, Lydia recorded on May 20, and Paul recorded on May 22. A searcher working *forward* from Daniel's grant to Lydia would stop at Lydia and not look for a second grant by Daniel — BUT that means Paul's recording IS within the chain only if Paul recorded *before* Lydia, which he did not. The actual barber/Paul recording *is* discoverable in the chain (it was recorded, just later). The "outside the chain of title" doctrine does not help C because the statute's race-notice prong resolves the priority directly: Lydia recorded first.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification

The statute in the question is a classic **race-notice recording act**. It has two prongs:

1. **Notice prong:** The subsequent purchaser must take in good faith and for valuable consideration (i.e., be a bona fide purchaser without notice of the prior conveyance).
2. **Race prong:** The BFP must be "first duly recorded" — i.e., must record before the prior grantee.

Both prongs must be satisfied for the subsequent purchaser to prevail. *See* ＆nbsp;LII/Wex, "notice statute" and "race-notice statute" (Cornell Law School); Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages ＆sect; 7.1 (applying recording-act principles to competing transferees).

**Authority:** Under a race-notice statute, "the subsequent purchaser must both lack notice of the prior conveyance and record first." *Wex*, race-notice statute, Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School (accessed 2025). The language in the question — "void as against any subsequent purchaser... in good faith and for a valuable consideration whose conveyance shall be first duly recorded" — matches the canonical race-notice formulation. *See also* the Nebraska recording statute, Neb. Rev. Stat. ＆sect; 76-238 (void as against subsequent purchaser for value without notice who records first); Clements v. Doak, 140 Neb. 265, 299 N.W. 505 (1941).

**Key principles confirmed:**

- A prior grantee who received the property as a gift is NOT required to have paid value to enforce the deed against a subsequent BFP. The value requirement runs only to the subsequent purchaser's qualification. The prior grantee's deed is valid upon delivery; recording secures priority.

- The shelter rule (Timothy deriving from Paul) does not help Timothy if Paul himself never obtained priority over Lydia. A transferee from a non-prevailing BFP takes subject to the prior grantee's superior title.

- "Outside the chain of title" is a doctrine applied when a recording could not be found by a reasonable title search — e.g., a grantor who records a deed to X, then later records a deed to Y; if the searcher starts at the grantor and finds X's deed, the searcher would not look for a second grant by the same grantor. But the key inquiry is timeline-dependent. Here, because Lydia recorded first, Paul's later recording IS in the chain but does not win the race.

**Conclusion:** The credited answer (D, corresponding to original C) is doctrinally correct. The tested rule, legal issue, and outcome are UNCHANGED from the original question.

#### Distilled Core Question

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks who wins between Lydia (the prior grantee) and Timothy (who derives from Paul, the subsequent BFP). The statute says an unrecorded conveyance is void against a BFP who records first. Lydia DID record — and she recorded before Paul. So Paul was not "first duly recorded." Prediction: Lydia wins. This points to either C or D (both say Lydia wins).
