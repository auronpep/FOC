7. **Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)**

The objection should be sustained. Lydia is offering the binders to prove the truth of the complaints inside them: that earlier discriminatory acts really happened. That is hearsay use under Rule 801(c), and Rule 802 bars the evidence unless an exclusion or exception fits. `Silver Key:` lock the offered purpose before reading the answers. Once the purpose is truth, a notice theory does not rescue the file. `Gold Key:` a regular business file does not automatically clean every outsider statement inside it. Even if the company routinely kept complaint binders, the employee complaints inside still need their own hearsay answer. None is supplied here, so the binders remain inadmissible.

8. **Full wrong-answer explanations**

- **A.** This choice is attractive because notice is a real non-hearsay use for complaint records, and workplace cases often tempt students to jump there. The breaker is the call's stated purpose: Lydia is not offering the binders to show the company was on notice; she is offering them to prove the prior discriminatory acts happened. `Silver Key:` freeze the offered purpose before hunting exceptions.
- **B.** This choice sounds lawyerly because "prepared in anticipation of litigation" feels like a sophisticated records objection. The breaker is that the problem gives no litigation-prep fact at all, and that doctrine is not the hearsay question being asked. This answer wanders into a different evidentiary lane.
- **D.** This choice is attractive because a regularly kept file can trigger the business-records instinct. The breaker is that the complaint binders contain employee assertions offered for their truth, so the inner statements still need their own hearsay answer. `Gold Key:` a business record exception covers the recordkeeping layer, not every complaint statement inside the file.

9. **Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer**

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original item. Under Fed. R. Evid. 801(c), hearsay is an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted. Under Fed. R. Evid. 802, hearsay is inadmissible unless another rule provides otherwise. Fed. R. Evid. 803(6) can admit a regularly kept business record, but Fed. R. Evid. 805 requires each layer of hearsay inside a combined statement to fit an exception. Here, the binders are offered to prove the prior discriminatory incidents actually occurred, so the employee complaints inside the binders are being used for their truth. The facts do not supply a separate exclusion or exception for those complaint statements. That means the hearsay problem survives even if the company regularly archived the binders. The official key therefore remains correct.
