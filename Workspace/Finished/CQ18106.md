## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A — "Peter is guilty because conspiracy does not require completion of the larceny." (DOMINANT TRAP)**

The cognitive move this choice sells: a student who studied inchoate crimes knows that "conspiracy doesn't require completion" is a real and accurate rule. Seeing a familiar, correct conspiracy principle in a choice feels reassuring — it looks like the choice that shows mastery of the doctrine.

The breaker: the call asks for Peter's **strongest argument**, not the prosecution's. A choice that says "Peter IS GUILTY" is the prosecution's conclusion, not Peter's argument. Additionally, even on the merits, the no-completion rule addresses a different element — whether the target crime was completed — not whether Peter agreed to join. The missing element on these facts is agreement, and the no-completion rule does not fill that gap. **Silver Key SK-CRIMINAL-CONSPIRACY-CALL-01** cuts this before the merits: "guilty" choices cannot be the defendant's strongest argument.

**Choice C — "Peter is not guilty only because the cashbox held only small-denomination coins."**

The cognitive move: the choice says "not guilty," matching the call direction (Peter's argument). A student who eliminates A and D might briefly consider whether coin value is somehow relevant — maybe a value threshold matters for larceny?

The breaker: the denomination of the coins in the cashbox has zero bearing on whether Peter agreed to join a conspiracy. The larceny value threshold (if any) might affect the degree of the crime, but it has nothing to do with whether Peter conspired. This is a misfit reason that fails to address the actual legal question. Peter's acquittal argument, if valid, must be grounded in the missing agreement — not in the target's contents.

**Choice D — "Peter is guilty because he heard the plan and did not object."**

The cognitive move: silence during a planning session feels like endorsement. A student might reason that if Peter heard everything and said nothing — didn't protest, didn't walk away — he effectively assented. Silence as tacit agreement is a natural inference in everyday life.

The breaker: conspiracy law requires affirmative agreement — words or conduct showing assent to the criminal objective. Passive presence and failure to object are not enough, without more, to prove conspiratorial agreement. This choice misstates the law. **Gold Key GK-CRIMINAL-CONSPIRACY-AGREEMENT-01** kills this trap: silence is not agreement; Peter had to do something affirmative to join the conspiracy.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Tested rule:** Conspiracy requires an agreement between two or more persons to commit a criminal act. Mere presence at the scene of planning, knowledge of a criminal plan, association with co-conspirators, and failure to object do not establish the agreement element. The prosecution must prove actual assent — words or conduct showing the defendant joined the criminal objective.

**Credited outcome:** Peter's strongest argument is that the prosecution cannot prove agreement — knowledge and presence without agreement are not enough for conspiracy. Unchanged from the original.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer):**
- Model Penal Code § 5.03: conspiracy requires "agreeing with such other person or persons" to commit or attempt to commit the criminal objective. Mere knowledge is not enough.
- Common law conspiracy: the actus reus is the agreement itself; every jurisdiction requires proof of an actual agreement, not merely proximity or knowledge. *Iannelli v. United States*, 420 U.S. 770 (1975): conspiracy is an agreement to commit an unlawful act.
- *United States v. Grassi*, 616 F.2d 1295 (5th Cir. 1980): "mere association with conspirators or mere presence at the scene does not make one a conspirator." Widely cited formulation of the rule.
- *Direct Sales Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 703 (1943): knowledge of a criminal scheme without more does not establish participation in a conspiracy.
- The "no completion required" rule in Choice A: accurate as a statement of inchoate crime doctrine (MPC § 5.03(1)), but addresses the completion element, not the agreement element. It does not cure the absence of proof of agreement.

**No doctrinal-currency hotlist item triggered.** Conspiracy agreement doctrine is unchanged by recent Supreme Court development.

#### choice_by_choice_walkthrough

---

**Choice A — "Peter is guilty because conspiracy does not require completion of the larceny." (DOMINANT TRAP)**

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** The call asks for Peter's **strongest argument**. This choice says Peter IS GUILTY. A guilty conclusion cannot be Peter's argument — it is the prosecution's position. **Silver Key SK-CRIMINAL-CONSPIRACY-CALL-01:** bracket any "guilty" choice when the call asks for the defendant's argument. Additionally, even on the merits, the no-completion rule is accurate but addresses the wrong element — it speaks to completion, not agreement. The missing element is agreement, which the no-completion rule does not supply.

**(2) Student label:** DOMINANT TRAP

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Peter is guilty because [he showed affirmative agreement — by words or conduct — to join the theft plan]." That would be a proper prosecution argument. But the facts negate it.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** The rule that conspiracy is an inchoate crime not requiring completion of the target offense is correct (MPC § 5.03; common law). But it addresses the completion element, not the agreement element. Where the prosecution's theory fails is the agreement element — Peter never agreed. The no-completion rule does not fill that gap. This choice is bait doctrine: a true conspiracy rule applied to the wrong fact gap.

---

**Choice B — "Peter is not guilty because the facts show knowledge and presence but no agreement to join the theft plan." (CORRECT)**

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is Peter's argument, stated correctly. It identifies the specific legal element the prosecution cannot prove: agreement. It accurately characterizes the facts (knowledge + presence only). Nothing in this choice is legally defective. **Gold Key GK-CRIMINAL-CONSPIRACY-AGREEMENT-01** confirms: presence + knowledge + silence do not establish the agreement element.

**(2) Student label:** CORRECT ANSWER

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true and responsive answer. It gives Peter's actual best defense grounded in the missing agreement element.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** Conspiracy requires proof of actual agreement to pursue the criminal objective. MPC § 5.03: "agrees with such other person or persons" is required. *United States v. Grassi*, 616 F.2d 1295 (5th Cir. 1980): "mere association with conspirators or mere presence at the scene does not make one a conspirator." *Direct Sales Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 703 (1943): knowledge without more does not establish participation in a conspiracy. Peter's facts — presence, knowledge, silence, no role, no help, no assent — fall squarely within the rule: no agreement, no conspiracy.

---

**Choice C — "Peter is not guilty only because the cashbox held only small-denomination coins."**

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice says "not guilty" — in the right direction for Peter. But the reason (coin denominations) is self-evidently irrelevant. The denomination of coins in the target cashbox has nothing to do with whether Peter agreed to conspire. This is a heuristic cut — no legal knowledge needed.

**(2) Student label:** ATTRACTIVE WRONG ANSWER

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Peter is not guilty only because the value of the cashbox fell below the larceny threshold" — that would at least engage a value element (even if irrelevant to conspiracy). But even that would be wrong, and "small denominations" is not a legal threshold for conspiracy liability.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** Property characteristics of the target — including the value, denomination, or ease of resale — are generally relevant to the degree of a property offense (e.g., petty vs. grand larceny thresholds) but not to the agreement element of conspiracy. Nothing about the cashbox's contents determines whether Peter agreed to join the scheme.

---

**Choice D — "Peter is guilty because he heard the plan and did not object."**

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** This says Peter IS GUILTY — the call asks for Peter's argument; a "guilty" conclusion cannot be his argument. **Silver Key SK-CRIMINAL-CONSPIRACY-CALL-01** cuts this on call-direction. On the merits: failure to object is not the legal standard for conspiratorial agreement. Peter had no duty to object to avoid conspiracy liability; silence is not assent. **Gold Key GK-CRIMINAL-CONSPIRACY-AGREEMENT-01** kills this trap: conspiracy requires affirmative words or conduct showing agreement.

**(2) Student label:** ATTRACTIVE WRONG ANSWER

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Peter is guilty because he nodded when the plan was described and agreed to serve as the lookout" — that would show affirmative assent and a role, both of which are present here in the D's fantasy but absent from the actual facts.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** Conspiracy law does not impose a duty to object or leave a planning discussion to avoid liability. Agreement requires affirmative words or conduct manifesting assent to the criminal objective. Passive presence and silence during others' planning do not create conspiracy liability. This is a flat misstatement of the agreement element.
