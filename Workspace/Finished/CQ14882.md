## 1. Final Question

In a dispute over the distribution of a $2 million estate, the order of death between a husband and wife — both killed in a small-boat collision on a lake — determines which family inherits. An applicable state statute creates a rebuttable presumption that, when two people die in a common disaster, neither survived the other. A bystander on the shore has been called to testify that, as the boat was sinking, she heard what she believed was a woman's voice cry out, "I can't breathe," though by the time rescuers reached the couple, both had drowned. Is the bystander's testimony admissible?

**Call:** Is the bystander's testimony admissible?

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The bystander's testimony is admissible because it satisfies the threshold requirement of relevance under Federal Rule of Evidence 401 and no other evidentiary rule bars its admission.

First, the testimony is relevant. Under FRE 401, evidence is relevant if it has "any tendency to make a fact more or less probable than it would be without the evidence" and the fact is "of consequence in determining the action." The fact at issue is whether the wife survived the husband — a fact that determines the distribution of the estate. Hearing a woman's voice cry out as the boat was sinking tends to make it more probable that the wife was alive at that moment, which in turn makes it more probable she survived the husband (or at least did not predecease him). The fact is plainly of consequence.

Second, the testimony is not barred by the hearsay rule. Under FRE 801(c), hearsay is a statement offered "to prove the truth of the matter asserted." Here, the bystander is not offering the wife's cry ("I can't breathe") to prove that the wife was, in fact, unable to breathe. The statement is offered for the circumstantial purpose of showing that the wife was alive and conscious enough to speak — which is relevant to the order of death. Because the statement is not offered for its truth, it falls outside the definition of hearsay. *See* FRE 801(c); *see also* Graham, *Federal Rules of Evidence in a Nutshell* (effect-on-listener and non-truth purposes).

Third, the bystander is competent to testify. Under FRE 602, a witness may testify to a matter only if evidence is introduced sufficient to support a finding that the witness has personal knowledge. The bystander's own testimony that she heard the cry from the lake is sufficient to establish personal knowledge. FRE 701 permits lay opinion testimony rationally based on the witness's perception — here, her belief that she heard a woman's voice is a lay perception, not specialized knowledge.

**Silver Key:** When you see a statement being offered not for what it asserts but for what it *shows about the speaker's condition or circumstances*, immediately think: "non-truth purpose = non-hearsay." That move kills the hearsay objection before it starts.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect.** This answer is attractive because it correctly identifies that a presumption exists — the statute does create a rebuttable presumption that neither spouse survived the other. But the answer treats the presumption as though it is conclusive, which it is not. Under the majority "bursting bubble" view followed in most civil courts, once the opposing party introduces evidence that would support a finding of the nonexistence of the presumed fact, the presumption disappears and the jury decides the issue as if the presumption had never existed. The bystander's testimony — hearing a woman's voice — is sufficient counter-evidence to rebut the presumption. The presumption therefore does not bar the testimony; it simply does not control the outcome. The testimony remains admissible.

**B is incorrect.** This is the dominant trap. It appeals to a common-sense intuition: "how can we trust a voice heard from the shore during a chaotic event?" But the law does not require a witness to be absolutely certain of what she perceived. Under FRE 701, lay witness testimony need only be "rationally based on the witness's perception." The bystander heard what she believed was a woman's voice — that is a perception-based observation, not speculation. The weight of her testimony is for the jury to decide; it is not a ground for exclusion. A witness is competent under FRE 602 if there is evidence sufficient to support a finding of personal knowledge, and her own testimony supplies that foundation. The testimony is not "too speculative" as a matter of law.

**D is incorrect.** This answer reaches the correct result (admissible) but for the wrong reason. It claims the hearsay rule "does not apply" to statements by decedents in will cases. That is not a recognized rule. The hearsay rule applies in all civil proceedings, including will disputes. The testimony is admissible not because the hearsay rule is inapplicable, but because the statement is *non-hearsay* — it is offered not for the truth of the matter asserted (that the woman couldn't breathe) but as circumstantial evidence that she was alive and speaking. The distinction matters: the hearsay rule is alive and well; this statement simply falls outside its definition. **Silver Key:** The move here is to spot that the statement has a non-truth purpose — that's what makes it non-hearsay, not some blanket exemption for decedent statements.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Tested rule:** Relevance and the hearsay rule's "truth of the matter asserted" requirement (FRE 401, FRE 801(c)).

**Governing authority:**
- **FRE 401** — Evidence is relevant if "(a) it has any tendency to make a fact more or less probable than it would be without the evidence; and (b) the fact is of consequence in determining the action." (U.S. Code, Title 28, Appendix, Federal Rules of Evidence, Rule 401.)
- **FRE 801(c)** — Hearsay is "a statement that: (1) the declarant does not make while testifying at the current trial or hearing; and (2) a party offers in evidence to prove the truth of the matter asserted in the statement." (U.S. Code, Title 28, Appendix, Federal Rules of Evidence, Rule 801(c).)
- **FRE 602** — A witness may testify only if evidence supports a finding of personal knowledge. Personal knowledge may be proved by the witness's own testimony.
- **FRE 701** — Lay opinion testimony must be rationally based on perception, helpful, and not based on specialized knowledge.
- **FRE 301** — In civil cases, a presumption imposes on the party against whom it is directed the burden of producing evidence to rebut it. The majority "bursting bubble" view treats the presumption as vanishing once contrary evidence is introduced. *See* Advisory Committee Notes to FRE 301 (rejecting the bursting bubble theory as a matter of federal practice but acknowledging it is followed by many state courts); *Mobile, J. & K.C.R. Co. v. Turnipseed*, 219 U.S. 35 (1910).

**Verification:** The original question's credited answer (D) is correct under the governing rules. The testimony is relevant under FRE 401, is not hearsay under FRE 801(c) because it is offered for a non-truth purpose, and the witness is competent under FRE 602/701. The presumption is rebuttable and does not bar the testimony. The transform preserves the credited outcome and the legal substance exactly.

### 2. The 17-section student case study

## Program Frame

This item teaches a foundational evidence skill: the two-step admissibility inquiry. Step one — is the evidence relevant under FRE 401? Step two — does any other rule of evidence bar its admission? The item also teaches a critical hearsay distinction: a statement offered for a non-truth purpose (here, circumstantial evidence that the declarant was alive) is not hearsay under FRE 801(c). The student learns that "out-of-court statement" does not automatically equal "hearsay" — the purpose for which the statement is offered determines whether the rule applies.

## Student Access Test

A student who has completed the C3 lessons on relevance and hearsay can solve this item. The required knowledge is: (1) FRE 401's "any tendency" test for relevance; (2) FRE 801(c)'s definition of hearsay as a statement offered "to prove the truth of the matter asserted"; (3) the concept that a statement offered for a non-truth purpose is non-hearsay; (4) the fact that a rebuttable presumption does not bar contrary evidence. All of these are taught within the C3 framework. No outside legal doctrine is required.

## Distilled Core Question

Two people died in a common disaster. A statute presumes neither survived the other (rebuttable). A witness heard what she thought was one decedent's voice calling out as the event was happening. Is the testimony admissible?

## Call and Prediction

**Call:** Is the bystander's testimony admissible?

**Prediction:** The testimony is admissible. It is relevant (makes it more probable the wife was alive) and it is not hearsay (offered for a non-truth purpose — to show she was alive, not that she was dying). No other rule bars it. I expect the answer to be "Yes, because it is relevant and not otherwise prohibited."

## Trigger Facts

1. **Common disaster:** Both husband and wife died in a boat collision. This creates the need to determine the order of death.
2. **Rebuttable presumption:** The statute presumes neither survived the other, but the word "rebuttable" is the key — it means the presumption can be overcome.
3. **Witness heard a voice:** The bystander heard what she believed was a woman's voice cry out as the boat was sinking. This is the evidence being challenged.
4. **Both drowned:** By the time rescuers arrived, both were dead. This confirms the order of death is the dispositive issue.

## Governing C3 Lane

**RELEVANCE → HEARSAY PURPOSE → NON-HEARSY IDENTIFICATION**

The governing lane is relevance under FRE 401, followed by the hearsay analysis under FRE 801(c). The student must determine: (1) is the testimony relevant? (yes — it makes the wife's survival more probable); (2) is it hearsay? (no — it is offered for a non-truth purpose); (3) is any other rule a bar? (no). The item resolves at the CUT phase — all three distractors fail on visible structural defects.

## Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

### Choice A: "No, because the matter is governed by the presumption that neither spouse survived the other."

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** The word "governed" treats the presumption as conclusive. But the statute says "rebuttable." A rebuttable presumption does not govern — it is a starting point that counter-evidence can displace.

**(2) Student label:** Presumption-as-conclusive trap.

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like:** "The presumption is rebuttable, so it does not bar the testimony. The testimony itself may be the evidence that rebuts the presumption."

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 301. A rebuttable presumption imposes a burden of production, not a bar on evidence. *See* Advisory Committee Notes to FRE 301.

### Choice B: "No, because the witness's testimony is too speculative to support a finding."

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** "Too speculative" sounds like a practical objection, but the law does not require certainty. FRE 701 only requires that testimony be rationally based on perception. The witness heard what she believed was a voice — that is perception-based.

**(2) Student label:** Certainty trap.

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like:** "The testimony is not excluded for uncertainty. FRE 701 requires only that it be rationally based on perception. Weight is for the jury."

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 701; FRE 602. A witness need not be certain. Personal knowledge is sufficient.

### Choice C: "Yes, because it is relevant and not otherwise prohibited."

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** The testimony makes it more probable the wife was alive when she spoke. That is relevance under FRE 401. It is offered to show she was alive, not that she was dying — so it is not hearsay under FRE 801(c). No other rule bars it.

**(2) Student label:** Correct — relevant and non-hearsay.

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like:** Same as the choice. The testimony is relevant under FRE 401 and is not hearsay under FRE 801(c) because it is offered for a non-truth purpose.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 401; FRE 801(c). Statement offered for a non-truth purpose (circumstantial evidence of declarant's condition) is not hearsay.

### Choice D: "Yes, because the hearsay rule does not apply to statements by decedents in actions to determine rights under a will."

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** There is no blanket exemption for decedent statements in will cases. The hearsay rule applies in all civil proceedings. The statement is admissible because it is *non-hearsay* (not offered for its truth), not because the rule is inapplicable.

**(2) Student label:** Right outcome, wrong doctrine.

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like:** "The hearsay rule applies. The statement is admissible because it is non-hearsay — offered for a non-truth purpose — not because the rule is inapplicable."

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 801(c). The hearsay rule applies in all proceedings. No exemption for decedent statements exists.

## Remediation Card

**Card ID:** REM-EVIDENCE-RELEVANCE-001
**Title:** Non-Truth Purpose = Non-Hearsay
**Signal:** A witness repeats an out-of-court statement, but the purpose is to show the declarant's condition or state of mind — not the truth of what was said.
**Student Move:** Ask: "Is the statement offered to prove what it says, or to prove something about the speaker?" If the latter, it is non-hearsay.
**Tiny Rule:** A statement offered for a non-truth purpose (circumstantial evidence of the declarant's condition, effect on listener, verbal act) is not hearsay under FRE 801(c).
**Trap:** Students see an out-of-court statement and immediately think "hearsay" without checking the purpose for which it is offered.
