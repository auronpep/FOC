## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

B is correct. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars a second prosecution for the same offense only once jeopardy has *attached*. **Gold Key:** jeopardy attaches when trial begins — when the jury is sworn (jury trial) or the first witness is sworn (bench trial). A grand jury proceeding is not a trial and is not jeopardy, so the State may present the same matter to a second grand jury as often as it likes without any Double Jeopardy bar. Here no trial ever began; the case never got past the grand jury. Because jeopardy never attached, the clause is not triggered and the motion must be denied. B states exactly that — denial because no trial has begun — and it is the only choice that gives the categorical, correct reason.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

- **D (dominant trap).** D is the seductive one because it reaches the right outcome — denied — and then offers a refined-sounding caveat, and the stem hands you the bait: the prosecutor blamed the no-bill on "public sympathy," so a prejudice exception feels like it must matter. **Gold Key:** but jeopardy never attached, so there is nothing to except from; the clause does not turn on why a grand jury declined or on the prosecutor's motive, and no "sympathy or undue prejudice" exception exists. The motion is denied flat — not "unless."
- **A.** A is attractive because "same offense" is the heart of Double Jeopardy, so a choice that hinges on whether the charges were identical sounds like it is applying the very test you studied. The same-offense analysis only does work *after* jeopardy has attached; with no trial begun, whether the two sets of charges match is irrelevant. The choice applies a real rule in a context where the threshold question forecloses it.
- **C.** C is tempting because a grand jury that "decided" the matter feels final, and res judicata is the word for "already decided." Res judicata is a civil-preclusion doctrine reserved for a final determination by a court; a grand jury's refusal to indict is neither a court judgment nor a final adjudication, and it has nothing to do with the Double Jeopardy Clause the motion invokes. The choice borrows the wrong vocabulary from the wrong body of law.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

Tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are **unchanged** from the source item: Double Jeopardy attachment; a grand jury no-bill is not jeopardy; the prosecutor may resubmit; the motion to dismiss is **denied**.

Affirmative legal reasoning: the Fifth Amendment Double Jeopardy Clause forbids placing a person twice in jeopardy for the same offense, but the protection does not exist until jeopardy *attaches*, which occurs at the commencement of trial. A grand jury proceeding is an investigative/charging step, not a trial; no jeopardy attaches, so successive grand jury presentations raise no Double Jeopardy problem. The motion is denied because no trial has begun.

Lawyer-confirmation authority (not part of the student path): U.S. Const. amend. V (Double Jeopardy Clause); *Serfass v. United States*, 420 U.S. 377 (1975) (jeopardy had not attached where the case was dismissed before trial — no jury empaneled, no evidence heard; no Double Jeopardy bar); *Crist v. Bretz*, 437 U.S. 28 (1978) (in a jury trial, jeopardy attaches when the jury is empaneled and sworn). The grand-jury-resubmission point follows directly: a no-bill is not an acquittal and not jeopardy. Research confirms rather than contradicts the official key, so the verdict is PASS (no NEEDS_HUMAN). Double Jeopardy is not on the doctrinal-currency hotlist; the attachment framing remains the current controlling test.
