## 6. Full right-answer explanation

**D is correct.** The call asks for the best reason to exclude Timothy’s **statement**, not the jewelry. The answer must connect the statement to police conduct after Timothy asked for counsel.

**Gold Key:** After a suspect in custody asks for counsel, police cannot keep trying to get a statement through the functional equivalent of interrogation. The key is not “custody alone” or “no lawyer appeared.” The key is police-arranged elicitation.

**Silver Key:** When the choices mention custody, lawyer, privacy, and a police-arranged meeting, pick the answer that explains **how the police caused the statement**. D is the only answer that ties the parent-room setup to obtaining an incriminating statement.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A is wrong.** Custody is only a threshold fact. It does not by itself exclude the statement. The missing piece is interrogation or its functional equivalent.

**B is wrong.** The request for counsel matters, but this answer frames the violation too broadly. The police were required to stop interrogation. The best argument is not merely that they failed to produce a lawyer; it is that they arranged a substitute way to get Timothy to incriminate himself.

**Gold Key:** A request for counsel blocks police-initiated interrogation; it does not create a standalone “lawyer must instantly appear” rule.

**C is wrong.** “Any private conversations” is too broad. The answer shifts away from the real statement-suppression problem and turns the issue into a sweeping privacy rule. The better path is the narrower police-elicitation theory.

**Silver Key:** Do not chase the broad privacy answer. The stem is testing the police-created path to the confession.

## 8. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

This variation preserves the tested rule and the official outcome. The official row states that D is correct because the police arranged the parent meeting and recorder after Timothy invoked counsel, making the setup a deliberate attempt to elicit incriminating statements. 

In lawyer-confirmation terms, **Edwards v. Arizona** confirms that once a suspect invokes the right to counsel during custodial interrogation, interrogation must cease until counsel is present, and police may not reinitiate interrogation at their instance. ([Justia Law][1]) **Rhode Island v. Innis** defines interrogation to include express questioning and its functional equivalent: police words or actions they should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. ([Justia Law][2]) **Arizona v. Mauro** is the useful contrast: the Court did not suppress a recorded spouse conversation where the spouse insisted on speaking and the police did not create the meeting as a ruse or indirect Miranda evasion. Here, the BarMatrix fact pattern flips that fact: the police asked the parents to speak with Timothy and secretly recorded the meeting, making D the best exclusion argument. ([Justia Law][3])
