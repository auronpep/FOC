## 1. Final Question

Lydia's Workshop, LLC, a private Christian craft company, bought a broad desert parcel that contained a vein of fine clay. Lydia planned to quarry the clay and make Scripture-themed tiles for sale. Before digging began, the company bought portable quarry equipment and kiln trailers. Congress then enacted a statute imposing new aquifer-protection buffers and land-reclamation requirements on all commercial clay-extraction operations in the United States. The statute made Lydia's planned clay operation economically infeasible. Lydia's Workshop sold the parcel to Ruth, who uses it as an olive farm. The sale price allowed Lydia's Workshop to recover its original investment in the land, but it did not cover the cost of the quarry equipment and kiln trailers or the profits Lydia expected to earn from the tile business. In an action filed against the appropriate federal official, Lydia's Workshop claims that the statute effected a taking for which it is entitled to just compensation equal to the equipment costs and expected profits.

Which of the following is the most appropriate result in the action?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

Does a federal land-use regulation require compensation for movable equipment costs or expected profits when it blocks an intended extraction business but the land retains economically viable use and the equipment can still be sold or used elsewhere?

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. The statute made Lydia's preferred clay operation uneconomical, but it did not leave the parcel economically idle. Lydia's Workshop recovered its original land investment by selling the parcel, and the land still has productive farm use. The movable quarry equipment and kiln trailers were not appropriated or made valueless in the constitutional sense; Lydia's Workshop can sell them or use them elsewhere. Expected business profits are not the property interest that turns this regulation into a compensable taking.

Gold Key: A land-use regulation is not a categorical taking just because it blocks the owner's planned or highest-value use; the exam anchor is whether economically viable use remains. Silver Key: Read the choices as a two-claim matrix - equipment costs yes/no and expected profits yes/no. Once both claims fail, the only responsive box is "neither."

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the attractive move that the equipment is different from profits because it is a concrete out-of-pocket purchase. The breaker is that no one took the equipment, and the regulation did not make it valueless; Lydia's Workshop still owns movable items that can be sold or used in another operation. Gold Key: sunk project costs do not become Takings Clause compensation when the property and equipment retain economically viable uses.

C sells the broad grievance version of the case: the federal statute ruined the plan, so every business loss should be paid. The breaker is the Takings Clause does not insure the owner's chosen business model. The land kept viable economic use, the equipment remained movable, and projected tile profits are too speculative to create the compensation claim.

D sells the idea that expected profits are the real loss because Lydia's tile business was the whole point of the purchase. The breaker is that expected profits are not the relevant property interest for this regulatory-takings claim. A frustrated profit stream does not supply compensation when the regulation leaves economically viable property use.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested legal issue and credited outcome are unchanged from source question 14248: whether a federal land-use regulation that prevents the owner's intended extraction business, while leaving the land with economically viable use and leaving movable equipment usable or saleable, requires just compensation for sunk equipment costs or expected profits. It does not.

The Fifth Amendment bars taking private property for public use without just compensation. For regulatory takings, Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City, 438 U.S. 104 (1978), asks about economic impact, interference with distinct investment-backed expectations, and the character of the government action. Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council, 505 U.S. 1003 (1992), recognizes a categorical taking when a regulation denies all economically beneficial use of land. Here, the regulation does not deny all economic use because the parcel can be sold and used as farmland. Andrus v. Allard, 444 U.S. 51 (1979), supports the treatment of retained personal property and speculative future profits: where owners retain economically useful rights, lost future profit alone is too thin a basis for a takings claim.
