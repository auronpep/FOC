## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** Under FRE 801(d)(1)(C), a statement of identification of a person made after perceiving that person is **not hearsay** if the declarant testifies at the trial or hearing and is subject to cross-examination concerning the statement. Here, Lydia is on the witness stand, testified at trial, and is available for cross-examination. Her prior lineup identification of the defendant falls squarely within this exemption. The objection should not be sustained.

> **Silver Key (SK-EVIDENCE-PRIOR-ID-01):** When a witness who is testifies at trial has previously identified the defendant (lineup, photo array, etc.), that prior ID is NOT hearsay under FRE 801(d)(1)(C) — as long as the witness is on the stand and available for cross.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (Yes — may not impeach own witness):**
This choice sells two misconceptions at once. First, it's simply false that a party cannot impeach its own witness — FRE 607 expressly permits any party to attack the credibility of any witness. Second, the prosecutor isn't impeaching Lydia. Impeachment means attacking a witness's credibility. The prosecutor is bolstering Lydia's testimony by showing she previously identified the defendant — the opposite of impeachment. This choice is wrong on both counts.

**B (Yes — hearsay not within any exception):** **[DOMINANT TRAP]**
This is the most attractive wrong answer because Lydia's prior lineup identification *is* an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted (that the defendant is the robber). Students who stop at "out-of-court statement = hearsay" without checking the 801(d) exemptions will pick this. But FRE 801(d)(1)(C) specifically exempts prior identifications from the hearsay definition when the declarant testifies and is available for cross-examination. The statement is not hearsay at all.

> **Gold Key (GK-EVIDENCE-PRIOR-ID-01):** FRE 801(d)(1)(C) says a prior identification is NOT hearsay — not an "exception to hearsay," but excluded from the definition of hearsay entirely — if the declarant testifies at trial and is subject to cross-examination.

**C (No — disinterested witness):**
This choice reaches the right outcome (objection not sustained) but for the wrong reason. A witness's interest or lack thereof goes to the *weight* the jury gives the testimony, not to its *admissibility*. Lydia works at the robbed market — she may well be an interested witness. But even if she were disinterested, that wouldn't be the legal basis for admitting her prior identification. The correct basis is FRE 801(d)(1)(C).

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**FRE 801(d)(1)(C) — Prior Identification:**
A statement is not hearsay if "the declarant testifies at the trial or hearing and is subject to cross-examination concerning the statement, and the statement is ... one of identification of a person made after perceiving the person." (Fed. R. Evid. 801(d)(1)(C).)

**FRE 607 — Who May Impeach:**
Any party, including the party that called the witness, may attack the credibility of any witness. (Fed. R. Evid. 607.) This eliminates the "can't impeach own witness" distractor.

**FRE 801(c) — Hearsay Definition:**
Hearsay is a statement that "(1) the declarant does not make while testifying at the current trial or hearing; and (2) a party offers in evidence to prove the truth of the matter asserted in the statement." (Fed. R. Evid. 801(c).) A prior lineup identification satisfies both elements — but 801(d)(1)(C) removes it from the hearsay category entirely.
