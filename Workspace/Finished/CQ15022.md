Naomi owned five adjoining cottage parcels, labeled A through E, all fronting on Psalm Lane. The parcels were in a zoning district that allowed one- and two-family residences. Last month, Naomi conveyed Parcel D to Timothy. The deed to Timothy contained no use restriction. The recorded deed from Naomi to Lydia appeared in the title report that Timothy's lawyer examined. Timothy obtained a building permit and began construction of a two-family residence on Parcel D.

Which is the most appropriate comment concerning the outcome of this action?

Timothy's lawyer saw that deed in the title report, so Timothy took with record notice. **Gold Key:** an express restrictive covenant or equitable servitude in a recorded deed can bind a later buyer of the burdened parcel who has notice, but only benefited owners or true common-scheme owners can enforce. **Silver Key:** draw the parcel map and mark which deeds contain the restriction. ## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sounds precise because damages are a real remedy for some covenant violations. The breaker is scope. It gives damages to every plaintiff and denies the injunction route, but the deed map puts Lydia inside the covenant and leaves the other owners outside it.

**B.** This is the dominant trap. It turns a row of one-family homes into a common residential development scheme for all parcel owners. **Gold Key:** a common scheme is not created just because neighbors built similar homes; the deed pattern matters. Parcels A, C, and E were sold without restrictions, and the only written restriction tied Parcels B and D. **Silver Key:** mark the restricted deed before deciding who can enforce.

**C.** This choice treats the building permit and zoning ordinance as the final word. **Gold Key:** zoning permission does not erase a stricter private restrictive covenant. Timothy may comply with zoning and still violate Lydia's private servitude.

## 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The source row is Q15022, a Real Property / Rights in Land item keyed to the outcome that the owner of the parcel benefited by the recorded one-family covenant can obtain an appropriate remedy against the later buyer of the burdened parcel, while the owners of unrestricted parcels cannot recover merely as neighbors. The verified outline code is **81020502**, "Non-Possessory Rights and Interests in Land > Equitable Servitudes / Restrictive Covenants > Running with the land."

Under the traditional MBE rule, an equitable servitude or restrictive covenant restricting land use can bind a later owner if the servitude was intended to run, touches and concerns the land, and the later owner has notice; equitable servitudes do not require privity and are enforced through equitable remedies. Cornell's Wex entry on covenants distinguishes real covenants from equitable servitudes and explains that equitable servitudes require notice rather than privity and can be enforced by equitable relief when monetary damages are inadequate. [1] Sanborn v. McLean is the classic reciprocal-negative-easement/common-scheme case: the Michigan Supreme Court treated a residential plan by a common owner as enforceable against a later purchaser with actual or constructive notice, but the doctrine depends on a real common plan from the common owner, not mere neighborhood similarity. [2] Zoning and private covenants are separate systems; private covenants may be more restrictive than zoning ordinances. [3]

The variation preserves the legal issue and credited outcome. Lydia's recorded deed supplies intent, land-use relation, and notice to Timothy. The other parcel owners received unrestricted deeds and have no shown common scheme or express servitude benefit.

The call asks for the most appropriate outcome comment. Predict before choices: Lydia has the recorded covenant benefit; Timothy had record notice; the unrestricted neighbors are not automatically beneficiaries.

## trigger_facts

* Parcels A, C, and E were sold with no restrictions.
* Parcel B's deed restricted both Parcel B and Parcel D to one-family residential use.
