### 1. Final question
A city owns and operates a large public observatory dome. It rents the dome to any group that wants to hold a meeting, lecture, recital, contest, or community display. Each user must post a cleaning deposit and pay rent calculated only for the actual hours the dome is used. Reservations are made on a first-come, first-served basis.

A private organization called the Good Samaritan Kite League permits only men to serve on its National Steward Council, the League's highest offices. The League rented the observatory dome for its annual lantern-and-kite exposition and planned to install its new National Steward Council during that event. It broadly publicized the exposition and invited members of the general public to attend the installation ceremony at the city observatory.

No statute or administrative rule prohibits the League from restricting its highest offices to men. An appropriate plaintiff sues the League, seeking to enjoin it from using the city observatory for the installation of its new officers. The plaintiff's sole claim is that the League's use of the city observatory for the installation ceremony is unconstitutional because the League disqualifies women from serving in its highest offices. Will the plaintiff prevail?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. The plaintiff is trying to turn a private leadership rule into a constitutional violation because the private group rented a public building for the ceremony. Gold Key: the Fourteenth Amendment reaches state action, not merely private conduct; a private group's internal rule does not become state action merely because the group uses a city facility made available to all renters on neutral terms. Silver Key: on a constitutional claim against a private group, run the state-action threshold before jumping to discrimination merits. The city did not create, require, enforce, or significantly encourage the men-only leadership rule.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Constitutional Law, Individual Rights, State Action Requirement, and the verified outline code is `44040100`. The Fourteenth Amendment's state-action language is the governing constitutional floor. Constitution Annotated explains that the Fourteenth Amendment, by its terms, limits governmental entities rather than private parties, because the text speaks in terms of what a State may not do. See U.S. Const. amend. XIV, Section 1, and State Action Doctrine, https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/amdt14-2/ALDE_00000810/.

The Supreme Court's state-action cases confirm the official result. `Moose Lodge No. 107 v. Irvis`, 407 U.S. 163 (1972), held that a state liquor license issued to a discriminatory private club was not enough state action. See https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/407/163/. `Blum v. Yaretsky`, 457 U.S. 991 (1982), states the general rule that a state is normally responsible for a private decision only when it coerces or significantly encourages that decision. See https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/457/991/. `Lugar v. Edmondson Oil Co.`, 457 U.S. 922 (1982), supplies the contrast: a private party can become a state actor when it jointly participates with state officials in a deprivation. See https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/457/922/. `Brentwood Academy v. Tennessee Secondary School Athletic Assn.`, 531 U.S. 288 (2001), collects the attribution theories, including coercion, significant encouragement, joint activity, public function, and entwinement. See https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/531/288/.

The city observatory facts do not meet those attribution theories. The city rents the facility to any group on first-come, first-served, hourly terms. The city did not adopt the League's leadership rule, require it, enforce it, subsidize it in a targeted way, or create a symbiotic operating relationship. `Burton v. Wilmington Parking Authority`, 365 U.S. 715 (1961), found state action where a discriminatory restaurant was physically and financially integrated into a public parking facility, but this variant lacks that kind of joint participation or financial interdependence. See https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/365/715/. The source's credited outcome is legally sound: the plaintiff does not prevail on the sole Fourteenth Amendment claim against the private organization.

The freedom-of-association distractor is also overbroad. `Roberts v. United States Jaycees`, 468 U.S. 609 (1984), upheld application of a state public-accommodations law to a large private civic organization that excluded women from full membership, rejecting an absolute associational immunity theory on those facts. See https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/468/609/. Here, no such statute or rule exists, so Roberts does not create liability; it simply defeats the claim that the city could never regulate facility use.
