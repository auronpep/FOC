### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** The government is offering the insurance evidence for **motive**: Lydia had a possible financial reason to burn insured property. That is enough for relevance because the evidence tends to make a consequential fact more probable. **Gold Key:** insurance evidence is purpose-sensitive; it is not automatically excluded merely because the word “insurance” appears. **Silver Key:** lock the offered purpose first. Here the purpose is motive, not a blanket claim that insured people act wrongfully.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because “offered against a party” sounds like a broad admissibility shortcut. The breaker is the word **any**. No C3 student should accept a blanket rule that all party conduct comes in just because it is offered against that party.

**C.** This is the dominant trap. It sells the familiar insurance-exclusion idea and feels safer than admitting insurance evidence. **Gold Key:** the insurance rule is purpose-sensitive. The stem’s purpose is motive; this choice answers a different purpose, whether insurance proves wrongful conduct.

**D.** This choice is attractive because Rule 403 balancing sounds cautious and lawyerly. The breaker is the heavy threshold: the stem gives motive relevance and gives no strong unfair-prejudice or jury-confusion fact that substantially outweighs that relevance.
