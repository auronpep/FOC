### 1. Final question — revised stem + call

At a for-profit Bible-camp supply expo, Martha sold off the remaining stock from her “Loaves & Fishes” craft-kit shop. Weeks earlier, Lydia, a professional inventory consultant, had prepared a crate-count sheet solely so Martha could plan warehouse space and packing for the expo. **In Timothy’s negligent-misrepresentation claim against Lydia, how should the court rule?**

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A professional prepared an inaccurate count sheet only for a client’s internal planning. An unknown later buyer relied on a copy in a sale packet and lost money. Is the professional liable to that buyer for negligent misrepresentation?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** The court should rule for Lydia. The count sheet was made for Martha’s internal planning, not for Timothy, not for a known or limited group of buyers, and not for a specific sale Lydia was hired to guide.

**Gold Key:** A professional information supplier’s negligent-misrepresentation duty for economic loss does not run to every foreseeable later reader; it is limited to a known person or limited group for a transaction the supplier intended or knew the information would influence.

**Silver Key:** When one choice says “foreseeable reader” and another says “known or limited class for a specific transaction,” the fight is not general foreseeability. The fight is targeted-use duty.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells a shortcut: if the professional count is wrong, make the professional pay. The breaker is the call’s negligence frame; “strictly liable” answers a different liability theory and skips the duty limit.

**C.** This choice sells a safe-sounding “no” by making economic loss seem categorically barred. The breaker is the word **never**; negligent misrepresentation is one of the settings where economic loss can be recoverable when the limited information-supply relationship exists.

**D.** This choice sells the ordinary negligence reflex: foreseeable reader, foreseeable loss, plaintiff wins. **Silver Key:** the answer stops at open-ended foreseeability, while the keyed answer names the targeted-use axis. **Gold Key:** the professional-information rule requires a known or limited class tied to a specific transaction.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

The tested rule is unchanged. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 552 limits negligent-misrepresentation liability for pecuniary loss to persons, or a limited group of persons, for whose benefit and guidance the information is supplied, and to the transaction or substantially similar transaction the supplier intends or knows the recipient intends to influence. ([Richard Cheeks][1]) California’s Supreme Court in *Bily v. Arthur Young & Co.* adopted the Restatement § 552 plaintiff-scope limit for negligent misrepresentation and emphasized that an auditor is not on notice absent a specific transaction or well-defined type of transaction the report was meant to influence. ([Justia][2]) *Ultramares Corp. v. Touche* supplies the classic indeterminate-liability concern for accountants and unknown third-party readers. ([Justia][3])

The variation preserves the same legal issue: a professional prepared information for an internal client use, the client later exposed it to an unknown third party, the third party relied and suffered economic loss, and the professional is not liable because the plaintiff was not inside the targeted class or transaction.

The surface story changes from corporate accounting and stock investing to a Christian-themed commercial expo, craft-kit inventory, and a resale purchase. The dominant cognitive trap remains the same: treating broad foreseeability as enough.
