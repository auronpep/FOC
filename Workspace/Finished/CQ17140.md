## 7. Right-Answer Explanation

**A — admit both to rebut fabrication and as substantive evidence** is correct.

FRE 801(d)(1)(B)(i) designates a prior consistent statement as **not hearsay** when three conditions are met: (1) the declarant testifies at trial and is subject to cross-examination about the statement; (2) the statement is offered to rebut an express or implied charge of recent fabrication or improper influence or motive; and (3) the statement predates the alleged motive. Under *Tome v. United States*, the predating requirement is essential — a prior consistent statement can only rebut a fabrication charge if it was made before the alleged motive to fabricate arose.

Here, all three conditions are satisfied. Paul testifies and is subject to cross (condition 1). Defense counsel imputes fabrication after Paul's termination — that is a charge of recent fabrication (condition 2). The email was sent the evening of the meeting, before the termination that allegedly supplied the motive (condition 3). Because the statement qualifies as nonhearsay under FRE 801(d)(1)(B), it is admitted not only to rehabilitate Paul's credibility but also as substantive evidence — that is, for the truth that Naomi made the comment.

**Gold Key:** FRE 801(d)(1)(B) calls the statement "not hearsay" — not merely admissible for rehabilitation. When all three conditions are met, the statement comes in for BOTH purposes: to rebut the fabrication charge AND as proof of the matter asserted.

**Silver Key:** On any prior-consistent-statement question, lock three facts before choosing: (1) Is the declarant testifying and subject to cross? (2) Is there a charge of recent fabrication or improper motive? (3) Does the prior statement PREDATE the motive? All three yes → FRE 801(d)(1)(B) → admit for rehabilitation AND as substantive evidence. Never stop at "admit for credibility only."

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B — exclude because prior consistent statements are never admissible.**

The pull: many students learn that hearsay is generally excluded, and a prior consistent statement is a statement made out of court. A student who applies a reflexive "out-of-court statements = exclude" shortcut reaches this answer.

The breaker: FRE 801(d)(1)(B) expressly exempts qualifying prior consistent statements from the hearsay rule by designating them "not hearsay." "Never" is the tiered_absolute error — the rule itself carves out an exception. **Gold Key:** FRE 801(d)(1)(B) directly refutes "never" — SOME prior consistent statements are admissible, and this one qualifies.

**C — admit only if the defense first presents reputation evidence that Paul is untruthful.**

The pull: reputation for untruthfulness is a recognized form of credibility attack (FRE 608(a)), and students who know it exists may think the rehabilitative use of a prior consistent statement depends on the same foundation. The link between impeachment methods can feel logical.

The breaker: FRE 801(d)(1)(B) is triggered by a charge of recent fabrication or improper motive — not by reputation impeachment. These are distinct impeachment tracks. A prior consistent statement's admissibility does not hinge on the opponent having used reputation evidence; it hinges on the opponent having implied the witness fabricated after a motive arose. **Gold Key:** The trigger for FRE 801(d)(1)(B) is the fabrication/motive charge, not the reputation impeachment. No reputation-evidence prerequisite exists.

**D — admit only to show Paul told the same story before trial, not for its truth.** ← *Dominant trap*

The pull: this answer gets the admission decision right and even sounds evidentiary-rule-fluent. Students who know that hearsay is generally limited to non-substantive use, and that prior consistent statements are sometimes called "rehabilitation evidence," will gravitate here — they know the statement comes in but limit it to the "credibility" use they have learned.

The breaker: once FRE 801(d)(1)(B) applies, the statement is classified as **not hearsay**. That classification is the whole point — it means the statement is admitted for the truth of the matter asserted, not merely to show consistency. Limiting it to "showing Paul told the same story" is the pre-FRE 801(d)(1)(B) understanding that the rule was specifically designed to supersede. **Gold Key fires:** FRE 801(d)(1)(B) says the statement is "not hearsay" — so the use is not limited to non-substantive rehabilitation. **Silver Key:** When you see a prior consistent statement that predates the motive, resist the partial-admission answer. "Admit for credibility only" leaves the most important part of the rule on the table.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Tested rule:** FRE 801(d)(1)(B)(i): A statement is not hearsay if the declarant testifies and is subject to cross-examination about it, and the statement is consistent with the declarant's testimony and is offered to rebut an express or implied charge that the declarant recently fabricated it or acted from a recent improper influence or motive.

**Timing requirement:** *Tome v. United States*, 513 U.S. 150 (1995): The prior consistent statement must have been made before the alleged motive to fabricate arose. The Supreme Court held this timing requirement is embedded in FRE 801(d)(1)(B) — a statement made after the motive arose cannot rebut a fabrication charge premised on that motive.

**Application:** Paul testifies at trial; he is subject to cross (condition 1). Defense counsel implies Paul invented the account after his termination — an implied charge of recent improper motive (condition 2). The email was sent the evening of the meeting, before Paul was terminated, so it predates the alleged motive (condition 3). All conditions satisfied. The email is not hearsay under FRE 801(d)(1)(B) and is admitted both to rehabilitate Paul and as substantive evidence that Naomi made the comment.

**2014 amendment note:** FRE 801(d)(1)(B) was amended in December 2014 to add clause (ii), permitting prior consistent statements to rehabilitate when a witness's credibility is attacked on another ground. Clause (ii) does not require predating the motive and does not make the statement substantive. The tested scenario falls under clause (i) — the original rule requiring predating — not the 2014 addition.

#### crossover_intersection_map

**Evidence × Evidence (FRE 801(d)(1)(B) vs. FRE 608(a)):**
Both involve credibility. FRE 608(a) uses opinion/reputation evidence to attack or rehabilitate; FRE 801(d)(1)(B) uses a prior consistent statement to rebut a fabrication charge. The triggers and legal effects are entirely different — mixing the two is the choice C trap.

**Evidence × Evidence (Prior Consistent vs. Prior Inconsistent Statements):**

The impeachment implying a motive (D's cross-exam tactic) opens the door to the FRE 801(d)(1)(B) response (A's email). The sequence matters: the prior statement's admissibility depends on the fabrication charge being made first.
