## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The clerk offered to falsify an official record in exchange for $400. Under the statute, a person who offers to commit bribery is guilty of bribery in the second degree. The offer itself is the completed offense — no payment is required.

The clerk cannot be convicted of conspiracy to commit bribery because of **Wharton's Rule**. Wharton's Rule provides that when a crime inherently requires the agreement of exactly two people — one to give the bribe and one to receive it — those two people cannot be charged with conspiracy to commit that crime if only the minimum necessary participants are involved. The rationale is that the agreement is already an inherent element of the substantive offense, and the legislature is presumed not to have intended a separate conspiracy punishment unless it clearly said so. Bribery is a classic Wharton's Rule offense. Here, only the clerk and the contractor were involved — the minimum two persons required for bribery — so conspiracy is barred.

> **Gold Key:** Wharton's Rule bars conspiracy when (1) the crime by definition requires the concerted action of at least two persons, (2) the agreement involves only the minimum number of participants necessary to commit the crime, and (3) the legislature has not clearly expressed intent to punish conspiracy separately. Classic Wharton's Rule crimes: bribery, bigamy, adultery, dueling, gambling, buying/selling contraband.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original B): Conspiracy to commit bribery only.** This choice is attractive because the clerk and the contractor clearly reached an agreement, and students may think "there was an agreement, so there's conspiracy." But this choice makes two errors. First, it ignores that the clerk committed bribery in the second degree the moment she made the offer — the offer itself is the completed statutory offense, so "conspiracy only" discards a real substantive crime. Second, it gets the conspiracy analysis backwards: Wharton's Rule *bars* conspiracy here rather than supporting it. The agreement between the clerk and the contractor is exactly the kind of two-person concerted action that Wharton's Rule addresses, and with only the minimum participants, conspiracy cannot be charged.

**B (original D): Bribery in the second degree and conspiracy to commit bribery.** This is the dominant trap. A student who correctly identifies the bribery in the second degree charge will be drawn to this answer because it seems "more complete" — it includes both the substantive offense and the inchoate offense. The pull is especially strong because there really was an agreement between two people. But Wharton's Rule specifically prohibits the conspiracy charge when the underlying crime (bribery) requires exactly two participants and only two are involved. The agreement to commit bribery is already baked into the bribery offense itself. > **Gold Key:** Wharton's Rule — bribery is a two-person crime by definition, so when only the briber and the bribee are involved, conspiracy is barred.

**D (original C): Bribery in the second degree or conspiracy to commit bribery, but not both.** This choice presents a false either/or. It correctly recognizes that conspiracy might not be available, but it frames the relationship as a choice between the two crimes. In reality, the clerk is guilty of bribery in the second degree (the offer is the completed offense) and is *not* guilty of conspiracy (because Wharton's Rule bars it). The "but not both" framing is misleading — it's not that the clerk could be convicted of one or the other; it's that one crime is clearly established and the other is legally barred. Additionally, this choice is structurally wrong because conspiracy does not merge with the substantive offense — they are separate crimes, and the issue here is whether conspiracy can be charged at all, not whether it merges.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Wharton's Rule** is a common-law doctrine providing that an agreement by two persons to commit a particular crime cannot be prosecuted as a conspiracy when the crime is of such a nature as to necessarily require the participation of two persons for its commission. *See* 16 Am. Jur. 2d *Conspiracy* § 63; *Iannelli v. United States*, 420 U.S. 770, 785 (1975) (describing Wharton's Rule as a judicial presumption that applies "in the absence of legislative intent to the contrary"). The rule is also called the "concert-of-action rule." *See* Merriam-Webster Legal, "Wharton's Rule"; USLegal, *Wharton's Rule Law and Legal Definition*.

The rule applies to crimes that inherently require a plurality of participants: bribery, bigamy, adultery, dueling, gambling, and buying/selling contraband. *Iannelli*, 420 U.S. at 785. The rationale is that the agreement is an inherent element of the substantive offense, and the legislature is presumed not to have intended to punish the agreement twice — once as the crime and again as conspiracy — unless legislative intent clearly provides otherwise. *Id.*

**Bribery** is a classic Wharton's Rule offense because it necessarily involves two parties: the giver and the receiver. When only those two are involved, conspiracy to commit bribery is barred at common law. *See* definitions.uslegal.com/w/whartons-rule/; definitions.lsd.law/whartons-rule.

**Bribery in the second degree** (offer to commit bribery): The statute here criminalizes the *offer* to commit bribery as a completed offense. The clerk's offer to falsify records in exchange for payment satisfies this — no payment or performance is required.

## Distilled Core Question

A public official and a private citizen agree to a bribe. Only the offer is completed. Under common law, can the official be convicted of both bribery and conspiracy to commit bribery?

## Call and Prediction

**Call type:** Conviction scope — which crimes can the clerk properly be convicted of. This is a standard "which crimes" call. No negative stem, no EXCEPT trap, no adjacent-call inversion.

**Prediction:** The clerk committed bribery in the second degree (the offer is the completed offense). Conspiracy is barred by Wharton's Rule because bribery requires two participants and only two are involved. Answer: **C**.

## Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

### Choice A: "Conspiracy to commit bribery only."

**1. Student-accessible C3 signal:** The clerk made an offer to falsify an official record for money. The statute says the *offer* to commit bribery is itself a crime (bribery in the second degree). This choice says "conspiracy only" — it completely ignores the completed substantive offense. If the clerk really did commit a real crime (bribery in the second degree), a choice that says she can only be convicted of something else (conspiracy) doesn't answer the question.

**2. Student label:** "Conspiracy only — ignores the completed substantive offense."

**3. What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would need to account for the bribery in the second degree charge. "Conspiracy to commit bribery only" is not responsive because it omits an established crime.

**4. Lawyer confirmation:** Bribery in the second degree is completed upon the offer. The statute explicitly criminalizes the offer. A choice that says "conspiracy only" discards a real, completed offense. Additionally, conspiracy is independently barred by Wharton's Rule — but the first cut is that this choice is not responsive to the call.

### Choice B: "Bribery in the second degree and conspiracy to commit bribery." ⚠️ DOMINANT TRAP

**1. Student-accessible C3 signal:** This looks right because the clerk clearly committed bribery in the second degree, and there was an agreement between two people. But ask: does the law allow *both* charges? Bribery is a crime that needs two people by definition — one to give, one to receive. When only those two are involved, there's a special rule (Wharton's Rule) that says you can't stack conspiracy on top of the substantive crime. The agreement is already baked into the bribery offense.

**2. Student label:** "Both crimes — the dominant trap."

**3. What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would correctly state that the clerk is guilty of bribery in the second degree but NOT conspiracy to commit bribery (because Wharton's Rule bars it).

**4. Lawyer confirmation:** Wharton's Rule bars conspiracy when the underlying crime inherently requires two participants and only those two are involved. *Iannelli v. United States*, 420 U.S. 770, 785 (1975). Bribery is a classic Wharton's Rule offense.

> **Gold Key:** Wharton's Rule bars conspiracy when (1) the crime by definition requires the concerted action of at least two persons, (2) the agreement involves only the minimum number of participants necessary, and (3) the legislature has not clearly expressed intent to punish conspiracy separately. Classic Wharton's Rule crimes: bribery, bigamy, adultery, dueling, gambling, buying/selling contraband.

### Choice C: "Bribery in the second degree only." ✅ CORRECT

**1. Student-accessible C3 signal:** The clerk offered to falsify an official record for $400. The statute says the offer to commit bribery is bribery in the second degree. That's one clear crime. What about conspiracy? Bribery needs two people (giver + receiver), and only two people were involved. Wharton's Rule says no conspiracy when the crime itself requires the same number of participants. So the clerk is guilty of bribery in the second degree only.

**2. Student label:** "Bribery in the second degree only — correct."

**3. What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true and responsive version. It correctly identifies the established crime (bribery in the second degree) and correctly excludes the barred crime (conspiracy).

**4. Lawyer confirmation:** Correct. The statute criminalizes the offer to commit bribery. Wharton's Rule bars conspiracy because bribery requires two participants and only two are involved.

### Choice D: "Bribery in the second degree or conspiracy to commit bribery, but not both."

**1. Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice frames the issue as if the clerk could be convicted of one crime or the other, but not both. That's the wrong framing. It's not a choice between two alternatives — bribery in the second degree IS established, and conspiracy is legally barred. The "but not both" language misstates the relationship between the two offenses.

**2. Student label:** "False either/or — misstates the legal relationship."

**3. What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would state that the clerk is guilty of bribery in the second degree and is NOT guilty of conspiracy (because Wharton's Rule bars it). The relationship is not mutual exclusion — it's substantive crime plus legal bar.

**4. Lawyer confirmation:** The "but not both" construction falsely implies the two crimes are alternatives. In reality, one is established and the other is barred by doctrine. Additionally, conspiracy does not merge with the substantive offense — they are separate crimes, and the issue is whether conspiracy can be charged at all, not whether it merges.
