## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The statute is a bill of Attainder. Article I, Section 9, Clause 3 prohibits Congress from passing bills of attainder — legislative acts that single out specific persons (here, Barnabas, Paul, and Daniel, named in the statute) and impose punishment on them without a judicial trial. Deprivation of the right to hold federal employment constitutes punishment within the meaning of the Bill of Attainder Clause. *See* United States v. Lovett, 328 U.S. 303 (1946) (statute barring named federal employees from government pay held unconstitutional bill of attainder). The three were identified by name in a committee report and then singled out by Congress for a permanent bar on federal employment — no trial, no adjudication, no opportunity to contest the findings. That is precisely the evil the Bill of Attainder Clause was designed to prevent: the legislature acting as judge and jury.

**Gold Key:** A bill of Attainder requires (1) specificity (the law targets named or easily identifiable individuals) and (2) punishment (the imposition of a burden that historically or functionally constitutes punishment, such as deprivation of employment, political rights, or property). *See* Nixon v. Adm'r of Gen. Servs., 433 U.S. 425, 468–73 (1977).

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original B) is incorrect — DOMINANT TRAP.** This choice is attractive because the deprivation of government employment naturally triggers due-process instincts: the students think "they lost their jobs, they deserve notice and a hearing." But procedural due process protections — the right to notice and a hearing before deprivation of a liberty or property interest — constrain executive and judicial action, not legislative acts. Congress acting by statute is not required to afford individualized due process. The statute here is a legislative act, so the Due Process Clause is not the proper vehicle for challenging it. **Gold Key:** The Bill of Attainder Clause, not the Due Process Clause, is the constitutional provision that constrains Congress when it singles out individuals for punishment by legislation.

**B (original D) is incorrect.** The Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment prohibits the government from taking private property for public use without just compensation. The statute here does not take anyone's physical property or real property. The right to hold federal employment is not a property interest that triggers the Takings Clause. Students who select this choice are misframing the injury — they sense something was "taken" but apply the wrong constitutional provision.

**D (original C) is incorrect.** The Ex Post Facto Clause prohibits retroactive criminal legislation — laws that criminalize past conduct or increase the punishment for a crime after it was committed. The statute here does not impose a criminal penalty; it bars the three individuals from federal employment, which is a civil disability, not a criminal punishment. Moreover, the Ex Post Facto Clause applies only to criminal or penal measures, not to civil regulatory or employment consequences. Students who pick this choice see "past conduct" and "punishment" and conflate the ex post facto prohibition with the bill of attainder prohibition.

## 9. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 9, cl. 3 ("No Bill of Attainder or ex post facto Law shall be passed.").

**Definition:** A bill of attainder is a legislative act that inflicts punishment on a specific, identifiable person or group without a judicial trial. *United States v. Lovett*, 328 U.S. 303, 315–16 (1946); *Nixon v. Adm'r of Gen. Servs.*, 433 U.S. 425, 468 (1977).

**Two-part test (Nixon):** A statute is a bill of attainder if it (1) applies with specificity to identifiable individuals or a defined group, and (2) imposes punishment without a judicial trial. 433 U.S. at 468–73.

**Punishment:** The Court has interpreted "punishment" broadly — it includes not only death or imprisonment but also the deprivation of political rights, employment, or other burdens imposed as a penalty. *Lovett*, 328 U.S. at 315–16 (barring named employees from federal salary constituted punishment); *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 441–42 (1965) (statute making it a crime for Communist Party members to serve as union officers was a bill of attainder).

**Application to this fact pattern:** The statute (1) names Barnabas, Paul, and Daniel specifically, satisfying the specificity requirement, and (2) bars them all from any federal employment — a permanent deprivation of the right to work for the government, which constitutes punishment. No trial was held. This is a textbook bill of attainder under *Lovett*.

**Why the other clauses fail:**
- **Due Process (B/original B):** Procedural due process applies to executive/judicial deprivations, not to legislative acts. The statute is legislation, not an executive deprivation. *See* *Lovett*, 328 U.S. at 313–14.
- **Ex Post Facto (C/original C):** Applies only to retroactive criminal laws. The statute imposes a civil disability (employment bar), not a criminal penalty. U.S. Const. art. I, § 9, cl. 3; *Calder v. Bull*, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 386, 390 (1798).
- **Takings (D/original D):** Requires a taking of private property for public use. The right to federal employment is not "property" within the meaning of the Takings Clause. *See* *United States v. Lovett*, 328 U.S. at 315–16.

**Verification:** The tested rule (Bill of Attainder Clause), the legislative (not executive) posture, the specificity of naming individuals, the punishment of employment deprivation, and the credited outcome are all unchanged from the original. Research confirms the official key. **PASS.**
