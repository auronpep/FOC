## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

A state imposes a tax on the "income" of each resident. The statute defines "income" to include the fair rental value of the personal use of any digital media kit supplied by the taxpayer's employer. The federal government supplies rugged tablets, portable projectors, and hotspot cases to some employees who live in the state so they can run emergency-preparedness briefings at remote federal sites. Ruth, one of those employees, receives a kit for that purpose and may also use it for her own personal activities, including preparing slides for a private Bible-study group. Assume there is no federal legislation on this subject.

May the state collect this tax on the fair rental value of the personal use of the kits furnished by the federal government to these employees?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

Can a state tax residents on the fair rental value of a federal employee's personal use of employer-provided equipment when the tax is imposed on employees and does not discriminate against federal employees?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. No, because the equipment is primarily used by the employees in carrying out federal duties, so the tax is a tax on activities performed on behalf of the United States.

B. Yes, because the tax is imposed on the employees rather than on the United States, and the tax does not discriminate against persons employed by the United States.

C. Yes, because exempting federal employees from this tax would deny other residents the equal protection of the laws.

D. No, because taxing the personal-use value of equipment furnished by the federal government is a tax on the United States.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B** is correct. The statute taxes the employee's income benefit from personal use of the kit. It does not place the legal incidence of the tax on the United States, and it does not single out federal employees for worse treatment. **Gold Key:** intergovernmental tax immunity blocks a state tax when the legal incidence falls on the United States or the tax discriminates against the federal government or its workers, not merely because a federal employee receives a personal benefit from federal property. **Silver Key:** separate the employer-owned asset from the tax target; the target here is the employee's personal-use value, not the federal briefing work.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. No, because the equipment is primarily used by the employees in carrying out federal duties, so the tax is a tax on activities performed on behalf of the United States.**

This is the strongest trap because the kit really is issued for federal work, and "primarily used" sounds like it should control the immunity question. The breaker is the tax target: the statute reaches only the personal-use value treated as employee income, so **Silver Key:** separate official-use facts from the personal-use benefit being taxed.

**C. Yes, because exempting federal employees from this tax would deny other residents the equal protection of the laws.**

This is attractive because it reaches the same yes/no result while sounding constitutionally serious. The breaker is the reason: equal protection is not what authorizes the tax, and a properly justified exemption would not automatically deny equal protection to other residents.

**D. No, because taxing the personal-use value of equipment furnished by the federal government is a tax on the United States.**

This is attractive because the property came from the federal government. The breaker is the **Gold Key:** a tax on the employee's personal-use benefit is not treated as a direct tax on the United States when the legal incidence falls on the employee and the tax is nondiscriminatory.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule remains intergovernmental tax immunity. A state may not impose a tax whose legal incidence falls directly on the United States, its property, or its operations, and it may not discriminate against the United States or federal employees because of the federal source of the employment or benefit. But a nondiscriminatory tax imposed on federal employees for their personal benefit from federal property is permissible when the legal incidence is on the employees rather than the United States.

Lawyer-confirmation authority: *United States v. County of Fresno*, 429 U.S. 452 (1977), upheld a nondiscriminatory tax imposed on federal employees for their beneficial use of federally supplied housing because the legal incidence fell on the employees rather than the United States. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/429/452/. *Davis v. Michigan Department of Treasury*, 489 U.S. 803 (1989), confirms that intergovernmental tax immunity forbids discriminatory taxation of federal employees. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/489/803/. The same nondiscrimination principle appears in 4 U.S.C. section 111 for state taxation of federal employee pay or compensation. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/4/111.

The transformed question keeps the legal issue and credited outcome unchanged. The state may collect the tax because the tax is imposed on the employees, reaches only personal-use value, and does not discriminate against federal employment.
