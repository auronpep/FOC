*The breaker:* The castle doctrine allows deadly force only when the occupant is present and facing an imminent threat of death or serious bodily harm — not to protect property in an unoccupied space. More fundamentally, the law draws a sharp line: human life > property rights. Deadly force for property alone is never justified, whether the owner is present or not. The spring-gun rule exists precisely to prevent owners from doing in their absence what they couldn't do in person. This choice states a rule that is far broader than the actual law.

Second, the categorical rule against deadly force for property protection applies regardless of what crime the victim was committing. A burglar does not forfeit all legal protection against deadly force merely by trespassing, absent a threat to persons.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** Deadly force may not be used solely to protect property. A mechanical spring gun or booby trap is treated as though the property owner personally used the force. If the owner could not lawfully shoot the intruder themself (because there was no threat of death or serious bodily harm to any person), the owner cannot set a device to do so in their absence. *Katko v. Briney*, 183 N.W.2d 657 (Iowa 1971) (civil case establishing the spring-gun rule; seminal authority applied in criminal contexts). See also Wayne R. LaFave, *Criminal Law* § 10.4(c) (6th ed. 2021) ("deadly force in defense of property is not justified unless the person reasonably believes the intrusion threatens death or serious bodily harm to the occupant"). Model Penal Code § 3.06(3)(d) (deadly force to protect property justified only when the person believes the intruder is attempting to dispossess them of their dwelling and the person believes deadly force is necessary to prevent the intrusion — a narrow exception that does not apply to unoccupied commercial workshops).

**Authority:** *Katko v. Briney* is the leading authority for the spring-gun rule, cited in both tort and criminal law contexts. The rule is recognized in the Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 85–86 and incorporated into criminal law as a limitation on the defense-of-property justification. No state permits deadly force solely to protect unoccupied property.

**choice_by_choice_walkthrough (4-part blocks):**

**Choice A — "Lydia is criminally liable only if she knew a human being would trigger the device."**

(1) Student-accessible C3 signal: The choice says "only if" — that's adding a condition. When a choice adds a condition the rule doesn't require, the choice is overclaiming or fabricating. The spring-gun rule is categorical — it doesn't turn on whether the owner knew the specific victim.

(2) Student label: "Fabricated mens rea requirement — the rule applies regardless of knowledge of the specific victim"

(3) What a true/responsive version would look like: "Lydia is criminally liable because using a deadly mechanical device to protect property alone is never justified, regardless of Lydia's specific knowledge about who would trigger it."

(4) Lawyer confirmation: Intent to set a deadly booby trap is sufficient criminal intent. The law does not require knowledge of the specific victim. The categorical rule against deadly force for property alone is the dispositive issue, not Lydia's subjective mental state about who might open the door.

**Choice B — "Lydia is criminally liable because using a deadly mechanical device to protect property alone is never justified." ← CREDITED ANSWER**

(1) Student-accessible C3 signal: This is a clean, categorical rule statement. It identifies the precise legal barrier without adding conditions or importing foreign doctrine. The spring-gun rule is narrow and specific — it fits the facts exactly.

(2) Student label: "Correct — categorical prohibition"

(3) What a true/responsive version would look like: This IS the true/responsive version.

(4) Lawyer confirmation: Katko v. Briney, 183 N.W.2d 657 (Iowa 1971) (spring-loaded shotgun in unoccupied farmhouse = owner personally using force; deadly force for property alone not permitted). LaFave, Criminal Law § 10.4(c) ("deadly force in defense of property is not justified"). MPC § 3.06(3)(d) (extremely narrow deadly-force exception for dispossession of a dwelling, not applicable here). The rule is categorical and Lydia's conduct falls squarely within it.

**Choice C — "Lydia is not liable because property owners may use any means necessary to protect their workspace from thieves." ← DOMINANT TRAP**

(1) Student-accessible C3 signal: "Any means necessary" — absolute language. The law almost never gives unlimited force. This sounds like a castle-doctrine overextension. The spring-gun rule is the specific limitation.

(2) Student label: "Dominant trap — castle doctrine overextension"

(3) What a true/responsive version would look like: "Lydia is liable because the castle doctrine allows deadly force only when the occupant is present and facing imminent threat, and her workshop was unoccupied with no threat to any person."

(4) Lawyer confirmation: The castle doctrine permits deadly force only when the occupant is in the dwelling and reasonably believes an intruder will cause death or serious bodily harm. It does not extend to unoccupied properties, does not authorize booby traps, and does not grant "any means necessary" to protect property. This choice states a rule far broader than any jurisdiction's actual law.

**Choice D — "Lydia is not liable because Hannah was committing a burglary and assumed the risk."**

(1) Student-accessible C3 signal: "Assumption of risk" — that's a Torts term. This is a Criminal Law question. Two different subjects. The phrase alone tells you this choice belongs to a different part of the exam.

(2) Student label: "Tort concept in a criminal case — wrong subject"

(3) What a true/responsive version would look like: Criminal law has no assumption-of-risk defense. A burglar's crime does not justify the use of deadly force to protect property when no person is threatened.

(4) Lawyer confirmation: Assumption of risk is a tort defense, not a criminal defense. Even in tort, it applies to negligence claims, not intentional torts like battery. In criminal law, the victim's criminal conduct does not create a justification for deadly force that the law otherwise denies.
