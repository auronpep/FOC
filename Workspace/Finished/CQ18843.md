### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Lydia is **not** guilty of common-law larceny. Larceny is the **trespassory taking and carrying away of the personal property of another, with intent to permanently deprive** the owner. The intent-to-permanently-deprive element is the linchpin on these facts. The stem supplies clear temporary-use and intent-to-return facts: she took the pressure washer for a single defined task (cleaning the side patio for a Saturday family gathering), she planned to return it the next day, and she had no use for the property beyond her own home. A taking for a short, safe, temporary use with an actual return plan is the textbook non-larceny "borrowing" pattern. The trespassory element may be present (she took it without asking), but larceny requires **all** elements including the specific intent to permanently deprive, and that element is missing. **Gold Key:** unauthorized borrowing is not larceny unless the borrower's intended use creates a substantial risk of permanent loss or otherwise shows intent to permanently deprive. **Silver Key:** on a larceny question, look for the intent-to-permanently-deprive anchor before locking any "Yes" answer.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18843, a Criminal Law item on common-law larceny. The official key is C: no larceny where the defendant took the property for a short, safe temporary use and return, because the **intent to permanently deprive** element is missing.

Common-law larceny has four elements: (1) a **trespassory** taking, (2) **carrying away** (asportation) of (3) **personal property of another**, (4) with the **intent to permanently deprive** the owner. The fourth element — the specific-intent-to-permanently-deprive element — is the doctrinal hinge on these facts.

The long-standing common-law rule, traced through the classic treatises and the Restatement, is that a taking for short, safe, temporary use and return is **not** larceny. See, e.g., Rollin M. Perkins & Ronald N. Boyce, *Criminal Law* 305-06 (3d ed. 1982) (a "mere borrowing" unaccompanied by intent to steal does not constitute larceny; the borrowing becomes larceny only where the borrower's intended use creates a substantial risk of permanent loss). The Model Penal Code § 223.1 codifies a similar posture: a person is guilty of theft if he "unlawfully takes, or exercises unlawful control over, movable property of another with purpose to deprive him thereof" — and the purpose-to-deprive element, defined in § 223.0(1), reaches the borrowing case only when the borrowing is so extended or risky that the deprivation is "permanent or for an indefinite period" or creates a "substantial risk" of permanent loss. The MBE-style black-letter formulation tracks the common-law rule.
