Paul, who runs a small shop making hand-carved Scripture stands in Oregon, wrote a signed note to Barnabas, a supplier of portable reading lamps and Bible-journaling kits in Savannah. The note said:

## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is the NOT accurate statement.**

**Silver Key:** On a NOT-accurate call, flip the normal task. Three choices may be accurate legal statements. The answer is the one inaccurate statement.

**Gold Key:** Performance can accept a unilateral offer, but notice can still matter when the offeror otherwise lacks an adequate way to learn that the performance occurred. Barnabas did give Paul prompt notice. So the promise is not enforceable “whether or not” notice was given. That phrase makes B too broad and makes it the target of the negative call.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

### A

The trap sells the strongest concrete event in the fact pattern: Barnabas actually gave Lydia the goods on credit. That is exactly the performance Paul requested, so it is an accurate unilateral-contract statement, not the NOT accurate statement.

**Silver Key:** The negative stem makes this accurate statement a wrong answer.

### C

The trap sells formality. A signed writing matters because Paul is promising to answer for Lydia’s debt if Lydia defaults. That makes the statement accurate, so it cannot be the answer to a NOT accurate call.

### D

The trap sells the idea that Paul personally received nothing from Lydia. But Barnabas supplied the requested exchange by extending credit to Lydia, so the statement is accurate and not the negative-stem target.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule and credited outcome remain unchanged from the source row: the official row credits original D because the guarantor’s offer invited acceptance by performance, but the circumstances required notice of that acceptance. 

Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 54 states the governing acceptance-by-performance rule. Notice is not normally necessary for acceptance by performance unless requested, but if the offeree has reason to know the offeror lacks an adequate means of learning of the performance with reasonable promptness and certainty, the offeror’s duty is discharged unless the offeree gives reasonable notice, the offeror learns within a reasonable time, or the offer says notice is unnecessary. ([Matthew Miner][1])

The supporting signed-writing statement is also legally grounded. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 110 includes “a contract to answer for the duty of another” within the suretyship branch of the Statute of Frauds, requiring a writing absent an applicable exception. ([LexisNexis][2]) Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 112 describes when a promise qualifies as a suretyship promise: the promisee is the obligee of another’s duty, the promisor is surety for the other, and the promisee knows or has reason to know of the suretyship relation. ([Matthew Miner][3])

The consideration statement is legally grounded too. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 71 provides that consideration may be a bargained-for performance and that the performance may be given to the promisor or to another person. ([Open Casebook][4]) Here, Barnabas’s credit extension to Lydia is the requested performance supporting Paul’s promise.
