### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Lydia's state citizenship is **Texas**. **Gold Key:** a natural person's state citizenship for diversity purposes is the state of **domicile** — the state of physical presence coupled with intent to remain indefinitely. Lydia is physically present in Massachusetts for a fixed nine-month teaching appointment, but she has not formed the intent to remain in Massachusetts indefinitely. She keeps her permanent home, voter registration, and driver's license in Texas and plans to return to Texas when the appointment ends. Her domicile is therefore Texas, and her state citizenship for diversity purposes is Texas. **Silver Key:** when the call is "for diversity purposes, what is the person's citizenship," translate the question to "where is the person's domicile" and test the two-element definition (physical presence + intent to remain indefinitely) before picking.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. Massachusetts, because she currently resides there.**

This is the **dominant trap**. It is the most direct and most common misconception: students confuse residence with domicile. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: domicile requires both physical presence AND intent to remain indefinitely. Lydia is physically present in Massachusetts, but her intent is to return to Texas at the end of the nine-month appointment. The two-element test fails on the second element. Residence alone does not establish domicile. Test the two-element definition before picking "where she currently lives."

**C. Both Texas and Massachusetts, because individuals have citizenship wherever they live during a year.**

This is the dual-citizenship trap. The student half-remembers that diversity has residency concepts and assumes an individual can hold two state citizenships at once. The breaker is that an individual has **one domicile at a time** for diversity purposes. Lydia's domicile remains Texas. The Massachusetts apartment is a residence, not a competing domicile. "Both" is structurally impossible on these facts because the two-element test resolves to a single state.

**D. No state, because she is away from her permanent home.**

This is the edge-case trap. The student reads "away from her permanent home" and concludes there is no state. The breaker is that domicile is not lost by mere physical absence. Lydia's domicile in Texas persists during her Massachusetts stay because she has not formed the intent to abandon Texas and remain in Massachusetts indefinitely. Domicile is sticky: a person keeps a domicile until acquiring a new one. Lydia has not acquired a Massachusetts domicile, so her Texas domicile is intact.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17412, a Civil Procedure item involving a person (Maya) who grew up in Nevada, keeps her permanent ties there, accepts a nine-month medical fellowship in Oregon, and plans to return to Nevada. The official key is C: Nevada, because she remains domiciled there despite her temporary residence in Oregon.

The diversity jurisdiction statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a)(1), gives federal district courts original jurisdiction over civil actions between citizens of different States. The Supreme Court has long held that a natural person's state "citizenship" for diversity purposes is the state of domicile, not the state of mere residence. The leading articulations of the domicile rule trace to cases such as Stifel v. Hopkins, 477 U.S. 407 (1986), and the domicile formula is summarized in treatises and in the federal courts' own rules.

Domicile has two elements: (1) physical presence in the state, and (2) intent to remain in the state indefinitely. Both elements must be present to acquire a new domicile; neither alone is sufficient. A person keeps her existing domicile until she acquires a new one by satisfying both elements in a different state. The intent element is measured subjectively (where does the person intend to remain), and the Supreme Court has long observed that "domicile" is a "mixed question of law and fact" with the intent element judged by objective indicators such as the location of the permanent home, voter registration, driver's license, tax filings, and stated plans.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: Lydia's domicile is Texas. Her nine-month Massachusetts teaching appointment is a fixed-term purpose with the intent to return to Texas. Her Texas domicile persists because she has not acquired a Massachusetts domicile (no intent to remain indefinitely in Massachusetts). The federal court therefore has diversity jurisdiction over her suit against the Massachusetts defendant.
