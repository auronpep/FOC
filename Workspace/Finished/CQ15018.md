## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

The Jubilee Cottages Association governs a small residential community where each cottage lot is individually owned. After several owners began placing bright blue ceramic birdbaths in their front prayer gardens, the association’s board adopted a new rule stating that, going forward, owners and occupants may place only plain white stone benches in front gardens. The board said the rule would make the cottages look “more reverent and orderly” from the street. After the rule was adopted, Stephen bought several blue ceramic birdbaths to place in his front garden. The recorded community documents are silent about the board’s authority to regulate decorative objects, garden structures, or landscaping placed on individually owned lots.

Is the new rule enforceable against Stephen?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A private community association adopts an unwritten board rule controlling decorative landscaping on individually owned lots for aesthetics only, but the governing documents give no authority for that kind of rule. Is the rule enforceable?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. Yes, because the rule is not illegal, unconstitutional, or against public policy.

B. No, because the rule is unreasonable, and the association’s governing documents do not authorize its enforcement.

C. No, because any rule not written in the recorded community documents is unenforceable.

D. Yes, because the rule is reasonably related to furthering a legitimate purpose of the association.

### 4. Correct answer

**B**. Original key: **D**.

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** The rule targets objects and landscaping on individually owned lots. The documents are silent on the board’s authority to regulate that area, and the stated reason is only aesthetic uniformity. **Gold Key:** A common-interest board rule regulating individually owned property needs a valid authority/reasonableness path; “looks better” alone is not enough. **Silver Key:** Do not pick the answer that uses only the association’s lowest possible validity floor; the winning answer accounts for both missing authority and unreasonable aesthetic-only enforcement.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells the idea that any non-illegal private association rule should stand. The breaker is that legality/public-policy is only a floor; the enforceability question still needs the reasonableness/authority path.

**C.** This choice sells a clean “not in documents = not enforceable” shortcut. The breaker is the absolute word “any”; some association rules can be adopted outside the documents when properly authorized and reasonable, so this answer says too much.

**D.** This was the dominant trap. It borrows the attractive deferential phrase “reasonably related to a legitimate purpose,” which sounds like the board should win. The breaker is that the stem’s stated purpose is only aesthetic uniformity on individually owned property, and the documents are silent on board authority. **Gold Key:** the board needs a valid authority/reasonableness route; the phrase “legitimate purpose” does not supply it by itself.

### 8. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule is unchanged: a common-interest-community association rule restricting individually owned property must be supported by the association’s governing authority and must satisfy a reasonableness standard; a purely aesthetic, unwritten rule with no governing-document authorization is not enforceable against the owner. The closest outline placement is **81020500**, “Equitable Servitudes / Restrictive Covenants,” a valid Real Property Rights in Land code in the supplied reference. 

Authority floor: *Nahrstedt v. Lakeside Village Condominium Assn.* confirms the reasonableness framework for common-interest-development restrictions. Nevada’s *Moretto* materials applying Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes §§ 6.7 and 6.9 confirm the specific point that an association does not have inherent power to regulate individually owned property and that rules governing use/design of individually owned lots require the appropriate authority/reasonableness path. ([Justia Law][1])
