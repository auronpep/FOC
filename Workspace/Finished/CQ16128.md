### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**B is correct.** Robbery is larceny plus force or threat. Lydia used a threat, but the robbery charge still needs the larceny core: a trespassory taking of another's personal property with intent to steal. Lydia honestly believed she was reclaiming her own specific satchel. That honest claim of right defeats the intent to steal even though her belief was unreasonable.

**Gold Key:** For larceny-based property crimes, an honest claim that the specific item is yours negates intent to steal; the belief need not be reasonable for the MBE/common-law rule. **Silver Key:** Do not let the force fact finish the question by itself. Robbery adds force to larceny, so the larceny intent still has to survive.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. Unreasonable belief as guilt.** This choice is attractive because the prosecution proves Lydia's mistake was careless and the exam emphasizes that fact near the end. The breaker is the standard. **Gold Key:** the claim-of-right rule asks whether Lydia honestly believed the specific satchel was hers, not whether that belief was reasonable.

**C. No proof Lydia could carry out the threat.** This choice tries to make robbery turn on Lydia's actual ability to carry out the threat. The breaker is that fear or intimidation can satisfy the force/fear element without proving Lydia could complete every threatened harm. The real reason Lydia is not guilty is her honest claim of right, not a defect in the threat.

**D. Threat as enough.** This choice sells the most visible bad act: Lydia threatened Ruth and got the satchel. The breaker is the missing larceny intent. **Silver Key:** force or threat answers only the robbery add-on; it does not replace the need to prove an intent to steal property Lydia knew was another's.

### 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source: robbery is larceny accomplished by force or fear, and larceny requires a trespassory taking and carrying away of the personal property of another with intent to steal. A good-faith claim of ownership to the specific property taken negates that larcenous intent. On the MBE/common-law framing, the belief can be honest even if unreasonable.

Lawyer-confirmation authority: *People v. Tufunga*, 21 Cal. 4th 935 (1999), states the common-law rule that a good-faith belief, even if mistaken, that the defendant has a right or claim to the property negates the felonious intent needed for theft or robbery, and explains that robbery was treated as aggravated larceny for this purpose. *People v. Green*, 5 N.Y.3d 538 (2005), notes that a good-faith but mistaken claim to a particular chattel might defeat robbery by negating larcenous intent, while also illustrating that some statutes limit a separate claim-of-right instruction in robbery cases. That statutory caveat does not change the MBE/common-law outcome for this item.
