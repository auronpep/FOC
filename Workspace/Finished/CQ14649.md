### 5. Correct answer

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**D is correct.** Gold Key: **If the actor’s intended completed conduct would still not be a crime, the problem is legal impossibility, not factual impossibility.** Daniel intended to burn his own warehouse. The jurisdiction’s arson definition requires burning a building “of another” without the owner’s consent. Even if the fuse had worked and Daniel’s plan had succeeded exactly as intended, he still would not have burned a building “of another” without the owner’s consent. That means the completed intended act would not be arson. So he has not committed attempted arson.

**Silver Key:** When the stem gives the crime definition, plug the intended finished act into the definition before taking the “attempt” bait.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A is wrong.** It states a true neighboring rule: factual impossibility is usually no defense to attempt. But the problem is not that Daniel tried to commit arson and failed because of a factual obstacle. The problem is that his intended completed act would not be arson at all. Gold Key: legal impossibility kills the trap.

**B is wrong.** It uses the dominant trap. A general mistake-of-law answer sounds familiar, especially because Paul gave bad legal advice. But the call is not whether Daniel can excuse a completed arson charge by saying “my lawyer told me wrong.” The call is whether his intended completed conduct would be arson. It would not.

**C is wrong.** It reaches the right “No” result for the wrong reason. Daniel did intend to burn the building. The reason he is not guilty of attempted arson is not that his mental state disappeared. The reason is that the intended completed act fails the statute’s “of another / without consent” requirement.

### 8. Black-letter verification and right-answer legal reasoning

Under the supplied official explanation, the governing distinction is legal impossibility versus factual impossibility: the owner’s intended completed act would not have satisfied the arson definition because the building was his own and he consented to the burning.  Common-law legal-impossibility doctrine is classically illustrated by *People v. Jaffe*, where an attempt conviction failed because the completed intended conduct would not have been the substantive crime. ([New York Courts][1]) A useful formulation is that legal impossibility exists when the actor’s completed intended acts still fail to fulfill all elements of the substantive offense. ([Oklahoma Court of Criminal Appeals][2])
