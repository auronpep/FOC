## 1. Final question

At a Christian weekend retreat, Barnabas drove a paid shuttle van that backed into Peter's folding tent and crushed Peter's borrowed sound equipment. Peter sued Barnabas in State A state court for damages. Barnabas defended on the ground that Peter was contributorily negligent, a complete defense under State A law. The jury returned a general verdict for Barnabas and made no special findings.

Six weeks later, Lydia, another retreat counselor injured in the same backing incident, filed a jurisdictionally proper diversity action against Barnabas in State A federal court.

Will preclusion prevent litigation of the negligence claim in Lydia's lawsuit?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A defendant won the first negligence case by general verdict with no issue findings. A different injured plaintiff later sues the same defendant over the same incident. Does claim or issue preclusion bar the later negligence claim?

## 3. Final answer choices (post-shuffle)

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**B is correct.**

Lydia was not a party to Peter's first lawsuit and the stem gives no privity between Lydia and Peter. Claim preclusion therefore cannot bar Lydia's separate negligence claim. Issue preclusion also fails. The first jury returned only a general verdict for Barnabas; that verdict could rest on Barnabas not being negligent or on Peter's contributory negligence. Because the stem gives no finding that Barnabas's negligence was actually decided and essential to the first judgment, Lydia cannot use the first case to lock that issue.

**Gold Key:** Issue preclusion needs a clear prior decision on an actually litigated and essential issue; a general verdict for the defendant may leave the defendant's negligence undecided.

**Silver Key:** Read the choices as two gates: claim preclusion asks whether the same claimant or a privy is trying to relitigate the same claim; issue preclusion asks whether the same issue was clearly decided against the party to be bound.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

### A

The attractive move is that A correctly sees why Lydia is not issue-precluded, then reaches for claim preclusion as a backup bar. That backup is not responsive to the party gate. Claim preclusion requires the same claimant or someone in privity with that claimant; Lydia was neither.

**Silver Key:** Do not let a correct issue-preclusion cut slide into a false claim-preclusion conclusion. The same-party requirement still controls claim preclusion.

### C

The attractive move is to treat Barnabas's general verdict as if it announced every issue in his favor. That is the dominant trap. A general verdict for the defendant can rest on more than one complete defense, so the jury may have found no negligence, or it may have found contributory negligence. Without a clear issue determination, Lydia is not precluded from litigating Barnabas's negligence.

**Gold Key:** A general verdict for the defendant does not necessarily decide that the defendant was not negligent when an independent complete defense was also in the case.

### D

The attractive move is to treat Barnabas as bound because he was a party and the first case involved the same incident. That overclaims the effect of a verdict in his favor. Issue preclusion requires a determination against the party to be bound, and the general verdict did not determine that Barnabas was negligent.

**Gold Key:** A winning defendant is not bound by a general verdict to prove an issue that the verdict did not decide against him.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

**Authority floor:** Restatement (Second) of Judgments §§ 24, 27; Restatement (Second) of Judgments § 22 for merger/claim-preclusion structure; Parklane Hosiery Co. v. Shore, 439 U.S. 322 (1979); Federated Department Stores v. Moitie, 452 U.S. 394 (1981). Supporting reference: Cornell LII Wex entries for issue preclusion and res judicata.

Claim preclusion bars relitigation of the same claim only for the same parties or their privies. Restatement (Second) of Judgments §§ 22, 24; Federated Department Stores v. Moitie, 452 U.S. 394, 399-400 (1981). Lydia was not Peter's party and the stem supplies no privity, so claim preclusion cannot bar Lydia's claim.

Issue preclusion requires that the same issue was actually litigated and determined, and that the determination was essential to a valid final judgment. Restatement (Second) of Judgments § 27; Cornell LII Wex, issue preclusion, https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/issue_preclusion. A general verdict for Barnabas is ambiguous because State A recognized contributory negligence as a complete defense. The verdict could mean Barnabas was not negligent, or it could mean Peter's own negligence barred recovery even if Barnabas was negligent. That ambiguity prevents the later plaintiff from treating the first judgment as a clear decision on Barnabas's negligence.

Nonmutual use of issue preclusion can be available in some circumstances, but due process and fairness limits matter. Parklane Hosiery Co. v. Shore, 439 U.S. 322, 326-31 (1979). Those limits do not rescue Lydia here because the issue was not clearly determined and Lydia was not a party or privy for claim preclusion.
