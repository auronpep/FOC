## 0. File header (YAML frontmatter above)

## 1. Final question

A retired missionary and widower named Barnabas conveyed the riverside homestead acreage by quitclaim deed to his daughter Lydia and his son Silas “as joint tenants in fee simple.” The language of the deed was sufficient to create a common law joint tenancy with right of survivorship, which is unmodified by statute. Lydia then duly executed a will devising her interest in the acreage to her close friend and prayer partner Priscilla. Silas then duly executed a will devising his interest in the acreage to his cousin Timothy. Silas died, and later Lydia died. Neither had ever married. Priscilla and Timothy survived. After both wills have been duly probated, who owns what interest in the acreage?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

D is correct. Because a joint tenancy with right of survivorship existed, on the death of Silas the interest he held was extinguished by the right of survivorship and Lydia’s interest automatically enlarged to the entire fee simple. Lydia therefore owned the whole acreage alone when she later died, and her will was effective to pass the entire fee simple to Priscilla. Timothy receives nothing.

**Silver Key (navigational move):** Apply the right of survivorship to the *first* death before reading either will or the second death. The call asks who owns what “after both wills have been duly probated,” but the survivorship operates at the moment of the first death and removes one interest from the equation before any will can speak to it.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is incorrect. Silas had no separate interest left to devise when he died. The right of survivorship had already extinguished his share and enlarged Lydia’s ownership to 100%. Priscilla therefore takes the whole fee under Lydia’s later will; Timothy takes nothing. (This choice sells the attractive but false picture that both wills “count” and the first death did not change the ownership map.)

B is incorrect. Timothy’s interest under Silas’s will never came into existence because Silas’s joint-tenancy interest terminated at his death. There were never two separate interests that could pass to Priscilla and Timothy and then be held by them as new joint tenants. Only Lydia’s will had anything to operate on after the first death.

C is incorrect. As explained above, Silas’s interest terminated on his death and he had no interest to convey to Timothy. The two devisees never held simultaneous interests that could ripen into a tenancy in common. Lydia alone held the entire fee when she died, so only her devisee takes.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested black-letter rule is unchanged: in a common-law joint tenancy with right of survivorship (expressly created by deed language sufficient at common law and unmodified by statute), the interest of a deceased joint tenant is extinguished at the moment of death and passes by operation of law to the surviving joint tenant(s). A joint tenant’s interest is not devisable or inheritable. A will executed by a joint tenant can pass only what that tenant owns at the moment of her own death.

**Lawyer-confirmation layer (authority):** The question itself states the governing rule: the deed created “a common law joint tenancy with right of survivorship, which is unmodified by statute.” Under that unmodified common-law regime, the four unities plus the right of survivorship mean that on the death of one joint tenant the survivor holds the whole by survivorship, not by inheritance or devise. See, e.g., standard treatises and MBE-tested black-letter statements (the interest of the deceased JT “drops out” and enlarges the survivor’s share to 100%). No modern statutory modification is present, so the common-law result controls exactly as the original question framed it.

The credited outcome is identical to the source row: the survivor (Lydia) owned the fee simple alone after the first death and could (and did) devise the entire parcel to her chosen beneficiary. The cousin’s will had nothing left to operate on.
