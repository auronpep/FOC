## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

A is correct. Under the common-law doctrine of equitable conversion, when a contract for the sale of real property is capable of specific performance and is silent regarding the risk of loss, equitable title passes to the buyer at the moment the contract becomes enforceable. The buyer therefore bears the risk of any casualty to the property between contract and closing, even if the buyer has neither taken possession nor obtained insurance. The leading statement of this rule is the English case *Paine v. Meller* (1801), which held that a buyer remained bound to pay the full purchase price for land after a house on the property burned down between contract and closing.

Here, the contract was unconditional, satisfied the statute of frauds, and was silent as to financing, risk of loss, and insurance. Those are the two facts that activate equitable conversion: (i) the contract is enforceable (capable of specific performance) and (ii) the parties did not allocate the risk themselves. Equitable title therefore passed to Daniel and Ruth on the date Mary signed the acceptance. The cabin burned three weeks later. Daniel and Ruth — not Mary — bear the loss, so the contract remains enforceable and Mary wins.

**Gold Key:** The Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act (UVPRA) is a *statute*, not a common-law rule. When the question says "no applicable statute," the UVPRA is OFF the table; the common-law default (equitable conversion, risk on the buyer as equitable title holder) controls.

**Silver Key:** Read the jurisdiction flag first. "No applicable statute" is the kill switch for the UVPA — if the question says "no statute," the common-law default (equitable conversion, risk on buyer) controls.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B (dominant trap, UVPA / bait_doctrine).** This distractor is the dominant trap because the rule it states is real, in the right doctrinal neighborhood, and easy to misapply. The Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act (UVPRA) does in fact place risk of loss on the party in possession in the (small minority of) states that have adopted it. But the UVPRA is a *statute*, and the stem expressly says "There is no applicable statute." That phrase is the kill switch: it takes the UVPRA off the table, and the common-law default — equitable conversion — fills the silence. A student who reaches for the UVPA here has confused the minority statutory rule for the default common-law rule. The seller had actual possession of the cabin throughout (Mary was the only party in actual possession), and Daniel and Ruth had no constructive possession at the contract date. (Silver Key: read the jurisdiction flag first — "no applicable statute" tells you immediately that the UVPRA is not the rule and that the common-law default governs.)

**C (misfit, contra proferentem).** The court did not construe the contract against Daniel and Ruth, and contra proferentem is not the rule that resolved this dispute. Contra proferentem applies when an *ambiguous* contract term is read against the drafter; here the contract was not ambiguous on risk of loss — it was *silent*. Silence on risk of loss is filled by operation of law (equitable conversion), not by an interpretive canon about who drafted the document. The choice sells the cognitive move "the buyers wrote it, so they lose," but the legal question is not who drafted the paper; it is which rule of law fills the silence.

**D (wrong_element, financing-conditional).** The lender's written commitment did not make the contract "fully binding." The contract was already binding when Mary signed the acceptance: the offer was unconditional and satisfied the statute of frauds. The mortgage commitment was a financing step for Daniel and Ruth personally (it let them close), but it was not a condition to the existence of the contract. Under equitable conversion, the buyer takes equitable title the moment the contract becomes enforceable, regardless of any subsequent financing. The choice confuses a real-world financing step with the legal moment of binding effect.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule.** When a contract for the sale of real property is silent as to risk of loss, and the jurisdiction has not adopted a statute to the contrary, the common-law doctrine of equitable conversion places the risk of loss on the buyer from the date the contract becomes enforceable, because the buyer holds equitable title from that moment.

**Governing authority.**
- *Paine v. Meller* (1801) — leading English authority establishing that a buyer remains bound to pay the full purchase price when property is destroyed between contract and closing under the doctrine of equitable conversion.
- *Brush Grocery Kart, Inc. v. Sure Fine Market, Inc.*, 47 P.3d 680 (Colo. 2002) — leading U.S. case discussing the Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act (UVPRA) and its relationship to the equitable-conversion rule.
- Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act (UVPRA) — model act adopted in a minority of states (e.g., California, Hawaii, Michigan, Nevada) that places risk of loss on the seller unless the buyer is in possession; controls only where enacted.
- New York and Massachusetts — minority common-law rules in which the seller continues to bear the risk of loss until title or possession transfers.

**Application.** Here, the contract was unconditional, satisfied the statute of frauds, and was silent as to financing, risk of loss, and insurance. Equitable title passed to Daniel and Ruth on the date Mary signed the acceptance. The cabin burned three weeks later, before closing. Because the jurisdiction has no applicable statute, the UVPRA does not apply; the Massachusetts and New York minority rules also do not apply. The common-law rule of equitable conversion therefore controls, placing the risk of loss on the buyer. Mary wins.

**Outcome unchanged.** The transform preserves the tested rule, the legal issue, and the credited outcome (Mary, the seller, wins because the buyer bears the risk under equitable conversion).

## distilled_core_question
Buyer and seller signed an enforceable, unconditional real-property sales contract that is silent on risk of loss. The property is destroyed before closing, neither party is insured, and there is no statute in the jurisdiction. Who bears the risk of loss — and why?

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A — Correct
(1) **Student-accessible C3 signal.** Enforceable unconditional contract + silence on risk of loss + no statute → common-law default (equitable conversion) applies → buyer (Daniel and Ruth) holds equitable title from acceptance → risk of loss is on the buyer.
(2) **Student label.** Correct. The common-law default fills the silence.
(3) **What a true-and-responsive version would look like.** Same as written.
(4) **Lawyer confirmation.** Equitable conversion passes equitable title to the buyer the moment the contract is enforceable. The buyer bears the risk of loss between contract and closing. *Paine v. Meller* (1801); *Brush Grocery Kart, Inc. v. Sure Fine Market, Inc.*, 47 P.3d 680 (Colo. 2002).

### B — Dominant trap
(1) **Student-accessible C3 signal.** B describes the UVPA (Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act), which is a statute. The stem says "no applicable statute" — that is the kill switch. The UVPA is OFF the table; the common-law default (equitable conversion, risk on the buyer) controls.
(2) **Student label.** DOMINANT TRAP. The rule is real, but it is a statute; there is no statute here.
(3) **What a true-and-responsive version would look like.** A UVPA-style choice would have to appear in a stem that says the jurisdiction has adopted the UVPA — not "no applicable statute." In a UVPA jurisdiction, this choice would be the credited answer.
(4) **Lawyer confirmation.** The UVPA places risk on the party in possession in the minority of states that have adopted it. In a no-statute jurisdiction, the common-law rule of equitable conversion controls. *Brush Grocery Kart v. Sure Fine Market*, 47 P.3d 680 (Colo. 2002).

### C — Misfit
(1) **Student-accessible C3 signal.** Contra proferentem resolves *ambiguity* in a written term. The contract is not ambiguous on risk of loss — it is *silent*. Silence is filled by operation of law, not by an interpretive canon about who drafted the paper.
(2) **Student label.** Misfit. The doctrine of contra proferentem does not fill silent terms.
(3) **What a true-and-responsive version would look like.** A contra proferentem choice would have to confront a contract term that is genuinely ambiguous (e.g., "good condition" or "usual fixtures") — not a contract that is silent on a material allocation.
(4) **Lawyer confirmation.** Contra proferentem is a canon of contract construction. It applies to ambiguous written terms. Silence on a material allocation is filled by operation of law.

### D — Wrong element
(1) **Student-accessible C3 signal.** The stem says the contract was "unconditional" and that "an enforceable contract resulted" at acceptance. The mortgage commitment is mentioned only as a separate later fact (two weeks after the contract); it is not a condition to the contract's existence. The contract was already binding at acceptance.
(2) **Student label.** Wrong element. The mortgage commitment is a financing step, not the moment of binding.
(3) **What a true-and-responsive version would look like.** A financing-conditional choice would have to confront a stem where the contract expressly conditions closing on the buyer obtaining financing — i.e., a contract that says "this contract is conditioned on Buyer's ability to obtain a mortgage."
(4) **Lawyer confirmation.** An unconditional offer that satisfies the statute of frauds becomes enforceable at acceptance. A subsequent mortgage commitment is a financing step for the buyer; it does not retroactively determine when the contract was binding.
