## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Naomi is the lead Sunday school teacher at a local church school. Mary runs a Christian parenting newsletter that goes out weekly to several hundred subscribers in the area, most of them parents whose children attend the church school. A parent at the church school emailed Mary alleging that Naomi had been stealing from the classroom supply fund, a charge the parent could not substantiate and that was in fact false. Mary had no independent reason to believe the charge was true but, in her next newsletter, repeated the accusation under the heading, "A parent in our school community writes: this teacher has been stealing from the classroom supply fund." Naomi sues Mary for defamation.

Is Mary liable to Naomi as a publisher of the defamatory statement?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Mary is liable to Naomi as a **publisher**. The defamation tort treats a republisher as a publisher of the defamatory content for purposes of liability, even when the republisher names the original source. The original speaker remains liable on an original-publication theory, and the republisher is liable on a republication theory; the two are independent. **Gold Key:** attribution is not a defense to republication — repeating a defamatory charge to a new audience is itself a publication, and the republisher is on the hook for the defamatory sting. **Silver Key:** lock the call to whether the editor is a publisher of the defamatory statement, not to whether the email sender is also a publisher.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. No, because attributing the accusation to "a parent in our school community" insulates the newsletter editor from liability for the underlying charge.**

This is the dominant trap and the classic attribution-as-defense misconception. The student hears "I said it was someone else" and assumes attribution is a shield. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: attribution is not a defense to republication. Repeating a defamatory charge to a new audience is itself a publication, and the republisher is on the hook for the defamatory sting even if the republisher names the source. A republisher can of course raise ordinary defenses (truth, privilege, opinion, etc.), but attribution is not one of them.

**B. Yes, but only if the parent who wrote the original email is also joined as a defendant in the suit.**

This is the "you have to sue the original speaker too" trap. The student half-remembered that there is joint-and-several liability among publishers and converts it into a rule that the republisher cannot be sued without the original. The breaker is structural: defamation plaintiffs may sue any one publisher they choose; they are not required to join all publishers. The original speaker (the parent) is independently liable for the original publication, and the republisher (Mary) is independently liable for the republication; one is not a precondition to suit against the other.

**D. No, because a newsletter is not the kind of medium that counts as "publication" for defamation purposes.**

This is the "medium doesn't count" trap. The student tries to draw a line around traditional print or broadcast media and exclude email-distributed newsletters. The breaker is the definition of publication: communication of the defamatory content to a third party. A newsletter distributed to several hundred subscribers is a textbook publication; the medium (email, print, podcast, broadcast) does not change the publication element.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17111, a Torts item involving a podcast host who repeated a caller's false accusation under attribution. The official key is A: yes, the host is a publisher because repeating the accusation to listeners is republication.

Defamation is the publication of a false statement of fact to a third party that harms the plaintiff's reputation. Publication is satisfied by intentional or negligent communication to at least one person other than the plaintiff. A republisher is treated as a publisher of the defamatory content for purposes of liability. The classic statement of the rule is that "[o]ne who repeats or otherwise republishes a defamatory statement is liable for the defamation to the same extent as the original publisher, even if the source is identified." Restatement (Second) of Torts § 578 codifies the rule that one who republishes a defamatory statement is subject to liability as if he had originally published it.

Attribution does not insulate the republisher. Identification of the original source goes to mitigation of damages and to the republisher's credibility for the factfinder; it is not a defense to liability. The republisher's act of repeating the defamatory charge to a new audience is itself a publication, and the republisher can be held liable on the republication theory, with or without the original speaker as a defendant.

The medium of communication is not dispositive. Publication is satisfied by communication to a third party, regardless of medium. Print, broadcast, podcast, email newsletter, social media post, and live announcement all qualify if the communication reaches a third party. There is no "podcast exception" or "newsletter exception" to the publication element of defamation.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original. Mary, who repeated the parent's accusation to several hundred subscribers with attribution, is a publisher of the defamatory statement and is liable to Naomi on a republication theory.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. No, because attributing the accusation to "a parent in our school community" insulates the newsletter editor from liability for the underlying charge.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice asserts a fabricated rule: that attribution is a defense to republication. The doctrinal assertion is the overclaim; without the Gold Key, a student may accept attribution as a shield.
2. **Student label:** Attribution-as-defense trap.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Attribution might matter for mitigation of damages, but it does not insulate the republisher from liability for the defamatory sting."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Attribution is not a defense to republication. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 578 treats a republisher as a publisher of the defamatory content. Identifying the source goes to damages mitigation, not to liability.

### B. Yes, but only if the parent who wrote the original email is also joined as a defendant in the suit.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice converts a joint-and-several-liability observation into a joinder rule. The cut is structural: defamation plaintiffs may sue any one publisher they choose; they are not required to join all publishers.
2. **Student label:** Joinder defense misframe.
3. **True/responsive version:** "A plaintiff may sue the original speaker, the republisher, or both. Joinder is a procedural option, not a precondition to liability."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** A defamation plaintiff may sue any one publisher (original or republisher) without joining the others. Joinder is a procedural option, not a precondition to liability.

### C. Yes, because republishing a defamatory accusation to the newsletter's subscribers is publication, even though the editor attributed the charge to the parent.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the residual after the medium, joinder, and attribution defenses are cut. It treats republication as itself a publication and rejects attribution as a defense.
2. **Student label:** Residual answer.
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Mary is a publisher of the defamatory statement. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 578; republication to several hundred subscribers satisfies the publication element; attribution is not a defense.

### D. No, because a newsletter is not the kind of medium that counts as "publication" for defamation purposes.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice asserts a medium-based exception that does not exist. Publication is communication to a third party, regardless of medium. The cut is structural: there is no newsletter/email/podcast exception.
2. **Student label:** Medium-based defense misframe.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Publication is communication to a third party; the medium (print, broadcast, podcast, email) does not change the analysis."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Publication is communication of the defamatory content to a third party, regardless of medium. Print, broadcast, podcast, email newsletter, social media post, and live announcement all qualify.

## residual_answer

The residual is **C**. Mary is a publisher of the defamatory statement because she republished the accusation to several hundred subscribers; attribution is not a defense to republication.
