## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

When both parties still have unperformed obligations under a contract, they may agree to cancel the entire agreement. This is called **mutual rescission**. The key requirement is that the contract must be **executory on both sides** — neither party has fully performed. Once the parties validly agree to mutual rescission, most courts hold that neither party is obligated to pay for any benefits already received under the contract before the rescission. The tutor and the mother both had remaining obligations (the tutor had 12 weeks left; the mother had 12 weeks of payment remaining), so mutual rescission was available, and the tutor cannot recover for the tutoring already rendered.

**Silver Key:** When a question asks whether a party can recover after both sides agree to cancel, check whether both parties still had unperformed duties at the time of cancellation. If yes, mutual rescission bars recovery for past benefits — regardless of who asked first.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (No — because the tutor asked first):** This choice appeals to the intuitive sense that the person who breaks the deal should bear the cost. But mutual rescission does not depend on which party initiated the request. The law looks at whether both parties agreed to cancel and whether both still had unperformed obligations. Who asked first is legally irrelevant. **Silver Key:** The "who asked" fact is a red herring — mutual rescission turns on mutual assent and bilateral executory status, not on who spoke first.

**B (Yes — because the mother received value):** This choice sounds fair — the mother's daughter did receive 8 weeks of tutoring, so shouldn't the tutor be paid? But mutual rescission extinguishes the contractual obligation to pay for benefits already received. The mother's receipt of value does not, by itself, create an obligation to pay after the parties have agreed to cancel the whole contract. This is the common temptation to apply ordinary fairness reasoning instead of the mutual rescission rule.

**D (Yes — because the tutor is entitled to payment for partial performance):** This choice appeals to the general principle that a party who performs should be compensated. But partial performance alone does not create a right to payment when the parties have validly agreed to rescind the entire contract. The mutual rescission agreement cancels both future obligations and the obligation to pay for past benefits. The tutor's remedy, if any, would have to come from a theory outside the contract (such as unjust enrichment), not from the contract itself — and most courts deny recovery on that basis too after mutual rescission.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing Rule:** Mutual rescission occurs when both parties to an executory contract agree to cancel it. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 283 recognizes that parties may agree to discharge a contract by mutual assent. The traditional rule, applied in most jurisdictions, is that when parties agree to mutual rescission, neither party is obligated to pay for benefits already received under the contract. *See* 17A Am. Jur. 2d Contracts § 512 ("Where parties agree to a mutual rescission of a contract, neither party is entitled to compensation for services rendered prior to the rescission."); *see also* Corbin on Contracts § 1266 (mutual rescission extinguishes all obligations under the contract, including obligations for past performance).

**Confirmed:** The tested rule (mutual rescission bars recovery for past benefits), the legal issue (whether partial performer can recover after mutual rescission), and the credited outcome (no recovery) are all unchanged from the original question.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 283; 17A Am. Jur. 2d Contracts § 512.

- Recovery step: Re-read the stem. The question is not "who caused this?" The question is "what is the legal effect of both parties agreeing to cancel?" The identity of who asked is a fairness instinct, not a legal rule. Go back to the mutual rescission rule.

**If a student picked B (received-value trap):**
- Recovery step: Ask: "Does receiving value always mean you have to pay for it?" Not always — if the parties agree to cancel the whole deal, the value received under the deal does not have to be paid for. The mutual rescission rule overrides the general fairness instinct.

**If a student picked D (partial-performance entitlement trap):**
- Recovery step: Ask: "When does partial performance create a right to pay?" Only when the OTHER side breaches. Here, both sides agreed to cancel — that's not a breach, that's a mutual rescission. Different legal rule, different outcome.

**Placement:** Contracts > Defenses to Enforceability > Mutual Rescission

**Fills:**

#### Crossover Intersection Map

- **Contracts + Restitution:** A student might think "unjust enrichment" or "quantum meruit" could allow recovery. Crossover note: mutual rescission generally bars restitutionary recovery for past benefits too — the agreement to cancel is treated as a waiver.
- **Contracts + Torts:** No significant crossover here — this is a pure contracts question.

#### Review Truth

When both parties still have unperformed obligations, they may mutually rescind the contract, and neither is required to pay for benefits already received.
