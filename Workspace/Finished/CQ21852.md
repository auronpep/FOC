### 1. Final Question

Lydia and Silas agreed to buy a collection of rare, 18th-century Bibles that they believed had been stolen from a local church’s historical archive. Their plan was to resell the Bibles to private collectors for a significant profit. In reality, the Bibles were high-quality replicas created by the police specifically for a sting operation, and the church archive had never actually been burglarized. Lydia and Silas are charged with conspiracy to receive stolen property. At trial, they argue that they cannot be guilty because it was impossible to receive "stolen" property when the Bibles in question were never actually stolen.

Is the fact that the Bibles were not stolen a valid defense to the conspiracy charge?

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

(B) is correct. Conspiracy is an inchoate crime that is complete upon the agreement to commit an unlawful act (plus an overt act in most jurisdictions). The essence of the crime is the agreement itself and the criminal intent of the parties. Under both modern law and the Model Penal Code, factual impossibility is not a defense to conspiracy. If the conspirators agree to perform acts that would be criminal if the facts were as they believed them to be, they are guilty of conspiracy. Here, Lydia and Silas believed they were agreeing to buy stolen Bibles. The fact that the Bibles were actually police replicas (making the completed crime of receiving stolen property factually impossible) does not negate the criminal nature of their agreement. Gold Key: Factual impossibility (a circumstance unknown to the actors that prevents completion) does not defeat a conspiracy charge.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

(A) This choice is attractive because it highlights the impossibility of completing the crime. However, it is incorrect because the focus of conspiracy is the agreement and the subjective intent of the parties. The objective impossibility of completing the target crime does not excuse the criminal agreement.

(C) This choice is attractive because it appeals to the idea of "merger" or "no harm, no foul." However, it is a flat misstatement of law. Conspiracy is an independent offense that is complete before the target crime is even attempted; completion of the target crime is never an element of conspiracy.

(D) This choice is attractive because it attempts to provide a "policy" reason for the conviction. However, it is incorrect because the impossibility rule applies regardless of the nature of the property. The Bibles' status as church property is narrative texture and does not change the underlying legal principle of factual impossibility.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: Conspiracy — Factual Impossibility Defense. Factual impossibility occurs when the intended substantive crime cannot be committed due to a physical or factual condition unknown to the defendant. Most jurisdictions and the MPC hold that factual impossibility is not a defense to inchoate crimes like conspiracy. The test is whether the agreed-upon conduct would be a crime if the facts were as the conspirators believed them to be. Authority: State v. Moretti, 52 N.J. 182 (1968); MPC § 5.03(1). The outcome (No defense) is consistent with the source item.
