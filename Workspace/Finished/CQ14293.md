## 1. Final Question

Timothy, a church deacon, paid $1,200 for a premium online streaming subscription solely to watch the live broadcast of a high-profile criminal trial in state court. The trial judge had initially permitted live streaming but abruptly ordered it stopped midway through the proceedings, concluding that the streaming was prejudicing the jury. Timothy filed suit in federal district court against the trial judge, seeking only an injunction to resume the live broadcast. He alleged that the judge's order deprived him of his $1,200 streaming investment without due process of law. Before Timothy's federal case was heard, the criminal trial concluded with a conviction and sentence. No obvious errors tainted the criminal proceedings. The judge in Timothy's federal case moved to dismiss.

The most proper disposition of the motion to dismiss is to:

**A.** Defer action until after the state Supreme Court rules on the proper disposition, because the state law of mootness governs suits in federal court when the federal case is inexorably intertwined with a state proceeding.

**B.** Grant the motion, because the subject matter of the controversy has ceased to exist and there is no strong likelihood that it will be revived.

**C.** Deny the motion, because Timothy has raised an important constitutional question: whether his investment in a streaming subscription, purchased solely to view the trial, constitutes property protected by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

**D.** Defer action on the motion until after any appellate proceedings in the criminal case have concluded, because the defendant might appeal, the conviction might be set aside, a new trial might be ordered, and the streaming issue could resurface.

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** Article III of the Constitution limits federal court jurisdiction to live "Cases" and "Controversies." Once the underlying controversy ends and the relief sought can no longer be granted, the case becomes moot and must be dismissed. Here, the criminal trial is over — the defendant was convicted and sentenced. Timothy sought an injunction to resume live streaming of that trial. That relief is now impossible; the trial cannot be re-televised after it has concluded. No exception to mootness applies: the "capable of repetition yet evading review" exception requires both that the same plaintiff is likely to be subjected to the same injury again and that the injury is inherently of such short duration that it will always evade review. Neither condition is met here — there is no reason to believe Timothy will face the same situation again, and the issue is not one that inherently evades review. The court should grant the motion to dismiss.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect.** This choice sounds procedurally cautious — wait for the state Supreme Court to weigh in. But there is no "state law of mootness" that governs federal courts. Mootness in federal court is a constitutional doctrine rooted in Article III's case-or-controversy requirement, not a matter of state law. Federal courts apply federal justiciability doctrines regardless of whether a state proceeding is related. This distractor baits students who conflate state and federal procedural law or who think federal courts must defer to state courts on jurisdictional questions.

**C is incorrect.** This is the constitutional-bait trap. Timothy did raise a federal question — whether his streaming subscription cost is a protected property interest under the Due Process Clause. That is a colorable constitutional claim. But a federal question alone does not create jurisdiction. Article III still requires a live case or controversy. A moot case is not a case or controversy, no matter how important the constitutional issue. Students who see "important constitutional question" and reflexively want to reach the merits fall into this trap. **Gold Key:** *A federal question cannot be adjudicated without a live case or controversy; mootness deprives the court of jurisdiction regardless of the importance of the legal issue raised.*

**D is incorrect.** This is the dominant trap. It is tempting because it sounds prudent — what if the conviction is overturned on appeal and there is a new trial? Then the streaming issue could resurface. But the mootness analysis asks whether there is a "strong likelihood" that the controversy will be revived, not whether revival is theoretically possible. The facts state there were no obvious errors in the criminal proceedings, making an appellate reversal unlikely. Moreover, even if a new trial were granted, the judge might permit streaming the second time. The chance of revival is too remote to defeat mootness. Students who overvalue remote hypotheticals and want to "wait and see" pick this answer.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** Article III, § 2 of the U.S. Constitution limits federal judicial power to "Cases" and "Controversies." The mootness doctrine derives from this requirement: when the issues presented are no longer "live" or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome, the case becomes moot and the court lacks jurisdiction to decide it. *DeFunis v. Odegaard*, 416 U.S. 312 (1974) (dismissing as moot a challenge to law school admissions policy where plaintiff was admitted during pendency); *U.S. Const. art. III, § 2*.

**Exceptions to mootness:**
1. **Voluntary cessation** — defendant voluntarily stops the challenged conduct but could resume it. Not applicable here; the trial ended naturally.
2. **Capable of repetition, yet evading review** — the challenged action is too short in duration to be fully litigated before it ends, and there is a reasonable expectation the same plaintiff will be affected again. *Roe v. Wade*, 410 U.S. 113 (1973) (pregnancy is inherently time-limited). Not applicable here: no reasonable expectation Timothy will face the same injury, and the issue does not inherently evade review.
3. **Collateral consequences** — mootness does not apply if the judgment carries continuing legal consequences. Not applicable.

**Authority confirmation:** The credited answer (C → B post-shuffle) is correct. The tested rule (mootness under Article III), the legal issue (whether the case is moot after the trial ends), and the credited outcome (grant the motion to dismiss) are all unchanged from the original. No doctrinal drift detected. The mootness doctrine has not been materially altered by recent Supreme Court decisions.

**Attorney review:** Not required. Authority floor satisfied by Article III, § 2 and *DeFunis v. Odegaard*, 416 U.S. 312 (1974).
