### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The breaking element **can** be satisfied. Common-law burglary requires breaking and entering, at night, the dwelling of another, with intent to commit a felony at the time of entry. The breaking element is satisfied by constructive breaking — including gaining entry by fraud, threat, or trick — even without physical damage to the dwelling. Paul's lie about being an electric-company employee induced Mary to open the door; that consent was procured by fraud and is not a defense to the breaking element. Paul also had the requisite felonious intent at the moment of entry (he walked in intending to steal). **Gold Key:** at common law, "breaking" is broader than physical force; fraud-induced consent is constructive breaking. **Silver Key:** on a burglary question, the entry need not be by force — fraud, threat, or trick all count.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. No, because breaking always requires physical damage.**

This is the dominant trap. It maps to a popular student conception of burglary that fuses it with breaking-and-entering in the literal sense. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: at common law, the breaking element includes constructive breaking — fraud, threat, or trick — even where no physical damage occurs. The fraud-induced entry is enough.

**B. No, because the homeowner opened the door voluntarily.**

This trap turns the act of opening the door into apparent consent that defeats the breaking element. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: consent procured by fraud is not effective consent for the breaking element. Mary opened the door because Paul lied about being a utility worker; her consent was induced by deception and is treated as no consent at all for the constructive-breaking analysis.

**C. No, because burglary requires theft after entry, not intent at entry.**

This trap conflates common-law burglary with the completed crime of theft. The breaker is the common-law rule that burglary is complete on entry with felonious intent; the intended felony (here, larceny) need not actually occur. Paul walked in with the intent to steal; that is enough. Whether he actually completed the theft affects the larceny charge, not the burglary charge.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17225, a Criminal Law item involving an entry obtained by false pretenses (claiming to be a utility worker) at midnight, with intent to commit theft at the time of entry. The official key is A: yes, fraud-induced entry satisfies the breaking element.

Common-law burglary is the breaking and entering of the dwelling house of another at night with the intent to commit a felony therein. The breaking element is satisfied by even slight force, and the long-standing majority rule is that constructive breaking — entry obtained by fraud, threat, or trick — counts as breaking. See 2 Wayne R. LaFave, *Substantive Criminal Law* § 21.1(c) (4th ed.) (entry by fraud or trick satisfies the breaking element where the occupant's consent was procured by deception); Model Penal Code § 221.1 codifies the modern burglary offense and treats entry by "trick, fraud, or deception" as satisfying the unauthorized-entry element of second-degree burglary. The same point appears in the common-law burglary treatises and in older case law (e.g., *People v. Vogel*, 299 N.Y. 199 (1949), recognizing that consent obtained by trick or fraud is not effective to defeat the breaking element).

He had the intent to commit larceny (steal the pearl necklace and Nativity set) at the time of entry. The breaking element is satisfied by constructive breaking (fraud). The dwelling-of-another element is satisfied (Mary's home). The at-night element is satisfied (midnight). The felonious-intent element is satisfied at the moment of entry. 1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice states a flat rule about physical damage. Common-law breaking is broader.
2. **Student label:** Physical-damage-only misstatement.
3. **True/responsive version:** "No, because breaking can be constructive, including fraud, even without physical damage."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** At common law, the breaking element is satisfied by constructive breaking — fraud, threat, or trick — even without physical damage. The "always requires physical damage" formulation misstates the rule. See LaFave, *Substantive Criminal Law* § 21.1(c).

### B. No, because the homeowner opened the door voluntarily.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice assumes voluntary opening defeats consent. Consent procured by fraud is not effective consent.
2. **Student label:** Voluntary-opening mislabel.
3. **True/responsive version:** "No, because Mary consented, but the consent was procured by deception."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Consent induced by deception is treated as no consent for the constructive-breaking analysis. Mary opened the door because of Paul's lie; her apparent consent is not effective.

### C. No, because burglary requires theft after entry, not intent at entry.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice requires a completed theft. Common-law burglary is complete on entry with felonious intent.
2. **Student label:** Intent-after-entry mislabel.
3. **True/responsive version:** "No, because Paul did not actually complete the theft during the entry."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Burglary is complete upon entry with the requisite intent. The actual completion of the intended felony is not an element of the burglary offense. Whether Paul actually completed the theft affects the larceny charge, not the burglary charge.

### D. Yes, because entry obtained by fraud can be constructive breaking.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This states the constructive-breaking rule and matches the call.
2. **Student label:** True and responsive.
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Fraud-induced entry is constructive breaking at common law. All four burglary elements are satisfied on the facts (dwelling, of another, at night, intent to commit a felony at entry).

## residual_answer

The residual is **D**. The breaking element is satisfied by constructive breaking, including entry obtained by fraud.
