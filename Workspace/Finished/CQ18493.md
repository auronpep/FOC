# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question — stem + call

Silas was charged with aggravated assault. At the plea hearing, the judge confirmed that Silas had gone over the proposed plea with his attorney, that no promises beyond the announced plea agreement had been made to him, and that Silas was offering his plea of his own free will. The judge did not tell Silas that, by pleading guilty, he would be giving up his right to a trial by jury, his privilege against self-incrimination, and his right to confront the witnesses against him. Silas pleaded guilty. On appeal, Silas contends that his plea cannot stand.

**Was Silas's guilty plea constitutionally valid?**

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A judge accepts a guilty plea after confirming only that it is free and voluntary, never advising the defendant on the record that the plea waives his rights to a jury trial, against self-incrimination, and to confront witnesses. Is the plea valid?

## 3. Final answer choices (A–D, post-shuffle)

- **C.** No, because Silas's plea was not entered in open court and so was not supported by an adequate record.
- **D.** Yes, because the judge confirmed that the plea was free and voluntary, which is all that due process requires.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

The call asks only whether the plea is constitutionally valid, and validity turns on what the record shows. A guilty plea surrenders three specific constitutional rights at once — trial by jury, the privilege against self-incrimination, and confrontation — so the record must affirmatively show the defendant was advised of and understood that he was waiving each of them. The judge here confirmed voluntariness and counsel's involvement but never put the three waivers on the record, so the plea is defective. **Gold Key:** a plea is valid only when the record affirmatively shows a knowing waiver of jury trial + self-incrimination + confrontation — a "free and voluntary" finding does not supply it. **Silver Key:** in a 2-valid/2-invalid plea array, the "valid" choices each spotlight a single prong (voluntary, counsel) and are traps; among the "invalid" choices, cut the one that rests on a fact the stem never gives. **B** is the only choice that is both "No" and pinned to the dispositive defect.

The breaker: the knowing-waiver showing is the court's job to make on the record; counsel's presence does not stand in for the judge advising the defendant of the three rights. *Gold Key:* the record itself must show the waivers.
- **C (plea not entered in open court).** It is attractive because it reaches the correct outcome ("No, invalid") and "needs an adequate record" sounds like the right vocabulary. The breaker: voluntariness is necessary but not sufficient — without the affirmative on-record waiver of the three rights the plea fails. *Gold Key:* voluntary ≠ valid; the three-right colloquy is independently required.

## 9. Black-letter verification and right-answer legal reasoning

Confirmed UNCHANGED from the original: tested rule (Boykin three-right on-record waiver), legal issue (constitutional validity of a guilty plea), and credited outcome (invalid). Under *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), a guilty plea simultaneously waives the Sixth Amendment right to a jury trial, the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination, and the Sixth Amendment right to confront one's accusers; because the plea waives these fundamental rights, the record must affirmatively disclose that the defendant entered the plea knowingly and voluntarily, and a silent record will not support a presumption of waiver. A general voluntariness inquiry that omits the three advisements is constitutionally insufficient (see also *Brady v. United States*, 397 U.S. 742 (1970), confirming a plea must be both voluntary and intelligent). **Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):** *Boykin v.
