## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Daniel promised to buy the cottage, paid a deposit, and then refused to close because his job was moved far away. That is a personal reason for nonperformance, not a legal excuse. The contract also gave Lydia the option to keep the deposit as liquidated damages if Daniel defaulted. The $6,000 deposit is modest against a $132,000 purchase price, and the clause is not framed as a penalty. **Gold Key:** a reasonable liquidated damages deposit in a land-sale contract is enforced when the buyer defaults, even if the seller later proves a smaller actual loss. **Silver Key:** do not convert a valid liquidated damages clause into a post-resale accounting problem; the clause is the agreed remedy for the buyer's breach.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**Rule verified.** Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 356, parties may set liquidated damages for breach if the amount is reasonable in light of anticipated or actual loss and the difficulty of proving loss; an unreasonably large amount is an unenforceable penalty. Cornell Wex summarizes the same rule in its penalty-clause entry: liquidated damages are generally enforceable, while penalty clauses are not. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/penalty_clause.

**Land-sale confirmation.** Real-estate deposits are a common liquidated damages device because seller losses from a failed closing can be hard to measure at the time of contract. Wallace Real Estate Investment Inc. v. Groves, 124 Wn.2d 881, 881 P.2d 1010 (1994), treated reasonableness at the time of contracting as central and rejected proof of actual damages as a prerequisite to enforcement. Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/washington/supreme-court/1994/61448-2-1.html.

**Excuse confirmation.** Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 261 requires objective impracticability caused by a supervening event whose nonoccurrence was a basic assumption of the contract. A job reassignment is personal inconvenience, not objective impossibility of buying land. The subjective/objective distinction is summarized in a contracts discharge discussion noting that "I cannot do it" is not the same as "the thing cannot be done." Source: https://biz.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Civil_Law/Book%3A_Introduction_to_Contracts_Sales_and_Product_Liability/15%3A_Discharge_of_Obligations/15.01%3A_Discharge_of_Contract_Duties.

Daniel paid Lydia a $6,000 deposit to be credited against the purchase price at closing. The contract stated that, if Daniel defaulted, Lydia could elect to keep the deposit as liquidated damages.

Closing was scheduled for two weeks ago. Seven weeks before the closing date, Daniel's employer reassigned him to a job 850 miles away. Daniel immediately told Lydia that he would not close and demanded the return of the $6,000 deposit. Lydia refused. She waited until the scheduled closing date had passed, listed Selah House through a broker, and then conveyed it to Mary for $129,000. Mary paid the full purchase price and promptly recorded her deed. Mary knew about Daniel's earlier contract with Lydia.

In an appropriate action, Daniel seeks to recover the $6,000 deposit from Lydia. The most probable result will be that Lydia

A. must return the $6,000 to Daniel, because Daniel was legally justified in refusing to close.  
B. may keep the $6,000 deposit, because Daniel breached the contract.  
C. must return the $6,000 to Daniel, because Lydia can no longer carry out her contract with Daniel.  
D. must return $3,000 to Daniel, because Lydia's loss on the resale was only $3,000.

## distilled_core_question

Land buyer defaults for a personal job transfer; the contract lets the seller keep a modest deposit as liquidated damages; the seller later resells for a smaller actual loss. Can the buyer recover the deposit?

## call_and_prediction

The call asks for the seller's most probable result in the buyer's deposit-recovery action. Predict that the contract's default remedy controls unless the buyer has a real legal excuse or the liquidated damages clause is a penalty.

## trigger_facts

- Daniel signed a written contract to buy land.
- The contract says Lydia can keep the deposit as liquidated damages if Daniel defaults.
- Daniel refused to close before the closing date.
- The reason for refusal was a job reassignment, not destruction, illegality, or title failure.
- The deposit is about 4.5 percent of the price.
- Lydia later resold for $3,000 less than Daniel's contract price.
- Mary knew of Daniel's earlier contract, but Daniel is suing for the deposit after his own refusal to close.

## governing_c3_lane

Lane: land-sale contract remedy after buyer default. The answer array tests whether the student treats liquidated damages as the agreed remedy or collapses into actual-damages arithmetic.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### Choice A

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Daniel's job moved, but the cottage was still purchasable and the contract obligation could still be performed.
2. **Student label:** Personal hardship treated as legal excuse.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** Daniel would need a legal discharge such as objective impossibility, illegality, or a contract condition that failed.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 261 is about objective impracticability. A job reassignment is not enough.

### Choice B

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Daniel refused to close, and the contract tells what happens if he defaults.
2. **Student label:** Buyer default plus reasonable liquidated damages.
3. **What a true/responsive version looks like:** The seller keeps a reasonable agreed deposit after the buyer defaults.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 356 supports enforcing reasonable liquidated damages and rejecting penalties.

### Choice C

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Lydia's resale happened after Daniel had already said he would not close.
2. **Student label:** Later resale answers the wrong moment.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** C would matter more if Lydia breached before Daniel's performance was due and Daniel was ready, willing, and able to close.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** A seller's post-default resale does not erase the buyer's earlier breach or invalidate a reasonable liquidated damages clause.

### Choice D

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The arithmetic is clean: the resale was $3,000 lower than Daniel's contract price.
2. **Student label:** Actual-damages offset trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** D would fit if the contract had no valid liquidated damages clause and the seller were limited to actual damages.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Reasonable liquidated damages are not automatically reduced to later actual damages.

## residual_answer

B remains. It is the only choice that uses both decisive facts: Daniel breached, and the contract lets Lydia keep a reasonable deposit as liquidated damages.

## final_student_script

Daniel refused to close. The contract says Lydia can keep the deposit if Daniel defaults. A job transfer is not a legal excuse, and the later resale math does not rewrite a valid liquidated damages clause. The only answer that honors the default clause is B.

**GK-REAL_PROPERTY-LIQUIDATED-DEPOSIT-01:** A seller may keep a reasonable land-sale deposit as liquidated damages when the buyer defaults; the seller does not have to reduce the deposit dollar-for-dollar to later resale loss.

**GK-REAL_PROPERTY-JOB-TRANSFER-01:** A personal job transfer or changed convenience is not objective impossibility or impracticability of buying land.
