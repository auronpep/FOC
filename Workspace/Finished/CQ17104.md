### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. M'Naghten is a cognitive test. It asks whether, because of mental disease, the defendant knew the nature and quality of the act or knew the act was wrong. Gold Key: the nature-and-quality prong is satisfied when the defendant does not understand what physical act she is really doing, even if she is aware of a body movement. Silver Key: lock the call to M'Naghten before choosing between insanity answers; an impulse-control answer may sound sympathetic, but it belongs to a different test. Martha believed she was slicing bread and had no awareness that she was stabbing a person, so the defense succeeds under the nature-and-quality prong.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because it turns the story into a personal-responsibility question about prior diagnosis and supervision. The breaker is that M'Naghten does not ask whether Martha should have managed her illness earlier; it asks her cognitive state at the time of the killing.

C is attractive because impulse control sounds like a natural way to talk about mental disease and criminal blame. The breaker is the Gold Key: basic M'Naghten is cognitive, not volitional, so inability to resist an impulse is the wrong insanity standard for this call.

D is attractive because Martha knew she was moving her hand and using a knife, so the answer feels grounded in a visible fact. The breaker is the Silver Key: the nature-and-quality question is not whether she perceived a muscle movement; it is whether she understood she was stabbing a human being rather than cutting bread.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue is Criminal Law and Procedure, Defenses, Excuse Defenses, Responsibility, and the verified outline code is `72060101`. The source row's listed `72060201` code is a source defect because that node is self-defense, while this item tests insanity.

The governing authority is M'Naghten's Case, 8 Eng. Rep. 718, 10 Cl. & Fin. 200 (1843). The classic formulation excuses a defendant who, due to disease of the mind, did not know the nature and quality of the act or did not know the act was wrong. Justia's report of M'Naghten's Case states the same disjunctive cognitive rule, and Cornell Wex describes the two prongs as individually sufficient. See https://law.justia.com/cases/foreign/united-kingdom/8-eng-rep-718.html and https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/insanity_defense. The University of Minnesota Law Library identifies M'Naghten's Case as 8 English Reports 718 and summarizes the same rule. See https://lawlibrarycollections.umn.edu/classic-cases-criminal-mnaghten-rule.

The credited outcome is unchanged. Martha's delusion prevented her from knowing the true nature and quality of her act: she believed she was slicing bread, not stabbing a human being. That satisfies the first M'Naghten prong without needing to prove that she also failed to know wrongfulness. The irresistible-impulse concept remains outside the basic M'Naghten test and does not supply the reason this defense succeeds.
