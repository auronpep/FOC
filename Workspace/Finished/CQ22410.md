### 2. The 15-section student case study

## program_frame

This is a suppression question. Do not start with the closed box. Start with the actor.

The court excludes evidence for a constitutional search problem only if the search is tied to the government. Here, the first search was done by the brother’s guest.

## student_access_test

This item is **anchor-assisted**.

A student can structurally see that C overstates the stem: “maybe look around” is not the same as “directed.” But the main unlock is doctrinal: the Fourth Amendment applies to government action, not ordinary private searches. The student needs the private-search Gold Key.

## call_and_prediction

**Call:** Should the court grant the motion to exclude the methamphetamine?

**Prediction:** No. The man found the methamphetamine during a private search. The officer gave an idea, but did not direct the search.

**Adjacent-call trap:** Was the closed wooden box private? That is not first. Actor first. Privacy second.

## trigger_facts

1. The man, not the officer, searched the house.
2. The officer suggested asking the brother directly, or maybe looking around.
3. The man searched the next day, while the brother was away.
4. The drugs were inside a closed wooden box.
5. The man called the officer after finding the drugs.

The splitting fact is the actor: **the private man opened the box and found the drugs.**

## governing_c3_lane

**Lane:** Criminal Procedure threshold question.

**Gold Key:** Fourth Amendment exclusion normally requires government action. A private person’s search does not trigger suppression unless the private person acted as a government agent.

**Silver Key:** Actor first; privacy second.

The U.S. Supreme Court’s *Jacobsen* opinion states the key private-search principle: the Fourth Amendment is directed at governmental action and is inapplicable to a search by a private individual not acting as a government agent; it also explains that private invasions, because of their private character, do not violate the Fourth Amendment. ([Justia Law][1])

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. No, because the brother left the man alone in the house.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice answers an access/permission question. It does not answer the suppression threshold.
2. **Student label:** Wrong-element answer.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** “No, because the man was a private person, not a government actor or agent.”
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Whether the brother left him alone might explain opportunity. It does not supply the Fourth Amendment reason. The Fourth Amendment issue is state action, not household access.

### B. No, because the man found the methamphetamine.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice uses the actor fact. The man found it before police arrived.
2. **Student label:** Private-search survivor.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This answer would be stronger if it said “because the man was a private person not acting as a government agent.” But its legal substance points to the decisive fact.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Correct. The evidence is not excluded because the search was private and the officer did not direct it. Under *Jacobsen*, private searches do not become Fourth Amendment violations merely because police later receive the evidence. ([Justia Law][1])

### C. Yes, because the police officer directed the man’s search.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The answer changes the stem. The officer gave a suggestion; the answer says “directed.”
2. **Student label:** Suggestion-upgraded-to-direction.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** “Yes, if the officer directed or controlled the man’s search so that the man acted as a government agent.”
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** If police had directed the private person to conduct the search, the agency issue could change the result. But this stem gives non-directive advice, not command or control.

### D. Yes, because the search violated the man’s right to privacy by opening the closed wooden box.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the strongest privacy trap. It jumps to the box before asking who opened it.
2. **Student label:** Privacy-before-actor trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** “Yes, if a government actor or government agent opened the closed wooden box without a warrant or exception.”
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** A closed container is a privacy fact in a government-search case. But the initial opening here was done by a private person, so the Fourth Amendment threshold is missing. *Jacobsen* frames the government actor/private actor distinction as the threshold. ([Justia Law][1])

## residual_answer

**B survives.**

It is not perfect wording, but it points to the decisive fact: **the man found the drugs.** The officer did not direct the search. The search was private. No exclusion.
