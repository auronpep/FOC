### 1. Final question
To staff a new public-school choral program, a state education board began using a loyalty screen for music-teacher certificates. The screen made an applicant ineligible if the applicant now belonged to, or had ever belonged to, an organization the board classified as subversive. On the revised certificate form, Lydia was asked to disclose whether she currently belonged, or had previously belonged, to any such organization. Lydia had otherwise completed the academic, classroom, and character requirements for certification, but she left that item blank and said the state could not condition a teaching certificate on that associational disclosure. The board refused to certify her on that basis. In Lydia's First Amendment challenge, is she likely to prevail?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. A state licensing board is a government actor, and the First Amendment limits the state's ability to burden association as a condition of receiving a public license. The state may investigate fitness for a sensitive public role, but this loyalty screen sweeps across current and past membership without tying denial to knowing, active support for illegal aims. Gold Key: mere membership, especially past membership, is not enough; the state needs a narrower link to knowing active membership and specific intent to further unlawful conduct. Silver Key: once both yes answers survive, choose the narrower yes that preserves that specific-intent safety valve, not the answer that says any association always wins.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because the setting is licensing, not a courtroom, and "professional licensing" can sound like ordinary state housekeeping. The breaker is visible in the stem: the decisionmaker is a state education board conditioning a public certificate. That is state action, so the First Amendment applies.

C is attractive because "subversive organization" feels dangerous, and danger makes students want to convert membership into endorsement. The breaker is the Gold Key: association is not endorsement unless the applicant knowingly and actively joined with specific intent to further unlawful aims. C skips that intent link.

D is attractive because it reaches the right constitutional side and sounds protective of First Amendment association. The breaker is its absolute word "any." The state is not powerless to consider knowing active membership tied to specific intent to further illegal activity; D overprotects association and would block even a properly narrowed inquiry.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Constitutional Law, Individual Rights, Freedom of association, and the verified outline code is `44040504`. The First Amendment, applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, protects associational freedom from state burdens. In the teacher-disclosure setting, Shelton v. Tucker, 364 U.S. 479 (1960), invalidated a broad requirement that public-school teachers disclose organizational ties because the state's legitimate fitness inquiry did not justify an unlimited associational sweep. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/364/479/

The bar-admission line confirms the same rule for public professional entry. Baird v. State Bar of Arizona, 401 U.S. 1 (1971), held that a state may not exclude a qualified applicant from a profession solely because of political membership or belief, and that broad inquiries into beliefs and associations carry a heavy First Amendment burden. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/401/1/ In re Stolar, 401 U.S. 23 (1971), likewise rejected a broad bar-admission question asking whether the applicant had been or was a member of an organization advocating forceful overthrow. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/401/23/

The limiting rule is real. Scales v. United States, 367 U.S. 203 (1961), sustained liability only on a narrowed construction requiring active, knowing membership and specific intent to further illegal aims, not mere nominal or passive membership. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/367/203/ Current compelled-disclosure doctrine is consistent: Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta, 594 U.S. 595 (2021), applies exacting scrutiny and requires narrow tailoring for compelled disclosure of advocacy affiliations. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/594/19-251/

Those authorities confirm the official key. The state may ask relevant, narrowly focused fitness questions, and Konigsberg v. State Bar of California, 366 U.S. 36 (1961), allows consequences for refusing substantially relevant inquiries. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/366/36/ But this item's rule disqualifies anyone who is or ever was a member of a subversive group and asks a broad current-or-past membership question. Denial on that basis is not narrowly tied to knowing active membership plus specific intent. The credited outcome is unchanged.
