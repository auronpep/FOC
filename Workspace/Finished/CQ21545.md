# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

In a federal embezzlement trial, the prosecutor, Lydia, asks the witness, Barnabas, how many grain sacks were loaded onto the cart that night. Barnabas answers, "Forty." Lydia asks the same question two more times, and Barnabas gives the same answer each time. Lydia then asks it a fourth time. Opposing counsel objects. What is the best ruling?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A lawyer asks a witness the same question a fourth time after the witness already gave the same clear answer three times; the other side objects. How should the judge rule?

## 3. Final answer choices (A–D, post-shuffle)

- **B.** Sustain the objection because the question has been asked and answered.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (B)

The judge runs the courtroom. Once Barnabas has given the same clear answer three times, a fourth identical question adds nothing but delay, and the court may shut it down. **Gold Key:** under the court's reasonable-control power the question has already been *asked and answered*, so the repetition is barred to avoid wasting time. **Silver Key:** when the array splits into two "sustain" choices and two "overrule" choices, lock the call on the visible fact — the answer is settled and unchanged — then cut whichever choices bolt an extra condition onto that fact. B states the rule cleanly with no added condition; that is what makes it the residual.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

- **A — Overrule unless Barnabas changes his answer.** This feels reasonable because it sounds like patience: maybe one more ask shakes something loose. The breaker is that it invents a trigger the rule does not have — control over repetitive questioning does not wait for the witness to flip; the question is barred precisely *because* the answer is settled, not in spite of it. **Silver Key:** the "unless he changes" condition is the tell — it makes the wrong fact (a settled answer) into a reason to keep going.
- **C — Overrule because a party may repeat any relevant question.** This is the strongest pull: relevance is real and salient, so "it's a relevant question" feels like a complete defense. The breaker is the word "any" — relevance gets a question *asked*, it does not license asking it without limit; the court's time-control power overrides bare relevance. **Gold Key:** relevance is necessary, not a license for repetition — asked-and-answered defeats it.
- **D — Sustain only if the repeated question is also hearsay.** This is attractive because hearsay is the reflex evidence objection and "sustain" matches the right outcome. The breaker is that hearsay is a different doctrine entirely; asked-and-answered is a courtroom-control objection that stands on its own and needs no hearsay hook.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning (right answer)

The credited answer is correct under **FRE 611(a)**, which gives the trial court reasonable control over the mode and order of examining witnesses so as to make procedures effective for determining the truth and **avoid wasting time**. The "asked and answered" objection is the standard application of that control: once a witness has clearly answered a question, the examiner may be barred from asking it again merely to repeat or emphasize the answer. No leading case is needed; the governing authority is the rule itself (FRE 611(a)). Hearsay (FRE 801–802) is not implicated — the objection is to repetitive questioning, not to an out-of-court statement offered for its truth. The tested rule, the legal issue (courtroom control over repetitive questioning), and the credited outcome (sustain) are UNCHANGED from the original. Authority is confined to this lawyer-confirmation layer.

*Authority floor:* FRE 611(a) (court's reasonable control / asked and answered). Confidence: high; rule-number citation only, no case fabricated.
