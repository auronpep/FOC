## 7. Right-Answer Explanation

The identification is likely admissible. A one-person showup is suggestive, but suggestiveness alone does not require automatic suppression. Under the due process test from *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967), and *Manson v. Brathwaite*, 432 U.S. 98 (1977), the court evaluates whether the procedure was unnecessarily suggestive under the totality of the circumstances, considering reliability factors: (1) the witness's opportunity to view the criminal at the time of the crime, (2) the witness's degree of attention, (3) the accuracy of the witness's prior description, (4) the level of certainty at the confrontation, and (5) the time between the crime and the confrontation. Here, every factor cuts in favor of reliability: the manager saw the robber face-to-face under bright lights, the identification occurred minutes later, the suspect was detained one block away carrying the victim's property, and the manager identified the suspect immediately. Despite the suggestiveness of a one-person showup, the strong reliability indicators mean the identification is likely admissible.

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap):** This choice sells the intuitive overgeneralization that suggestive procedures are *per se* inadmissible. Students who fixate on the showup's suggestibility may leap to an always/never conclusion. But the Supreme Court explicitly rejected a per se exclusionary rule in *Manson v. Brathwaite*, holding instead that reliability factors govern. The word "always" is the structural tell — absolute language in the answer text signals an overclaim. **Gold Key:** Suggestiveness triggers a reliability analysis, not automatic exclusion.

**B:** This choice preys on students who know the Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies to some identification procedures but overextend it. The right to counsel under *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218 (1967), attaches only at post-charge lineups and showups — not at pre-charge identifications. *Kirby v. Illinois*, 406 U.S. 682 (1972), confirmed that the Sixth Amendment right has not yet attached at a pretrial showup conducted before formal charges. **Silver Key (trap_spotting):** When the answer text says "all pretrial identification procedures," pause — the right to counsel is triggered by formal charging, not by the fact of a showup alone.

**D:** This choice exploits the reasonable intuition that police custody makes a showup inherently unfair. But detention is the normal context for a showup; it does not by itself create a constitutional violation. The due process inquiry is whether the procedure as a whole created a substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification — not merely whether the suspect was detained.

## 9. Black-Letter Legal Verification

**Governing rule:** The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires suppression of eyewitness identification evidence only when the identification procedure was so unnecessarily suggestive that it created a substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification. *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967); *Neil v. Biggers*, 409 U.S. 188 (1972); *Manson v. Brathwaite*, 432 U.S. 98 (1977).

**Reliability factors** (*Neil v. Biggers*, 409 U.S. at 199-200): (1) opportunity to view; (2) degree of attention; (3) accuracy of prior description; (4) level of certainty; (5) time between crime and confrontation.

**Right to counsel distinction:** The Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies at post-charge lineups and showups (*United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218 (1967); *Moore v. Illinois*, 434 U.S. 220 (1977)), but not at pre-charge identification procedures (*Kirby v. Illinois*, 406 U.S. 682 (1972)). A showup conducted before formal charges does not trigger the right to counsel, though it remains subject to the due process reliability test.

**Authority:** *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967); *Neil v. Biggers*, 409 U.S. 188 (1972); *Manson v. Brathwaite*, 432 U.S. 98 (1977); *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218 (1967); *Kirby v. Illinois*, 406 U.S. 682 (1972).
