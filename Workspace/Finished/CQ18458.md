Barnabas owns a row of rented artisan workshops that open onto a single walled, gated courtyard. Barnabas keeps the courtyard locked, maintains it, and controls who passes through it; every tenant and every tenant's visitor must cross that shared courtyard to reach a workshop. ### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)
B is correct. A landlord does owe a duty to people foreseeably on the land, including a tenant's guests crossing a common area the landlord controls. But that duty is only a duty to act reasonably. Barnabas had no notice that the ram was dangerous, so his failure to guard against that risk was not unreasonable, and there is no breach. No notice, no breach, no liability. **Gold Key:** a landlord is liable for a tenant's dangerous animal only when the landlord had actual knowledge of the animal's dangerous propensities and the power to remove it; with no notice, the reasonable-care duty is not breached. **Silver Key:** when two choices reach the same outcome ("No"), do not stop at the outcome — pick the one whose REASON is correct, because the trap is a true result resting on a false rule.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because it reaches the right bottom line (Barnabas wins) and offers a clean, easy-to-remember rule to get there: a landlord simply owes nothing to someone else's guest. The breaker is that the rule is false — a landlord does owe a duty of reasonable care to foreseeable persons, including a tenant's guests, in common areas under the landlord's control; Barnabas wins because he did not breach that duty, not because the duty never existed. **Gold Key:** the landlord's duty to foreseeable guests in common areas is real but is only a duty of reasonable care. **Silver Key:** the right-result-wrong-reason answer is the dominant trap — confirm the rationale, not just the verdict. (Dominant trap preserved.)

C is attractive because a ram with a known habit of butting people sounds like exactly the kind of dangerous animal that triggers strict liability, and "strictly liable" feels like the heavyweight answer. The breaker is that strict liability for a dangerous domestic animal falls on the animal's possessor who knows of its propensity — here Stephen — not on a landlord who never possessed the ram; and even possessor strict liability requires knowledge of the unusual danger. **Gold Key:** strict liability for a dangerous domestic animal attaches to the knowing possessor, not to the landlord.

D is attractive because the nondelegable-duty rule is real: a landlord cannot contract away the duty to keep the property's physical conditions safe, such as broken stairs or cracked walks. The breaker is that a tenant's animal is not a "dangerous condition of the property," so the nondelegable-duty doctrine is being pulled into a context it does not govern; and even where it applies, the duty is still only reasonable care, which Barnabas did not breach without notice. **Gold Key:** the nondelegable duty runs to physical dangerous conditions of the premises, not to a tenant's animal, and it is still measured by reasonable care.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue is Torts, Negligence, Duty > In general. The verified outline code is `61020101`, which appears verbatim in `OUTLINE_CODES_COMPLETE.md` as "Negligence > Duty > In general."

The governing exam rule has three parts. First, a landlord owes a duty of reasonable care to persons foreseeably on the premises — including a tenant's guests — in the common areas the landlord retains control over. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 360 imposes liability on a landlord who retains control of a portion of the leased premises for physical harm to the tenant and others lawfully there caused by a dangerous condition the landlord could have made safe by reasonable care; § 361 extends this to parts the landlord is under a duty to maintain. The duty is reasonable care, not insurance.

Second, the landlord is not strictly liable for a tenant's animal. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 509 places strict liability for harm done by an abnormally dangerous domestic animal on the possessor who knows or has reason to know of the animal's dangerous propensity — here the tenant Stephen, not the landlord Barnabas, who never possessed the ram. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/strict_liability

Third, a landlord becomes liable for a tenant's dangerous animal only when the landlord had actual knowledge of the animal's dangerous propensities and retained the ability to remove it (by retaking possession or terminating the tenancy). The leading and frequently cited authority is Uccello v. Laudenslayer, 44 Cal. App. 3d 504 (1975), which holds that a residential landlord's duty of care toward a tenant's invitees regarding a tenant's vicious dog arises only on actual knowledge of the dangerous animal coupled with the right to remove it, and that the landlord has no duty to inspect for such animals. Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/44/504.html

Applying these: Barnabas owed Timothy a duty of reasonable care in the courtyard he controlled, so the no-duty rationale (A) misstates the law; Barnabas was never the ram's possessor, so strict liability (C) does not reach him; the ram is not a physical "dangerous condition of the property," so the nondelegable-duty doctrine (D) is misapplied. Because Barnabas had no notice of the ram's dangerous propensities, he did not breach his reasonable-care duty, and Timothy will not prevail.
