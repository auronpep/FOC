### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**Gold Key:** *Pinkerton Liability Rule:* Once a conspiracy exists, each conspirator is vicariously liable for substantive offenses committed by any co-conspirator **in furtherance of the conspiracy**—even if the specific crime was not part of the original plan, was unforeseeable, or was unnecessary to the scheme's success. The only requirement is that the substantive offense was committed to advance the conspiratorial objective, and the defendant has not withdrawn.

Here, Peter and Daniel agreed to steal a truck and sell it—a conspiracy. Daniel's act of driving the stolen truck across the state line was done to evade police and thus to aid their escape with the stolen vehicle. That makes it an act **in furtherance of the conspiracy**. Under Pinkerton, Peter is vicariously liable for the Dyer Act violation even though he was asleep and did not know Daniel would cross the state line. The foreseeability, specific agreement, and necessity arguments in choices A, B, and C are all irrelevant under the Pinkerton doctrine.

**Answer: C (guilty).**

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (not guilty — could not have anticipated the state-line crossing):**
This choice sells the idea that a conspirator can only be liable for a co-conspirator's crimes if the specific act was foreseeable or anticipated. That's a natural instinct—students want to limit liability to what the defendant knew or expected. But the Pinkerton doctrine doesn't require foreseeability. The question is only whether the act was committed *in furtherance of* the conspiracy. Daniel's evasion of police while driving their stolen truck was plainly in furtherance, so Peter is liable regardless of whether he anticipated the route. **Gold Key:** The Pinkerton Liability Rule defeats this trap—no foreseeability requirement exists.

**B (not guilty — did not agree to transport across a state line) [DOMINANT TRAP]:**
This is the most tempting wrong answer. It reframes the conspiracy as an agreement limited to the specific acts the defendant personally envisioned—steal and sell, nothing more. Students who pick this are treating conspiracy like a contract with narrow terms: "I agreed to steal, not to transport across state lines." But conspiracy liability doesn't work that way. The agreement is to pursue a criminal objective, and any crime a co-conspirator commits to advance that objective is attributable to all conspirators. Daniel's interstate driving was a step in their joint criminal project. Peter doesn't get to disclaim it because interstate transport wasn't in his mental blueprint. **Gold Key:** The Pinkerton Liability Rule—the agreement is to the criminal objective, not to each specific act.

**D (not guilty — interstate transport not necessary to the scheme's success):**
This choice invents a "necessity" requirement: the co-conspirator's crime must have been necessary for the conspiracy to succeed. No such requirement exists. The doctrine asks whether the act was *in furtherance of* the conspiracy, not whether it was indispensable. Daniel's evasive driving was plainly in furtherance—it helped them keep the stolen truck and avoid capture. Whether they *needed* to cross the state line is irrelevant. **Gold Key:** The Pinkerton Liability Rule uses "in furtherance of," not "necessary to."

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule — Conspiracy vicarious liability (Pinkerton doctrine):**
Under *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640 (1946), each member of a conspiracy is vicariously liable for substantive offenses committed by any co-conspirator in furtherance of the conspiracy, provided the defendant has not withdrawn. The doctrine requires only: (1) a conspiracy existed, (2) the substantive offense was committed in furtherance of the conspiracy, and (3) the defendant remained a member at the time. It does **not** require that the defendant knew of, foresaw, agreed to, or found necessary the specific substantive offense. *See also* 18 U.S.C. § 2312 (Dyer Act — interstate transportation of stolen vehicles); *Pinkerton*, 328 U.S. at 646–48.

**Application:**
- Conspiracy: Peter and Daniel agreed to steal a truck and sell it. This is a conspiracy to commit theft.
- Substantive offense: Daniel drove the stolen truck across a state line, violating the Dyer Act (18 U.S.C. § 2312).
- In furtherance: Daniel crossed the state line to evade police while transporting the stolen vehicle—an act that directly advanced the conspiratorial objective of profiting from the theft by avoiding capture.
- Peter was still a member of the conspiracy (he had not withdrawn). His being asleep is irrelevant; withdrawal requires an affirmative act communicated to co-conspirators.
- Therefore, Peter is vicariously liable for the Dyer Act violation.

**Authority:** *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640 (1946); 18 U.S.C. § 2312.

**Verification:** The tested rule (conspiracy vicarious liability), legal issue (whether a conspirator is guilty of a co-conspirator's substantive offense), and credited outcome (guilty) are all unchanged from the original. Research confirms the official key is correct.
