The defendant previously received transactional immunity and the jurisdiction has a "dead man's statute." Is the statement admissible?

### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** Under FRE 804(b)(2), an out-of-court statement is admissible as a dying declaration in a homicide prosecution **or in a civil case** if (i) the declarant is now unavailable, (ii) the statement was made upon the declarant's personal knowledge, (iii) it concerns the cause or circumstances of what the declarant believed to be impending death, and (iv) the declarant made it while believing death was imminent. Each element is satisfied here: Stephen is dead and thus unavailable; he said "I saw Paul strike me with the statue" on personal knowledge; the statement identifies the assailant and the manner of injury (cause/circumstances of the believed-impending death); and the opening words "I'm dying" supply the requisite sense of immediately impending death. The motion to strike should therefore be **denied**, and the statement comes in as a substantive exception to the hearsay rule.

> **Gold Key (GK-EVIDENCE-DYING-DECL-01):** *Civil-or-criminal scope.* The Federal Rules of Evidence extend the dying-declaration exception beyond the common-law criminal-homicide-only rule to **any civil case** as well. The common-law limitation is a trap, not the law. See FRE 804(b)(2) (primary text: "In a prosecution for homicide or in a civil case …").

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations (one per distractor)

**A is wrong (D in original letters — the *dead-man's-statue* trap).** The distractor is engineered to feel "right" because the stem explicitly names a dead man's statute, so a student scanning for what the statute *does* may assume it helps the case along. It does the opposite: where a dead man's statute applies, it **excludes** certain evidence (typically testimony by an interested party about a transaction or communication with a deceased person whose estate is a party). The curator is neither an interested party nor testifying about a transaction with Stephen; even setting that aside, the statute could only *exclude* the statement, not *admit* it. And in any event FRE 804(b)(2) supplies an independent basis for admission. *Silver Key (SK-EVIDENCE-DYING-DECL-01):* **When a stem names a statute, ask what that statute *does* before assuming the named effect is the correct one** — the dead man's statute's name does not tell you its function.

**B is wrong (B in original letters — the *transactional-immunity* trap).** This choice sounds powerful — "immunity" reads as a complete shield — and that is precisely the misdirection. Transactional immunity bars the government from **criminally prosecuting** the witness for the conduct to which the immunity extends. It does **not** foreclose a **civil** action by a private party; civil plaintiffs are not the government, and transactional immunity creates no private right of immunity. The motion here is in a wrongful-death civil suit brought by Stephen's widow; transactional immunity gives Paul no defense to that civil claim, and it has no bearing on whether the curator's testimony comes in. *Silver Key:* **Match the doctrine to the forum** — immunity is a criminal-procedure shield, not an evidence rule or a civil defense.

**D is wrong (A in original letters — the *common-law-only* trap; the dominant).** This distractor is engineered to attract the student who remembers that "dying declaration" is a narrow hearsay exception and is unsure how narrow. The common-law rule *did* limit dying declarations to criminal homicide prosecutions. But the Federal Rules of Evidence expressly **expand** the exception to civil cases: "In a prosecution for homicide **or in a civil case** …" FRE 804(b)(2). The action here is a civil wrongful-death suit, so the civil-or-criminal scope is satisfied. *Gold Key (GK-EVIDENCE-DYING-DECL-01):* **Common-law dying-declaration rule = criminal-homicide-only; FRE 804(b)(2) = homicide prosecution or civil case.** The "only" in the distractor is the giveaway — it is a `tiered_absolute` C3 cut.

### 8. Black-Letter Verification & Legal Reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule.** FRE 804(b)(2) — "Statement Under the Belief of Imminent Death" — a hearsay exception, applicable in a "prosecution for homicide or in a civil case," that admits a declarant's statement made while believing the declarant's death to be imminent about the cause or circumstances of that death, where the declarant is now unavailable.

**Authority (primary).** Federal Rules of Evidence, Rule 804(b)(2). Primary text (Cornell LII): *"In a prosecution for homicide or in a civil case, a statement that the declarant, while believing the declarant's death to be imminent, made about its cause or circumstances."* (Rule 804(b)(2) is a hearsay exception under Rule 804, which is itself a hearsay exception conditional on the declarant's unavailability under Rule 804(a).) Cornell LII / Legal Information Institute, *Rule 804. Hearsay Exceptions; Declarant Unavailable* (https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_804) — tracking parameters stripped, plain URL retained.

**Affirmative reasoning for C (kept unchanged from the source row).** Stephen is dead and therefore unavailable under FRE 804(a)(4) ("cannot be present or testify … because of death"). His statement "I saw Paul strike me with the statue" was made on personal knowledge (he perceived the assault) and concerns the cause of the death he believed to be imminent (identifying the assailant and the weapon). The opening clause "I'm dying" supplies the sense of immediately impending death. Because the case is a civil wrongful-death action — and FRE 804(b)(2) by its terms applies "in a prosecution for homicide or in a civil case" — the statement is admissible in this forum. The motion to strike is properly denied.
