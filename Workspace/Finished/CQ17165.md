### 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. At common law, larceny by trick covers a defendant who fraudulently obtains possession of another's personal property, while the owner intends to transfer only possession and not title, and the defendant converts the property with intent to permanently deprive. False pretenses is the neighboring category where the fraud induces transfer of both title and possession. Embezzlement requires lawful possession before the later conversion. A modern statutory phrase like "theft by deception" may merge theft categories, but the common-law MBE distinction still asks for the precise historical category.

The authority layer supports the source key. In *People v. Ashley*, 42 Cal.2d 246, 258 (1954), the California Supreme Court distinguished larceny by trick from false pretenses by the possession/title line and noted that modern consolidation of theft offenses does not erase the elements of the older forms. Source: https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/people-v-ashley-24018. Cornell's Wex summary states the same exam-relevant distinction for larceny by trick: fraud secures possession, but not title. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/larceny_by_trick. The older common-law example commonly associated with larceny by trick is *Rex v. Pear*, 1 Leach 211, 168 Eng. Rep. 208 (1779), involving possession obtained by pretense with an intent to sell. Source used for citation confirmation: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R_v_Pear.

Ruth's case fits larceny by trick. Martha intended only a one-evening loan, not a sale or title transfer. Ruth already intended not to return the Psalter and sold it that night. The official common-law answer remains larceny by trick.
