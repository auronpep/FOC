### 1. Final Question

Lydia bought a vacant parcel on a salt-marsh island just outside a harbor town known for its annual hymnwriters' festival. ### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. The county did not merely reduce Lydia's profit or limit one preferred design. On these facts, the ordinance bars all construction and leaves the parcel unable to be rented or sold at any price. That is the total-loss regulatory-takings lane.

**Gold Key:** A regulation that denies all economically beneficial or productive use of land is treated as a taking requiring just compensation, unless background property or nuisance principles already barred the proposed use. Physical occupation and title transfer are sufficient ways to create a taking, but they are not required.

**Silver Key:** Do not make "physical invasion" the gate unless the call asks about a physical taking. When the stem says the owner cannot use, rent, sell, or get value from the land, move to the total-economic-use axis.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is the dominant trap because it sounds like a clean takings rule from the physical-occupation cases. Physical occupation is a powerful shortcut when it exists, but it is not the only route to a taking. **Gold Key:** total denial of economically beneficial use can itself require compensation, so the absence of an authorized occupation does not defeat Lydia's claim.

C sells the title-transfer version of the same mistake. A formal acquisition of title is enough for a taking, but it is not necessary. The county can owe just compensation when a regulation goes so far that it effectively takes the parcel's usable value.

D is attractive because it notices that the county's conservation purpose is doing the harm. The breaker is the legal test. Regulatory takings doctrine does not ask whether the conservation objective is "important enough" in a free-floating scrutiny balance. The better answer is tied to the regulation's effect: it denied all economically beneficial use.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, credited outcome, and distractor structure are unchanged. The Fifth Amendment Takings Clause, applied to local governments through the Fourteenth Amendment, requires just compensation when private property is taken for public use. The ordinary regulatory-takings framework considers economic impact, interference with reasonable investment-backed expectations, and the character of the governmental action under **Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City**, 438 U.S. 104 (1978). But **Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council**, 505 U.S. 1003 (1992), recognizes a categorical rule for land-use regulations that deprive land of all economically beneficial or productive use, subject to background principles of property and nuisance law.

That rule confirms the supplied key. Lydia bought land for development, the county later prohibited all construction for the first time, and the parcel became worthless and unsalable. The facts do not show a preexisting nuisance or background-property-law limitation that already barred the use. The ordinance therefore effects a compensable taking.

The physical-invasion distractor remains wrong. **Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.**, 458 U.S. 419 (1982), and **Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid**, 594 U.S. 139 (2021), confirm that government-authorized physical occupations or access rights can be per se takings, but they do not make physical occupation necessary for every taking. The title-transfer distractor is wrong for the same reason: outright acquisition is sufficient, not required. The public-purpose/balancing distractor is wrong because the decisive issue is not whether habitat preservation is compelling enough, but whether the regulation has gone so far that compensation is required.
