### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Complete diversity is destroyed. The choice is **A**. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1332(c)(1), a corporation is a citizen of (i) every state by which it has been incorporated and (ii) the state where it has its principal place of business. The Supreme Court has interpreted "principal place of business" as the place where the corporation's high-level officers direct, control, and coordinate the corporation's activities — the "nerve center." Hertz Corp. v. Friend, 559 U.S. 77 (2010). Here, RiverStone is incorporated in Nevada and has its nerve center in Tennessee. Mary is a citizen of Tennessee. Mary and RiverStone share Tennessee citizenship through the corporation's principal place of business, destroying complete diversity. **Gold Key:** a corporation has DUAL citizenship for § 1332 — both the state of incorporation and the state of the nerve center count. The amount in controversy ($100,000) exceeds the $75,000 threshold, and a state-law claim is proper in diversity when § 1332 is otherwise satisfied. **Silver Key:** when the call asks whether complete diversity is present, run the dual-citizenship check on the corporate defendant first; if the corporation's nerve center is in the plaintiff's home state, diversity is destroyed.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. Yes, because RiverStone is incorporated in Nevada.**

This is the dominant trap. It sounds correct on its face — RiverStone IS incorporated in Nevada — and the student who half-remembers that corporate citizenship is "about where the company is incorporated" picks this. The breaker is the **Gold Key** — corporate citizenship is DUAL, and the principal place of business is also a citizenship. The corporation is a Nevada citizen AND a Tennessee citizen. With Mary as a Tennessee citizen, complete diversity is destroyed.

**C. No, but only because the claim is based on state law.**

This distractor reframes the no-diversity outcome as if state-law claims cannot be heard in federal court. The breaker is misframing: a state-law claim is exactly the kind of claim that § 1332 diversity jurisdiction exists to hear, provided the diversity and amount requirements are met. The amount in controversy is $100,000 (above $75,000), and the only defect is the destroyed diversity — not the state-law nature of the claim.

**D. Yes, because a corporation can have only one citizenship.**

This is a flat misstatement of the diversity statute. A corporation has DUAL citizenship under § 1332(c)(1): both its state of incorporation and the state of its principal place of business. The "only one citizenship" claim is a categorical exclusion the doctrine does not contain.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18196, a Civil Procedure item involving a plaintiff suing a corporation that is incorporated in one state and headquartered in the plaintiff's home state. The official key is C: complete diversity is destroyed because both the plaintiff and the corporation are Delaware citizens through the corporation's principal place of business.

The black-letter rule sits at 28 U.S.C. § 1332(c)(1), which provides: "a corporation shall be deemed to be a citizen of every State and foreign state by which it has been incorporated and of the State or foreign state where it has its principal place of business." The Supreme Court has interpreted "principal place of business" as the place where the corporation's high-level officers direct, control, and coordinate the corporation's activities — the "nerve center." Hertz Corp. v. Friend, 559 U.S. 77, 80-81 (2010). Before Hertz, the circuits had been split between the "place of operations" or "business activities" test and the "nerve center" test; Hertz resolved the split in favor of the nerve center test. The complete-diversity rule traces back to Strawbridge v. Curtiss, 7 U.S. 267 (1806), and applies strictly: no plaintiff may share citizenship with any defendant. State Farm Fire & Cas. Co. v. Tashire, 386 U.S. 523 (1967).

The transformation preserves the identical legal substance. The RiverStone logistics case is the analog of the HarborTech case: a plaintiff sues a corporation that is incorporated in one state and has its principal place of business in the plaintiff's home state; the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000; the claim is a state-law claim.
