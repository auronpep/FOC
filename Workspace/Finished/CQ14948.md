## 7. Full right-answer explanation (C — credited)

A mortgage debt is a single, indivisible obligation, and the lien secures the whole of it until the whole of it is paid. Peter and John bound themselves together to Stephen; Stephen's lien sits on the entire storehouse, not on two severable halves. Tendering one-half of the defaulted balance does not redeem anything — redemption requires payment of the **entire** secured debt. Because Peter never paid (or tendered) the full amount, the mortgage was never discharged as to any part of the property, and Stephen was entitled to refuse a release of Peter's undivided interest. **Gold Key:** to redeem mortgaged property the redeeming party must pay the *whole* secured debt; the lien is indivisible, so a co-tenant who pays only his share obtains a right of *contribution* against his co-tenant — never a release of his own undivided interest. **Silver Key:** both "win" choices secretly depend on the same false premise (that Peter's half can be peeled off the lien); naming that shared premise lets you cut them as a pair.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

- **A (win, because the cotenancy was in common and not joint) — DOMINANT TRAP.** The label "tenants in common" advertises *separate, undivided, alienable* shares, so it feels natural that each co-tenant can deal with his half independently — including redeeming it alone. The form of co-ownership, however, says nothing about the mortgage: the two owners signed one obligation secured by one indivisible lien on the whole parcel, and the lien does not fracture along the cotenancy lines. **Gold Key:** the indivisibility of the mortgage lien defeats this — paying a fractional share never compels a fractional release, whatever the co-ownership form.
- **B (lose, because the lender's title had been warranted by an express provision of the mortgage).** It reaches the right outcome and points to a real clause in the instrument, so it reads as the lawyerly explanation for why Peter loses. The general warranty covenant protects the *lender's* title against defects; it has nothing to do with whether Peter may redeem his half, so the reason is non-responsive to the call even though the result happens to be correct.
- **D (win, because Peter is entitled to marshalling).** "Marshalling" sounds like the precise equitable doctrine that rearranges security to protect a junior interest, and reaching for a named property remedy feels safer than plain reasoning. Marshalling applies where one creditor has access to two funds and another to only one; there are no two funds and no competing creditors here, so the doctrine is simply not in play.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

**Credited answer C is correct under the governing rule.** The equity of redemption permits a mortgagor to reclaim the property only by satisfying the *entire* secured obligation; a partial tender does not redeem and does not entitle the payor to a release. The mortgage lien is indivisible — it encumbers the whole estate until the whole debt is paid — so co-mortgagors holding as tenants in common cannot compel the mortgagee to release one undivided interest upon payment of that owner's proportionate share. The paying co-tenant's remedy is **contribution** against the non-paying co-tenant, not a release from the lender.

- **Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):** Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 3.1 (equity of redemption — redemption is accomplished by payment of the obligation secured); the common-law indivisibility-of-the-mortgage-lien doctrine.
