### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Antioch cannot rely on the January license. The answer is **B** — the later online advertising exceeded the license's time and medium scope. Appropriation of likeness is governed by the scope of the plaintiff's consent: a license to use a photo for *one printed brochure* in *January* does not authorize a *different medium* (online ads) in a *later time period* (March) for a *different product line* (new supplements). **Gold Key:** a license to use a likeness is limited by its scope — time, medium, and purpose — and use outside the agreed scope can support an appropriation claim. **Silver Key:** the call asks "Can Antioch rely on the January license?" — to answer, you compare the January license's scope (printed brochure, January) to the March use (online ads, new product line); the comparison shows the scope was exceeded.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Yes, because any consent to use a photo is permanent and unlimited.**

This is the dominant trap. The cognitive move it sells is the "once-consented-always-consented" intuition: a person who signs a license is bound forever, regardless of what the licensee does with the photo. That is the wrong move. **Gold Key:** a license to use a likeness is limited by its scope — time, medium, and purpose — and use outside the agreed scope can support an appropriation claim. The "permanent and unlimited" reading is a tiered-absolute overclaim.

**C. Yes, because the photo was flattering.**

This distractor is a misfit. The attractiveness or flattering nature of the photo is irrelevant to the appropriation analysis. The right question is whether the licensee stayed within the scope of the consent; the photo's aesthetic quality is a non-doctrinal consideration that does not authorize a scope-exceeding use.

**D. No, but only because the company stated a false fact about the instructor.**

This distractor is a misfit. The "false fact" theory belongs to *defamation* or *false light* invasion of privacy, not to *appropriation* of likeness. The facts do not require a false statement — the wrong is the unauthorized commercial use of the likeness, regardless of whether the use also makes a false statement. The right theory here is appropriation, not false light.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18676, a Torts item involving a fitness instructor who licensed her photo for one printed brochure in January, and the company used the same photo in online ads in March for a new product line. The official key is A: "No, because the later online advertising exceeded the license's time and medium scope."

Appropriation of likeness is one of the four privacy torts recognized under modern privacy law. The Restatement (Second) of Torts § 652C provides that a person is subject to liability for intrusion upon seclusion, appropriation of name or likeness, publicity given to private life, or false light publicity. Section 652E addresses appropriation specifically: a person who appropriates for his own use or benefit the name or likeness of another is subject to liability for the invasion of privacy.

The scope-of-consent rule is central to the appropriation analysis. A consent or license to use a likeness is limited by its terms — including the time period, medium, and purpose. Use outside the agreed scope is not authorized and can support an appropriation claim. The Restatement (Second) of Torts § 652F ("Limitations on liability for appropriation") confirms that consent is a defense, but the consent is construed in light of the scope of the permission granted. The Restatement's reporter notes observe that a "consent to take pictures for one purpose does not automatically constitute consent to publish them for other purposes."

Modern case law treats the scope question as one of fact: did the licensee stay within the agreed scope (time, medium, purpose)? If not, the use is unauthorized and the appropriation claim survives. A license for *one printed brochure* in *January* does not authorize *online ads* in *March* for a *different product line* — three scope dimensions (medium, time, purpose) all exceeded the agreement.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: Antioch cannot rely on the January license because the March online ads for the new product line exceeded the license's time and medium scope. Mary's appropriation claim survives.
