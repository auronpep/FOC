## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Two days before a Thursday evening handoff, Stephen accepted $12,400 from Hannah and let Hannah haul away his hand-painted Jonah puppet stage.

Earlier, Ruth and Stephen had signed a written agreement under which Ruth would buy that stage from Stephen for $9,800, with the price due at 7 p.m. Thursday at Stephen’s storage workshop. The agreement provided, and both intended, that time was of the essence.

At 7 p.m. Thursday, Ruth arrived at the workshop with a cashier’s check ready to pay. Stephen was away, so Ruth called to ask where he was. Stephen said that the stage had been sold to Hannah.

If Ruth sues Stephen for breach of contract, is Ruth likely to prevail?

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Stephen’s sale of the promised puppet stage to Hannah before Ruth’s payment time was the operative breach trigger. **Gold Key:** a party can anticipatorily repudiate not only by words, but also by a voluntary act that makes the promised performance impossible or apparently impossible. **Silver Key:** do not stop at “Yes.” The because-clause must point to the breach trigger. B does that: Stephen sold the promised item before performance was due.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells the familiar instinct that the buyer must hand over the money on the due date before suing. The breaker is sequence: Stephen’s prior transfer of the promised item is the breach trigger, so Ruth’s performance duty is not the dispositive issue.

**C.** This choice uses impossibility language, which sounds plausible because Stephen no longer has the stage. **Gold Key:** self-created inability is not an impossibility excuse; it is the repudiating act.

**D.** This choice is the dangerous one because the outcome is right. **Silver Key:** a correct “Yes” still dies if the because-clause names the wrong trigger. Notice is not the reason Ruth prevails; Stephen’s sale of the stage is.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

The tested rule is unchanged: a seller’s voluntary act before the performance date that makes the promised performance impossible or apparently impossible is an anticipatory repudiation. Restatement (Second) of Contracts §250 treats a voluntary affirmative act that renders the obligor unable or apparently unable to perform without breach as a repudiation. ([Open Casebook][1])

Because this is a sale-of-goods frame, U.C.C. §2-610 also supports the result: when a party repudiates as to not-yet-due performance in a way that substantially impairs the contract’s value, the aggrieved party may await performance, resort to breach remedies, and suspend its own performance. ([Legal Information Institute][2])

The impossibility distractor does not defeat the buyer’s claim. Restatement §261’s impracticability rule requires the performance difficulty to arise without the party’s fault; Stephen’s own sale of the promised stage created the inability. ([Open Casebook][3])
