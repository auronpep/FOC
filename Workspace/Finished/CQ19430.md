### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**A is correct.** The deed uses a role description, not Daniel’s personal name. **Gold Key:** A deed can identify a grantee by an office or role if one person fitting that description is reasonably ascertainable when the deed is delivered. **Silver Key:** Read the recipient phrase before reading the formalities traps: “to the harp instructor” points first to the person who holds that role, not automatically to the school and not back to the grantor.

Daniel was the only harp instructor at the moment of delivery, and he accepted the deed. That makes Daniel the ascertainable grantee.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B.** This choice sells the familiar “both sides must sign” contract instinct. The breaker is that a deed is a conveyance by the grantor; Daniel’s acceptance matters, but his signature is not the required validity gate. **Gold Key:** Grantee signature is not the standard deed-signature requirement.

**C.** This choice sells a clean formalities myth: if the deed does not say “Daniel,” the transfer must fail. The breaker is the Gold Key rule: a role description can be enough when one person fits it at delivery.

**D.** This choice sells the organization shortcut. The phrase names the role holder of the school, not the school itself, so the school does not become the grantee merely because its name appears inside the description.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

Lawyer-confirmation layer only: the tested rule is unchanged from the source item. The legal issue is grantee ascertainability in a deed. The credited outcome is unchanged: the person who uniquely fits the role description at delivery takes title.

Authority check: courts recognize that a grantee need not always be personally named if the deed describes the grantee so the person can be definitely or reasonably ascertained; ConocoPhillips Co. v. Hahn quotes Vineyard v. O’Connor for the proposition that a grantee “need not be named” if described and ascertainable. ([Justia][1]) Texas Property Code § 5.021 states the conveyance must be written, subscribed, and delivered by the conveyor or the conveyor’s authorized agent, which supports the grantor-signature focus and defeats the grantee-signature trap. ([Justia][2])
