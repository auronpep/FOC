### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B is the best answer.** It connects the injury to Lydia’s own premises duty. The claim is against Lydia, not Barnabas and not the manufacturer. The added fact says a reasonable inspection by Lydia would have revealed the improper swing before Mary was injured. That supplies the owner-side breach path the call is asking for.

**Gold Key:** For a business invitee, the owner’s live duty is reasonable inspection and protection against dangerous conditions the owner knows about or would discover by reasonable care. **Silver Key:** Lock the named defendant first. The answer must create liability for Lydia, not merely explain how the device failed, whether Barnabas was careful, or whether Mary paid.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Mary had paid the workshop fee before the panel struck her.**
This sells the idea that a completed payment is what turns Mary into the protected entrant. The breaker is that payment does not supply Lydia’s breach; the missing owner-linked fact is inspection-discoverability or repair.

**C. Mary’s injury resulted from a defect in the pressure mat.**
This sells the product-liability frame because a defective component sounds like the cause. The breaker is defendant-targeting: a defect in the mat points toward the product chain unless it also connects to what Lydia knew or should have discovered.

**D. Barnabas was not negligent in installing the Jericho Wall display.**
This sells the “somebody must be blameworthy” frame by focusing on the contractor. The breaker is that removing Barnabas’s negligence does not create Lydia’s negligence.

### 8. Full black-letter verification and right-answer legal reasoning

The supplied official explanation confirms that the credited path is negligence against the premises owner, not intent or strict products liability. It also states that the winning added fact is that a reasonable inspection by the owner would have disclosed the danger. 

The black-letter grounding matches the transform. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 332 treats a business visitor or public visitor as an invitee, and § 343 imposes liability when the possessor knows or by reasonable care would discover a dangerous condition, should expect invitees not to protect themselves, and fails to use reasonable care to protect them. ([Open Casebook][1]) Section 402A strict products liability is directed at one who sells a defective product while engaged in the business of selling that product; a premises owner who did not sell the injuring device is not made strictly liable merely by the device defect. ([Biotech Law][2])

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: a public-facing business visitor is injured by a dangerous premises condition; the fact most likely to create judgment against the premises owner is that reasonable inspection by that owner would have revealed the danger.
