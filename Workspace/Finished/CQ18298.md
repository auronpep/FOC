### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The court should find for **the stranger**. The tort of assault requires the defendant to act intending to cause, and thereby cause, the plaintiff to apprehend imminent harmful or offensive **contact with the plaintiff herself**. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 21 (assault — intent to cause apprehension of imminent harmful or offensive contact, and causation of such apprehension). On these facts, the stranger's conduct was directed entirely at Mary; the stranger never made any threatening gesture toward Daniel, never approached him, and never looked at him. Daniel could not have apprehended imminent contact with himself. Because the contact feared (if any) was with Mary, not with Daniel, the elements of assault are not met as to Daniel. **Gold Key:** the transferred-intent doctrine applies to battery (and to certain other intentional torts against the person), not to assault — the assault plaintiff must be the person put in apprehension of contact with herself.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. the stranger, because Daniel did not perceive an injury being inflicted on Mary.**

This is the second trap. It picks the right party (the stranger) but for the wrong reason. The "perception of injury" framing is off: the assault test is whether the plaintiff apprehended contact with **herself**, not whether the plaintiff perceived an injury to a third party. The breaker is the **call focus**: even if Daniel perceived the injury to Mary in detail, the assault claim fails because Daniel did not apprehend contact with himself. **Silver Key:** when a bystander claims assault over harm to a third party, the deciding axis is whether the bystander apprehended contact with HERSELF; perception of injury to a third party is not the test.

**C. Daniel, because the court will transfer the stranger's intent to grab Mary to Daniel's assault claim.**

This is the **dominant trap**. The transferred-intent doctrine is a real doctrine that applies when the defendant intends to commit a tort against one person but commits a different tort against a different person (e.g., intends to hit A but hits B — the intent transfers from the battery against A to the battery against B). Restatement (Second) of Torts § 16. But the transferred-intent doctrine is doctrinally limited to certain intentional torts against the person (notably battery); it does not supply a missing plaintiff-side apprehension for an assault claim. The stranger's intent to grab Mary cannot be transferred to make Daniel an assault plaintiff, because Daniel was never put in apprehension of contact with himself. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: transferred intent does not apply to assault.

**D. Daniel, because the stranger was aware that his conduct would frighten Daniel.**

This is the awareness-of-fright trap. It is true that the stranger was aware that grabbing Mary in a church fellowship hall would frighten Daniel (and anyone else watching). But the assault test is not whether the defendant's conduct was frightening to the plaintiff; it is whether the plaintiff apprehended imminent **contact with herself**. Awareness that the conduct would frighten the plaintiff is not a substitute for apprehension of contact with the plaintiff. The breaker is the **call focus**: the assault elements require plaintiff-side apprehension of contact with the plaintiff, not defendant-side awareness that the conduct would frighten the plaintiff.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18298, a Torts item involving a girl who was kidnapped and the girl's mother's claim of assault against the kidnapper. The official key is D: the court should find for the kidnapper, because the girl's mother had no reason to expect to be touched by the kidnapper.

The common-law tort of assault is committed when the defendant acts intending to cause, and thereby causes, the plaintiff to apprehend imminent harmful or offensive contact with the plaintiff. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 21. The intent element is intent to cause the specific apprehension of contact with the plaintiff; the act element is the act that causes the apprehension. The plaintiff must be the person put in apprehension; the contact feared must be with the plaintiff, not with a third party. The classic bystander pattern is the failure point: a third-party witness to violence directed at a third person is not, by virtue of witnessing the violence, an assault plaintiff. The transferred-intent doctrine is a related but doctrinally limited rule: it applies to battery (and to certain other intentional torts against the person) when the defendant intends to commit a tort against one person but commits a different tort against a different person. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 16. Transferred intent does not supply a missing plaintiff-side apprehension for an assault claim; the assault plaintiff must be the person put in apprehension. The "awareness of fright" trap is a separate failure mode: even if the defendant knows that his conduct will frighten a bystander, the bystander has no assault claim unless she apprehended contact with herself. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 21 cmt. d, e.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: the court should find for the stranger because Daniel had no reason to expect to be touched by the stranger. The stranger never made any threatening gesture toward Daniel, never approached him, and never looked at him; Daniel could not have apprehended imminent contact with himself.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. the stranger, because Daniel had no reason to expect to be touched by the stranger.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the residual answer. The deciding element is whether Daniel apprehended contact with HERSELF; Daniel did not.
2. **Student label:** Residual answer (no apprehension of contact with self).
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Assault requires the plaintiff to apprehend imminent harmful or offensive contact with herself. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 21. The stranger never made any threatening gesture toward Daniel, never approached him, and never looked at him.

### B. the stranger, because Daniel did not perceive an injury being inflicted on Mary.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice picks the right party for the wrong reason. The assault test is whether the plaintiff apprehended contact with HERSELF, not whether she perceived injury to a third party. 3. **True/responsive version:** "Even if Daniel perceived the injury to Mary in detail, the assault claim fails because Daniel did not apprehend contact with himself."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 21. The elements of assault are intent to cause, and causation of, apprehension of contact with the plaintiff. Perception of injury to a third party is not the test.

### C. Daniel, because the court will transfer the stranger's intent to grab Mary to Daniel's assault claim.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the dominant trap. The transferred-intent doctrine is a real doctrine, but it does not apply to assault. **Gold Key:** transferred intent applies to battery and certain other intentional torts against the person, not to assault.
2. **Student label:** Transferred-intent misapplication.

4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 16. The transferred-intent doctrine is doctrinally limited to certain intentional torts against the person (notably battery); it does not apply to assault.

### D. Daniel, because the stranger was aware that his conduct would frighten Daniel.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The "aware that his conduct would frighten" framing misstates the assault elements. The elements are about plaintiff-side apprehension, not defendant-side awareness.
2. **Student label:** Awareness-of-fright overclaim.
3. **True/responsive version:** "The stranger may have been aware that his conduct would frighten Daniel, but the assault elements are about whether Daniel apprehended contact with himself, not whether the stranger was aware that his conduct would frighten Daniel."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 21 cmt. d, e. The intent element is intent to cause the specific apprehension of contact with the plaintiff; defendant-side awareness of fright is not a substitute.
