### 1. Final question
Peter scheduled a private telehealth appointment with Dr. Lydia, a licensed psychotherapist, after returning from a weekend worship retreat. Before the appointment began, Peter asked his friend Barnabas to remain on the video call for prayer support, although Barnabas was not needed for treatment or to help Peter communicate with Dr. Lydia. While Barnabas remained on the call, Peter admitted that he had hidden a stolen soundboard in a storage unit. ### 7. Full right-answer explanation
Choice A is the best ruling. The psychotherapist-patient privilege protects confidential therapy communications, not every statement made somewhere inside a therapy setting. Peter chose to speak while Barnabas, a friend who was not needed for treatment or communication, remained on the call. That voluntary third-party presence defeats confidentiality as to Barnabas's testimony. Gold Key: psychotherapist-patient privilege turns on confidentiality, and an unnecessary third party who hears the statement breaks that confidentiality as to that person. Silver Key: when privilege answers split between setting and confidentiality, test who heard the statement before protecting the appointment label.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
**B.** This is the dominant trap because it grabs the strongest privilege-looking fact: the statement occurred during a licensed psychotherapist's appointment. The breaker is that the setting is only the doorway into the privilege. The communication still must be confidential, and Peter made it in front of Barnabas even though Barnabas was not needed for treatment or communication. Gold Key: the office, video session, or therapist label does not save a communication disclosed to an unnecessary third party.

**C.** This choice sells compassion as a rule. A prayer-support friend may feel naturally connected to a counseling session, but "always" is the tell. A third person preserves confidentiality only when that person is reasonably needed for treatment, diagnosis, or communication. These facts say Barnabas was not needed, so the privilege does not block Barnabas's testimony.

**D.** This choice reaches the correct overrule result but adds a condition that does not belong. Barnabas personally heard Peter's statement. The court does not need Dr. Lydia to repeat the same statement before Barnabas can testify to what he heard. Silver Key: in a right-result wrong-reason answer, cut the extra condition if the call asks about the named witness's testimony.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome remain unchanged from the source row. In a federal case, privilege claims are governed by Federal Rule of Evidence 501 unless another controlling source supplies a different rule. FRE 501 leaves federal privilege law to federal common law interpreted in light of reason and experience. Source: https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&path=/prelim@title28/title28a/node232/article5

`Jaffee v. Redmond`, 518 U.S. 1 (1996), recognized the federal psychotherapist-patient privilege under FRE 501 for confidential communications made to a licensed psychotherapist in the course of diagnosis or treatment. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/518/1/

The privilege depends on confidentiality and may be waived by the patient. Federal cases applying `Jaffee` treat disclosure of the substance of therapy communications to unrelated third parties as a waiver or as a failure of confidentiality. See, e.g., `United States v. Bolander`, 722 F.3d 199, 223 (4th Cir. 2013). That matches the source rule: a patient cannot invite an unnecessary friend to hear the statement and then use the privilege to silence that friend.

Peter spoke in Barnabas's presence, and Barnabas was not needed for treatment or communication. The statement therefore was not confidential as to Barnabas. The correct ruling is to overrule the objection as to Barnabas's testimony. Choice B is legally wrong because a therapy appointment is not enough without confidentiality. Choice C is legally wrong because friends are not categorically necessary to therapy. Choice D is legally wrong because Dr. Lydia's testimony is irrelevant to whether Barnabas heard a nonconfidential disclosure.
