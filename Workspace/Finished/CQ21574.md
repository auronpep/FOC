## Final Question

In 1998, Naomi died, leaving a will that devised her hill-country retreat tract, "to Lydia for life, and then to Barnabas." Lydia lived several states away and never took possession of the tract. In 2000, Timothy, Lydia's cousin, moved onto the tract without telling Lydia and without getting her permission. ## Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is the best argument for Barnabas. Timothy's possession could run against Lydia while she held the present life estate, because Lydia had the right to possession during her life. But Barnabas held only a future interest while Lydia was alive. He had no present right to eject Timothy until Lydia died. Gold Key: adverse possession runs only against a person who has a present right to possession. Silver Key: when a clock answer names a party, ask whether that party could have sued for possession during the years being counted. Barnabas could not sue for possession until 2023, so Timothy's long occupation before then did not cut off Barnabas's remainder.

## Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because it turns "open and notorious" into actual notice to the life tenant. The breaker is that open and notorious possession is a visibility concept, not a requirement that Lydia actually learned of the occupation. Fencing most of the tract, paying taxes, building a cabin, and living there are the kind of public possession facts the rule is looking for.

C is attractive because "tacking" sounds like a technical adverse-possession rule that might block Timothy. The breaker is that Timothy is not trying to add one possessor's time to another possessor's time. This is a one-possessor clock question, so a tacking formula answers a different problem.

D is attractive because family status can feel inconsistent with hostile possession. The breaker is that "hostile" does not mean angry or unrelated. It means possession without the true possessor's permission and against that possessor's rights. Timothy entered without Lydia's permission, so the cousin relationship does not defeat hostility.

## Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested issue is unchanged: whether adverse possession that begins during a life estate bars the remainderman when the remainderman sues after the life tenant dies. The credited outcome is unchanged: the remainderman wins because the statute of limitations did not run against him before his right to possession accrued.

Adverse possession generally requires possession that is actual, open and notorious, exclusive, continuous, and hostile for the limitations period. The limitations period runs against the person who has the present right to recover possession. A future-interest holder, such as a remainderman after a life estate, ordinarily has no present right of possession during the life estate, so the adverse-possession clock does not run against that remainderman until the life estate ends. The adverse possessor may acquire only the life tenant's possessory estate during the life tenant's life; that interest terminates when the life estate terminates.

Authority check: Maxwell v. Hamel, 138 Neb. 49, 292 N.W. 38 (Neb. 1940), states the adverse-possession period begins when both the right of entry and right of possession exist, and that a remainderman is not required to quiet title during the life estate to protect the remainder. Nebraska's official statutory annotations for Neb. Rev. Stat. section 25-21,117 summarize Maxwell the same way. Missouri's official annotations to Mo. Rev. Stat. section 516.010 likewise cite Revare v. Lee, 257 S.W.2d 676 (Mo. 1953), for the rule that limitations do not begin to run against remaindermen until they become entitled to possession at the life tenant's death. Research did not contradict the supplied official key.
