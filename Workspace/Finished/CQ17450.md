## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The UCC Statute of Frauds (UCC § 2-201) requires a writing only for contracts for the sale of goods priced at $500 or more. Lydia's contract is a contract for services — bar-prep tutoring — even though she also provides printed materials. Under the predominant-purpose test, when services are the primary purpose of the contract and goods are merely incidental, the UCC does not apply. The common-law Statute of Frauds does not require a writing for service contracts of this type. Therefore, the Statute of Frauds defense fails: the contract was not required to be in writing.

**Gold Key (GK-CONTRACTS-SOF-01):** The UCC Statute of Frauds covers only contracts for the sale of goods. If the predominant purpose is services, UCC § 2-201 does not apply — no writing required, regardless of the value of incidental materials.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A** is attractive because it states the correct $500 threshold from UCC § 2-201 — students who memorize "goods over $500 must be in writing" may latch onto the materials price and stop there. The breaker: the $500 goods trigger only applies when the contract is *for the sale of goods*. Here the contract is for tutoring services; the materials are incidental. The Statute of Frauds defense does not apply. **Gold Key GK-CONTRACTS-SOF-01** would have killed this trap: the UCC SoF covers only goods contracts, not services.

**B** is the dominant trap. It adds the plausible-sounding element of "intent at the time of contracting," which mirrors how students think about contract formation. A student who knows the SoF applies to goods >$500 may reason: "The parties intended expensive materials, so the SoF applies." The half-truth is that the $500 goods rule is real — but it never reaches a services-dominant contract. The intent element is a red herring; the threshold legal question is whether the contract is one for goods at all. **Gold Key GK-CONTRACTS-SOF-01** defeats this: the SoF doesn't apply to services contracts, period.

**D** is attractive to students who try to synthesize A and B into a "more complete" answer. It stacks two conditions (actual price + intent) and sounds lawyerly. But it suffers the same fundamental defect as A and B: it assumes the UCC SoF applies to this contract at all. It doesn't, because this is a services contract. **Gold Key GK-CONTRACTS-SOF-01** would have steered the student off this choice.

## 8. Black-Letter Legal Verification

**Rule:** UCC § 2-201(1) provides: "A contract for the sale of goods for the price of five hundred dollars or more is not enforceable by way of action or defense unless there is some writing sufficient to indicate that a contract for sale has been made between the parties and signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought."

**Scope limitation:** Article 2 of the UCC applies to "transactions in goods" (UCC § 2-102). A mixed contract containing both goods and services is governed by the UCC only if the *predominant purpose* is the sale of goods. *See* Griffith Davison, "Contracts for Services Vs. for the Sale of Goods" (2022) (predominant-purpose test determines whether UCC or common law governs a mixed contract).

**Application:** The contract between Peter and Lydia is a tutoring services contract. The printed outlines and practice materials are incidental to the service of providing bar-prep instruction. The predominant purpose is services, not the sale of goods. Therefore, UCC § 2-201 does not apply, and no writing is required.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-201(1); UCC § 2-102; UCC § 2-105(1) (defining "goods").
