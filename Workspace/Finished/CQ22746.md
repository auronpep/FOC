## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** A draft report prepared by a testifying expert is protected trial-preparation material under Rule 26. When protected material is produced in discovery, the producing party may still assert the claim by notifying the receiving party of the claim and the basis for it. After that notice, the receiving party may not use or disclose the material until the claim is resolved.

**Gold Key:** Draft expert reports are protected trial-preparation material, and Rule 26 gives the producing party a notice-based clawback step after production. The first move is notice of the claim and basis, not automatic surrender and not an immediate protective-order motion.

**Silver Key:** When the call asks whether the lawyer may still claim protection after accidental production, separate the two "yes" choices by procedure. The built-in notice procedure answers the call; a protective order is a court remedy detour.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### Choice A

**Why it is attractive:** Protective orders are real discovery tools, and the choice keeps the answer on the "yes" side. A student who remembers court control over discovery may jump to the judge before checking whether the rule itself gives a direct assertion step.

**The breaker:** The call asks whether the attorney may still claim protection for produced material. Rule 26(b)(5)(B) supplies the direct step: notify the receiving party of the claim and basis. **Silver Key:** when one answer names the rule's built-in notice step and another sends the lawyer to court first, pick the built-in step unless the stem asks for court intervention.

### Choice B

**Why it is attractive:** The word "draft" can feel less official than a final trial report, and the final expert report is the document students most often associate with expert disclosure. That makes the draft seem like it might fall outside protection.

**The breaker:** The rule runs the other way. Draft expert reports are protected trial-preparation material; the final report required for disclosure is the one likely to be produced. **Gold Key:** Rule 26 protects drafts of expert reports regardless of the form in which the draft is recorded.

### Choice D - Dominant Trap

**Why it is attractive:** Accidental disclosure feels like waiver. The choice sells a clean common-sense penalty: once the other side has the document, protection is gone.

**The breaker:** Rule 26 does not make inadvertent production an automatic waiver for this purpose. It creates a notice process: the producing party may notify the receiving party of the claim and basis, and the receiving party must return, sequester, or destroy the material or present it under seal while the claim is resolved. **Gold Key:** after production of protected material, notice keeps the protection claim alive pending resolution.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

**Current authority checked:** Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26(b)(4)(B) protects drafts of reports or disclosures required under Rule 26(a)(2), and Rule 26(b)(5)(B) allows a producing party to assert privilege or trial-preparation protection after information has been produced by notifying any receiving party of the claim and basis. The receiving party must then return, sequester, or destroy the material, must not use or disclose it until the claim is resolved, and may submit it under seal for court determination. Rule 26(c) separately authorizes protective orders for good cause, but it is not the required answer to this call.

**Authority floor:** FRCP 26(b)(4)(B), FRCP 26(b)(5)(B), and FRCP 26(c). Research source used: Legal Information Institute, Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26, https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_26. The 2025 amendment noted in the rule affects discovery-plan handling of privilege-log issues and does not change the tested notice-after-production rule.
