### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Attorney-client privilege protects confidential communications made for the purpose of obtaining or giving legal advice. The voice memo began inside that protected circle because Daniel made it for his attorney. But the privilege depends on confidentiality. When Daniel played the memo for Barnabas, a neighbor who was not part of the attorney-client relationship or needed to help counsel give legal advice, Daniel voluntarily disclosed the substance of the communication to an outsider. That disclosure destroys the confidentiality needed for the privilege.

**Gold Key:** The attorney-client privilege protects the legal-advice circle, not a message once the client voluntarily shows it to an outsider. Sharing the protected communication with a friend, neighbor, or family member for ordinary reactions waives the privilege.

**Silver Key:** For a privilege-subpoena call, ask which choice breaks the confidentiality circle. Usefulness, impeachment value, criminal flavor, and witness unavailability do not answer that first question.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A** sells the idea that the court should get the memo because Daniel can no longer testify. That feels practical, especially because subpoenas often chase unavailable evidence. The breaker is the privilege rule itself: attorney-client privilege does not expire merely because the client dies. **Gold Key:** death is not a confidentiality breaker for ordinary attorney-client communications.

**B** sells a strong impeachment instinct. A memo with inconsistent statements would be useful to cross-examine Daniel, and useful evidence feels discoverable. The breaker is that usefulness is not the privilege test. Unless confidentiality was waived or an exception applies, the memo remains protected even if it might contradict later testimony.

**D** sells the crime-flavored exception. Students remember that crime and fraud can defeat attorney-client privilege, then over-apply that memory to any lawsuit with criminal allegations. The breaker is the missing element: the communication must be made to further a future or ongoing crime or fraud. A complaint alleging past criminal behavior does not itself strip privilege from a legal-advice communication.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule.** Under Federal Rule of Evidence 501, federal privilege questions are governed by common law as interpreted in light of reason and experience unless a Constitution, federal statute, or Supreme Court rule provides otherwise. The federal common law attorney-client privilege protects confidential communications between attorney and client made for the purpose of obtaining or providing legal advice. *Upjohn Co. v. United States*, 449 U.S. 383 (1981), confirms the privilege's role in encouraging full and frank communications with counsel.

**Why the credited answer is right.** A client who voluntarily discloses a privileged communication to an outsider generally waives the confidentiality necessary for attorney-client privilege. The final variation preserves that rule: Daniel made the voice memo for counsel, but then played it for Barnabas, a non-agent neighbor, to get ordinary reactions. That voluntary third-party disclosure is the only answer choice that breaks confidentiality and makes the memo most likely subject to subpoena.

**Why the other legal theories do not defeat privilege.** Alleged criminal conduct alone does not invoke the crime-fraud exception; the communication must be in furtherance of future or ongoing illegal conduct. *United States v. Zolin*, 491 U.S. 554 (1989), recognizes the crime-fraud exception in that frame. Possible inconsistency with deposition testimony may affect impeachment value but does not answer whether the communication remains privileged. Death does not terminate the privilege; *Swidler & Berlin v. United States*, 524 U.S. 399 (1998), held that attorney-client privilege survives the client's death.
