## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** To acquire a prescriptive easement, the claimant's use must be adverse (or hostile) to the servient estate owner for the full statutory period. While Timothy used the path across Tract 2 for 13 years total, the use was *not adverse* during the first 5 years — because Peter owned both Tract 1 and Tract 2 at that time. A person cannot hold an easement against oneself; there must be both a dominant and a servient estate in different ownership for adversity to exist. The prescription clock therefore started only when Peter sold Tract 2 to Lydia 8 years ago. Eight years of adverse use falls short of the 10-year statutory period, so Timothy's prescriptive easement never vested.

> **Gold Key — Unity of Title Bars Prescriptive Clock:** A prescriptive easement requires adverse use. Use is *not adverse* while the same person owns both the dominant and servient estates. The prescription period begins only when the two estates come into different ownership. If the time between the severance of ownership and the lawsuit is shorter than the statutory period, the claimant loses.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect** — but it's the most attractive distractor because it mirrors the logic of an easement *by necessity*: if the dominant estate already has adequate access (here, the rear dirt lane), one might think no easement can exist. But this question is about a prescriptive easement, not an easement by necessity. The availability of alternative access is irrelevant to whether a prescriptive easement vested. The reason Timothy loses isn't that he has another way in — it's that he hasn't met the prescriptive period. This distractor baits students who confuse prescriptive easements with easements by necessity.

**B is incorrect** — Timothy might think that because the deed from Peter to Lydia didn't mention a right-of-way, Lydia took the land free of any easement. But prescriptive easements arise by operation of law through long use, not by express grant. They need not be mentioned in any deed. The absence of a deed reference is legally irrelevant to whether a prescriptive easement exists.

**D is incorrect** — this statement is actually backwards as a general legal proposition. Using another's land without permission is *the classic definition* of adverse (hostile) use for prescriptive easement purposes. The statement is a true-sounding truism that doesn't explain why Timothy loses. Timothy's use *was* adverse once the estates were in different hands — the problem is that it wasn't adverse for long enough. This distractor traps students who know that "adverse use" is required but don't see that the real issue is *when* the adversity clock started.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** A prescriptive easement is acquired through open, notorious, continuous, and adverse use of another's land for the statutory period. The use must be "adverse" (also called "hostile") — meaning without the owner's permission and under a claim of right.

**Authority:** The Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 2.17 (2000) states that a prescriptive easement requires use that is "adverse to the person with the right to possession of the servient estate." Under the "unity of title" doctrine, when the same person owns both the dominant and servient parcels, use of one parcel for the benefit of the other is not adverse — it is merely an owner using his own land. The prescriptive period does not begin to run until the two parcels come into different ownership. *See* 25 Am. Jur. 2d Easements and Licenses § 39; *see also* *Beech v. DeMarco*, 157 Or. App. 176 (discussing adversity requirement for prescriptive easements).

**Application:** Peter owned both Tract 1 and Tract 2 until 8 years ago. During the first 5 years of Timothy's use (years 13–8 ago), there was no adversity because one person owned both tracts. The prescription clock started only when Peter sold Tract 2 to Lydia. From that point, only 8 years elapsed before Lydia blocked the path — short of the 10-year requirement. Timothy's prescriptive easement never vested.

**Verification:** The tested rule (prescriptive easement / unity of title / adversity requirement), the legal issue (whether Timothy acquired a prescriptive easement), and the credited outcome (Timothy loses) are all unchanged from the original.
