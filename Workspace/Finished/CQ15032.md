### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** Partition in kind is the preferred remedy, but the preference is overcome when physical division would be *inequitable* — not merely when it is physically impossible. A court will order partition by sale when one co-tenant's share, though physically separable, would be rendered substantially less valuable or unusable by zoning restrictions.

**C is correct.** Although the land can be physically divided into two rectangular lots, the sister's proposal would give the brother a one-acre lot — below the zoning ordinance's two-acre minimum for a buildable lot. Conveying a non-buildable lot to the brother would be inequitable, even though the acreage ratio matches the ownership shares. The court will therefore order partition by sale, with proceeds divided one-third to the brother and two-thirds to the sister.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**D (Original A) — Dominant Trap:** This is attractive because it correctly identifies that the brother will prevail, and it sounds authoritative — "partition by sale is the preferred remedy" mirrors real legal language. The trap is that it reverses the actual preference: partition *in kind* is preferred, not partition by sale. **A (Original C):** This distractor appeals to the common-sense idea that if you can cut the land in half, you should. The physical divisibility of the land makes this tempting. ### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** When co-tenants cannot agree on the use or disposition of jointly owned property, any co-tenant has a right to seek judicial partition. Courts generally prefer partition in kind (physical division) over partition by sale. However, partition by sale is ordered when partition in kind would be inequitable — including when a resulting parcel would violate zoning restrictions and be substantially less valuable or unusable.

**Authority:** The Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 4.1 addresses partition principles, confirming that courts consider the fairness and practicality of physical division. The Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act (UPHPA), adopted in many jurisdictions, codifies the principle that partition in kind is preferred unless it would result in "manifest prejudice" to the co-owners. Zoning restrictions that render a divided parcel non-buildable constitute manifest prejudice. See also *McNamara v. Mossman*, 230 P.3d 1286, 1288 (Colo. App. 2010) (partition in kind "shall be ordered unless doing so would result in manifest prejudice to the parties").

**Application:** Here, the sister's proposed division is physically possible and matches the ownership ratio. But the brother's one-acre lot would not meet the zoning ordinance's two-acre minimum for a buildable lot. Giving the brother a lot he cannot build on is inequitable. The court will order partition by sale.
