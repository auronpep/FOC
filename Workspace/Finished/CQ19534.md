### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Martha obtained the cash by threatening immediate harm. The threat created the intimidation needed for robbery even though the threatened gunman did not exist. Robbery is larceny aggravated by force or fear, so the same taking also contains larceny. Gold Key: robbery includes larceny inside it; for one taking, the defendant may be convicted of robbery or the included larceny, but not separately convicted of both. Silver Key: the call asks what crimes Martha can be convicted of, so the last move is not "what elements appeared?" but "which conviction package survives merger?"

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A. The attraction is the lie: Martha got the clerk to part with cash by making a false statement about a rifleman. The breaker is title. False pretenses requires the victim to transfer title through deception; Stephen surrendered possession of cash under an immediate threat, so the taking is trespassory and force/fear based.

C. The attraction is strong because the stem really contains both larceny and robbery elements. The breaker is the conviction package. Gold Key: robbery is aggravated larceny, and larceny is the lesser-included offense; for a single taking, "robbery and larceny" overclaims the conviction result.

D. The attraction is that Stephen physically handed over the tote, which can feel like possession was voluntarily delivered. The breaker is entrustment. Embezzlement starts with lawful possession and then conversion; Martha never had lawful possession of the store's cash before the crime.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from source Q19534. The source metadata listed outline code 73030801 for receipt of stolen property, but the actual tested rule is robbery as aggravated larceny, plus the distinction between robbery/larceny, false pretenses, and embezzlement. The verified outline code used here is 73030400, "Crimes against Property > Robbery."

Common-law robbery is larceny from the person or presence of another by violence or intimidation. State v. Young, 462 S.E.2d 683 (N.C. Ct. App. 1995), citing State v. Smith, states the common-law formulation as a nonconsensual taking from another or the person's presence by violence or fear, and that the fear must induce the victim to part with property. Robbery is commonly treated as aggravated larceny, and larceny is included within robbery for the same taking. Carter v. United States, 530 U.S. 255 (2000), discusses the established common-law meanings of robbery and larceny; Justice Ginsburg's dissent collects the historical common-law authorities treating robbery as aggravated larceny. False pretenses requires obtaining title by a false representation, while larceny by trick or robbery involves possession without valid title. Embezzlement requires lawful possession followed by fraudulent conversion.
