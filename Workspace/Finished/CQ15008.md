## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Lydia's lot has a bakery on it — it is improved land. The common law right to lateral support imposes **strict liability** on an adjoining landowner who removes support for land in its **natural state**. However, once the neighboring land is improved with buildings, the excavating landowner is **not** strictly liable for damage to those improvements. Instead, the improved-land owner must prove that the excavator was **negligent** in the excavation. Here, Barnabas used shoring that met all applicable codes, but code compliance does not automatically negate negligence — it is evidence of due care, not a complete defense. Lydia can recover only if she can prove Barnabas failed to exercise reasonable care in excavating. The key point is that mere proof of removed support is **not enough**; negligence must be established.

**Gold Key:** Lateral support splits at the improvement line: natural-state land gets strict liability; improved land gets negligence. The building's weight is a defense to strict liability but not to negligence.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**D (dominant trap) is attractive because** strict liability for lateral support is one of the most heavily tested real-property rules, and students instinctively reach for it. The choice says Barnabas is liable simply because he removed support — which would be true if Lydia's land were in its natural state. But the presence of the bakery changes the standard to negligence. **The Gold Key unlock:** the improvement line splits the rule. Strict liability applies only to land in its natural condition; improvements trigger the negligence standard. D ignores this split entirely.

**A is attractive because** it sounds like a plausible limitation — that improving your land means giving up the right. In fact, the opposite is true: improvement does not destroy the right to lateral support. It merely changes the standard from strict liability (for the land itself) to negligence (for damage to the improvements). A flatly misstates the law.

**B is attractive because** Barnabas did comply with all safety codes, and students may assume that regulatory compliance satisfies all duties. But the common-law duty of lateral support is independent of building codes. Code compliance is evidence of due care in a negligence analysis but does not automatically absolve the excavator. B confuses regulatory compliance with the common-law standard.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

The common-law right of lateral support derives from the principle that each landowner is entitled to have her land in its natural state remain in its natural position without being caused by an adjoining owner's excavation. *See* Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 4.8 (2000); 2 Thompson on Real Property § 604 (2d ed.).

**Natural state — strict liability:** If an adjoining landowner excavates and causes the neighbor's soil in its natural condition to subside, the excavator is strictly liable. No proof of negligence is required. The right attaches to the land itself.

**Improved land — negligence standard:** When the neighbor's land carries buildings or other improvements, the excavator is **not** strictly liable for damage to those improvements. The improved-land owner must prove the excavator failed to exercise reasonable care. *See, e.g.*, Noone v. Price, 298 S.E.2d 214 (W. Va. 1982); Gorton v. Schofield, 349 N.E.2d 356 (Mass. App. 1976). The rationale is that the weight of the improvements may themselves contribute to the subsidence, and it would be unfair to impose strict liability for damage that might not have occurred but for the neighbor's decision to build.

**Code compliance:** Compliance with safety regulations is relevant evidence of due care but does not constitute a complete defense to a negligence claim.

**Authority floor:** Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 4.8 (2000) (lateral and subjacent support). The rule is well-established in case law across multiple jurisdictions.
