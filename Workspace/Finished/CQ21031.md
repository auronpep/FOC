## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Deacon Stephen was indicted for embezzling funds from his church’s building committee and was released on bail pending trial. Two weeks later, at the direction of the local police, Stephen’s close friend from the congregation, Mark, wore a hidden recording device and visited Stephen at his home. Mark repeatedly asked Stephen where he had hidden the missing committee funds, and Stephen eventually admitted the specific location. Stephen did not know that Mark was cooperating with the police.

Should the recorded admission be suppressed under the Sixth Amendment?

(A) No, because Stephen was at his own home and was not in police custody at the time of the conversation.

(B) Yes, because any statement made by a defendant after an indictment has been returned is inadmissible at trial.

(C) Yes, because a government agent deliberately elicited incriminating statements about the charged offense from an indicted defendant in the absence of counsel.

(D) No, because Mark was a private citizen and a personal friend rather than a known government official or police officer.

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

(C) is correct. The Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches once formal adversarial judicial proceedings have commenced (e.g., by indictment or arraignment). Once this right has attached, the government (and its agents) are prohibited from "deliberately eliciting" incriminating statements from the defendant regarding the charged offense without counsel being present or a valid waiver being obtained (*Massiah v. United States*). Here, Stephen had already been indicted, so his Sixth Amendment right had attached. Mark was acting as a government agent because he was wearing a wire and acting "at the direction of the local police." By repeatedly asking Stephen about the hidden funds (the subject of the indictment), Mark "deliberately elicited" the statements. Thus, the Sixth Amendment was violated. Gold Key: A Sixth Amendment violation occurs when a government agent deliberately elicits statements about a charged crime from an indicted defendant without counsel.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

(A) This choice is attractive because it correctly identifies that Stephen was not in custody, which would be relevant for a Fifth Amendment Miranda analysis. However, it is incorrect because the Sixth Amendment right to counsel (Massiah) does not require the defendant to be in custody; it only requires that formal charges have been filed and that the government deliberately elicited the statements.

(B) This choice is attractive because it reflects a general suspicion of post-indictment statements. However, it is an overclaim and incorrect because the Sixth Amendment only bars statements that are deliberately elicited by the government. Spontaneous statements or statements made to individuals who are not acting as government agents remain admissible.

(D) This choice is attractive because Mark is indeed a friend and a private citizen. However, it is incorrect because a private citizen becomes a "government agent" for constitutional purposes when they act at the behest or direction of the police. Because Mark wore a wire at the police's request to obtain information about Stephen’s crime, he was a government agent.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: Sixth Amendment Massiah Doctrine (Deliberate Elicitation). Once the Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches (upon indictment), the state may not "deliberately elicit" incriminating statements from the accused about the offense charged. This rule applies even when the defendant is not in custody and when the elicitation is done by an undercover informant acting at police direction. Mark's active questioning about the missing funds constitutes deliberate elicitation, and his cooperation with police makes him a state agent. Authority: *Massiah v. United States*, 377 U.S. 201 (1964); *United States v. Henry*, 447 U.S. 264 (1980); *Maine v. Moulton*, 474 U.S. 159 (1986). The outcome (Suppression) matches the source.

### 10. Rigor and Difficulty Preservation

The item tests the fundamental distinction between the Fifth Amendment (Miranda) and the Sixth Amendment (Massiah). The primary difficulty lies in recognizing that "custody" is not an element of a Sixth Amendment violation. The "undercover friend" fact tests the student's understanding of state agency.
