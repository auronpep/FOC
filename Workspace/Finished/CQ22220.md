### 7. Full right-answer explanation
Choice A is the only answer that lands on the right side of the privilege fight: Daniel has sued Ruth for malpractice, and Ruth's account of Daniel's instruction goes straight to whether Ruth was negligent. Gold Key: the attorney-client privilege does not block a communication that is relevant to a breach-of-duty dispute between lawyer and client. ### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
**B.** This is the dominant trap because it reaches the right outcome and sounds crisp: once the sides are fighting, confidentiality must be over. The breaker is that the privilege does not generally disappear when representation ends or litigation begins; the narrower rule is that a communication relevant to the lawyer-client breach dispute may be used in that dispute. Silver Key: in a two-"No" clash, the wrong answer is often the one that overstates the scope of the exception.

**C.** This choice sells a made-up precision test. The breaker is that attorney-client privilege does not turn on whether the statement was "necessary" to the consultation, and even a qualifying confidential statement can still come in when the client sues the lawyer over the representation. Gold Key: the malpractice-dispute exception defeats the objection without any extra necessity element.

**D.** This choice sounds close to the usual privilege formulation because the statement did concern the same matter for which Daniel hired Ruth. The breaker is that the answer stops too early: that fact may support privilege in the ordinary case, but it does not survive once the communication is relevant to the malpractice claim between Daniel and Ruth. Gold Key: an ordinarily privileged subject-matter link does not beat the breach-of-duty exception.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome remain unchanged from the source row. Common-law attorney-client privilege protects confidential communications made for the purpose of obtaining legal advice, but the client cannot use that privilege to silence the attorney when the client has put the attorney's conduct at issue in litigation. In `Hunt v. Blackburn`, the Supreme Court described the privilege and explained that, once the client put the attorney communication at issue, the client could not insist on keeping the attorney silent. `Hunt v. Blackburn`, 128 U.S. 464, 470-71 (1888): https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/128/464/

Modern codifications reflect the same breach-of-duty rule. For example, Wis. Stat. sec. 905.03(4)(c) states that there is no privilege for a communication relevant to an issue of breach of duty by the lawyer to the client or by the client to the lawyer. That codified formulation matches the common-law malpractice-defense principle tested here. https://law.justia.com/codes/wisconsin/chapter-905/section-905-03/

Daniel sued Ruth for malpractice, so Daniel's instruction not to file the quiet-title action is relevant to whether Ruth breached any duty. That is why the objection should not be sustained. Choice B is legally wrong because litigation does not generally terminate the privilege. Choice C is legally wrong because privilege has no separate necessity element. Choice D is legally wrong because, although the statement was related to the representation, that ordinary privilege point yields in a lawyer-client breach-of-duty dispute.
