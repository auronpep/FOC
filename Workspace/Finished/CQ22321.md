### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. Lydia committed larceny because she made a trespassory taking and carrying away of Peter's personal property with intent to permanently deprive him of it. Her permission was limited to feeding the fish; it did not authorize her to take the jar or destroy the tablets. The fact that Peter's possession of the tablets was unlawful does not make Lydia's taking nonlarcenous. Larceny protects possession, and the taker does not get a free pass because the victim should not have had the item.

Lydia did not commit burglary under the statute given in the stem. The statute requires a breaking and entering into another's premises for the purpose of committing larceny. Lydia used a key that Peter had voluntarily given her so she could enter the apartment. Even though she returned with a larcenous purpose, the entry was not a breaking under the supplied common-law-style burglary definition.

Gold Key: Contraband can still be "property of another" for theft purposes when it has value or is possessed by another person; the victim's unlawful possession does not license a private person to steal or destroy it.

Gold Key: When burglary requires a breaking, permission to enter with a key defeats the breaking element unless the permission was obtained by fraud or the entry was outside the permission's time/place limits.

Silver Key: Treat the two crimes as a 2x2 grid. The taking proves larceny; the key-with-permission fact blocks burglary.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law and Procedure, Other Crimes, Crimes against Property, Larceny, and the verified outline code is `73030100`.

Common-law larceny is the trespassory taking and carrying away of the personal property of another with intent to permanently deprive the possessor of it. The source item's contraband twist does not defeat larceny because the law of theft protects possessory interests; the taker cannot defend by saying the victim's possession was unlawful. `State v. Dwyer`, 383 N.W.2d 838 (Neb. 1986), held that marijuana, although contraband, could be the subject of robbery if it had value. See https://law.justia.com/cases/nebraska/supreme-court/1987/966-8.html. Robbery includes a larceny-type taking plus force, so the case confirms the exam-relevant proposition that contraband status alone does not make property unstealable. `People v. Dillon`, 34 Cal. 3d 441 (1983), likewise rejected an archaic property limitation in the marijuana-robbery setting. See https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/people-v-dillon-23300.

Burglary under the supplied statute requires breaking and entering into another's premises for the purpose of committing larceny. A breaking must be an unconsented breach of the security of the premises. `State v. Thibeault`, 402 A.2d 445 (Me. 1979), explains the common-law rule that consent to enter defeats burglary because burglary requires a trespassory entry. See https://law.justia.com/cases/maine/supreme-court/1979/402-a-2d-445-0.html. `Whitaker v. Commonwealth`, 2008 Va. App. LEXIS 1, applying `Davis v. Commonwealth`, states that use of one's own key with permission meant there was no actual or constructive breaking. See https://valawyersweekly.com/fulltext-opinions/2008/01/02/whitaker-v-commonwealth-of-va/. Cornell's Wex summary of breaking and entering is consistent: entry by a person licensed or privileged to enter is not breaking and entering. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/breaking_and_entering.

Applied here, Lydia's taking was trespassory because she had permission only to feed fish, not to take Peter's jar. Her destruction of the tablets shows intent to permanently deprive. That proves larceny. But her entry was by a key Peter gave her for apartment access, so the breaking element is missing. The transformed item preserves the original rule, issue, trap structure, and credited outcome: larceny only.
