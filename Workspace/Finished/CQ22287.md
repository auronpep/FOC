## 1. Final Question

Timothy had lived in the United States since childhood, when his family immigrated from overseas when he was eight years old. He was never naturalized. ## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** Under *Lee v. United States*, 137 S. Ct. 1958 (2017), a noncitizen defendant establishes prejudice from ineffective assistance of counsel in the guilty-plea context by showing a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's erroneous advice about deportation consequences, the defendant would have rejected the plea and insisted on going to trial. The defendant does **not** need to show a reasonable probability of acquittal at trial. The question is whether the defendant would have rejected the plea—not whether trial would have produced a better outcome.

Here, Timothy's attorney incorrectly told him that a guilty plea could not lead to deportation. Timothy relied on that advice and pleaded guilty only because of it. Under *Lee*, this establishes prejudice: but for the bad advice, Timothy would have rejected the plea and gone to trial. The strength of the government's case is irrelevant to this prejudice inquiry. Timothy has a valid ineffective assistance claim.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. No, because Timothy was not a United States citizen.**

*Attractive because:* Students who haven't internalized the scope of the Sixth Amendment may assume it protects only citizens. The fact pattern emphasizes Timothy's noncitizen status, inviting that assumption.

*Breaker:* The Sixth Amendment right to effective assistance of counsel applies to **all** persons in U.S. criminal proceedings, regardless of citizenship. *Padilla v. Kentucky*, 559 U.S. 356 (2010), and *Lee v. United States* both involved noncitizen defendants. Citizenship is irrelevant to the Sixth Amendment analysis. **(Gold Key: The Sixth Amendment protects all defendants in U.S. criminal proceedings, not only citizens.)**

**B. No, because the government's evidence against Timothy was overwhelming.**

*Attractive because:* Students instinctively think "prejudice" means "would have gotten a better result." If the evidence was overwhelming, a student reasons, Timothy would have been convicted anyway—so how was he prejudiced?

*Breaker:* Under *Lee*, the prejudice inquiry in the guilty-plea context asks whether the defendant would have **rejected the plea and gone to trial**, not whether trial would have produced a different outcome. The strength of the government's case does not defeat a prejudice showing when the defendant's decisional calculus was driven by the attorney's erroneous advice about deportation. **(Gold Key: Prejudice in the plea context = would have rejected the plea, not would have won at trial.)**

**D. Yes, because there was a reasonable probability Timothy would have been acquitted at trial.**

*Attractive because:* This answer sounds like the standard *Strickland* prejudice analysis—"but for counsel's error, the result would have been different." Students who apply the general *Strickland* framework without the *Lee* refinement will pick this.

*Breaker:* This misstates the prejudice test for the guilty-plea context. Under *Lee*, the defendant must show a reasonable probability that he would have **rejected the plea and gone to trial**—not a reasonable probability of acquittal. The question is about the decision to plead, not about the likely trial outcome. **(Gold Key: The correct test is whether the defendant would have rejected the plea, not whether he would have been acquitted.)**

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule:** *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), establishes the two-prong test for ineffective assistance of counsel: (1) counsel's performance was deficient (objectively unreasonable), and (2) the deficient performance prejudiced the defense (a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's error, the result of the proceeding would have been different).

**Plea-context refinement:** *Lee v. United States*, 137 S. Ct. 1958 (2017), held that a defendant who pleads guilty in reliance on counsel's incorrect advice about deportation consequences establishes prejudice by showing a reasonable probability that, but for the erroneous advice, the defendant would have rejected the plea and insisted on trial. The defendant need **not** show a reasonable probability of acquittal. The Court emphasized that avoiding deportation can be the determinative issue for a defendant, and the strength of the government's case does not negate prejudice when the defendant's decision was driven by counsel's bad advice on that specific point.

**Duty to advise:** *Padilla v. Kentucky*, 559 U.S. 356 (2010), held that defense counsel must inform a noncitizen client whether a guilty plea carries a risk of deportation. Failure to do so (or affirmatively giving wrong advice) constitutes deficient performance under *Strickland*.

**Authority floor:** *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984) (leading case); *Lee v. United States*, 137 S. Ct. 1958 (2017) (leading case); *Padilla v. Kentucky*, 559 U.S. 356 (2010) (leading case). Sixth Amendment, U.S. Constitution.
