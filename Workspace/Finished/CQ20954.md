## 1. Final Question
After Miranda warnings, Ruth said, "I do not want to talk." Detectives immediately stopped and returned Ruth to a holding cell. Three hours later, a different detective gave fresh Miranda warnings and asked only about a separate burglary at a church retreat. Ruth waived and confessed to that burglary. Is the later confession automatically barred by Ruth's earlier silence invocation?

## 2. Distilled Core Question
Ruth said she did not want to talk, police stopped, waited three hours, re-Mirandized her, and asked only about a separate burglary. Is the later confession automatically barred?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation
A is right because a suspect's invocation of the right to remain silent must be respected, but it is not an automatic permanent ban on all later questioning. Here, the detectives stopped immediately, let time pass, gave a fresh set of warnings, and then conducted a limited follow-up interview about a separate burglary. Under that sequence, the later waiver can be valid.

Gold Key: Michigan v. Mosley teaches that the right to cut off questioning must be scrupulously honored, not frozen forever.
Silver Key: A later interview can still count if the first questioning stopped, enough time passed, fresh warnings were given, and the renewed interview stayed narrow.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations
B is attractive because it turns the silence invocation into a bright-line permanent bar, which feels safe and easy to apply. Gold Key: Mosley does require the police to honor the suspect's choice to stop talking, but the rule is not an across-the-board forever ban. The breaker is that these detectives stopped, waited, re-Mirandized, and then asked about a separate crime.

C is attractive because it focuses on the fact that a different detective handled the second interview, which sounds like a fresh pressure point. Silver Key: the real issue is the sequence and scope of the renewed questioning, not the badge number of the officer who asked the later questions. The breaker is that the earlier invocation had already been honored, and the later interview was properly restarted.

D is attractive because it tries to collapse silence and counsel into one rule. The breaker is that an invocation of silence does have legal effect; it simply does not always create a permanent bar to later, properly renewed questioning after fresh warnings and a limited waiver.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification AND Legal Reasoning
The credited answer is correct under Michigan v. Mosley, which holds that after a suspect invokes the right to remain silent, police may later reapproach if the right to cut off questioning was scrupulously honored. The key facts here match that rule: questioning stopped right away, there was a meaningful time gap, fresh Miranda warnings were given, and the second detective kept the interview limited to a separate burglary. That makes the later confession potentially admissible rather than automatically barred.

Authority used for verification:
- [Michigan v. Mosley, 423 U.S. 96 (1975)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/423/96/)
- [State of Michigan v. Richard Bert Mosley, 423 U.S. 96 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/423/96)
- [Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/384/436)

Mosley is the controlling authority for a renewed interrogation after invocation of silence. Miranda supplies the baseline requirement that custodial interrogation be preceded by warnings and respected when the suspect asserts the right to stop. On these facts, the later confession is not automatically barred because the police honored the initial invocation and restarted the interview with fresh warnings and a narrow topic.
