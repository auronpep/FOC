### Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

Ruth's testimony is admissible for two independent reasons. First, it is admissible to impeach Stephen's credibility as a prior inconsistent statement — Stephen testified at trial that he never received overseas donations, and his private admission to Ruth directly contradicts that testimony. Impeachment use of a prior inconsistent statement is not hearsay because it is offered not to prove the truth of the out-of-court statement but to show that the witness is not credible. Second, the statement is admissible as substantive evidence under FRE 801(d)(2)(A) because it is an admission by a party-opponent — Stephen is the defendant, and his own statement is offered against him. Under the Federal Rules of Evidence, a party-opponent's own statement is defined as "not hearsay" and is admissible as substantive evidence of the facts admitted. Because both exemptions apply, Ruth's testimony is admissible both for impeachment and as substantive evidence.

**Gold Key:** A party's own out-of-court statement, when offered against that party, is not hearsay at all under FRE 801(d)(2)(A) — it is admissible as substantive evidence. This is not an "exception" to the hearsay rule; it is an exemption that defines the statement as non-hearsay. The rationale is adversarial fairness: a party cannot complain about the inability to cross-examine himself.

### Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Authority

**Governing Rule:** Federal Rule of Evidence 801(d)(2)(A).

**Text:** "A statement that is offered against an opposing party and was made by the party in an individual or representative capacity" is defined as "not hearsay." Fed. R. Evid. 801(d)(2)(A).

**Impeachment basis:** A prior inconsistent statement offered solely to impeach (not for its truth) is not hearsay under FRE 801(c) because it is not offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted — it is offered to show the witness is not credible. See Fed. R. Evid. 801(c); *Powell v. United States*, 414 A.2d 530, 533 (D.C. 1980) (admissions received as substantive evidence, not merely to contradict).

**Substantive use:** Under FRE 801(d)(2)(A), a party's own statement offered against that party is admissible as substantive evidence. The rationale is adversarial fairness — a party "can hardly object that he had no opportunity to cross-examine himself." *McCormick on Evidence* §254; *Harris v. United States*, 834 A.2d 106, 116 (D.C. 2003).

**Authority floor:** FRE 801(d)(2)(A) (rule number); *Powell v. United States*, 414 A.2d 530 (D.C. 1980); *Harris v. United States*, 834 A.2d 106 (D.C. 2003); *McCormick on Evidence* §254.

This item tests the dual admissibility of a party-opponent's out-of-court statement under the Federal Rules of Evidence. #### Question Data

**Stem:** Stephen, a missionary, was charged with perjury for having falsely testified in a prior IRS civil audit that he had never received any unreported overseas donations. At the perjury trial, Stephen again testified that he had never received the overseas funds. In rebuttal, the prosecutor called Ruth to testify that after the audit was over, Stephen admitted to Ruth privately that he had in fact received the overseas donations.

**Call:** Is Ruth's testimony in the perjury trial admissible?

**Choices:**
