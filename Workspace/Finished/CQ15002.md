# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT — Q15002

## 1. Final Question

Four years ago, a rancher and a horse-boarding operator entered into a six-year written lease of a 200-acre ranch outside town. The lease provided that if any portion of the ranch—but not all of it—was taken by eminent domain, the lease would terminate and the rancher would receive the entire condemnation award. Last spring, the county took approximately 130 acres of the ranch to extend a public highway. The operator notified the county and the rancher that an independent appraisal showed the operator's possessory interest in the remaining land was worth substantially more than the operator's remaining rent obligation, and that the operator was entitled to a share of the award. The appraisal was accurate.

In an appropriate action among the rancher, the operator, and the county as to the right of the operator to a portion of the condemnation award, for whom will the court likely find?

**A.** The operator, because the taking of leased land through eminent domain is not a breach of the rancher's implied warranty of quiet enjoyment.

**B.** The operator, because otherwise the rancher would be unjustly enriched.

**C.** The rancher, because the parties specifically agreed as to the consequences of a partial condemnation.

**D.** The rancher, because the condemnation superseded and canceled the lease.

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A lease provided that upon a partial condemnation, the lease terminates and the landlord gets the entire award. A partial condemnation occurred. The tenant's possessory interest exceeds the remaining rent. Can the tenant share in the award?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**C is correct.** The lease between the rancher and the operator expressly provided that if any portion of the ranch (but not all) was taken by eminent domain, the lease would terminate and the rancher would receive the entire condemnation award. The county took approximately 130 of the 200 acres—a partial, not total, condemnation. Under the undivided fee rule, the condemnation award is valued as a lump sum for the whole property, and the parties' contractual agreement governs how that sum is divided between them. Because the lease clearly allocated the entire award to the rancher upon a partial taking, the operator has no right to share in the proceeds, even though the operator's possessory interest may exceed the remaining rent obligation. The parties' agreement controls.

**Gold Key:** When a lease contains an express condemnation clause allocating the award between landlord and tenant, that clause governs the distribution of condemnation proceeds—even when the tenant's possessory interest exceeds the tenant's remaining rent obligation. The tenant's right to share in the award is a default rule that yields to the parties' agreement.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is attractive** because it states a true proposition of law—eminent domain is not a breach of the implied warranty of quiet enjoyment—and it correctly concludes that the operator should not prevail on a quiet-enjoyment theory. A student who recognizes that the landlord did nothing wrong might be drawn to this answer. **But the breaker is:** this answer does not address the actual dispute. The operator is not claiming a breach of quiet enjoyment; the operator is claiming a share of the condemnation award based on the value of the possessory interest. The correct answer turns on the lease provision, not on quiet-enjoyment doctrine. This is a misfit—right area, wrong issue.

**B is attractive** because the facts support the intuition: the operator's possessory interest is worth more than the remaining rent, so the rancher appears to receive a windfall. The unjust-enrichment narrative feels compelling. **But the breaker is:** where a valid, enforceable contract governs the parties' rights, unjust enrichment is unavailable. The lease expressly allocated the entire award to the rancher. The operator agreed to this term. Unjust enrichment cannot override an express contractual provision. (Silver Key: When a lease has an express term governing the very issue in dispute, unjust enrichment is displaced—the contract governs.)

**D is attractive** because it reaches the correct conclusion (the rancher wins) and invokes a superficially plausible theory—that condemnation automatically supersedes and cancels the lease. Many students know that a total condemnation terminates a lease by operation of law and may assume the same applies to a partial taking. **But the breaker is:** the reason the rancher prevails is not that condemnation automatically cancels the lease. If the lease had been silent, the operator might have been entitled to share in the award. The rancher wins here because the parties specifically agreed that upon a partial condemnation, the lease terminates and the rancher receives the entire award. The contractual provision, not the mere fact of condemnation, is dispositive. (This is the dominant trap: correct outcome, wrong reasoning.)

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** Under the Fifth Amendment, the government must pay just compensation when it takes private property for public use. When leased property is condemned, both the landlord and the tenant have compensable interests. However, the distribution of the condemnation award between landlord and tenant is governed by their lease agreement. Where the lease contains an express condemnation clause specifying how the award is to be divided, that clause controls. *See* 2A Nichols on Eminent Domain § 5.02[3][a] ("The rights of the landlord and tenant as between themselves, with respect to the condemnation award, are determined by the provisions of the lease."); *Motiva Enterprises, LLC v. McCrabb*, 248 S.W.3d 211, 214 (Tex. App.—Houston [1st Dist.] 2007) ("Unless a lease provides that it terminates upon condemnation, the tenant will recover compensation for the unexpired term."); *Fort Worth Concrete Co. v. State*, 416 S.W.2d 518, 521 (Tex. Civ. App.—Fort Worth 1967) ("A tenant whose lease provides for its termination upon the taking of the leased premises for a public use, is entitled to no compensation when it is condemned.").

**Application:** The lease here expressly provided that upon a partial condemnation, the lease would terminate and the rancher would receive the entire award. The county condemned approximately 130 of 200 acres—a partial taking. The lease provision is triggered. The operator's possessory interest, even if it exceeds the remaining rent obligation, does not override the parties' agreement. The court will enforce the lease as written.

**Quiet enjoyment:** The taking of leased land by eminent domain is not a breach of the landlord's implied warranty of quiet enjoyment because the landlord did not cause the taking. *See* Restatement (Second) of Property: Landlord & Tenant § 6.1 cmt. d. This proposition is true but irrelevant to the award-allocation question.

**Unjust enrichment:** Unjust enrichment is an equitable remedy that is unavailable when a valid, enforceable contract governs the parties' rights. *See* *Ingham ex rel. Cobalt Asset Mgmt., L.P. v. Thompson*, 88 A.D.3d 607 (1st Dep't 2011). The lease's express condemnation clause governs, precluding an unjust-enrichment claim.

**Verified:** The tested rule (express condemnation clause governs award allocation), the legal issue (partial condemnation of leased land), and the credited outcome (landlord wins per the lease) are all unchanged from the original.

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

#### Program Frame

**Subject:** Real Property — Rights in Land
**Topic:** Actual Takings
**Difficulty:** Core
**C3 Lane:** Hard Structural — CLASH phase decides
**Verdict:** C3_SOLVE

This item tests whether students can identify that an express lease provision governing condemnation awards controls over default rules. The answer is structurally visible from the stem: the lease says what happens upon a partial condemnation, a partial condemnation occurred, the provision is triggered.

#### Student Access Test

A student who has learned the C3 method and the Gold Key for condemnation clauses in leases can solve this item without outside legal doctrine. The key structural move is: (1) identify the lease provision, (2) confirm the triggering event matches the provision's condition, (3) apply the provision. The distractors are all eliminable through visible structural failures (wrong issue, displaced by contract, misstated rule).

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Four years ago, a rancher and a horse-boarding operator entered into a six-year written lease of a 200-acre ranch. The lease provided that if any portion of the ranch (but not all) was taken by eminent domain, the lease would terminate and the rancher would receive the entire condemnation award. Last spring, the county took approximately 130 acres to extend a public highway. The operator notified the county and the rancher that an independent appraisal showed the operator's possessory interest substantially exceeded the remaining rent obligation, and that the operator was entitled to share in the award. The appraisal was accurate.

**Call:** In an appropriate action among the rancher, the operator, and the county as to the right of the operator to a portion of the condemnation award, for whom will the court likely find?

**Choices:**
- **A.** The operator, because the taking of leased land through eminent domain is not a breach of the rancher's implied warranty of quiet enjoyment.
- **B.** The operator, because otherwise the rancher would be unjustly enriched.
- **C.** The rancher, because the parties specifically agreed as to the consequences of a partial condemnation.
- **D.** The rancher, because the condemnation superseded and canceled the lease.

#### Distilled Core Question

A lease provided that upon a partial condemnation, the lease terminates and the landlord gets the entire award. A partial condemnation occurred. The tenant's possessory interest exceeds the remaining rent. Can the tenant share in the award?

#### Call and Prediction

**Call type:** "For whom will the court likely find?" — a standard outcome call. No negative-stem trap, no EXCEPT/LEAST inversion.

**Prediction before looking at choices:** The lease has an express provision. A partial condemnation occurred. The provision says the rancher gets everything. The rancher should win, based on the lease agreement.

#### Trigger Facts

1. **Express clause (visible):** Lease provides that partial condemnation → lease terminates + rancher gets entire award.
2. **Partial taking (visible):** County took 130 of 200 acres (not all).
3. **Windfall fact (visible):** Operator's possessory interest exceeds remaining rent.
4. **Government actor (visible):** County exercised eminent domain.

#### Governing C3 Lane

**CUT** → eliminate the choice that answers a different question.
**CLASH** → when three survive, name the axis (what legal basis governs?) and apply the splitting fact (the lease has an express provision).
**CALL** → not needed; CLASH resolves all distractors.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A:**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice is about quiet enjoyment. The question is about who gets the condemnation award. These are different legal issues. Even if the legal proposition is true, it doesn't answer the call.
2. **Student label:** True law, wrong issue.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice stating that the operator cannot prevail on a quiet-enjoyment theory AND explaining why the operator also cannot share in the award.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Eminent domain is indeed not a breach of quiet enjoyment (Restatement (Second) of Property: Landlord & Tenant § 6.1 cmt. d), but this is irrelevant to the award-allocation question.

**Choice B:**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The facts make the unjust-enrichment story tempting (the rancher gets a windfall). But the lease has an express provision giving the rancher the entire award. When a contract governs, you can't use equity to override it.
2. **Student label:** Feels right, but contract governs.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice explaining that unjust enrichment is unavailable when a valid contract governs the specific issue.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** A valid, enforceable contract precludes an unjust enrichment claim. The lease's express condemnation clause controls.

**Choice C:**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The lease says exactly what happens upon a partial condemnation. A partial condemnation happened. The provision is triggered. The parties' agreement controls.
2. **Student label:** The lease provision controls.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the correct answer.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Under the undivided fee rule, the parties' lease agreement governs distribution of the condemnation award. 2A Nichols on Eminent Domain § 5.02[3][a].

**Choice D:**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice gets the right answer (rancher wins) but for the wrong reason. Condemnation doesn't "automatically supersede" a lease—if it did, the lease provision would be unnecessary. The rancher wins because of the lease clause, not because of some automatic legal effect.
2. **Student label:** Right party, wrong reason.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice correctly identifying that the lease provision (not automatic legal effect) is the basis for the rancher's right to the award.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** A partial condemnation does not automatically terminate a lease unless the lease so provides. Here, the lease does provide—but that is a contractual choice, not an automatic legal consequence.

**If student chose B:** The student followed moral intuition instead of the contract. Recovery: When a lease has an express term governing the dispute, the contract controls. Unjust enrichment is an equitable fallback for when there's NO contract. Practice: always check for an express contract term before reaching for equitable remedies.

**If student chose D:** The student knew the landlord wins but couldn't identify the correct reasoning.
