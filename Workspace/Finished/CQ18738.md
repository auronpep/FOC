### 1. Final question
Stephen, a potter, kept a stall in a crowded market. A local ordinance prohibited merchants from extending stall awnings out over the public lane during the busy daytime market hours; the ordinance was enacted solely to keep the lane clear so that loaded carts and pack-animals could pass freely. In violation of the ordinance, Stephen stretched his awning out over the lane. That afternoon, a sudden and unexpected hailstorm piled hail onto the outstretched awning, and the unexpected weight tore it loose, so that it dropped onto Hannah, a shopper passing beneath, breaking her arm. Hannah sued Stephen to recover for her injury. At trial, the only evidence Hannah offered as to negligence was Stephen's admission that he had violated the ordinance. At the conclusion of the evidence, both parties moved for a directed verdict. How should the trial judge proceed?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)
D is correct. Hannah put in no evidence of negligence except Stephen's admitted ordinance violation. That ordinance existed solely to keep the lane clear for carts and pack-animals — to move traffic — not to protect passersby from awnings dislodged by freak weather. The violation therefore falls outside the law's protective purpose and is no evidence of negligence at all. And because the hailstorm was sudden and unexpected, nothing else in the record shows that erecting the awning was unreasonable. With no evidence raising a jury question on negligence, the court must direct a verdict for Stephen. **Gold Key:** a statutory or ordinance violation counts toward negligence only when the law was meant to guard against the type of harm that occurred and the class of person harmed; outside that purpose the violation is legally irrelevant. **Silver Key:** when the plaintiff's only proof of negligence is a violation, read the four choices as two "send it to the jury" against two "directed verdict," then test the law's stated purpose — if it does not fit the harm, the violation drops out, no evidence is left, and you cut both "to the jury" choices and the plaintiff's directed-verdict choice.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because students are taught that breaking a safety rule is at least "some evidence" of negligence the jury can weigh, so an admitted violation feels like an automatic ticket past a directed verdict. The breaker is that a violation does that work only when the law was designed to protect against this type of harm and this kind of plaintiff; this ordinance existed solely to keep the lane clear for traffic, not to protect people from falling awnings, so the violation is irrelevant and there is nothing for the jury to consider. **Gold Key:** before a violation may count, ask what the law was for — wrong type of harm or wrong class means the violation is worth nothing. (Dominant trap — preserved with care.)

B is attractive because an admitted violation feels conclusive: he broke the law, so surely the injured plaintiff wins as a matter of law. The breaker is that even a qualifying violation does not hand the plaintiff a directed verdict — causation and the effect of the violation remain in play — and this violation does not qualify at all, because it falls outside the ordinance's protective purpose; "because of the admitted violation," full stop, is an overclaim.

C is attractive because a broken arm under a collapsed awning looks like carelessness, inviting the intuition that a jury could simply infer someone was negligent. The breaker is that the stem flags the hailstorm as sudden and unexpected, so the awning's failure shows nothing about unreasonable conduct; the bare fact that the awning was up and then fell is not evidence of a breach, and an inference of negligence cannot rest on it.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule, the legal issue, and the credited outcome are unchanged from source row 18738. Negligence per se: an unexcused violation of a statute or ordinance establishes the standard of conduct (or, in jurisdictions treating it as evidence, may be considered by the jury) ONLY if the enactment was designed to protect against the type of harm that occurred and the injured plaintiff is within the class of persons the enactment was meant to protect. See Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical & Emotional Harm § 14 ("An actor is negligent if, without excuse, the actor violates a statute that is designed to protect against the type of accident the actor's conduct causes, and if the accident victim is within the class of persons the statute is designed to protect."); Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 286, 288 (a court will not adopt a legislative standard whose purpose is found to be the protection of an interest or against a harm other than the one the plaintiff suffered); Gorris v. Scott (1874) L.R. 9 Ex. 125 (statute requiring pens for animals at sea was to prevent the spread of disease, not to prevent animals from being washed overboard; harm outside the statute's purpose defeated liability). Here the ordinance's sole purpose was to keep the market lane clear for traffic; the harm — a passerby struck by an awning dislodged by freak hail — is outside that purpose, and Hannah is not within a class the traffic-clearance ordinance was meant to protect. The violation therefore neither establishes negligence per se nor supplies evidence of negligence. The only other circumstance, the awning's collapse, resulted from a sudden and unexpected hailstorm, so no common-law breach can be inferred. With no evidence creating a jury question on negligence, the defendant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, i.e., a directed verdict. Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only): Restatement (Third) of Torts § 14; Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 286, 288; Gorris v. Scott, L.R. 9 Ex. 125 (1874). Research confirms the supplied official key (original C / new D); nothing in the transform changes the credited outcome. Negligence per se is not on the doctrinal-currency hotlist, so no currency flag applies. bank_validation_verdict: PASS.

### 2. The 17-section student case study
**program_frame**  
C3 case study on negligence per se and its protective-purpose limitation. The item teaches that an ordinance violation evidences negligence only when the law was designed to protect against the type of harm that occurred and the class of person harmed; an ordinance enacted solely to move traffic does not support negligence when the harm is a falling awning, so with no other evidence of breach the court directs a verdict for the defendant.

**distilled_core_question**  
When a plaintiff's only evidence of negligence is the defendant's admitted violation of an ordinance enacted solely to keep a public way clear for traffic, and the injury instead resulted from a sudden, unexpected natural event, may the case go to the jury, or must the court direct a verdict for the defendant?

**call_and_prediction**  
The call is "How should the trial judge proceed?" Prediction: students gravitate to the ordinance-as-evidence choice (A — the dominant trap) because they have learned that any safety-statute violation is at least "some evidence" of negligence, or to the "violation means the plaintiff wins" choice (B). The resolving signal is the ordinance's stated purpose — to move traffic — which has nothing to do with a falling awning.

**trigger_facts**  
- The ordinance was enacted solely to keep the lane clear for traffic (its protective purpose).  
- The violation is admitted and is the only evidence of negligence offered.  
- The hailstorm was sudden and unexpected (no foreseeable breach).  
- The harm — a passerby struck by a falling awning — is outside the ordinance's purpose.
