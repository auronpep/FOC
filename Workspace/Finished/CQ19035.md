## 1. Final Question

Lydia owned a small cedar cottage near a lakeside Bible-study retreat and rented it to visiting families a few weekends each year. After a late drive through heavy fog, she stopped at one of several nearly identical cottages at night, thinking it was hers. If Lydia is charged with burglary, which of the following arguments most accurately describes her legal position?

A defendant enters the wrong dwelling at night under an honest mistake and plans to take property she honestly believes is hers. Does that mistake defeat burglary specific intent?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

A is the best answer. Burglary requires the specific intent to commit a felony inside the dwelling at the time of entry. Gold Key: for a specific-intent crime, an honest mistake of fact can defeat the required mental state even if the mistake was careless or unreasonable. Lydia believed she was entering her own cottage and retrieving her own handbells. That belief, if credited, negates the larcenous intent and the burglary-specific intent. Silver Key: when two answers say "not guilty," choose the one that names the operative mental-state defect, not the one that merely restates the conclusion.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

B sells the completed-act move. Forcing the window supplies a breaking fact, but burglary also needs the required intent at entry. The actus reus cannot carry the whole crime when the specific intent is missing.

C sells a near-right shortcut. It reaches the right result, but it explains the position too generally and misses the reason the felony intent fails: Lydia's honest mistake made the planned taking non-larcenous in her mind. Silver Key: in a two-not-guilty clash, pick the answer that identifies the specific-intent mistake rule.

D sells the ordinary-negligence instinct. A reasonable person might have checked the cottage more carefully, but burglary is a specific-intent crime. Gold Key: the honest mistake matters even if it was unreasonable, so objective carelessness is not the legal test.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. Burglary at common law requires breaking and entering the dwelling of another at night with intent to commit a felony therein. Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575 (1990), identifies burglary's generic core as unlawful or unprivileged entry into a building with intent to commit a crime. Model Penal Code section 2.04(1) states the general mistake principle: ignorance or mistake is a defense when it negatives the required purpose, knowledge, belief, recklessness, or negligence. People v. Hendrix, S265668 (Cal. Aug. 22, 2022), reflects the same point in a burglary setting: a genuinely held mistaken belief can negate the specific criminal intent required for burglary even when the mistake is unreasonable. Lydia's belief that she was entering her own cottage and retrieving her own handbells negates the larcenous intent needed for burglary, even if the mistake was unreasonable.
