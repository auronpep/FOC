## 6. Full right-answer explanation

**A is correct.** Paul made a clear anticipatory repudiation on March 4. Ruth did not erase that repudiation by asking him to reconsider on March 22. Paul still had a possible retraction window, but that window closed when Ruth materially changed position in reliance on the repudiation by conveying the parcel to Naomi on July 30. Paul's October 15 tender came too late, so Ruth's refusal to close was not a breach of the January 10 contract.

**Gold Key:** A repudiating party can retract only before the injured party materially changes position in reliance on the repudiation or indicates that the repudiation is final.

**Gold Key:** Asking the repudiating party to perform or retract is not a waiver of the repudiation.

**Silver Key:** Build the timeline before reading the reasons: repudiation, request to reconsider, material reliance, attempted retraction.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B.** This answer sells a fairness-notice instinct: Paul may feel that Ruth should have warned him before selling the parcel to Naomi. The breaker is that the retraction cutoff is Ruth's material reliance, not Ruth's notice of a pending sale; no separate notice requirement protects a buyer who has already repudiated.

**C.** This answer sounds like ordinary closing law because land-sale closings usually involve concurrent exchange of price and deed. The breaker is that the closing-condition frame is no longer the live issue once Paul's repudiation became unretractable after Ruth's material reliance; the correct reason is late retraction, not Ruth's nonacceptance of tender.

**D.** This is the dominant trap. It attracts students who treat Ruth's March 22 request as forgiveness or election to keep the contract alive. **Gold Key:** an injured party does not waive a repudiation merely by urging the repudiating party to perform or retract; Ruth could ask Paul to reconsider without losing the right to rely on his repudiation if he stayed silent.

## 8. Full black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row. The source item tests whether a repudiating buyer can revive a land-sale contract after the seller has materially relied on the repudiation by conveying the land to another buyer. The answer remains no.

This is a common-law Contracts item, not a UCC sale-of-goods item. Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 253 treats a qualifying repudiation as a breach that can discharge the injured party's remaining duties. Restatement section 256 provides the retraction rule: a repudiation can be nullified by retraction only if the injured party receives the retraction before materially changing position in reliance on the repudiation or indicating that the repudiation is final. Restatement section 257 confirms that urging performance or retraction does not change the effect of the repudiation.

The legal research supports the official key. H2O's Restatement pages identify sections 253 and 256 as the governing Restatement provisions for the effect of repudiation and nullification of repudiation. The Supreme Court in *Mobil Oil Exploration & Producing Southeast, Inc. v. United States*, 530 U.S. 604 (2000), cites Restatement section 256 for the before-material-change retraction rule and section 257 for the point that urging performance is not waiver. Cornell's UCC section 2-611 page states a parallel sale-of-goods rule, but it is used here only as confirmation of the same timing concept, not as governing law for land.
