### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

The thermal-stress test records are relevant — they tend to show what the company knew about the kiln's safety before selling it. They are not privileged. Attorney-client privilege protects only confidential communications made to an attorney for the purpose of obtaining legal advice. These test records were created by the company in the ordinary course of business before any litigation was contemplated; they were not prepared to communicate with an attorney. Choice C is therefore wrong. Work product protection covers materials prepared by or for a party in anticipation of litigation, and the work-product privilege specifically shields an attorney's mental impressions. These records are neither — they are pre-existing business documents, not litigation materials, and they contain test data, not an attorney's thoughts. Choices A and B are therefore wrong. Because the records are relevant and no privilege applies, the court should require production. **Gold Key:** Pre-existing business records do not become privileged merely because they are later handed to an attorney. ### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. No, since they are privileged as an attorney's work product.**

This choice is attractive because the records were indeed turned over to an attorney, and students may conflate "given to a lawyer" with "work product." The breaker is that work product doctrine protects materials *prepared in anticipation of litigation* and, for the attorney's own work, the attorney's *mental impressions, conclusions, and legal theories*. These test records were generated in the ordinary course of business before the kiln was even sold — they are raw data, not litigation preparation, and they contain no attorney mental impressions. **Gold Key:** Work product requires anticipation of litigation at the time the material was created. Pre-existing business records are never work product.

**B. No, since they are privileged as materials prepared for litigation.**

This choice tempts students who focus on the fact that the records were used in the criminal trial and handed to the attorney "in preparation for trial." The breaker is timing: the records were created *before the kiln was marketed* — long before any litigation was anticipated. Materials prepared for litigation must be created *because of* or *in anticipation of* litigation. Pre-existing documents do not transform into litigation materials simply because they are later used in court. **Gold Key:** The critical date for work-product analysis is when the document was *created*, not when it was later used.

**C. No, since they are privileged as a confidential communication to an attorney.**

This is the dominant trap. The stem explicitly states the company "turned the test records over to its attorney," which sounds like a confidential communication. Students who focus on the act of handing documents to a lawyer — without asking *why* the documents were created — will pick this. The breaker is that attorney-client privilege protects communications *made for the purpose of obtaining legal advice*. These records were created as routine business/quality-control documents, not to communicate with an attorney. The fact that they were later shared with counsel does not retroactively make them privileged communications. **Gold Key:** A document is privileged only if it was *created* for the purpose of communicating with an attorney for legal advice — not merely because it was *later handed* to one.

### 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Attorney-Client Privilege:** Protects confidential communications made by a client to an attorney for the purpose of obtaining legal advice. *See* Upjohn Co. v. United States, 449 U.S. 383 (1981) (establishing the scope of corporate attorney-client privilege). The privilege extends to corporate communications but only where the communication was made for the purpose of securing legal advice. Pre-existing business documents — records created in the ordinary course of business — are not privileged merely because they are transmitted to an attorney. *See Fisher v. United States*, 425 U.S. 391 (1976) (the act of producing pre-existing documents does not make them privileged).

**Work Product Doctrine:** Under FRCP 26(b)(3), materials prepared in anticipation of litigation by or for a party or its representative are protected from discovery. The doctrine covers two categories: (1) ordinary work product (documents and tangible things prepared in anticipation of litigation) and (2) opinion work product (attorney mental impressions, conclusions, opinions, and legal theories). Pre-existing business records created before any litigation was contemplated are not "prepared in anticipation of litigation" and thus are not work product. *See* Hickman v. Taylor, 329 U.S. 495 (1947) (establishing the work product doctrine); *see also* FRCP 26(b)(3).

**Key application:** The test records here were created before the kiln was marketed — they are pre-existing business records. They were not created for the purpose of communicating with an attorney (killing attorney-client privilege) and were not created in anticipation of litigation (killing work product). The fact that they were later handed to an attorney does not change their character.
