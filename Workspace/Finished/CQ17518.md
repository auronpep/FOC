## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** Publication in slander does not require intent to broadcast to a third party. If the defendant should have foreseen that the statement would be overheard by others—such as shouting in a shared office with thin walls and other workers nearby—publication is satisfied negligently.

**C is correct.** Timothy's claim is slander per se because Peter's accusation—"You stole that money"—charges Timothy with criminal conduct (theft). Slander per se eliminates the need to prove special damages. The only live element is publication. Peter shouted the accusation in a workshop office separated from other craftsmen by a thin partition. He should have foreseen the statement would be overheard. That negligent publication is enough. Timothy prevails.

**Silver Key:** When the call asks whether the plaintiff "will prevail" in a slander suit, lock the four defamation elements first. If the statement is slander per se (criminal accusation), cross off special damages. Then hunt for the one element the facts actually test—here, publication. Don't let irrelevant facts (how the plaintiff got to the location, subsequent job, offer to rehire) pull you off the element the question is actually probing.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is attractive** because it sounds reasonable that an honest belief should protect someone from liability. Students who conflate defamation with malice-based torts or who overgeneralize the "truth is a defense" rule may gravitate here. **The breaker:** For a private individual on a matter of private concern, falsity is presumed and the plaintiff need not prove the defendant knew the statement was false. Good-faith belief in the truth of the accusation is not a complete defense—at most it may bear on fault in jurisdictions requiring negligence, but it does not bar the claim. **Gold Key:** A private-figure plaintiff on a private matter does not need to prove the defendant acted with knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard; good-faith belief does not defeat the action.

**B is attractive** because students may think "publication" requires an intent to spread the statement to others. If Peter only wanted Timothy to hear it, a student might reason there was no publication. **The breaker:** Publication can be negligent, not only intentional. Shouting a defamatory statement in a space where others are present and can hear it satisfies publication if the defendant should have foreseen the overhearing. Peter's subjective intent to limit the audience is irrelevant. **Silver Key:** Publication in slander includes negligent communication to a third party—intent to broadcast is not required.

**D is attractive** because the facts do support that Peter lured Timothy to the office under a false pretext (to "discuss the matter" rather than to actually hear his side). Students who spot this may think it strengthens Timothy's case. **The breaker:** How Timothy came to be in the office is irrelevant to any element of slander. The slander claim turns on the defamatory statement, publication, and damages—not on the circumstances that brought the plaintiff to the location. Timothy prevails, but not because of the inducement.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Authority

**Elements of defamation (slander):** (1) Defamatory language; (2) of or concerning the plaintiff; (3) published to a third person; (4) damages to reputation. For slander, the plaintiff must ordinarily prove special (pecuniary) damages unless the statement qualifies as **slander per se**.

**Slander per se categories** (majority/common-law rule): (i) accusation of criminal activity; (ii) statement imputing misconduct in the plaintiff's trade, profession, or business; (iii) statement imputing unchastity or sexual misconduct; (iv) statement that the plaintiff has a loathsome or contagious disease. *See* Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 570–574 (1977).

**Publication:** The defamatory statement must be communicated to a third party. Publication may be intentional or negligent. A defendant who speaks loudly enough that others in the vicinity can overhear has published the statement, even if the defendant did not intend for anyone other than the subject to hear. *See* Restatement (Second) of Torts § 577 (publication to one third person suffices); § 577A (negligent publication).

**Private figure / private concern:** When the plaintiff is a private individual and the statement involves a matter of private concern, the plaintiff need not prove "actual malice" (knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth). Falsity is presumed. *Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc.*, 418 U.S. 323 (1974). A growing number of states require at least negligence even for private-figure claims, but good-faith belief does not automatically satisfy a negligence standard if the defendant failed to investigate before speaking.
