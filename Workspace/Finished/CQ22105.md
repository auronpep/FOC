Hannah and Timothy, longtime friends from a Bible study group, owned a private lakeside cabin parcel as joint tenants. ### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. A joint tenant's interest disappears at death, and the surviving joint tenant owns the whole property by survivorship. Gold Key: in a lien-theory jurisdiction, one joint tenant's mortgage does not sever the joint tenancy; if the mortgaging joint tenant dies before foreclosure, the lien attached to that tenant's interest disappears with that interest. Silver Key: when foreclosure comes after the mortgagor's death, ask what property interest still exists for the lien to grab.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because Timothy signed a mortgage that purported to cover the whole cabin parcel. The breaker is ownership power: Timothy could not make Hannah's survivorship interest vanish, and his own liened interest disappeared when he died.

D is attractive because the bank gave Hannah a chance to step into the payments, which sounds procedurally fair. The breaker is that an offer to assume debt does not create collateral after the mortgagor's joint-tenancy interest has vanished.

C is attractive because Hannah's lack of notice feels like the reason she should be protected. The breaker is that notice is not the deciding rule; survivorship defeats the lien because Timothy's interest ended at death.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited outcome is unchanged: Covenant Bank may not foreclose. In a joint tenancy, each joint tenant has a right of survivorship. In a lien-theory jurisdiction, a mortgage by one joint tenant is treated as a lien on that tenant's interest, not as a conveyance that severs the joint tenancy. If the mortgaging joint tenant dies before foreclosure or severance, the mortgagor's interest is extinguished, the survivor takes the whole, and the lien has no surviving interest to attach to. Hannah's lack of notice and the bank's later assumption offer do not supply foreclosure authority.

Lawyer-confirmation authority: Harms v. Sprague, 105 Ill. 2d 215, 473 N.E.2d 930 (1984), held that a mortgage by one joint tenant did not sever the joint tenancy under lien theory and did not survive the mortgagor's death as a lien on the surviving joint tenant's property. The court reasoned that the surviving joint tenant became owner by survivorship and the mortgaging joint tenant's property right was extinguished at death.
