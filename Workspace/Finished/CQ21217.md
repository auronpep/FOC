## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

A Christian planner company copied Ruth's smiling photo from a public community-garden post and printed it on checkout-counter bookmarks that said, "Use our prayer planner and look this peaceful." Ruth never consented. She is not famous. The bookmark was complimentary and did not imply any false fact. Is Ruth likely to prevail on an appropriation claim against the company?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A business used a non-famous person's photo in advertising without consent, with no false statement or reputational harm. Does the person have an appropriation claim?

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Gold Key: appropriation is about unauthorized use of identity for another's benefit, especially advertising. Ruth's photo was used to promote the company's planner, and Ruth did not consent. The ad being flattering does not defeat the claim. Ruth's lack of fame does not defeat the claim. No false statement is required.

Silver Key: in an appropriation call, lock onto identity plus business advantage plus no consent before chasing defamation-style facts.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because celebrity examples are common in right-of-publicity questions. The breaker is the word "only." Appropriation protects a person's name or likeness from unauthorized use for another's benefit; the claimant need not be famous.

B is the dominant trap because the flattering tone and absence of reputational harm sound like they should make the ad harmless. The breaker is that reputational harm is not the target element. Gold Key: appropriation turns on unauthorized use of identity for benefit, not on whether the message was insulting.

D is attractive because false-light and defamation questions often ask whether the statement was false. The breaker is the "only because" reason. This stem says there was no false fact, and appropriation does not require falsity.

### 9. Black-Letter Verification And Legal Reasoning

The credited answer is legally consistent with the original. The tested rule is unchanged: unauthorized use of a person's name, likeness, or identity for the defendant's own use or benefit can create appropriation liability, especially when the use appears in advertising or commercial promotion.

Lawyer-confirmation authority: Restatement (Second) of Torts section 652C states the appropriation rule for using another's name or likeness for one's own use or benefit. Cornell Wex also describes the right of publicity as preventing unauthorized commercial use of an individual's name, likeness, or other recognizable identity aspects. Sources checked: https://cyber.harvard.edu/privacy/Privacy_R2d_Torts_Sections.htm and https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/publicity.

The result remains the same because the company used Ruth's photo to sell or promote its planner without consent. The favorable tone, lack of celebrity status, and absence of falsity do not defeat the appropriation claim.
