7. **Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)**

Choice B is the correct answer because Rule 610 bars using a witness's religious beliefs or opinions to attack or support credibility. The whole point of Mary's question is to suggest that Daniel's beliefs make him less likely to tell the truth under oath. Gold Key: once religion is being used as a credibility weapon, the inquiry is out. Silver Key: in a two-yes/two-no array, first kill the yes answers that let religion into the credibility analysis at all, then take the no answer that states the full bilateral rule rather than only half of it. Because the objection is proper, the court will not require Daniel to answer.

8. **Full wrong-answer explanations**

The breaker is that the oath-sincerity framing is still just a way of attacking credibility through religious beliefs. Gold Key: Rule 610 does not open a side door for religion-based attacks just because counsel says the question is about the oath.
- **C.** This choice feels close because it reaches the correct bottom-line result and notices one half of the rule. - **D.** This choice sounds plausible because rank inside the organization can feel like a reason the beliefs matter more. The breaker is that Rule 610 contains no exception for religious leaders, officers, or prominent members. The witness's title changes nothing.

9. **Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer**

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome remain the same: a witness's religious beliefs or opinions are inadmissible when offered to attack or support credibility. Federal Rule of Evidence 610 states that "[e]vidence of a witness's religious beliefs or opinions is not admissible to attack or support the witness's credibility." Rule 603 separately requires an oath or affirmation designed to impress the duty to testify truthfully on the witness's conscience, but Rule 603 does not authorize counsel to probe religion in order to argue that the oath means less to this witness. Here, Mary's cross-examination question tries to show that Daniel's religious beliefs make him less truthful or less bound by the oath, so the court should sustain the objection and refuse to require an answer. Choice A is wrong because the oath rationale is still a credibility attack through religion. Choice D is wrong because a witness's position in the group does not create a Rule 610 exception. Choice C reaches the right result but gives an incomplete reason, because the question is being used to attack credibility. Authority floor: Fed. R. Evid. 610; Fed. R. Evid. 603.
