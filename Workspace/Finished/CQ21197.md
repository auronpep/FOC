## 1. Final Question

Hannah worked in the quarry at a state labor camp. After a fight broke out among the workers, camp guards pulled Hannah from her bunk, shackled her wrists to a bolt on the wall of a windowless storage room, and told her she would stay there until the warden's investigators were done asking her about a new theft ring. The investigators questioned her for four hours without giving Miranda warnings, and Hannah confessed.

Should Hannah's confession be suppressed under Miranda?

## 6. Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** The Miranda custody test is functional, not formal. An inmate IS in Miranda custody for a NEW interrogation when the setting adds restraints comparable to formal arrest for that investigation. Ordinary prison life ≠ Miranda custody. But shackling to a wall in a locked room + "you're not leaving until we're done" = restraints beyond ordinary incarceration = custodial interrogation. No warnings → suppress.

Hannah was already confined, yes. But the guards didn't just walk up to her bunk and ask questions. They pulled her out, shackled her to a wall, locked her in a windowless room, and told her she couldn't return until the investigators finished. That's not ordinary prison life — that's arrest-level restraint layered on top of incarceration. Four hours of questioning without warnings. Suppress.

## 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**New A (original B): "Yes, because all questioning of incarcerated persons requires counsel to be present"** — This has a kernel of truth (the result is "yes, suppress") but the reasoning is wrong. The issue is custodial interrogation without warnings, not the mere fact of incarceration. This overclaim will attract students who know the right outcome but can't articulate the right reason. **Gold Key:** The trigger is custodial interrogation, not incarceration status. Ordinary prison interactions don't require warnings.

**New C (original A — CORRECT):** Explained above.

**New D (original C): "No, because Hannah was already confined at the labor camp before the questioning began"** — This is the colloquialism trap. It feels right: "She's already a prisoner, so how can she be 'in custody'?" But the law doesn't work that way. **New B (original D — DOMINANT TRAP): "No, because Miranda warnings are required only in police stationhouse settings, not labor camps"** — This is the flat misstatement. ## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule:** Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), requires warnings before custodial interrogation. "Custody" is determined by whether, under the totality of the circumstances, a reasonable person would have felt free to terminate the encounter and leave. For inmates already serving a sentence, the Supreme Court has held that ordinary prison life does not constitute Miranda custody, but when prison authorities add restraints comparable to formal arrest for a new interrogation, Miranda custody is established.

**Authority:**
- **Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966):** Established the warning requirement for custodial interrogation.
- **Mathis v. United States, 391 U.S. 1 (1968):** Miranda applies to prison interrogations. An inmate questioned about a new crime while incarcerated is entitled to Miranda warnings if the interrogation is custodial.
- **Howes v. Fields, 565 U.S. 499 (2012):** Clarified that imprisonment alone does not equal Miranda custody. The relevant question is whether the interrogation added restraints comparable to formal arrest beyond those ordinarily associated with imprisonment. The Court held that a reasonable person in the inmate's position would not necessarily feel deprived of freedom of action in the same way as a person arrested on the street, but that added restraints (isolation, physical restraint, explicit threats of continued confinement) can tip the analysis toward custody.

**Verification against source:** The source question's credited answer (A → "Yes, suppress because added restraints made it custodial") is fully consistent with Howes v. Fields and Mathis. The legal substance (Miranda custody for inmates = added restraints comparable to formal arrest) and credited outcome (suppress) are UNCHANGED.
