## Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The banker's testimony is admissible as circumstantial evidence of Barnabas's guilt. Under the Federal Rules of Evidence, relevant evidence is admissible unless excluded by another rule. Relevant evidence is evidence having any tendency to make the existence of any fact that is of consequence to the determination of the action more or less probable than it would be without the evidence (FRE 401). The fact that Barnabas deposited $235,000 while earning only $68,000 per year has a tendency to make it more probable that he embezzled the $195,000 than it would be without that evidence. The publishing house is offering the testimony to prove that Barnabas had possession of funds far exceeding his legitimate income, which is circumstantial evidence supporting the embezzlement claim. It is for the trier of fact to decide how much weight to give the evidence and whether the deposits came from legitimate sources. 

**Silver Key: Read the offered purpose and the call together.** The stem explicitly states the banker is called "to show that... Barnabas had deposited $235,000." This is an offer of substantive circumstantial evidence of the fact in issue (possession of the money), not an offer to impeach the denial Barnabas just gave. When evidence is offered to prove an element (here, that the defendant had more money than his salary), a student must resist the pull of the fresh denial and evaluate the evidence under the correct theory of admission.

## Full wrong-answer explanations

**A (original C — 403 prejudice).** This choice is attractive because the evidence is obviously damaging to Barnabas and a student may leap to "too prejudicial" without checking the actual 403 standard. The breaker is that the evidence is highly probative of the central fact (unexplained wealth supporting embezzlement) and its prejudicial effect does not *substantially* outweigh that probative value. 403 requires a strong showing of unfair prejudice, confusion, or waste of time; ordinary damage to the opponent's case is not enough. The testimony is classic circumstantial evidence that the rules of evidence are designed to admit.

**C (original B — impeachment, dominant trap).** This is the strongest wrong answer because the denial was just elicited from Barnabas on the stand, so the deposits feel like they "catch him in a lie" and therefore impeach. The breaker is that the banker's testimony is not being offered as impeachment at all, and it would not qualify as proper impeachment even if offered for that purpose. It does not qualify as a prior inconsistent statement (it is not a statement by Barnabas), bias, or other impeachment category. More importantly, the publishing house is calling the banker to prove the substantive fact of the deposits as circumstantial evidence of guilt, not to attack Barnabas's credibility as a witness. The choice reaches a superficially plausible theory with the wrong legal reasoning and the wrong offered purpose.

**D (original D — legitimate sources / weight).** This choice is attractive because it feels fair and common-sense: "but the money could have come from anywhere — inheritance, gifts, side work." The breaker is that relevance under FRE 401 is not defeated by the existence of possible innocent explanations. The deposits still have a tendency to make embezzlement more probable (a person with $235k in deposits on a $68k salary is more likely to have taken the money than one who did not). It is for the trier of fact to decide what weight to give the evidence and whether the alternative explanations are persuasive. Admissibility is a lower threshold than sufficiency or ultimate persuasion.

## Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested black-letter rule is the definition of relevant evidence under FRE 401 and the principle that relevant evidence is admissible (FRE 402) unless another rule excludes it. The credited outcome is that the banker's testimony is admissible as circumstantial evidence because the deposits make embezzlement more probable.

Governing authority: Fed. R. Evid. 401 ("Evidence is relevant if: (a) it has any tendency to make a fact more or less probable than it would be without the evidence; and (b) the fact is of consequence in determining the action."). The Advisory Committee Notes to Rule 401 expressly state that "the variety of relevancy problems is coextensive with the ingenuity of counsel in using circumstantial evidence as a means of proof" and that the rule is designed as a broad guide. Courts and commentators consistently hold that evidence of a defendant's possession of large sums of money or deposits far exceeding known legitimate income is relevant circumstantial evidence in embezzlement, theft, and fraud cases; any innocent explanation goes to weight, not admissibility. See, e.g., the principle applied in numerous federal and state cases involving financial crimes where "unexplained wealth" evidence is admitted.

The original question and this variation test identical doctrine with identical outcome: the evidence is relevant and admissible; the "could be legitimate" argument and the "prejudicial" argument fail; the "impeachment" theory is incorrect because the evidence is offered for its substantive value and does not fit impeachment categories. Research confirms the official key and explanations are correct under current FRE 401. No doctrinal drift or superseding authority affects this core relevancy definition.
