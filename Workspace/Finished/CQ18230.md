# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

When Daniel bought a small olive-press building and upstairs apartment near a Christian retreat district, he borrowed part of the purchase price from Covenant Bank. At the same closing where Daniel received title, he signed a promissory note payable to Covenant Bank and secured it with a mortgage on the building.

Later, Daniel sold the building to Lydia, who took the property subject to the mortgage and assumed the mortgage. Eighteen months after that, Lydia sold the building to Barnabas, who also took the property subject to the mortgage and assumed the mortgage. The next year, Barnabas sold the building to Ruth, who took the property subject to the mortgage but did not assume the mortgage.

For a year, Ruth made the payments on the note to Covenant Bank. Then a highway reroute sharply reduced foot traffic near the building, the property value dropped, and Ruth stopped making payments. Daniel's mortgage contained a clause expressly permitting later buyers to take the premises "subject to the mortgage" and/or to "assume the mortgage."

If Covenant Bank sues Daniel, Lydia, and Barnabas on the note, from which of them is Covenant Bank entitled to recover?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

The original mortgagor signed the note, two later buyers assumed the mortgage, and the last buyer took only subject to the mortgage. Which sued parties are personally liable on the note?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Daniel is personally liable because he signed the original note. Lydia and Barnabas are personally liable because each bought the mortgaged property and assumed the mortgage. An assumption is not just taking property with a lien still attached; it is a promise to pay the mortgage debt. A later sale to another buyer, even another buyer who also assumes, does not release an earlier personal obligor unless the lender agrees to a release or novation. Ruth's later payments do not erase Daniel's, Lydia's, or Barnabas's promises. Covenant Bank may recover from all three sued parties.

Gold Key: "Assume" means personal liability. "Subject to" means the land remains burdened, but the buyer does not personally promise to pay. Each person who signs the note or assumes the mortgage stays personally liable until the lender releases that person.

Silver Key: Build a liability ledger, not an ownership chain. Add Daniel when he signs the note; add Lydia and Barnabas when each assumes; do not subtract someone merely because the property is sold again.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because it keeps Daniel, who signed the note, and Barnabas, the last buyer who expressly assumed before Ruth. The trap treats Barnabas's later assumption as if it replaced Lydia's earlier assumption. The breaker is that later assumption adds another personal obligor; it does not release an earlier assumer without the lender's release.

C is attractive because Barnabas is the nearest person in the chain who assumed the mortgage before Ruth stopped paying, and Ruth did not assume. The trap makes "last assuming owner" do all the work. The breaker is that Daniel remains liable on the note, and Lydia remains liable because she also assumed the mortgage.

D is attractive because the bank is suing on the note, and Daniel is the person who originally signed it. The trap stops the liability ledger at the original borrower. The breaker is that Lydia and Barnabas each made their own personal promise by assuming the mortgage, so the bank can recover from them too.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited outcome is unchanged: the bank can recover from the original note maker and from both buyers who assumed the mortgage. In mortgage transfers, a buyer who takes property "subject to" a mortgage takes the land burdened by the mortgage but does not become personally liable for the debt merely by taking title. A buyer who "assumes" the mortgage makes a personal promise to pay the mortgage debt and becomes personally liable. Later transfers do not discharge earlier personal liability unless the lender releases the obligor or agrees to a novation.

Lawyer-confirmation authority: Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 5.1 recognizes the assumption-versus-subject-to distinction and the transferor-liability rule; § 5.1(b)(2) preserves transferor personal liability to the extent it existed before the transfer. See also Struble Brush v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A., No. H-10-5016, Document 100 (S.D. Tex. Feb. 27, 2013), discussing Restatement § 5.1 in this transfer-liability framework: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/texas/txsdce/4:2010cv05016/849620/100/

This item is not on the doctrinal-currency hotlist. The research agrees with the supplied key, so the transformed item earns PASS.
