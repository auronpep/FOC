A private company that stages ticketed Christian arts events signed a written production-services contract for a Psalm 23 drone-light show. The opening recital stated:

> “This Agreement, between Esther Events, LLC (hereafter called ‘Sponsor’), and Manna Media, Inc., and Lydia, its President (hereafter called ‘Producer’), witnesseth:”

Manna Media, Inc., later became insolvent and failed to produce the show. Esther Events sued Lydia individually for breach. At trial, Lydia offered evidence from negotiations before the contract was signed that only Manna Media, Inc., was to be legally responsible for producing the show.

If the court finds the written contract to be completely integrated, is Lydia’s proffered evidence admissible?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A completely integrated contract ambiguously lists an officer with a company, and the officer offers pre-signing negotiations to show only the company was bound; is the evidence admissible?

D. Yes, because the evidence would not contradict either the recital or the way Lydia signed the contract.

## 6. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.** The writing creates an identity problem: the recital groups the company and Lydia together as “Producer,” and Lydia’s signature appears without a separate “By” line while still describing her as president. The offered negotiations are not being used to add a new duty or contradict a stated term. They are being used to explain who the ambiguous writing made responsible.

**Gold Key:** A complete integration blocks prior negotiations used to add or contradict terms, but it does not block prior negotiations used to explain an ambiguity in the writing.

**Silver Key:** First label the proffer’s purpose. Here the purpose is interpretation of signer capacity, not addition of a side term.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This is the dominant trap. It sells the strong classroom memory that a complete writing stays inside its “four corners.” The breaker is the word **solely**: the answer turns integration into an absolute bar and erases the ambiguity exception. **Gold Key:** ambiguity evidence may explain the meaning of even an integrated writing.

**B.** This answer is attractive because prior understandings can sound like an attempt to change the legal effect of a signature. The breaker is the frame: Lydia is not offering the negotiations to change a clear signature; she is offering them to interpret an unclear signer-capacity setup.

**D.** This answer is attractive because “does not contradict” is a real parol-evidence checkpoint. The breaker is that non-contradiction is not enough in a complete-integration question; the responsive reason is ambiguity, not merely the absence of contradiction. **Silver Key:** in a two-yes/two-no array, pick the answer that gives the correct permission reason, not just the same result.

The rule is unchanged. A completely integrated agreement generally prevents prior or contemporaneous negotiations from adding to or contradicting the writing, but prior negotiations may be used to establish the meaning of the writing. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 214(c) states that prior or contemporaneous negotiations are admissible to establish the meaning of a writing whether or not the writing is integrated; § 213 and § 215 supply the integration/contradiction limits. ([LexisNexis][1]) Cornell’s Wex entries state the same exam-level distinction: the parol evidence rule bars outside evidence used to contradict or vary a complete writing, while ambiguity permits courts to use extrinsic evidence to determine meaning. ([Legal Information Institute][2])

The credited outcome is therefore stable. Lydia’s proffer is admissible because it explains the ambiguous signer-capacity language. It is not offered as an extra promise and not offered to contradict a clear term.
