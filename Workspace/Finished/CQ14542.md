## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Naomi, the owner of a private Bible-themed escape-room company, entered into a written agreement with Barnabas, a well-known puzzle-room designer respected for his unusual clue trails, in which Barnabas agreed, for a fixed fee, to design the puzzles and prop sequence for Naomi's new "Road to Emmaus" room and, upon Naomi's approval of the plan, to build and install the room accordingly. The agreement was silent as to assignment or delegation by either party.

Before beginning the work, Barnabas sold his puzzle-room business to Paul under an agreement in which Barnabas assigned to Paul, and Paul agreed to complete, the Naomi-Barnabas contract. Paul, also an experienced puzzle-room designer of excellent repute, advised Naomi of the assignment and supplied her with information confirming both Paul's financial responsibility and past commercial success. If Naomi allows Paul to perform and approves his puzzle plan, but Paul fails without legal excuse to complete the room as agreed, against whom does Naomi have an enforceable claim for breach of contract?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A customer contracts with a designer, the designer delegates and assigns the contract to a skilled successor who assumes the work, the customer allows the successor to begin and approves the plan, and the successor breaches. Can the customer sue the original designer, the successor, or both?

### 3. Final answer choices (A-D, post-shuffle)

A. Paul only, because Barnabas's duty to Naomi was discharged when Barnabas obtained a skilled puzzle-room designer to perform the Naomi-Barnabas contract.

B. Either Barnabas, because his agreement with Paul did not discharge his duty to Naomi, or Paul, because Naomi was an intended beneficiary of the Barnabas-Paul agreement.

C. Paul only, because Naomi was an intended beneficiary of the Barnabas-Paul agreement, and Barnabas's duty to Naomi was discharged when Naomi permitted Paul to do the work and approved Paul's puzzle plan.

D. Barnabas only, because Barnabas's agreement with Paul did not discharge his duty to Naomi, and Paul made no express promise to Naomi.

### 4. Correct answer

**B**. Original key: **D**.

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B** is correct. Naomi may sue Barnabas because Barnabas's sale, assignment, and delegation to Paul did not by itself release Barnabas from the original contract. A delegating obligor remains on the hook unless the obligee agrees to accept a substituted obligor in satisfaction of the original duty.

Naomi may also sue Paul. Paul promised Barnabas that Paul would complete the very performance Barnabas owed Naomi. That promise was made for Naomi's direct benefit, so Naomi is an intended beneficiary of the Barnabas-Paul agreement and may enforce Paul's assumed duty.

**Gold Key:** delegation plus assumption is not a novation. The original obligor remains liable unless the obligee agrees to a substituted contract that releases the original obligor.

**Gold Key:** when a delegatee promises the delegating obligor to perform the duty owed to the original obligee, the original obligee is an intended beneficiary of that promise.

**Silver Key:** split the answer into two gates. Gate one asks whether Barnabas was released; gate two asks whether Paul assumed a duty enforceable by Naomi. Both gates are yes for liability, so the "either" answer survives.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Paul only, because Barnabas's duty to Naomi was discharged when Barnabas obtained a skilled puzzle-room designer to perform the Naomi-Barnabas contract.**

This answer is attractive because Paul is skilled, reputable, financially responsible, and ready to do the same work. The breaker is the discharge jump. A skilled substitute may make performance acceptable, but skill alone does not release Barnabas from his contract duty. **Gold Key:** delegation plus assumption is not a novation unless Naomi agrees to release Barnabas.

**C. Paul only, because Naomi was an intended beneficiary of the Barnabas-Paul agreement, and Barnabas's duty to Naomi was discharged when Naomi permitted Paul to do the work and approved Paul's puzzle plan.**

This is the dominant trap. It keeps the true Paul-liability half, then treats Naomi's permission and plan approval as if those facts automatically release Barnabas. The breaker is the missing substituted-contract release. Naomi can allow Paul to perform without agreeing that Barnabas is discharged. **Gold Key:** customer cooperation with the delegatee is not the same thing as a novation. **Silver Key:** when a choice says "only," test whether it has killed the other defendant cleanly.

**D. Barnabas only, because Barnabas's agreement with Paul did not discharge his duty to Naomi, and Paul made no express promise to Naomi.**

This answer is attractive because it correctly keeps Barnabas liable and notices that Paul never spoke a promise directly to Naomi. The breaker is the express-promise myth. Naomi does not need Paul to make a separate express promise to her if she is the intended beneficiary of Paul's promise to Barnabas. **Gold Key:** an intended beneficiary may enforce the promisor's duty created by the contract made for that beneficiary's benefit.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row. The issue is the combined effect of delegation/assumption and third-party beneficiary enforcement. The verified outline code is `53070100`, listed in `OUTLINE_CODES_COMPLETE.md` as `Nonparties to Contract > Third-Party Beneficiary Contracts`.

Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 318(1) allows an obligor to delegate performance unless delegation is barred by public policy or the contract, and Section 318(3) provides that neither delegation nor a contract by the delegatee to assume the duty discharges the delegating obligor unless the obligee agrees otherwise. Restatement Section 279 explains substituted contracts, and the comments identify a substituted contract that brings in a new party as a novation. On these facts, Naomi allowed Paul to perform and approved the plan, but she did not agree to release Barnabas or accept Paul in satisfaction of Barnabas's duty, so Barnabas remains liable.

Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 302 defines intended beneficiaries, and Section 304 states that a promise creates a duty in the promisor to an intended beneficiary who may enforce that duty. Paul promised Barnabas to complete the same work Barnabas owed Naomi; recognizing Naomi's right to enforce Paul's assumed duty effectuates the purpose of the Barnabas-Paul agreement. The source key is therefore confirmed: Naomi has an enforceable claim against either Barnabas or Paul.
