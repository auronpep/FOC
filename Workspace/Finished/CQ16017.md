### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), an officer who reasonably suspects criminal activity may briefly detain a person and, if the officer reasonably believes the person may be armed and dangerous, conduct a limited pat-down (frisk) of the outer clothing **for weapons only**. The purpose of a *Terry* frisk is officer safety — to discover weapons that could be used against the officer. It is not a general search for evidence or contraband.

Here, the officer told Timothy she was "checking for the stolen cross." She was not looking for weapons; she was looking for contraband (the stolen property). A pat-down for contraband exceeds the permissible scope of a *Terry* frisk and constitutes an unlawful search under the Fourth Amendment. Because the handgun was discovered during an unconstitutional search, it must be suppressed under the exclusionary rule.

**Gold Key:** A *Terry* frisk is a search for **weapons only**. If the officer's purpose is to find contraband or evidence, the pat-down is not a valid *Terry* frisk — it is an unlawful search, regardless of what is found.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect** because reasonable suspicion justifies a brief investigative stop and questioning, but it does not justify a full search of the person. The Fourth Amendment requires that any warrantless search be strictly limited in scope to its permissible purpose. Here, even assuming the officer had reasonable suspicion to stop Timothy, that suspicion alone could not justify a pat-down for stolen property. A frisk is permitted only when the officer has reasonable suspicion that the person is **armed and dangerous** — not merely suspected of a crime. This choice confuses the standard for a stop with the standard for a frisk.

**B is incorrect** (Dominant Trap) because it assumes the pat-down was "lawful." The pat-down was only lawful if it was conducted for weapons under *Terry v. Ohio*. The officer explicitly stated she was checking for the stolen cross — contraband, not a weapon. A *Terry* frisk authorizes a search for weapons to protect officer safety; it does not authorize a search for evidence of a crime. By reframing this contraband search as a "lawful pat-down," this choice tempts students who remember that *Terry* allows pat-downs but forget the critical limitation: the frisk must be for weapons. **Gold Key:** A *Terry* frisk is a search for weapons only — not contraband.

**D is incorrect** because under the plain-feel doctrine (analogous to the plain-view doctrine), if a *Terry* frisk is valid, the officer may seize contraband or weapons discovered during the pat-down even if it is not what the officer was originally looking for. The problem is not that the officer found the "wrong" item — it is that the frisk itself was invalid from the start because it was conducted for contraband rather than weapons. This choice uses attractive "wrong-item" reasoning that would be correct if the frisk were valid, but the frisk was not valid.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** Fourth Amendment; *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968); *Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366 (1993).

**Authority:**
- **Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968):** Established that an officer may conduct a limited pat-down of a suspect's outer clothing for weapons when the officer has reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous. The Court emphasized that a frisk "is a reasonable search under the Fourth Amendment" only when it is "confined in scope to an intrusion reasonably designed to discover guns, knives, clubs, or other hidden instruments for the assault of the police officer." Id. at 29–30.
- **Minnesota v. Dickerson, 508 U.S. 366 (1993):** Confirmed that the *Terry* frisk is limited to weapons. The Court held that if an officer, during a lawful weapons frisk, feels an object whose identity as contraband is "immediately apparent" (plain feel), it may be seized. But the Court also held that the officer may not continue to manipulate or search for contraband beyond what is authorized by the weapons frisk. The search in *Dickerson* was unconstitutional because the officer continued exploring the suspect's pocket after determining it contained no weapon.
- **Sibron v. New York, 392 U.S. 40 (1968):** Decided the same day as *Terry*, the Court held that an officer who placed his hand in a suspect's pocket and discovered narcotics had conducted an impermissible search because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion for a frisk and, in any event, the search exceeded the scope of a weapons frisk.

**Application:** The officer frisked Timothy to find the stolen cross — contraband, not a weapon. Because the frisk was not conducted for weapons, it was not a valid *Terry* frisk. The handgun was discovered during an unconstitutional search and must be suppressed under the exclusionary rule (*Weeks v. United States*, 232 U.S. 383 (1914); *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643 (1961)).

**Verified:** The tested rule (Fourth Amendment *Terry* frisk scope), legal issue (whether a pat-down for contraband is a valid *Terry* frisk), and credited outcome (motion to suppress granted) are **unchanged** from the original.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "denied, because the officer had reasonable suspicion that Timothy stole the cross."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice confuses two different legal standards. The officer may have had reasonable suspicion to *stop* Timothy, but that doesn't mean the *search* was justified. The choice overclaims what reasonable suspicion authorizes.
2. **Student label:** "Confuses stop standard with search standard."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "denied, because the officer had probable cause to search Timothy." (Even this would be wrong on these facts, but it would at least name the correct standard for a search.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Under *Terry v. Ohio*, reasonable suspicion of criminal activity justifies a brief investigative stop. A frisk requires additional reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous. Mere reasonable suspicion of a crime does not justify a search.

**Choice B: "denied, because the handgun was discovered during a lawful pat-down search."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice assumes the pat-down was "lawful" — but was it? The officer said she was checking for the stolen cross. A *Terry* frisk is only lawful for weapons. If the officer was searching for contraband, the pat-down was not a valid *Terry* frisk.
2. **Student label:** "Assumes the pat-down was lawful."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "denied, because the handgun was discovered during a lawful pat-down search **for weapons**." (Adding "for weapons" would make the choice factually accurate and legally correct — but it would also make it inconsistent with the stem, which says the officer was checking for the cross.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** A *Terry* frisk is valid only when conducted for weapons. The officer stated she was checking for the stolen cross (contraband), so the pat-down was not a valid *Terry* frisk. The handgun was discovered during an unlawful search.

**Choice C: "granted, because the officer searched for contraband rather than weapons."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice correctly identifies the legal defect: the officer was searching for contraband (the stolen cross), not for weapons. A *Terry* frisk is only for weapons. Searching for contraband is an unlawful search.
2. **Student label:** "Correct — frisk was for contraband, not weapons."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This choice IS true and responsive. It correctly states the legal rule and applies it to the facts.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Correct under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). A frisk must be limited to a search for weapons. A pat-down for contraband exceeds the scope of a *Terry* frisk and violates the Fourth Amendment. The handgun must be suppressed.

**Choice D: "granted, because the officer was looking for the cross and not the handgun."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice proves the wrong element. The officer's *subjective target* doesn't determine whether the search was lawful. The legal question is whether the *type of search* (pat-down for weapons vs. pat-down for contraband) was authorized — not whether the officer found what she was looking for.
2. **Student label:** "Proves the wrong element (officer's target)."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "granted, because the officer's pat-down was for contraband rather than for weapons." (This would correctly identify the legal defect — which is exactly what Choice C says.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Under the plain-feel doctrine (*Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366 (1993)), if a *Terry* frisk is valid, items discovered during it are admissible even if not what the officer was seeking. The problem is not that the officer found the "wrong" item — it is that the frisk itself was invalid.
