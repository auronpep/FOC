### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Mary's claim against Paul is a **crossclaim**. Mary and Paul are co-defendants in Hannah's suit, and the claim arises out of the same parking-lot collision that is the subject of the original action. Under FRCP 13(g), a crossclaim may be asserted by a party against a co-party when the claim arises out of the same transaction or occurrence. **Gold Key:** a counterclaim runs against an *opposing* party (typically a plaintiff), not a co-defendant. The moment the second defendant is already a party on the same side of the "v.," the claim is a crossclaim. **Silver Key:** when the second defendant is already named in the caption, do not reach for impleader (Rule 14) — impleader exists precisely to pull a *non-party* in.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. A required-party motion, because Paul's fault must be adjudicated.**

This is the procedural-posture mislabel. The student hears "fault must be adjudicated" and reaches for Rule 19. The breaker is that Paul is already a named co-defendant; Rule 19 is about *joining* an absent person whose joinder is required for complete relief, not about labeling an existing party's claim. Mary's claim is a labeled pleading, not a motion to add someone.

**C. A counterclaim, because it is a claim asserted by a defendant.**

This is the dominant trap. The label "counterclaim" sounds like the generic name for "any claim a defendant asserts," and most students have written the word "counterclaim" more often than "crossclaim." The breaker is the **Gold Key**: a counterclaim runs against an *opposing* party — usually a plaintiff. Paul is not opposing Mary; they are co-defendants on the same side of Hannah's case. The label flips to crossclaim. **Gold Key:** crossclaim = co-party; counterclaim = opposing party.

**D. An impleader claim, because Paul may be liable for the plaintiff's damages.**

This is the Rule 14 wrong-frame. The student confuses "third-party practice" (a procedural device for asserting derivative liability against a non-party) with a simple labeled claim between two existing parties. The breaker is that Paul is already a named co-defendant; impleader exists to bring in someone *outside* the case (e.g., Mary's own insurance carrier or a non-party tortfeasor) who has not yet been sued. When the second defendant is already in the caption, impleader is not the right device.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17179, a Civil Procedure item involving two co-defendants sued for the same accident, where one defendant asserts a reimbursement claim against the other. The official key is B (crossclaim).

The governing rule is Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 13(g): "A pleading may state as a crossclaim any claim by one party against a co-party asserted in the same action or a related action. The crossclaim may include a claim that the co-party is or may be liable to the crossclaimant for all or part of a claim asserted in the action against the crossclaimant." A counterclaim, by contrast, is defined in Rule 13(a)–(h) as a claim by a party against an *opposing* party. Impleader is governed by Rule 14, which allows a defending party to "bring in" a person "who is or may be liable to the impleading party for all or part of the plaintiff's claim against the impleading party"; the device presupposes the third party is not already a party. Rule 19 governs the compulsory joinder of "required" persons whose absence would impair the court's ability to grant complete relief.

On these facts, Mary and Paul are co-defendants in Hannah's single suit. The reimbursement claim arises from the same parking-lot collision — the same transaction or occurrence that Hannah pleads. Rule 13(g) is therefore satisfied. The label is crossclaim. The transformed outcome is identical to the original: Mary's claim against Paul is a crossclaim.

### A. A crossclaim, because it is a claim by one co-party against another arising from the same accident.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The caption shows Paul on Mary's side of the "v.", and the claim arises from the same accident. Rule 13(g) crossclaim matches.
2. **Student label:** True and responsive.

4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRCP 13(g) authorizes a crossclaim by one party against a co-party arising from the same transaction or occurrence. The same parking-lot collision is the same transaction or occurrence. Crossclaim is correct.

### B. A required-party motion, because Paul's fault must be adjudicated.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Paul is already a named co-defendant; Rule 19 governs joinder of *absent* persons, not labeled claims between existing parties.
2. **Student label:** Procedural-posture mislabel.
3. **True/responsive version:** "A Rule 19 motion might be appropriate if someone were missing from the case and complete relief could not be granted without them; that is not the question here."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRCP 19 governs compulsory joinder of absent persons. It does not generate a labeled pleading, and it does not apply because Paul is already a party. The choice is wrong-frame.

### C. A counterclaim, because it is a claim asserted by a defendant.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Counterclaim is the everyday word for any defendant-asserted claim; the Gold Key flips the label: counterclaim runs against an *opposing* party.
2. **Student label:** Counterclaim is the everyday label.
3. **True/responsive version:** "A counterclaim would be the right label if Mary were asserting a claim against Hannah (the opposing party). The claim is against Paul, a co-defendant."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRCP 13(a) defines counterclaim as a claim against an opposing party. Paul is a co-party of Mary, not an opposing party. Crossclaim is the right label, not counterclaim.

### D. An impleader claim, because Paul may be liable for the plaintiff's damages.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Impleader is for non-parties; Paul is already a party.
2. **Student label:** Rule 14 wrong-frame.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Impleader would be the right device if Mary were trying to pull in a non-party (e.g., a non-party tortfeasor) for contribution."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRCP 14(a) authorizes impleader against a person not already a party. Paul is already a co-defendant, so impleader is unavailable; the existing labeled claim is a crossclaim.
