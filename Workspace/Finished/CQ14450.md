### 1. Final Question - The Chosen Variation

Lydia owns a small Christian craft-supply shop that sells materials to private retreat groups. On April 3, she received a signed note that Daniel had mailed the day before: "Please send me 600 blank wooden cross ornaments at your current catalog price." ### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** Daniel's signed order was an offer to buy goods at Lydia's current catalog price. Because the order asked Lydia to send goods and did not clearly limit the method of acceptance, Lydia could accept by a prompt promise to ship as well as by actual shipment. Her properly stamped and addressed acceptance letter was dispatched on April 3, before Daniel's April 4 phone call. Once that invited acceptance was put into the mail, the contract was formed. Daniel's later attempted revocation came too late.

**Gold Key:** A goods order looking to prompt shipment is not shipment-only unless the offer clearly says so. A prompt promise to ship can accept, and a properly dispatched invited acceptance beats a later attempted revocation.

**Silver Key:** In a because-choice array, do not stop at the first word, "Yes" or "No." The because-clause must also be true and responsive.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A** attracts the student who sees a received order and assumes every ordinary offer stays open for a reasonable time. The breaker is the reason: nothing in Daniel's order made it irrevocable. The revocation failed for a different reason: Lydia had already accepted before Daniel tried to revoke.

**C** is the dominant trap. It sells a natural reading of "Please send me" as if shipment were the only acceptance path. **Gold Key:** A goods order looking to prompt shipment may be accepted by a prompt promise to ship unless the offer clearly restricts acceptance to shipment only. Lydia's mailed promise to accept therefore formed the contract before Daniel's revocation call.

**D** sounds practical because Daniel's note did not write the exact $4.20 figure. The breaker is that Daniel chose Lydia's current catalog price as the pricing standard, and Lydia accepted at that current catalog price. The answer invents a missing-price problem the facts do not give.

### 9. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Authority

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row. The item tests formation of a contract for goods when an order is accepted by a mailed promise before a later attempted revocation.

Under UCC section 2-206, an offer to make a contract invites acceptance "in any manner and by any medium reasonable in the circumstances," unless the offer clearly indicates otherwise, and an order to buy goods for prompt or current shipment invites acceptance by either a prompt promise to ship or shipment. D.C. Code section 28:2-206 and Massachusetts General Laws chapter 106, section 2-206 adopt that rule. The UCC comment to D.C. Code section 28:2-206 states that a current-shipment order allows acceptance by actual shipment or by a prompt promise to ship and rejects the artificial single-mode theory. Under UCC section 2-204, a contract for sale of goods may be made in any manner sufficient to show agreement. Under UCC section 2-305, a price fixed by one party must be fixed in good faith, and a current or posted price can satisfy the price term.

The mailbox-rule timing is also consistent with Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 63: an acceptance made in a manner and medium invited by the offer is operative when put out of the offeree's possession, unless the offer provides otherwise. Here, Lydia mailed a properly stamped, correctly addressed acceptance before Daniel attempted to revoke. The official key is therefore confirmed.
