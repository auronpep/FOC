### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. Peter's slash sent Stephen into medical treatment for a serious wound. Ordinary negligent treatment of that wound is a foreseeable dependent event in the chain Peter started. The infection changed the medical path, but it did not become a new, independent cause that alone produced the death. Because the wound required treatment, and the treatment error was the ordinary kind of risk that follows from sending someone to the hospital, Peter remains the proximate cause of Stephen's death.

**Gold Key:** Ordinary medical negligence in treating the defendant's serious wound usually does not sever homicide causation; the later medical act must be the sole, independent cause of death before it breaks the chain.

**Silver Key:** Do not ask whether the first wound was fatal by itself. Ask whether the later event was a foreseeable treatment consequence or a new independent death cause.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
**A is attractive** because the expert says Stephen would have survived the arm wound with competent care. The breaker is that homicide causation does not require the first wound to be independently fatal. Gold Key: ordinary negligent treatment of the defendant's serious wound remains inside the causal chain unless the later medical act is the sole, independent cause of death.

**C is attractive** because "gross negligence" sounds like the tougher, more legally precise threshold. The breaker is the direction of the rule. More extreme medical misconduct is what might help break the chain; Peter does not need the hospital's negligence to be gross before his original act can remain a proximate cause. Silver Key: keep the axis straight -- foreseeable treatment consequence keeps causation; sole independent cause breaks it.

**D is attractive** because the hospital error directly caused the infection that killed Stephen. The breaker is that ordinary medical negligence is not independent in this setting; it is treatment made necessary by Peter's serious wound. Gold Key: ordinary negligent medical care for the wound is a dependent intervening cause, not a superseding cause, unless the death is solely attributable to the later medical act.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: a defendant who inflicts a serious wound remains a proximate cause of the victim's death when ordinary negligent medical treatment of that wound contributes to death. The later treatment is a foreseeable dependent intervening cause, not a superseding cause, unless the death is solely attributable to the later medical act and not induced by the initial wound.

The authority floor is satisfied by the common-law homicide causation cases and the general causation principle reflected in Model Penal Code Section 2.03. In People v. Griffin, 80 N.Y.2d 723, 727-28 (1993), the New York Court of Appeals reaffirmed People v. Kane, 213 N.Y. 260 (1915), explaining that improper medical assistance contributing to death does not relieve a defendant when the assault also caused the death; the relevant exception is where death is attributable solely to negligent medical treatment. Cornell's New York Court of Appeals archive: https://www.law.cornell.edu/nyctap/080_0723.htm. The California Supreme Court stated the same general rule in People v. McGee, 31 Cal.2d 229, 240-41 (1947): negligent, mistaken, or unskillful treatment contributing to death does not defeat homicide liability when the wound remains an operative cause. Stanford SCOCAL archive: https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/people-v-mcgee-23869. Model Penal Code Section 2.03 supplies the broader result-causation frame: the actual result must not be too remote or accidental to have a just bearing on liability. Here, Stephen's infection arose from ordinary negligent treatment of Peter's serious wound; it was not a remote, independent death cause. The source row's official key is confirmed.
