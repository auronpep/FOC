## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Ruth worked for a private studio that made Scripture-engraved ceramic tiles. During her shift, she operated a powered glaze mixer. To protect her hands, she wore gloves sold under the trade name "Safety Gloves" by a glove manufacturer. The package label stated that the gloves would protect only against small hot glaze splashes and tiny ceramic chips.

One afternoon, the mixer's metal paddle snapped, and pieces of the paddle flew outward with great force. One large piece struck Ruth's arm and tore one glove partly off her hand. Another large piece then cut and injured that hand. Ruth brought an action against the glove manufacturer for the hand injury. The jurisdiction adheres to the traditional common-law rule pertaining to contributory negligence.

In this action, will Ruth prevail?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

When a protective product accurately says it protects only against small hazards, can the user recover from the manufacturer after a large, forceful hazard outside that protection injures the user?

### 3. Final Answer Choices

A. No, because the gloves were not designed or sold for protection against the kind of hazard Ruth encountered.

B. Yes, because the gloves were sold under the trade name "Safety Gloves."

C. No, if Ruth will be compensated under workers' compensation law.

D. Yes, because the gloves were defective in that they did not protect Ruth from the broken mixer paddle.

### 4. Correct Answer

### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

A is correct. The gloves were sold with an accurate limitation: they protected only against small hot glaze splashes and tiny ceramic chips. Ruth's injury came from large metal pieces thrown by a broken mixer paddle with enough force to tear a glove partly off her hand. The manufacturer did not sell the gloves as protection against that kind of hazard. **Gold Key:** Product liability still requires a product defect or a legally operative representation; a product is not defective merely because it fails at a job it was not designed, sold, or warned to perform. **Silver Key:** Match the answer reason to the product's promised protection, not to the fact that an injury happened while the product was being worn.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B.** This choice sells the ordinary-language pull of the word "safety." The breaker is that a trade name does not automatically create an unlimited warranty that the product will protect against every workplace danger. **Gold Key:** look for a concrete representation about the product's protection; here, the concrete label limited the protection to small splashes and tiny chips.

**C.** This choice tries to move the case into the employment-compensation lane. The breaker is the party and claim: Ruth sued the glove manufacturer, not her employer. Possible workers' compensation benefits do not answer whether the gloves were defective or misrepresented.

**D.** This is the dominant trap because it feels natural to say a safety product was defective when it did not prevent the injury. The breaker is the scope of the promised protection. The gloves did not fail at protecting against small splashes or tiny chips; the injury came from large metal pieces outside the product's designed and stated use. **Silver Key:** the fact of nonprotection is not enough unless the hazard falls inside the product's legal protection lane.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row. The source key is C: the employee does not prevail because the glasses were not designed or sold to protect against the large wheel fragments that caused the injury. In the transformed item, the post-shuffle key is A for the same reason: the gloves were not designed or sold to protect against large metal paddle pieces.

Lawyer confirmation: Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability section 2 identifies the defect categories as manufacturing defect, design defect, and inadequate instructions or warnings. The American Law Institute's publication page describes that section as formulating those three distinct categories. The row's theory fails because the warning accurately limited the product's protective scope and the injury came from a different, larger hazard. UCC section 2-313 supports the warranty side of the audit: express warranties arise from affirmations of fact, promises, descriptions, samples, or models that become part of the basis of the bargain, while ordinary commendation or opinion does not create an express warranty. The trade name "Safety Gloves," read with the limiting label, does not create a warranty of protection against large metal projectiles.
