## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Timothy rented a portable sound system from a private event-rental shop for a Friday-night hymn sing. Unknown to the shop, someone had hidden a small explosive device inside one of the speaker cabinets before the rental. ### 6. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. The shop is judged by ordinary care under the circumstances. It checked the sound system for normal operating safety. The hidden explosive device was not a risk the shop had reason to anticipate, because there had been no prior incidents of people hiding explosive devices in rented sound equipment. Negligence does not require a business to perform every possible inspection that would have uncovered a bizarre danger in hindsight. Gold Key: negligence precautions are judged ex ante by foreseeable risk, severity, and burden, not by asking after the accident what extra search would have found the danger. Silver Key: make the two tempting frames clash on timing. A looks backward to what would have worked; C looks forward to what a reasonable shop had reason to do before the rental.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells the strongest trap: the missed cabinet search would have found the device. That sounds powerful because it gives a clean but-for prevention story. The breaker is that negligence requires reasonable precautions against foreseeable risks, not every possible precaution that would have found a rare hidden danger after the fact. Gold Key: an effective precaution is not automatically a required precaution when the risk was not reasonably foreseeable.

B borrows a strict-liability word set. A hidden explosive device is dangerous, and a student may want the danger itself to decide the case. The breaker is the call: this is a negligence action, so the key question is whether the shop acted unreasonably. Strict liability for abnormally dangerous activities is a different lane and would require the shop to be carrying on the abnormally dangerous activity, not merely renting ordinary sound equipment that a stranger secretly altered.

D points to a true fact: the shop did not place the device in the speaker cabinet. That can feel decisive because it separates the shop from the wrongdoer who planted the danger. The breaker is that negligence can rest on failing to take reasonable precautions even when the defendant did not create the hidden condition. The no-placement fact does not answer whether the shop should reasonably have searched for that kind of danger.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. Negligence asks whether the defendant failed to exercise reasonable care under the circumstances. Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical and Emotional Harm section 3 frames negligence as failure to exercise reasonable care under all the circumstances, including the foreseeable likelihood and severity of harm and the burden of precautions. Source checked: https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/15198-american-tort-law/resources/1.2.2.1-third-restatement-3/. Restatement (Second) of Torts section 291 likewise ties unreasonable risk to what a reasonable person would recognize as involving risk of harm and to the burden/value balance. Source checked: https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/9130-torts/resources/2.1.4-restatement-second-of-torts-on-risk-utility-balancing/.

That authority supports the credited answer. A hidden explosive in ordinary rented sound equipment is severe if it occurs, but the stem supplies no prior incidents and no reason the shop should have anticipated that kind of danger. The shop performed the ordinary operating-safety inspection. The law does not make a defendant negligent merely because an additional unusual search would have found the danger.

The abnormally dangerous activity distractor remains wrong. Abnormally dangerous activity doctrine is strict liability, not negligence, and it applies when the defendant carries on an activity that creates a highly significant foreseeable risk even with reasonable care and is not common usage. Sources checked: Cornell Wex, https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/abnormally_dangerous_activity, and Restatement (Second) of Torts section 520, https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/4643-tobia-torts-2022/resources/9.2.3-restatement-2d-520-abnormally-dangerous-activities/.

Authority floor satisfied: Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical and Emotional Harm section 3; Restatement (Second) of Torts sections 291, 519, and 520.
