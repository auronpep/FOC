### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. The order is best defended as an exercise of presidential foreign-affairs and military authority to carry out an existing mutual-defense treaty obligation. Gold Key: when the stem gives an already-ratified defense treaty and an immediate request from the ally, the strongest support is execution of that existing treaty obligation, not the President's power to create a treaty or a freestanding power to declare war. Silver Key: on a strongest-argument call, rank the choices by fit to the action being justified; the treaty-obligation answer fits the deployment itself, while the other presidential-power phrases are either false, too early, or too general.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because treaty language is all over the stem, and the Treaty Clause is a real presidential power. The breaker is timing: the treaty already exists, so a rule about making treaties does not explain the immediate troop order.

B is attractive because "Commander in Chief" sounds like the broadest military phrase in the Constitution. The breaker is fit: the stem asks for the strongest argument for deciding to send troops abroad in response to an ally's treaty request, and the more specific treaty-obligation ground beats the generic command-of-forces label. Silver Key: choose the answer that matches the government act in the stem, not the most familiar constitutional title.

D is attractive only if the student lets the word "attack" pull the answer into war-declaration language. The breaker is branch direction: Congress, not the President, has the constitutional power to declare war.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Constitutional Law, Separation of Powers, War Power, and the verified outline code is `42010102`. Article I, Section 8 gives Congress the power to declare war, so the President cannot defend the order by claiming a presidential power to declare war. Article II, Section 2 makes the President Commander in Chief and also gives the President treaty-making power with Senate advice and consent, but the treaty-making answer is not responsive because the treaty already exists.

The credited answer is preserved because the strongest pro-validity argument is that the President may deploy U.S. forces abroad to implement an existing mutual-defense treaty obligation, drawing on the President's Commander-in-Chief role and primary foreign-affairs responsibility. Constitution Annotated describes the long-running debate over overseas troop deployments and notes the executive-branch position that presidential foreign-relations and Commander-in-Chief duties include broad power to deploy forces abroad when necessary for national security and treaty-linked defense. It also discusses the NATO and UN-era argument that the President may use armed forces in carrying out foreign policy that implements treaties. See U.S. Const. art. I, sec. 8; U.S. Const. art. II, sec. 2; Constitution Annotated, "Presidential Power and Commander in Chief Clause," https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/artII-S2-C1-1-11/ALDE_00013473/; Constitution Annotated, "Use of Troops Overseas and Congressional Authorization," https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/artII-S2-C1-1-10/ALDE_00013472/.

Current-law nuance: the War Powers Resolution, 50 U.S.C. sec. 1547(a)(2), says authority to introduce U.S. armed forces into hostilities may not be inferred from a treaty unless implementing legislation specifically authorizes that introduction. That statutory caveat is not surfaced in the source call or answer set, and the official key asks for the strongest argument supporting validity rather than a complete modern litigation answer. The source key is therefore not changed, but the caveat should remain in the lawyer-confirmation layer for attorney review if this item is used outside ordinary MBE-style study. The transform does not add facts that would change the credited outcome.
