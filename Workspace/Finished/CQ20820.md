## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation
B is right because common-law voluntary manslaughter requires legally adequate provocation. Here, the entire trigger is verbal accusation and insult. That kind of words-only provocation does not satisfy the objective adequacy requirement, even if Esther truly lost her temper.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations
A is attractive because it points to the immediate emotional reaction and the short time gap. The breaker is that timing only matters after there is legally adequate provocation, and insulting words alone do not clear that threshold.

C is attractive because it sounds like a murder-grade concept and uses the language of culpability. The breaker is that premeditation belongs to murder analysis, not to voluntary manslaughter; the question here is provocation, and the provocation is legally insufficient.

D is attractive because it treats humiliation and actual anger as enough to soften the killing. The breaker is that the law asks for objective adequacy, not just a strong subjective reaction, so ugly words still do not become adequate provocation merely because they hit hard.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification AND Legal Reasoning
The credited rule is unchanged: at common law, mere words, however insulting, are generally not adequate provocation to reduce murder to voluntary manslaughter. That is the governing distinction here, and the answer stays no because Peter's conduct was verbal only.

Authority used for verification:
- [Allen v. United States, 164 U.S. 492 (1896)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/164/492)
- [LII Provocation overview](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/provocation)

Allen states the classic common-law point that mere words, even if aggravating, do not reduce murder to manslaughter. The Cornell overview tracks the same rule and explains that words alone are almost universally rejected as adequate provocation unless paired with conduct showing present bodily harm. Esther's reaction may be humanly understandable, but the legal threshold is objective adequacy, and that threshold is not met.
