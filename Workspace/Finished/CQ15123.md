## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**A is correct.** False imprisonment is an intentional tort. The defendant must have acted with the intent to confine the plaintiff (or with the intent to commit some other intentional tort). Here, Timothy did not know Lydia was in the bookstore. He was asked to lock up for the night, and he had no reason to believe anyone remained inside. Because Timothy lacked the intent to confine Lydia, the essential element of intent is missing, and Lydia does not have a viable claim for false imprisonment.

**Gold Key:** *Intent to confine is the engine of false imprisonment. Without it, the tort does not exist — even if the plaintiff was actually confined and aware of it.*

---

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B is incorrect.** This choice sounds reasonable — it was unusual for anyone to be in the bookstore after closing, so perhaps Timothy should be excused. But the unusualness of the circumstances is relevant only to a negligence analysis (would a reasonable person have checked?). False imprisonment is an intentional tort. The reasonableness of Timothy's conduct is legally irrelevant. If Timothy had intended to lock Lydia in, the unusualness of the hour would not excuse him. The real problem is that Timothy never intended to confine anyone at all.

**C is incorrect.** This is the dominant trap. Lydia was unquestionably aware that she was locked in — she tried the doors, couldn't get out, and called her husband. Awareness of confinement is indeed one element of false imprisonment. But it is not the only element. The defendant must also have intended to confine the plaintiff. Timothy did not know Lydia was there and had no intent to confine her. Awareness alone does not establish the tort. **Gold Key:** *Awareness of confinement is necessary but not sufficient — intent to confine is the element that decides this question.*

**D is incorrect.** This choice frames the claim as if Timothy was negligent — he should have checked before locking up. But false imprisonment is not a negligence claim. The question is not whether a reasonable retreat coordinator would have walked through the building before locking it. The question is whether Timothy intended to confine Lydia. He did not. The "should have checked" standard applies to negligence, not to intentional torts.

## 9. Black-Letter Legal Verification

**Elements of false imprisonment (intentional tort):**
1. The defendant acted with the **intent** to confine the plaintiff within a bounded area;
2. The plaintiff was **actually confined**; and
3. The plaintiff was **aware of the confinement** at the time, or suffered **actual harm** as a result.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 35, 36. Section 35 requires that the actor "act intending to confine the other." Section 36 provides that the plaintiff must be aware of the confinement or harmed by it. The intent element is the defining feature that separates false imprisonment from negligence.

**Application:** Timothy locked the doors of the rented bookstore at the pastor's request, unaware that Lydia was still inside. He did not intend to confine her or anyone else. The intent element is absent. Therefore, regardless of Lydia's actual confinement and her awareness of it, the tort of false imprisonment is not established.

**Credited outcome unchanged from original:** The original question tested the same rule — intent to confine is essential, and its absence defeats the claim regardless of confinement or awareness. The credited answer (No, because the defendant did not intend to confine) is preserved.
