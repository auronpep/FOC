### 1. Final question
Mary hired Daniel, a Christian luthier, to restore her cedar harp for a home concert. Their one-page service contract said Daniel would “restore the harp and repair any broken parts needed for performance.” Mary paid half up front. After delivery, Mary said the contract required a new soundboard and an inlaid panel; Daniel said he promised only to refinish the frame, replace loose pegs, and restring the instrument. They sued in federal court on diversity grounds.

The judge held a bench trial. Mary and Daniel were the only witnesses, and they sharply disagreed about the scope of work promised in the contract. At the end of trial, the judge stated findings of fact on the record: Daniel had promised only the refinish, loose-peg, and string work, not a new soundboard or inlaid panel. Neither party objected to those findings. The judge entered judgment for Daniel. Mary appealed, arguing that the findings should be overturned. Is the appellate court likely to overturn the findings?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. The appellate court reviews the trial judge's fact findings under the clear-error standard and must give due regard to the trial judge's opportunity to assess live witness credibility. Gold Key: Rule 52(a)(6) protects a trial judge's fact findings, especially credibility-based findings, unless the appellate court is left with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake was made. Silver Key: when more than one answer says “No,” choose the no answer that states the actual appellate-review rule, not the answer that invents a preservation requirement or overstates deference.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because bench trials sound judge-driven, and students may think “judge found the facts” means the appellate court is locked in. The breaker is the overbroad phrase “regardless of witness credibility”: Rule 52(a)(6) is deferential, but it does not make findings immune from clear-error review.

C is attractive because Mary and Daniel sharply disagreed, and disagreement feels like a reason to reverse. The breaker is the Silver Key: disagreement alone is not the appellate standard; the question is whether the findings are clearly erroneous, with special regard for the trial judge's credibility vantage.

D is the dominant trap because objections often matter at trial, so “Mary did not object” sounds procedural and familiar. Gold Key: Rule 52(a)(5) lets a party later question the sufficiency of findings whether or not the party requested findings, objected to them, moved to amend them, or moved for partial findings. D invents a required objection that the rule does not impose.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
**Student black-letter-law answer explanation:** In a federal bench trial, the trial judge's findings of fact are reviewed for clear error. The appellate court may not simply reweigh the evidence or substitute its own credibility judgment. Because the trial judge heard the only two witnesses and made findings about the contract scope, the appellate court must give due regard to that opportunity to judge credibility unless the findings are clearly erroneous.

**Lawyer-confirmation authority:** Fed. R. Civ. P. 52(a)(6) provides that findings of fact, whether based on oral or other evidence, must not be set aside unless clearly erroneous, and the reviewing court must give due regard to the trial court's opportunity to judge witness credibility. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_52

Fed. R. Civ. P. 52(a)(5) also confirms that a party may later question the sufficiency of the evidence supporting findings whether or not the party requested findings, objected to them, moved to amend them, or moved for partial findings. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_52

The leading Supreme Court case is Anderson v. City of Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564 (1985), which explains that a finding is clearly erroneous when, despite supporting evidence, the reviewing court is left with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made, and that credibility-based findings receive even greater deference. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/470/564
