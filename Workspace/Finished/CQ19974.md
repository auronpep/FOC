## 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. The company paid Esther only because Paul's tort was imputed to it through respondeat superior. That makes the company a derivative-liability payer, not the actual tortfeasor for allocation between the company and Paul. Gold Key: when one party pays only because it is vicariously liable for another party's tort, the paying party seeks complete indemnity from the actual wrongdoer. Silver Key: do not split the money just because the plaintiff could collect from either defendant; first ask whether the payer's liability was its own fault or only derivative.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells the most tempting split. It treats the company and Paul as if they were two true joint tortfeasors sharing one loss by contribution. The breaker is the company's posture: it paid only because Paul's conduct was imputed to it, so the remedy is complete indemnity, not a half-share contribution.

C uses the word "vicariously" as a stop sign. It assumes that because the plaintiff could collect from the company, the company must absorb the loss permanently. Gold Key: vicarious liability answers the plaintiff's collection rights; it does not erase the employer's indemnity claim against the employee whose tort created the liability.

D sounds like a fair middle position. It recognizes indemnity language but waters it down to a compromise number. The breaker is that derivative liability shifts the entire paid loss to the actual tortfeasor; partial indemnity is not the MBE rule for a payer liable only vicariously.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested issue is unchanged: whether an employer that paid a tort judgment solely because it was vicariously liable for an employee's tort may recover from the employee by contribution, partial indemnity, complete indemnity, or not at all.

Under respondeat superior, an employer may be held liable for an employee's tort committed within the scope of employment. Cornell Wex states the doctrine as one that makes an employer or principal legally responsible for an employee's or agent's wrongful acts within the scope of employment. The indemnity rule is different from the plaintiff-facing liability rule: Restatement (Third) of Torts: Apportionment of Liability § 22 recognizes indemnity when the indemnitee was not liable except vicariously for the tort of the indemnitor. ALI's publication page confirms that the Restatement Third apportionment volume covers derivative claims and contains Topic 3, "Contribution and Indemnity." Heco v. Foster Motors, 2015 VT 3, also quotes § 22(a)(2)(i) for indemnity where the indemnitee was not liable except vicariously for the tort of the indemnitor.

Sources consulted: https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/respondeat_superior; https://www.ali.org/publications/restatement-law-third/torts-apportionment-liability; https://law.justia.com/cases/vermont/supreme-court/2015/2013-323.html.

Because the company paid $120,000 only because Paul's tort was imputed to it, the company may recover the full $120,000 from Paul as complete indemnity. The official answer substance is confirmed.
