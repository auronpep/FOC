## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**Gold Key:** Under Rhode Island v. Innis, Miranda "interrogation" includes express questioning AND its functional equivalent — words or actions police should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. Routine custodial caretaking (water, light, temperature, bathroom, blanket) is NOT interrogation.

Daniel was in custody and had invoked his right to counsel. Officer Ruth's question — "Is the light in here too bright?" — was a routine custodial caretaking inquiry. It was not express questioning, and it was not the functional equivalent of questioning because no officer should have known that asking about room lighting was reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. Because there was no interrogation, Daniel's reply was a volunteered statement. Miranda protections apply only to statements resulting from interrogation; volunteered statements are admissible. The court should deny the motion.

**Silver Key:** On a motion to suppress a custodial statement, the first cut is always: "Was there interrogation?" If the police activity was only custodial caretaking, cut any answer that assumes interrogation occurred or names an interrogation doctrine.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. The court should grant the motion, because Daniel's request for an attorney should have been granted immediately.**

*What makes it attractive:* Students often believe that invoking the right to counsel means police must produce a lawyer on the spot.

*The breaker:* **Gold Key GK-CRIM-EDWARDS-COUNSEL-01:** Invoking the right to counsel under Edwards v. Arizona means police must cease interrogation. It does NOT create a right to have counsel produced immediately; police may hold the suspect without questioning while counsel is arranged. Edwards stops interrogation; it does not create an immediate-delivery right.

**C. The court should deny the motion, because there was no indication the delay in contacting the lawyer was intentional.**

*What makes it attractive:* It sounds like a plausible fairness argument about police conduct — if they weren't delaying on purpose, maybe the statement should come in.

*The breaker:* The intentionality of delay is irrelevant to whether a statement was volunteered without interrogation. This choice answers a different question (was the police delay fair?) rather than the question the call asks (should the statement be suppressed?). Cut any choice that talks about police delay intentionality when the issue is whether the statement was interrogation-induced.

**D. The court should grant the motion, because the statement was the product of an unlawful "two-step" interrogation.**

*What makes it attractive:* Two officers, a time gap, and a second contact trigger the Seibert two-step reflex — the student sees the surface pattern and reaches for the doctrine.

*The breaker:* **Gold Key GK-CRIM-SEIBERT-TWOSTEP-01:** Missouri v. Seibert's two-step rule applies only when police deliberately interrogate without Miranda warnings first, then give warnings and repeat the same interrogation. It does not apply when there was never any pre-warning interrogation. Here, Officer John gave warnings before any questioning, and Officer Ruth never interrogated Daniel at all. No first step, no two-step.

## 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing authority:** Rhode Island v. Innis, 446 U.S. 291 (1980); Edwards v. Arizona, 451 U.S. 477 (1981); Missouri v. Seibert, 542 U.S. 600 (2004).

**Tested rule:** The Miranda safeguards apply only where a suspect in custody is subjected to interrogation — defined as express questioning or its functional equivalent (words or actions police should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response). Volunteered statements, even in custody after counsel invocation, are outside Miranda's scope. Edwards v. Arizona establishes that once counsel is invoked, police must cease interrogation; it does not require immediate production of counsel. Missouri v. Seibert prohibits a deliberate question-first, warn-second, repeat-third tactic — but requires an actual pre-warning interrogation as the first step.

**Legal reasoning for the credited answer:** Daniel's statement was not induced by interrogation. Officer Ruth's question about room lighting was routine custodial caretaking, not express questioning or its functional equivalent under Innis. Because there was no interrogation, Miranda — including its Edwards cessation rule — simply does not apply to the statement. The statement was volunteered and is admissible.

**Confirmation of invariant layer:** The tested rule (Innis definition of interrogation + Edwards scope of counsel invocation + Seibert two-step requirements), the legal issue (admissibility of a post-invocation statement), and the credited outcome (deny suppression motion) are IDENTICAL to the original. ### remediation_card
**Card ID:** RC-CRIM-16016-VAN
**Title:** Custodial Caretaking vs. Interrogation
**Signal:** Police ask a suspect in custody about comfort or needs after counsel is invoked.
**Student move:** Ask: Was this express questioning or its functional equivalent under Innis? Routine caretaking is neither.
**Tiny rule:** Miranda applies to interrogation, not to volunteered statements or custodial caretaking.
**Trap:** Mistaking any police-suspect contact for interrogation; confusing Edwards "cease interrogation" with "produce counsel now."
**Confidence:** HARD_STRUCTURAL

## Gold Key(s)

**GK-CRIM-INNIS-CARETAKING-01** (type: rule)
> Under Rhode Island v. Innis, Miranda "interrogation" includes express questioning AND its functional equivalent — words or actions police should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. Routine custodial caretaking (water, light, temperature, bathroom, blanket) is NOT interrogation.
- **Unlocks:** The trap of treating any police-suspect contact as interrogation after counsel is invoked.
- **Trigger:** Police ask a custodial suspect about comfort, needs, or physical conditions after Miranda warnings.
- **Authority:** Rhode Island v. Innis, 446 U.S. 291 (1980)

**GK-CRIM-EDWARDS-COUNSEL-01** (type: distinction)
> Invoking the right to counsel under Edwards v. Arizona means police must cease interrogation. It does NOT create a right to have counsel produced immediately; police may hold the suspect without questioning while counsel is arranged.
- **Unlocks:** The myth that police must produce a lawyer on demand.
- **Trigger:** A choice claims police failed by not getting a lawyer to the suspect right away.
- **Authority:** Edwards v. Arizona, 451 U.S. 477 (1981)

**GK-CRIM-SEIBERT-TWOSTEP-01** (type: exception)
> Missouri v. Seibert's two-step rule applies only when police deliberately interrogate without Miranda warnings first, then give warnings and repeat the same interrogation. It does not apply when there was never any pre-warning interrogation.
- **Unlocks:** The bait of labeling any two-officer contact as a Seibert violation.
- **Trigger:** A stem involves two officers and a time gap, but no actual pre-warning questioning.
- **Authority:** Missouri v. Seibert, 542 U.S. 600 (2004)

## Silver Key(s)

**SK-CRIM-STATEMENT-SUPPRESSION-01** (type: cut)
> On a motion to suppress a custodial statement, the first cut is always: "Was there interrogation?" If the police activity was only custodial caretaking, cut any answer that assumes interrogation occurred or names an interrogation doctrine.
- **Navigates:** The temptation to see every police-suspect contact as a Miranda issue.
- **Trigger:** Motion to suppress a statement after Miranda warnings and counsel invocation.
