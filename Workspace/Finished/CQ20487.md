## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Under the doctrine of estoppel by deed (also known as after-acquired title), a grantor who conveys real property — or, as here, personal property treated under analogous principles — in which she has no interest is estopped from denying the validity of the conveyance if she later acquires title to that property. Here, when Lydia sold the flock to Barnabas in May, she did not own the sheep; title was still in Timothy. But in July, Timothy reconveyed title back to Lydia. Because Lydia subsequently acquired the very property she had purported to convey to Barnabas, she is now estopped from denying the validity of her conveyance to him. The court should rule in favor of Barnabas.

**Gold Key:** Estoppel by deed — if a grantor conveys property she doesn't own, and later acquires title to that property, title automatically passes to the grantee by operation of law. The grantor is "estopped" (legally barred) from denying the conveyance was valid.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect.** This choice tempts students who think recording statutes determine whether a grantee gets title from a grantor. Recording protects against *subsequent* bona fide purchasers — it has nothing to do with whether the grantee's rights vest against the grantor himself. Between Lydia (grantor) and Barnabas (grantee), recording is irrelevant. The sale is valid or invalid based on other doctrines, not recording. This is the **Silver Key** move: when you see a recording-statute answer in a dispute between grantor and grantee, cut it — recording is a shield against third parties, not a requirement for the grantee's rights against the grantor.

**B is incorrect.** This is the dominant trap. It is *true* that Lydia did not own the sheep when she sold them to Barnabas — that fact is undisputed. But the conclusion doesn't follow. The whole point of estoppel by deed is that a grantor who lacked title at the time of conveyance *cannot use that lack of title as a defense* if she later acquires the property. The fact that Lydia didn't own the sheep in May is precisely what triggers estoppel once she reacquires them in July. Students who stop at "she didn't own it, so the sale is void" miss the after-acquired-title doctrine entirely. **Gold Key:** Estoppel by deed — if a grantor conveys property she doesn't own, and later acquires title to that property, title automatically passes to the grantee by operation of law.

**D is incorrect.** This choice reaches the right party (Barnabas) but for the wrong reason. Nothing in the facts suggests Lydia committed fraud. She sold sheep she didn't own, but there's no indication she made false representations, intended to deceive, or that Barnabas relied on any misrepresentation. Fraud requires more than a seller lacking title — it requires a knowing false statement of material fact, intent to induce reliance, justifiable reliance, and damages. The facts here don't support any of that. Students who pick this are reasoning from outcome (Barnabas should win) backward to a justification, rather than identifying the correct doctrine. **Gold Key:** Right party, wrong reason — always check that the *reason* matches the facts, not just the party.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule:** Estoppel by deed (after-acquired title). When a grantor conveys real property by warranty deed (or equivalent instrument) at a time when the grantor does not hold title, and the grantor subsequently acquires title to that property, title passes to the grantee by operation of law. The grantor is estopped from denying the validity of the original conveyance.

**Authority:** *Wikipedia, Estoppel by Deed*, citing *Wallace v. Pruitt*, 1 Tex. Civ. App. 231, 20 S.W. 728 (1892) ("That the maker of a deed may be estopped to deny the truth of recitals therein is a well-settled doctrine of the common law."); W.E. Coldwell Co. v. Cowart, 138 Ga. 233, 75 S.E. 425 (1912); William B. Stoebuck & Dale A. Whitman, *The Law of Property* §11.5 (3d ed. 2000). See also *King v. Gilson's Adm'x*, 32 Ill. 348 (1863) (discussing covenants of seisin and right to convey).

**Application:** The original question tests the classic estoppel-by-deed pattern: Farmer conveys to Developer (passes title), then Farmer purports to convey same land to Builder (Farmer has no title at this moment), then Farmer reacquires title from Developer via reconveyance. Estoppel by deed operates: Farmer is estopped from denying Builder's title because Farmer later acquired the very property she had conveyed.
