### 6. Right-Answer Explanation

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel had already attached because formal proceedings (indiction) had begun and counsel had been appointed. However, the Sixth Amendment right, like other constitutional rights, may be waived if the waiver is knowing, intelligent, and voluntary. Here, Timothy initiated the contact by sending a letter to the chaplain asking to speak with detectives. The detectives then gave Miranda warnings, reminded Timothy that he had a lawyer and could stop at any time, and obtained a signed written waiver. Under these circumstances, Timothy validly waived his Sixth Amendment right to counsel, and the confession is admissible.

**Gold Key:** After the Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches, the accused may waive that right if the waiver is knowing, intelligent, and voluntary; Miranda warnings generally suffice to inform a post-indictment defendant of the right and the consequences of waiver. (*Patterson v. Illinois*, 487 U.S. 285 (1988).)

### 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap):** This choice states an absolute rule -- that an indicted defendant can *never* waive the Sixth Amendment right outside counsel's presence. The word "never" is the tell. The Sixth Amendment right to counsel is waivable, and the Supreme Court has held that a defendant who initiates contact and receives valid warnings can waive the right. The absolute prohibition stated here is overbroad and incorrect. **Gold Key:** The right is waiving if knowing, intelligent, and voluntary -- there is no rule requiring counsel's physical presence for a valid waiver.

**B:** This choice reaches the right outcome (admissible) but for the wrong reason -- that jailhouse or chaplain-related statements are categorically outside the Sixth Amendment. That is false. Post-indictment questioning in a jail setting can absolutely trigger Sixth Amendment protections. The confession here is admissible because of the valid waiver, not because the setting is exempt.

**D:** This choice incorrectly states that the detectives needed defense counsel's consent before interviewing Timothy. The constitutional question is whether *Timothy* validly waived his own right to counsel. Counsel's advance consent is not the governing test when the defendant initiates contact and waives personally.

### 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule:** The Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches once formal criminal proceedings have commenced (e.g., indictment). *McNeil v. Wisconsin*, 501 U.S. 171 (1991). After attachment, the accused may waive the right to counsel if the waiver is "knowing, intelligent, and voluntary." *Patterson v. Illinois*, 487 U.S. 285 (1988). Miranda warnings generally suffice to inform a post-indictment defendant of the Sixth Amendment right to counsel and the consequences of waiver. *Id.* A defendant who initiates contact with police after indictment may validly waive the right even without counsel present. *Montejo v. Louisiana*, 556 U.S. 778 (2009).

**Application:** Timothy was indicted and had appointed counsel, so the Sixth Amendment right had attached. He initiated contact via the chaplain letter. Detectives provided Miranda warnings, reminded him of his right to counsel, and obtained a signed written waiver. This constitutes a valid waiver. The confession is admissible.

**Authority floor:** U.S. Const. amend. VI; *Patterson v. Illinois*, 487 U.S. 285 (1988); *Montejo v. Louisiana*, 556 U.S. 778 (2009).

#### Wrong-Answer Recovery Paths

**If you picked A:** You thought the Sixth Amendment right could never be waived outside counsel's presence. Recovery: Constitutional rights are generally waivable. The test is whether the waiver was knowing, intelligent, and voluntary -- not whether counsel was physically present. Review Gold Key GK-CRIM-001.

**If you picked B:** You correctly said admissible but for the wrong reason. Recovery: The chaplain/jail setting does not create a Sixth Amendment exemption. The reason the confession is admissible is the valid waiver, not the setting. Always check whether the reasoning matches the facts.

**If you picked D:** You applied the wrong legal test. Recovery: The question is whether the defendant personally waived the right to counsel, not whether defense counsel consented. The defendant's own waiver is what matters.
