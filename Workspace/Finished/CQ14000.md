### 7. Full right-answer explanation
D is correct. The appeal turns on the trial court's constitutional determination that the punitive award was excessive, not on an ordinary discretionary choice about trial management. Gold Key: when punitive damages are reduced because they violate constitutional excessiveness limits, the court of appeals reviews that constitutional ruling de novo. Silver Key: when the stem mixes a Rule 59 weight-of-the-evidence complaint with a punitive-damages constitutional complaint, answer the review question for the constitutional piece the choice actually targets. That is why the appellate court independently reviews the constitutional reduction ruling.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because it sounds like a neat remittitur formula and gives the student a precise number-based rule to grab. The breaker is that federal remittitur does not require the judge to pick the single lowest amount any jury could have awarded; the court may condition denial of a new trial on the plaintiff's acceptance of a reduced amount supported by the record.

B is attractive because students often hear "jury province" and jump to a Seventh Amendment objection whenever a judge trims damages. The breaker is twofold: remittitur is permissible when the plaintiff may reject the reduction and take a new trial, and Silver Key: the call asks how the appellate court reviews this ruling, not whether every reduction is automatically forbidden.

C is attractive because a new-trial motion can sound like a pure legal sufficiency question where the judge must stay hands-off. The breaker is that Rule 59 allows the trial judge to weigh the evidence when deciding whether the verdict is against the weight of the evidence, so this choice flips the judge's role backwards.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Civil Procedure, Appealability and Review, Scope of Review, and the verified outline code is `93110300`. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 59 authorizes a new-trial motion after a jury trial. In resolving such a motion, the district court may weigh the evidence when deciding whether the verdict is against the weight of the evidence. Federal remittitur is also permissible when the plaintiff is given the choice between accepting the reduced award and taking a new trial. See Fed. R. Civ. P. 59; *Dimick v. Schiedt*, 293 U.S. 474 (1935); *Hetzel v. Prince William County*, 523 U.S. 208 (1998). But the specific appellate-review rule tested here comes from *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Leatherman Tool Group, Inc.*, 532 U.S. 424 (2001), which holds that courts of appeals apply de novo review to district-court determinations about the constitutionality of punitive-damages awards. That keeps the credited answer unchanged. The source explanation's Eighth Amendment phrasing is broader than needed; the governing review rule is grounded in constitutional excessiveness doctrine as framed in *Cooper Industries*, but the official key still correctly points to de novo review.
