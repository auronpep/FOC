## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam register)

The fight is a 2×2: **judge vs. jury** crossed with **may vs. must-not consider the affidavit**. Lock the decider first. Whether a hearsay exception applies — including whether a statement qualifies as a **dying declaration** — is a *preliminary question about admissibility*, and preliminary questions of admissibility are decided by the **court**, not the jury. That cuts both "jury" choices in one stroke. Now the affidavit: in deciding a preliminary admissibility question, the judge is **not bound by the rules of evidence** (except privilege), so the judge **may** rely on the physician's affidavit even though it is hearsay. Judge + may consider = **C**. *Silver Key:* decide the **decider axis before the affidavit axis** — the jury/weight framing is the dressed-up trap, and naming "admissibility = judge" collapses half the board instantly.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap — jury, may consider).** It feels right because the affidavit half is actually correct (the decider may consider hearsay) and "weight and credibility for the jury" is the phrase students reflexively attach to anything about a witness statement's reliability. The breaker: trustworthiness of an alleged hearsay *exception* is an **admissibility** determination, and admissibility is the judge's job under the preliminary-question rule — the jury never rules on whether the dying declaration comes in. *Gold Key:* qualifying a hearsay exception is a preliminary admissibility question for the court, not a jury weight question.

**B (judge, must not consider).** It draws the student who correctly identifies the judge as the decider, then over-applies the intuition that "you can't prove something with hearsay." The breaker: when the judge decides a preliminary admissibility question, the evidence rules — including the hearsay bar — **do not bind the court**, so the affidavit is fair game.

**D (jury, must not consider).** It stacks both errors at once — wrong decider and a false limitation on the affidavit — which is why almost no one bites. The breaker: the judge (not the jury) decides admissibility, and that judge may consider the affidavit.

## 9. Black-Letter Verification & Right-Answer Legal Reasoning

**Tested rule (unchanged):** Preliminary questions concerning the admissibility of evidence — including whether a hearsay exception such as a dying declaration applies — are determined by the **court**, and in making that determination the court is **not bound by the rules of evidence** except those on privilege; it may therefore consider affidavits and other hearsay.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):** Fed. R. Evid. **104(a)** (court decides preliminary questions of admissibility; "in so deciding, the court is not bound by evidence rules, except those on privilege"); Fed. R. Evid. **804(b)(2)** (dying-declaration exception); *Bourjaily v. United States*, 483 U.S. 171 (1987) (court may consider the proffered out-of-court statement itself, and other hearsay, in deciding a preliminary admissibility question by a preponderance). Issue, tested rule, and credited outcome are identical to the source item — judge decides, judge may consider the affidavit. **Verdict: PASS** (residual = credited answer; no key disagreement).
