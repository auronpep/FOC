## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. Common-law kidnapping requires unlawful confinement plus asportation, and the asportation element is satisfied by forced movement of the victim from one place to another. Gold Key: in a common-law kidnapping call, do not import a modern substantial-distance or incidental-movement limit unless the question gives that rule. Silver Key: lock the time frame in the call first; "common law" is the signal to use the older no-minimum-distance rule. Daniel forced Ruth from the vendor table to a separate supply nook. That is movement, so the common-law asportation element is satisfied.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is the dominant trap. It is attractive because modern kidnapping statutes and merger doctrines often ask whether the movement was merely incidental to another crime. The breaker is the call's common-law frame: the common-law asportation question is whether there was forced movement, not whether the movement had an independent purpose apart from the robbery. Gold Key: the modern incidental-movement limitation is not the common-law rule being tested.

C is attractive because using a victim as a shield sounds like the kind of purpose that makes kidnapping especially serious. The breaker is that common-law asportation does not depend on a law-enforcement-avoidance purpose; forced movement itself supplies the movement element.

D is attractive because twelve feet sounds too small for a separate kidnapping charge. The breaker is that common law has no minimum footage requirement. Silver Key: when an answer turns the issue into a number, ask whether the rule actually uses a number.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. The source item asks only the common-law asportation question for kidnapping. Under the traditional common-law-style rule tested here, forced movement from one place to another satisfies asportation even when the distance is short; a modern substantial-distance or incidental-movement limitation is a different statutory or merger doctrine.

Those sources confirm the exam distinction: the supplied common-law call credits the no-minimum-distance rule, while modern statutes may add incidental-movement limits.
