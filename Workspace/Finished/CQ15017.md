## PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Peter owned two adjoining parcels of land, Parcel A and Parcel B, in fee simple. Peter conveyed Parcel A to Daniel by warranty deed. The deed contained the following provision:

"Grantor, for himself, his heirs and assigns, covenants and agrees that one-half of any reasonable expense incurred by Grantee, his heirs or assigns, for the repair of the stone boundary fence situated on Parcel A along the common line with Parcel B shall be reimbursed by Grantor, his heirs or assigns; and by this provision the parties intend a covenant running with the land."

Daniel later conveyed Parcel A to Lydia by warranty deed in usual form; the deed made no reference to the fence or the covenant. Fifty years later, Lydia conveyed Parcel A to Timothy by warranty deed in usual form; this deed also omitted any reference to the fence or the covenant. All conveyances were for fair market value. The deed from Peter to Daniel was never recorded. All other deeds were promptly and properly recorded. Parcel B passed by intestate succession from Peter to his son, Barnabas, upon Peter's death. There is no applicable statute except a recording act and a 10-year adverse possession statute.

Timothy spent $3,500 repairing the stone boundary fence. He later discovered the covenant in Peter's original deed to Daniel and demanded $1,750 from Barnabas. When Barnabas refused, Timothy brought an appropriate action to recover that sum. Barnabas asserted all available defenses.

If judgment is for Barnabas, it will be because

**A.** Timothy's claim is barred by adverse possession.

**B.** Timothy did not know about the covenant until after he incurred the expenses and therefore could not have relied on it.

**C.** Timothy's expenditures were not proved to be reasonable and customary.

**D.** The deed from Peter to Daniel was never recorded.

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The covenant in Peter's deed to Daniel expressly conditions reimbursement on the requirement that repair expenses be "reasonable." This is a covenant running with the land — it was in writing, the parties stated their intent that it bind "heirs and assigns," the obligation to share fence-repair costs touches and concern the land (it directly affects the use and value of both parcels), horizontal privity exists (the covenant was created in a grantor-grantee conveyance), and vertical privity is satisfied at each transfer (each successor acquired the full fee simple). The burden of the covenant therefore runs to Barnabas as Peter's successor, and the benefit runs to Timothy as Daniel's remote grantee.

However, the covenant's own terms impose a condition precedent on recovery: only *reasonable* repair expenses are reimbursable. Timothy bears the burden of proving that his $3,500 expenditure was reasonable and customary for the type of repair performed. If he fails to carry that burden — if the trier of fact finds the expense unreasonable or if Timothy offers no evidence of reasonableness — then even though the covenant is enforceable, Timothy cannot recover. That is why judgment would be for Barnabas.

**Gold Key:** An affirmative covenant to share repair costs touches and concerns the land and runs with the land at law if writing, intent, touch and concern, horizontal privity, and vertical privity are all satisfied — but recovery is always limited to amounts the claimant proves are reasonable under the covenant's own terms.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is attractive** because adverse possession is mentioned in the facts (there is a 10-year statute), and students sometimes look for any statutory hook to resolve a dispute. But no party in these facts acquired any interest in land by adverse possession. Timothy holds Parcel A through a chain of warranty deeds from Daniel (who received it from Peter). Barnabas holds Parcel B by intestate succession from Peter. The adverse possession statute is a red herring — it has no application to any issue in the case. The claim fails because the facts do not support it, not because of any legal bar.

**B is attractive** because students may intuit that a party should not be able to recover on a promise they didn't know about when they acted. But reliance is not an element of covenant enforcement. A real covenant runs with the land and binds successors regardless of whether the promisee knew of it at the time of expenditure. The covenant's enforceability depends on the running requirements (writing, intent, touch and concern, privity, notice to the burdened party), not on the benefited party's subjective knowledge or reliance. Timothy's late discovery of the covenant is legally irrelevant.

**D is attractive** (and is the **dominant trap**, selected by 59% of students) because the unrecorded deed is a prominent fact, and students know that recording acts protect subsequent bona fide purchasers against unrecorded interests. But Barnabas is not a bona fide purchaser — he acquired Parcel B by intestate succession from Peter, not by purchase for value. Recording acts protect only BFPs who take for value without notice. An heir who takes by succession is not within the protection of the recording act and takes subject to all covenants binding the decedent's land. The unrecorded deed is therefore not a defense available to Barnabas.

### 9. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** Real covenants running with the land.

For the **burden** of a real covenant to run with the land at law, the following elements must be satisfied:
1. **Writing** — The covenant must be in writing (Statute of Frauds). ✓ Here, the covenant appears in the deed.
2. **Intent to run** — The original parties must intend the covenant to bind successors. ✓ The deed states "the parties intend a covenant running with the land" and binds "heirs and assigns."
3. **Touch and concern** — The covenant must affect the parties as landowners. ✓ A covenant to share the cost of maintaining a boundary fence directly affects the use, value, and enjoyment of both parcels.
4. **Horizontal privity** — The original covenanting parties must share an interest in the land independent of the covenant. ✓ Grantor-grantee relationship at conveyance satisfies horizontal privity.
5. **Vertical privity (strict)** — The successor must acquire the entire durational estate. ✓ Each transfer was of the full fee simple.
6. **Notice** — The burdened party must have notice. ✓ Barnabas, as Peter's heir, has at least inquiry notice (the fence is a visible, physical feature on the boundary).

For the **benefit** to run: writing, intent, touch and concern, and relaxed vertical privity (any portion of the estate) are sufficient. ✓ Timothy, as a remote grantee of Parcel A, holds the benefit.

**Authority:** Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 5.1 (2000) (covenants running with the land); Restatement (First) of Property § 537 (1944) (burden runs with the land); *Caullett v. Stanley Stilwell & Sons, Inc.*, 67 N.J. Super. 111 (App. Div. 1961) (affirmative covenants to repair can run with the land); *Moseley v. Bishop*, 470 N.E.2d 773 (Ind. 1984) (covenant to maintain fence touches and concerns). See also 20 Am. Jur. 2d Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions § 27; Powell on Real Property § 60.04 (same).

**Affirmative covenants and reasonableness:** When a covenant conditions payment on "reasonable" expenses, the claimant bears the burden of proving reasonableness as a condition precedent to recovery. *See* Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 6.10 cmt. e (burden of proof on the party seeking to enforce the covenant to show compliance with its terms). The covenant here does not guarantee reimbursement of all repair costs — only reasonable ones. If Timothy fails to prove his $3,500 was reasonable, he cannot recover even though the covenant is valid and runs with the land.

**Recording act analysis:** Recording statutes protect subsequent *bona fide purchasers* for value without notice. *See* 23 Am. Jur. 2d Deeds § 143. An heir who takes by intestate succession is not a "purchaser" and is not protected by the recording act. *See* Powell § 82.02[3] (heirs take subject to all encumbrances on the decedent's land regardless of recording). Barnabas therefore takes Parcel B subject to the covenant regardless of whether the deed was recorded.

**Verification:** The tested rule (covenants running with the land; affirmative repair covenants; reasonableness condition), the legal issue (enforceability of a repair-cost covenant against a remote successor), and the credited outcome (judgment for the defendant because reasonableness was not proved) are all unchanged from the original.

#### Program Frame

This item tests whether you can enforce an affirmative covenant running with the land — specifically, a covenant to share the reasonable cost of repairing a shared boundary structure. The core skill is recognizing that (a) affirmative covenants CAN run with the land, (b) the covenant's own terms (here, "reasonable" expenses) create a condition precedent that the claimant must satisfy, and (c) common distractors (recording acts, reliance, adverse possession) fail when you apply the actual elements.

A grantor covenanted to reimburse half the *reasonable* repair costs for a boundary fence. The remote grantee spent money and sued the grantor's successor. Why does the successor win?

#### Call and Prediction
