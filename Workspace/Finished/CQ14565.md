## 1. Final question

Daniel was charged with arson after a storage shed at a Christian camp burned. At a preliminary hearing, the magistrate dismissed the charge on the ground that the evidence was insufficient. The prosecutor then presented the case to a grand jury. After hearing the prosecutor's evidence, that grand jury refused to return an indictment. A few months later, after a new grand jury had been impaneled, the prosecutor presented the same case again. The new grand jury returned an indictment charging Daniel with arson. Daniel moved to dismiss the indictment on double jeopardy grounds. Should the motion be granted?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

After a preliminary-hearing dismissal and one grand jury's refusal to indict, does double jeopardy bar a later indictment by a new grand jury?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is the best answer. The preliminary hearing and the grand-jury presentations occurred before trial. Jeopardy had not attached, so the prosecutor was not barred from presenting the case to a new grand jury and obtaining an indictment. Gold Key: double jeopardy protects against a second jeopardy after jeopardy has attached; grand-jury proceedings and preliminary hearings are not that point. Silver Key: on an attachment question, ignore result-sounding words like "dismissed" and "refused to indict" until you locate the first trial moment.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A. This choice sells the idea that a magistrate's insufficiency ruling is trial-like. The breaker is attachment. A preliminary hearing is not the point where jeopardy begins.

C. This choice sells finality from the first grand jury's refusal. The breaker is that a grand jury refusal to indict is not jeopardy and does not bar a later presentation to a new grand jury.

D. This is the dominant trap because it reaches "No" and sounds close to the Double Jeopardy Clause's protection against repeated prosecutions. The breaker is that the tested trigger is attachment, not merely whether a conviction or acquittal already exists. Gold Key: the clean reason is that no trial jeopardy had attached.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The legal issue and credited outcome are unchanged: double jeopardy does not bar the later indictment because jeopardy had not attached during the preliminary hearing or grand-jury proceedings. Serfass v. United States, 420 U.S. 377 (1975), explains that jeopardy does not attach before a defendant is put to trial before the trier of facts. In a jury trial, attachment occurs when the jury is empaneled and sworn; in a bench trial, it occurs when the court begins to hear evidence. A preliminary-hearing dismissal and a grand jury's refusal to indict are pretrial events, so the second grand jury's indictment does not violate double jeopardy.
