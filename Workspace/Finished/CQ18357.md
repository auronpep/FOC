### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is the only answer that includes every Massiah trigger in the stem: indictment, government direction, a codefendant acting as an agent, deliberate questioning about the charged fraud, no counsel, and no waiver. Gold Key: after formal charges, the government cannot bypass counsel by sending an agent to deliberately elicit charged-offense statements. Silver Key: do not ask whether the questioner looks like police; ask who directed the questioning and what offense the questions targeted.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because indictment and codefendant contact feel dangerous. The breaker is the word "all": suppression turns on government agency plus deliberate elicitation, not on every conversation with a former business partner.

B is attractive because many interrogation rules are taught through police-station scenes. The breaker is that Massiah is not limited to stationhouse questioning; the problem is government-directed elicitation after the right to counsel has attached.

D is the strongest trap because Ruth began as a private codefendant. Gold Key: a private person can become a government agent when prosecutors direct that person to draw out charged-offense statements.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The credited answer remains unchanged in substance. Once adversarial criminal proceedings have begun, the Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches for the charged offense. The government violates that right when it deliberately elicits incriminating statements about the charged offense from the accused through a government agent without counsel or a valid waiver. A cooperating codefendant is not automatically a private actor if prosecutors direct the questioning.

Authority floor: Massiah v. United States, 377 U.S. 201 (1964), held that the government violated the right to counsel by deliberately eliciting incriminating post-indictment statements from the defendant in counsel's absence. United States v. Henry, 447 U.S. 264 (1980), applied the doctrine to a government informant who deliberately obtained statements from an indicted defendant. Maine v. Moulton, 474 U.S. 159 (1985), confirms that the State may not knowingly circumvent the accused's right to counsel by using a cooperating codefendant to obtain charged-offense statements. Sources checked: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/377/201/, https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/447/264/, and https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/474/159/.
