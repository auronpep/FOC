### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** Common-law burglary is a breaking and entering of the dwelling of another at night with intent to commit a felony inside. The timing matters. The felonious intent must exist when the defendant breaks and enters. Stephen opened a closed kitchen door and crossed into Lydia's townhouse at night, so the entry facts are dangerous. But the stem says he entered to ask Lydia to pay or return the handbells. He formed the idea to take the handbells only after he was already inside and saw them. That later decision does not retroactively supply the intent required for burglary.

**Gold Key:** Common-law burglary freezes intent at the moment of breaking and entering; later-formed intent may support another offense, but it does not complete burglary.

**Silver Key:** Separate the entry mechanics from the intent timing. A choice can lose even if it attacks a real element, because the best defense is the element the facts actually defeat.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A** sells the strongest surface trap: the door was unlocked, so the entry feels like "no breaking." The breaker is that common-law breaking does not require damage, lock-picking, or violence. Opening a closed door is enough even if the door was unlocked. **Gold Key:** A closed barrier can be "broken" by slight force, including turning a knob or pushing the door open; the defense must aim at missing intent, not at the unlocked door.

**C** looks morally helpful because Stephen left a signed tag explaining that he was reclaiming rental goods. The breaker is timing. Burglary, if committed, is complete at the breaking and entering with the required intent. A note left after entry does not undo an already complete burglary, and if burglary was not complete at entry, the note is still not the decisive reason.

**D** attracts the student who uses ordinary speech: no force means no breaking. The breaker is that common-law breaking requires only the slight force used to create an opening. Turning a handle and pushing open a closed door is enough. The problem with the prosecution is not lack of force; it is lack of felonious intent at the entry moment.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. At common law, burglary required breaking and entering the dwelling house of another at night with intent to commit a felony therein. Blackstone, quoting Coke, gives the classic formulation: a burglar is one who by night breaks and enters a mansion house with intent to commit a felony. See Blackstone, *Commentaries on the Laws of England*, Book IV, ch. 16, hosted by Yale Avalon Project: https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/blackstone_bk4ch16.asp. Blackstone also explains that opening a window, picking a lock, opening with a key, lifting a latch, or undoing a fastening may satisfy breaking, while the breaking and entry must be accompanied by felonious intent. Cornell Wex states the same exam rule in modern summary form: common-law burglary is breaking and entering the dwelling house of another at nighttime with intent to commit a felony, and slight force such as pushing open a door can be enough for breaking. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/breaking_and_entering.

The source key is consistent with that rule. Stephen's opening of the closed kitchen door supplies breaking even though the door was unlocked, so the door and force defenses fail. His strongest defense is that when he opened the door and entered, he intended only to contact Lydia about payment or return of goods. Because the burglary intent must exist at the entry moment, later taking the handbells does not make the earlier entry burglary.

Authority check: no doctrinal-currency hotlist issue is triggered. This is a common-law burglary element question, not a modern statutory burglary or Fourth Amendment issue.
