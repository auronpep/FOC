## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question — revised stem + call

Lydia Learning LLC, a private company that runs Bible-verse calligraphy camps, agreed in writing to buy from Ruth twelve hand-painted mobile coffee carts branded for “Mustard Seed Morning” events at a fixed price per cart. Lydia Learning was a subsidiary of Emmaus Market Group. Just before the written contract was signed, Martha, Lydia Learning’s purchasing director, said to Ruth, “Of course, our commitment to buy is subject to approval from Emmaus Market Group.” Ruth replied, “Fine. No problem.”

Ruth later refused to deliver the carts, arguing that Lydia Learning had never asked Emmaus Market Group to approve the contract. That was true. Barnabas, the chief executive officer of Emmaus Market Group, is prepared to testify that Emmaus Market Group would have routinely approved the contract if asked. Lydia Learning can also prove that it sold a delivery van to raise money for the coffee-cart purchase, although it admits that it had not anticipated any need to sell the van when it entered the contract.

If Lydia Learning sues Ruth for breach of contract, is Lydia Learning likely to prevail?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A buyer’s purchase duty was conditioned on parent approval; the buyer never requested approval, but the buyer-side parties are ready to close. Can the seller refuse to perform based on that non-occurrence?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. No, because the express condition of Emmaus Market Group’s approval had not occurred before the lawsuit.

B. No, because obtaining Emmaus Market Group’s approval was within Lydia Learning’s control, and Lydia Learning’s failure to obtain it was itself a material breach of contract.

C. Yes, because the approval condition, being designed to protect only Lydia Learning and Emmaus Market Group, can be and has been waived by those entities.

D. Yes, because Lydia Learning detrimentally relied on Ruth’s commitment by selling its delivery van to finance the coffee-cart purchase.

### 5. Correct answer

**C**. Original key: **A**.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.** The approval condition was phrased as a limit on Lydia Learning’s own commitment to buy. It protected the buyer side: Lydia Learning and its parent, Emmaus Market Group. The facts show that both protected entities are ready to proceed. **Gold Key:** a condition inserted only for one side’s benefit can be waived by that protected side. **Silver Key:** before asking whether the approval happened, ask whose duty the condition qualifies. Here, the condition qualified the buyer’s duty, not Ruth’s duty to deliver. Once the buyer-side condition is waived, Ruth cannot use its non-occurrence as her reason not to perform.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because the approval event really did not occur before suit. It sells the clean “condition failed” frame. The breaker is the missing waiver step: the condition protected Lydia Learning and Emmaus Market Group, and those protected parties are ready to proceed. **Gold Key:** non-occurrence of a protected-side condition does not defeat the claim after the protected side waives it.

**B.** This is the dominant trap. It is attractive because Lydia Learning controlled the approval request and neglected to make it. The breaker is the wrong layer. The call asks whether Ruth can refuse to perform. The approval language limited Lydia Learning’s commitment; it did not automatically create a seller-side defense. **Silver Key:** sort whose duty the condition qualifies before treating non-occurrence as breach.

**D.** This choice is attractive because it uses a real reliance-sounding fact: Lydia Learning sold a delivery van to finance the purchase. The breaker is the missing foreseeability link. The stem says Lydia Learning did not anticipate that sale when it entered the contract, so the van sale does not supply the reason Lydia Learning wins.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The source row supplies the official key, pick percentages, and official explanation: the approval condition affected the buyer’s obligation, not the seller’s, and the buyer-side parties could waive the non-occurrence.  The outline mapping is verified at **56050604**, which is the listed Contracts code for “Performance, Breach, and Excuse > Excuse of Condition/Discharge of Duty > Waiver.” 

Lawyer confirmation: Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 225 treats a duty subject to a condition as not due unless the condition occurs or its non-occurrence is excused; the same Restatement commentary recognizes waiver as a way non-occurrence may be excused. ([Open Casebook][1]) Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 84 states the waiver/promise-to-perform principle for a duty despite non-occurrence of a condition. ([Law Insider][2]) Secondary contract authority also states the classic formulation that a condition inserted for a party’s benefit may be waived by that party. ([Chest of Books][3]) No researched authority contradicted the supplied key.
