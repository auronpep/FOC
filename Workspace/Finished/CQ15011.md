## 1. Final question

Daniel owned a private mountain tract called Psalm Ridge. The front portion touched a county road, and the tract had no access to any other road. Eighteen years ago, Daniel conveyed the rear meadow to Ruth and at the same time granted Ruth an express driveway easement across Daniel's retained front land to the county road. Ruth used the driveway until she conveyed the meadow back to Daniel 11 years ago. The deed back to Daniel did not mention the easement. Six years ago, Daniel again conveyed the rear meadow, this time to Lydia, by a deed that did not mention any easement to the county road. Recently, Daniel told Lydia that she could no longer cross Daniel's front land to reach the county road. Naomi, a neighbor, told Lydia that Lydia could cross Naomi's land to reach a different county road if Lydia paid for that right. ## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**D is correct.** Lydia wins because the current access claim comes from the later conveyance of a landlocked parcel, not from Ruth's old express easement. **Gold Key:** an easement by necessity is implied when a common owner severs land and the severance leaves one parcel without legal access to a public road. **Silver Key:** read the chain in two chapters. ## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The source row is Q15011, a Real Property / Rights in Land item keyed to the grantee of the rear parcel because an easement by necessity will be implied. The row supplies measured selection data: 45% chose the credited implied-easement answer, while 36% chose the merger-only answer, making that the dominant trap.

The governing rule is stable. An implied easement by necessity arises when land held in common ownership is severed and the severance leaves one parcel without legal access; the elements are prior unity of ownership and necessity at the time of severance. LII's Wex summary states those elements and explains that a landlocked parcel may carry the easement through later transfers. Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 2.15 states the same principle: a conveyance that would otherwise deprive the conveyed or retained land of rights necessary to reasonable enjoyment implies a servitude unless the parties clearly intended otherwise. See LII, implied easement by necessity, https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/implied_easement_by_necessity; Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 2.15, quoted in John Huron v. Vladimir Kruglyak, https://law.justia.com/cases/tennessee/court-of-appeals/2024/e2022-01812-coa-r3-cv.html.

The old express easement was terminated by merger when Daniel again owned both the dominant and servient parcels. Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 7.5 provides that a servitude terminates when all benefits and burdens come into a single ownership and does not revive merely because the parcels are later separated. That rule is quoted in Pergament v. Loring Properties, Ltd., https://law.justia.com/cases/minnesota/supreme-court/1999/cx981031.html. It confirms why the old express easement does not survive, but it also shows why the credited answer is not based on that old easement. The later conveyance to Lydia created a fresh landlocked severance, so the implied easement by necessity arises then. Naomi's later paid-access offer does not change the necessity that existed at the severance.

The variation preserves the legal issue and credited outcome. Daniel's old express easement to Ruth merges away, but the later deed of the landlocked rear meadow to Lydia implies a new easement by necessity.
