## 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B** is the best answer. The order is an ordinary document-production order. The fact that retrieving and scanning the documents will cost money does not make the order immediately appealable.

**Gold Key:** Collateral-order review is narrow. A discovery order must fit the small class of orders that are effectively unreviewable after final judgment; ordinary discovery-cost complaints do not.

**Silver Key:** In an appealability array, read the fight as **review now vs review later**. Cut the extreme “any/every/never/automatically” answers first, then ask whether final-judgment review is enough.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells the real idea that collateral orders are separate from the merits. The breaker is that separateness is only one prong; the choice turns one prong into the whole test. **Gold Key:** collateral-order review also demands narrowness and effective unreviewability after final judgment.

**C.** This choice tries to exploit the student’s fear of interlocutory appeals by using a total bar. The breaker is the word **never**; ordinary discovery orders generally wait, but later review or extraordinary review can exist in rare cases.

**D.** This choice feels practical because discovery can be costly. The breaker is the word **automatically**; cost and inconvenience do not convert ordinary litigation-management orders into collateral orders.

## 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

Under 28 U.S.C. § 1291, federal courts of appeals generally review final decisions. The collateral order doctrine is a narrow exception for a small class of orders that conclusively determine an important issue separate from the merits and are effectively unreviewable after final judgment. The Supreme Court has emphasized that the doctrine must remain narrow and has held that even disclosure orders adverse to attorney-client privilege do not qualify for immediate appeal when postjudgment review and other mechanisms suffice. That confirms the supplied key: an ordinary nonprivileged document-production order based on expense is not immediately appealable under the collateral order doctrine. ([Justia Law][1])
