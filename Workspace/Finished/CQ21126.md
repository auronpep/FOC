### 1. Final question
At a private Christian homeschool makers' market, Lydia offered Timothy a used cedar bookshelf for his Bible-study nook. While Timothy looked it over, Lydia said the shelf was "the crown jewel of any study room" and "better-looking than anything a furniture store could sell today." Timothy bought it. The shelf was plain but sturdy. Timothy sues Lydia for fraud based only on those statements. Is Timothy likely to establish fraud?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
Can a buyer prove fraud based only on a seller's vague praise about a used item after the buyer inspected it and the item turned out ordinary but usable?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
A is correct. Lydia gave Timothy broad sales praise, not a concrete factual assertion about the shelf. Gold Key: fraud needs a false statement of material fact; ordinary puffery, opinion, or subjective praise usually does not satisfy that false-fact element. Silver Key: first separate a measurable fact from a sales compliment. "Crown jewel" and "better-looking" are praise signals, not inspection-ready fact claims, so the fraud claim fails on the statements identified in the call.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
B is attractive because it treats exaggeration as the same thing as a false fact. The breaker is the Gold Key: fraud needs a false statement of material fact, and ordinary sales puffery does not become actionable just because it is overstated.

C is attractive because it reaches the same "No" result and sounds like it is protecting ordinary sales from fraud claims. The breaker is the absolute word "never": fraud can arise in a sale of goods if the seller makes an actionable false statement of material fact and the other elements are met.

D is attractive because buyer's remorse feels like the harm that made the lawsuit happen. The breaker is that regret is not the missing fraud element; the call asks whether the statements support fraud, and regret does not turn subjective praise into a factual misrepresentation.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested legal issue is unchanged: common-law fraud or fraudulent misrepresentation requires an actionable misrepresentation, and vague subjective sales praise usually is not a false statement of material fact. The source row's economic-torts interference label did not match the tested rule. The deepest valid outline node that fits this tested rule is `62050200`, "Other Torts > Misrepresentation," verified in `OUTLINE_CODES_COMPLETE.md`.

The authority floor is satisfied by Restatement rule numbers. Restatement (Second) of Torts Section 525 states the general fraudulent-misrepresentation liability rule. Restatement (Second) of Torts Section 538A defines an opinion representation as one expressing the maker's belief or judgment rather than certainty about an objective fact. Section 542 addresses when reliance on an opinion is justified, which confirms that opinion/puffery requires special circumstances before it can support liability. Research confirmation sources used: Open Casebook mirror of Restatement (Second) of Torts Section 525, https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/4201-contracts-2022/resources/7.2.6-restatement-2d-of-torts-section-525/; Rodowicz v. Massachusetts Mutual Life Insurance Co., 192 F.3d 162, quoting Restatement Section 538A on opinion, https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/192/162/594010/; Loula v. Snap-On Tools Corp., discussing puffery as seller opinion not actionable as misrepresentation of fact, https://law.justia.com/cases/wisconsin/court-of-appeals/1993/92-1518-6.html.

Lydia's statements that the shelf was the "crown jewel" and "better-looking than anything" are vague and subjective. Timothy inspected the shelf, and the shelf was ordinary but sturdy. Those facts preserve the credited outcome: the buyer is not likely to establish fraud based only on those statements.
