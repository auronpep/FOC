### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Lydia has already accepted and recorded the deed, so the fight is no longer an executory land-sale contract fight about marketable title. Gold Key: after deed acceptance, marketable-title objections generally merge into the deed, and the future covenants of warranty and quiet enjoyment require a disturbance by someone with superior title. ### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested issue remains Real Property, Titles, Covenants of title. The verified outline code is `84040205`, which appears verbatim in `OUTLINE_CODES_COMPLETE.md` as "Titles, Deeds, and Conveyancing > Conveyance by Deed > Covenants of title."

The source key is confirmed. Under the common-law merger doctrine, a buyer who accepts a deed generally loses contract-based title objections, including the implied covenant of marketable title, unless the obligation survives closing. After closing, the buyer's remedies must come from the deed's covenants. A general warranty deed includes present covenants and future covenants, but the answer choice that claims breach of warranty and quiet enjoyment depends on the future covenants. Those covenants are breached only when the grantee suffers an actual or constructive eviction or disturbance by a paramount titleholder. *Brown v. Lober*, 75 Ill. 2d 547 (1979), states the conventional rule that the covenants of warranty and quiet enjoyment are prospective and are not breached by the mere existence of a title problem without a qualifying disturbance by superior title. Research source: https://law.justia.com/cases/illinois/supreme-court/1979/51270-6.html.

The access fact also does not create the missing easement. Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes Section 2.15 states the common-law servitude-by-necessity rule, and its comments explain that common-law necessity servitudes arise from severance of rights held in unity of ownership. Research source: https://www.decoulos.com/kitras2/Restatement_Necessity.pdf. The stem says none of the adjoining parcels ever shared common ownership with the parcel, so no easement by necessity is supplied on these facts. The absence of an easement benefit is not itself an encumbrance burdening the conveyed parcel. Lack of access may make a title unmarketable before closing, but after deed acceptance it does not by itself breach the covenants of warranty or quiet enjoyment. The oral-agreement answer also fails because the parties fully performed and the deed controls the post-closing dispute.
