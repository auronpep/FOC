### 6. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. Ruth’s promise was not merely to reimburse Timothy later. It was to pay the tutoring fee directly to whoever taught him. Lydia fits that described provider class once Timothy hired her. **Gold Key:** intended-beneficiary status turns on the contracting parties’ intent to benefit the provider, not on whether the provider was named or knew about the promise at the start. **Silver Key:** read the array by outcome plus rationale; the correct “yes” answer uses the provider-benefit rationale, while the other “yes” answer uses reliance.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells the strongest trap: the stem highlights that Lydia was unnamed and unaware, so those facts feel disqualifying. The breaker is that those facts address a neighboring issue, such as vesting or later modification, not whether Ruth and Timothy made the tutoring provider an intended beneficiary. **Gold Key:** a future provider can be an intended beneficiary when the contract points payment to that provider class. **Silver Key:** highlighted negative facts do not automatically answer the call.

**B.** This choice sells a familiar writing-requirement trap. The breaker is that the suretyship frame fits a promise made to a creditor or obligee to answer for another’s debt; Ruth’s promise was made to Timothy, the person arranging the lessons. **Gold Key:** the suretyship clause is a creditor-promise frame, not a promise-to-the-debtor frame.

**D.** This choice sells the right outcome through the wrong route. Promissory estoppel would need Lydia to know about and rely on Ruth’s promise when she agreed to teach, but the stem says she did not know of it then. The enforceability route is intended-beneficiary status, not reliance.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

**Verified rule.** Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts §302, a beneficiary is intended when recognizing a right to performance is appropriate to effectuate the parties’ intent and either performance will satisfy the promisee’s obligation to pay the beneficiary or the circumstances show that the promisee intended to give the beneficiary the benefit of the promised performance. ([LexisNexis][1]) Section 302 also distinguishes intended beneficiaries from incidental beneficiaries, and the comments note that a beneficiary need not be the person who furnished consideration or be connected to the transaction in other ways. ([LexisNexis][1]) Cornell’s Wex summary states the same exam-facing point: the beneficiary need not be identifiable when the contract is formed, and an intended beneficiary may enforce even without direct privity. ([Legal Information Institute][2])

**Right-answer application.** Ruth promised Timothy that she would pay the full tutoring fee to whoever taught him. When Timothy hired Lydia on credit, Ruth’s performance would satisfy Timothy’s payment obligation to Lydia. That makes Lydia an intended beneficiary and gives her the enforcement route.

**Suretyship verification.** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §112 states that a contract is not within the suretyship Statute of Frauds unless the promisee is an obligee of the other’s duty, the promisor is a surety, and the promisee knows or has reason to know of the suretyship relation. ([Institute of Law][3]) Restatement §123 states that a contract to discharge a duty owed by the promisee to a third person is not within the suretyship provision, and its illustration covers an oral promise to the debtor to discharge the debt. ([Institute of Law][3])

Title: Future provider beneficiary
Signal: “I will pay whoever provides X.”
Student move: separate provider intent from provider knowledge.
Tiny rule: intent creates beneficiary status; knowledge and reliance affect other issues.
Trap: unnamed/unaware provider.
Confidence: ANCHOR_ASSISTED.

## Gold Key(s)

**GK-CONTRACTS-KOINE-TUTOR-01**
A future provider can be an intended beneficiary even if unnamed and unaware at formation when the promise directs payment to whoever provides the goods or services.
Type: distinction.
Trigger: promise says to pay whoever provides the thing or service.
Tested choice: A.

**GK-CONTRACTS-KOINE-TUTOR-02**
The suretyship Statute of Frauds frame requires a promise to the creditor or obligee to answer for another’s duty; a promise made to the debtor to discharge that debtor’s bill is a different frame.
Type: distinction.
Trigger: promise made to the person who will incur the bill, not to the provider.
Tested choice: B.
