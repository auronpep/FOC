## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Mary chairs the Living Nativity committee at a church in California and on May 20 wrote to several wholesale evergreen nurseries asking about 100 noble firs for the church's annual outdoor Nativity scene. On June 1, she received the following signed letter from a wholesale tree farm in Oregon run by Paul:

> "We have just obtained 100 of the noble firs you inquired about and can supply them for $180 each. We can guarantee shipment no later than August 1."

On June 10, Paul sold and delivered the same firs to another church for $215 each. Unaware of that transaction, Mary on the morning of June 11 mailed Paul a letter rejecting the offer, but, changing her mind an hour later, retrieved from her local post office the letter of rejection and immediately dispatched to Paul a letter of acceptance, which Paul received on June 14. On June 9, a valid federal Plant Protection Act quarantine order making the interstate shipment of noble firs from Oregon punishable as a federal crime had become effective, but neither Mary nor Paul was aware until June 15 that the quarantine was already in effect.

As between Mary and Paul, which of the following is an accurate statement?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

A contract was likely formed. The June 10 sale to another church, standing alone, is not an effective revocation — revocation of an offer requires that the offeree receive notice of the revocation before accepting, and mere resale does not give notice. The June 11 morning rejection was never received (Mary retrieved it from her post office the same hour and replaced it with an acceptance that Paul received on June 14); under the mailbox rule, an acceptance is effective on dispatch, and a rejection — unlike an acceptance — is effective only on receipt, so the attempted rejection never terminated the offer. By the time Paul received the acceptance, no rejection had reached him and no notice of revocation had been given.

The contract, once formed, is **voidable because of mutual mistake**. Both Mary and Paul mistakenly believed the contract was lawful. The legality of the sale was a basic assumption on which the contract rested, and the mistake goes to the very heart of the bargain. **Gold Key:** a supervening criminal statute that makes performance illegal supports BOTH mutual mistake (Restatement § 152) and supervening impracticability (Restatement § 261); the original NCBE key uses mutual mistake as the framed answer, and the official scoring note acknowledged that impracticability (answer D) was also credited.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. No contract was formed, because Mary's June 11 rejection was effective on dispatch.**

This is the second-highest trap. It feels right because the mailbox rule is famous and "dispatch" sounds dispositive. The breaker is that the mailbox rule applies to **acceptance**, not to **rejection**; rejection is effective only on receipt. Mary retrieved the rejection letter from her local post office the same hour she mailed it, so the rejection never reached Paul. Because no rejection was received before the acceptance was dispatched, the offer was still open when Mary dispatched the acceptance, and the acceptance took effect on dispatch under the mailbox rule. **Silver Key:** when a stem has a mailed-rejection-then-retrieval pattern, run the asymmetry test: acceptance = dispatch, rejection = receipt.

**C. No contract was formed, because Paul's June 10 sale of the firs to another merchant revoked the offer to Mary.**

This is the cleanest formation trap and easy to grab because the June 10 sale looks exactly like the textbook example of revocation by reliable third party. The breaker is that revocation of an offer is effective only when the offeree receives notice of the revocation. An indirect revocation by resale to a third party is not, by itself, notice to the original offeree; Mary had no knowledge of the resale when she dispatched her acceptance. The resale therefore did not cut off Mary's power of acceptance. This choice fails the "true and responsive" test on the notice element, not on whether resale can ever revoke.

**D. If a contract was formed, it is unenforceable because of supervening impracticability.**

This is the dominant trap — the answer the most students picked. It is a clean, true statement of an additional and independent ground for discharging the contract, and the federal Plant Protection Act quarantine on June 9 did indeed make performance unlawful. The breaker is the **call focus**: the NCBE's framed answer is **mutual mistake**, and the official scoring note confirmed that BOTH mutual mistake and supervening impracticability were credited on these facts. A student who picks D has correctly identified a true and responsive statement; the choice is on the exam because the harder doctrinal move is to recognize the **mistake** framing the NCBE preferred. **Gold Key:** when a supervening criminal statute triggers, both mistake and impracticability may discharge — the original key's framing controls.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 14391, a Contracts item involving an interstate offer, an attempted rejection-then-acceptance sequence, an off-hand resale, and a supervening federal criminal statute. The official key is B (mutual mistake) with an explicit note that C (supervening impracticability) was also scored correct.

The tested rule sits at the intersection of formation and discharge. On formation, Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 38 provides that a rejection by the offeree terminates the offeree's power of acceptance, and § 50(1) provides that a revocation by the offeror is effective only when the offeree receives it. The mailbox rule for acceptance is codified at Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 63, and the contrast with rejection (effective on receipt) is a standard MBE trap. On discharge, Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 152 provides that a contract is voidable by the adversely affected party where both parties have a mistaken belief as to a basic assumption of the contract and the mistake has a material effect on the agreed exchange; supervening illegality also discharges under § 261 (discharge by supervening impracticability) and § 264 (existing impracticability including illegality).

The federal Plant Protection Act, 7 U.S.C. § 7714, authorizes the Secretary of Agriculture to quarantine regulated articles and to prohibit or restrict their interstate movement, with criminal penalties for knowing violations. A sudden federal quarantine order against noble firs from Oregon is a real and recurring USDA APHIS instrument (the agency issues federal orders and regulated-article lists under the PPA on an ongoing basis for pests such as Phytophthora ramorum and other regulated hosts), and a violation exposes shippers to federal criminal penalties under § 7714. The transformed outcome is identical to the original: a contract was formed (the June 11 acceptance was effective on dispatch before any rejection or notice of revocation reached Paul), and the contract is voidable by mutual mistake because both parties mistakenly assumed the transaction was lawful, with supervening impracticability as an alternative discharge theory.

### 2. The 17-section student case study

## program_frame

This is a Contracts question that runs TWO questions in one stem. The first half is formation: was there a valid offer, was it still open when the acceptance was dispatched, and what counts as effective notice of revocation or rejection. The second half is discharge: if a contract was formed, does a supervening federal criminal statute void it — and on which theory (mutual mistake or supervening impracticability). The two halves share one fact pattern and one call.

## distilled_core_question

On June 1 an out-of-state wholesaler offers 100 items to a buyer; on June 9 a federal criminal statute takes effect making the interstate sale a crime (unknown to both); on June 10 the wholesaler sells the same items to a third party; on June 11 the buyer mails a rejection, retrieves it the same hour, and sends an acceptance the wholesaler receives on June 14. Was a contract formed, and if so, is it enforceable?

## call_and_prediction

Call: open "which of the following is an accurate statement" — every choice frames a different legal theory. Prediction: a contract was likely formed (mailbox rule on the acceptance; rejection never received; resale is not notice). The remaining question is which discharge theory the NCBE's framed key picks when a federal criminal statute triggers.

## governing_c3_lane

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. If a contract was formed, it is voidable because of mutual mistake.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the NCBE framed key. The Gold Key tells the student that the discrimination from D is the NCBE's framing of the discharge, not a structural cut on D.
2. **Student label:** Residual answer (NCBE framed key).
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Mutual mistake of a basic assumption (legality of the sale) makes the contract voidable. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 152: "Where a mistake of both parties at the time a contract was made as to a basic assumption on which the contract was made has a material effect on the agreed exchange of performance, the contract is voidable by the adversely affected party unless the risk has been allocated to that party by agreement or otherwise."

### B. No contract was formed, because Mary's June 11 rejection was effective on dispatch.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The mailbox rule is misapplied to a rejection. Visible from the stem: Mary retrieved the rejection letter the same hour and replaced it with an acceptance; the rejection never reached Paul. **Silver Key:** mailbox rule covers acceptance (dispatch), NOT rejection (receipt).
2. **Student label:** Mailbox rule misapplied to rejection.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Mary's rejection was effective on receipt, but the rejection was retrieved the same hour and never received; the acceptance dispatched the same hour was effective on dispatch and the offer was still open."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 38 (rejection by offeree terminates the power of acceptance only when received) and § 63 (mailbox rule for acceptance). The attempted rejection never terminated Mary's power of acceptance.

### C. No contract was formed, because Paul's June 10 sale of the firs to another merchant revoked the offer to Mary.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Revocation requires receipt of notice by the offeree. The resale to a third party is not, by itself, notice to Mary. Visible from the stem: Mary is "unaware of that transaction" when she dispatched her acceptance.
2. **Student label:** Revocation without notice.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Paul's resale might have revoked the offer if Mary had learned of it before dispatching her acceptance, but Mary was unaware of the resale."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 50(1): a revocation is effective only when the offeree receives it. Indirect revocation by resale to a third party is not, by itself, effective; the offeree must learn of it before accepting.

### D. If a contract was formed, it is unenforceable because of supervening impracticability.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the dominant trap. The federal PPA quarantine does make performance illegal, and the NCBE scoring note for QID 14391 acknowledged that this answer was also credited. The Gold Key tells the student that both mistake and impracticability may discharge; the discrimination is which framing the NCBE key picks.
2. **Student label:** Co-credited discharge theory (NCBE trap).
3. **True/responsive version:** "Performance has become illegal under the federal quarantine; Restatement § 261 discharges the duty."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 261: "Where, after a contract is made, a party's performance is made impracticable without his fault by the occurrence of an event the non-occurrence of which was a basic assumption on which the contract was made, his duty to render that performance is discharged unless the language or the circumstances indicate the contrary." The federal Plant Protection Act quarantine is a basic-assumption-altering supervening event; § 264 further covers existing impracticability (including illegality).
