### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Peter is a nonresident citizen challenging a state law that discriminates against him in a common calling — the commercial sale of books for his livelihood. The direct and ordinary constitutional basis for this claim is the **Privileges and Immunities Clause of Article IV, Section 2** (the "Comity Clause"), which prohibits states from discriminating against citizens of other states with respect to fundamental privileges, including the right to earn a livelihood in a common calling. The Fourteenth Amendment Privileges or Immunities Clause is not the ordinary route here. After the *Slaughter-House Cases* (1873) effectively gutted the Fourteenth Amendment clause, Article IV became the primary constitutional vehicle for nonresident-citizen discrimination claims involving fundamental economic privileges. *Toomer v. Witsell*, 334 U.S. 385 (1948), applied Article IV to strike down a South Carolina statute that charged nonresident commercial shrimp fishermen a license fee 25 times higher than the resident fee. Peter's claim follows the same pattern: a nonresident citizen, a common calling, and state discrimination based on residency. Article IV is the direct lane.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original B) — The dominant trap.** This choice is attractive because the Fourteenth Amendment *does* contain a Privileges or Immunities Clause, and students who have just studied the Reconstruction Amendments may reflexively reach for the Fourteenth Amendment whenever they see "privileges or immunities" language. The trap is the word "every": the Fourteenth Amendment P/I Clause does not control *every* interstate discrimination case. After the *Slaughter-House Cases*, the Fourteenth Amendment clause was narrowly construed to protect only rights of *national* citizenship (e.g., the right to travel to the seat of government), not the broad range of fundamental state-citizen privileges. Article IV is the proper clause for nonresident discrimination in common callings. The choice overclaims the scope of the Fourteenth Amendment clause.

**D (original C).** This choice is attractive because it seems to deny the plaintiff any remedy, which might appeal to students who are unsure which clause applies and default to "nothing protects this guy." But it is flatly wrong: Article IV *does* protect nonresident citizens regarding fundamental privileges, including the right to pursue a common calling. *Toomer v. Witsell* and *United Building & Construction Trades Council v. Camden*, 465 U.S. 208 (1984), confirm this. The statement that "no constitutional provision protects nonresident citizens" is simply not the law.

**B (original D).** This choice is attractive because it introduces a seemingly technical condition — corporate status — that sounds like a bar-exam-worthy distinction. But corporations are *not* "citizens" under Article IV, and the Fourteenth Amendment P/I Clause is not the ordinary route for *any* nonresident discrimination claim in a common calling. The corporate-plaintiff angle is a misfit: it distracts from the real issue (which clause applies to a natural-person nonresident citizen) by introducing an entity-status question that the original question never raised.
