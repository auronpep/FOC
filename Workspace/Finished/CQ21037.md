## 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. The call asks whether there is sufficient evidence of knowledge at the time Lydia received the goods. Silver Key: do not isolate one suspicious fact when the call asks about sufficient evidence; stack the receipt-time facts and ask what they collectively point toward. Gold Key: knowledge can be satisfied when the actor is aware of a high probability that the property is stolen and deliberately avoids confirming it. Here the cheap bulk sale, retail anti-theft wraps, store inventory stickers, late-night setting, cash payment, and "do not ask" exchange support the inference that Lydia knew or deliberately avoided knowing the speakers were stolen.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

### A

The attraction is that low price by itself can feel too thin for a mental-state finding. The breaker is that this answer shrinks the evidence to one fact when the stem gives a stack: anti-theft wraps, inventory stickers, the stranger's warning, Lydia's refusal to know, cash, and timing.

### C

The attraction is that resale sounds like the act that would prove she meant to profit from stolen goods. The breaker is that the receiving offense is complete when she knowingly receives or controls the stolen property; a later resale is not the knowledge trigger.

### D

The attraction is that an express confession by the seller would make the case easy. Gold Key: direct admission is not required; knowledge may be inferred from circumstances and deliberate avoidance. The breaker is that the law does not require the seller to say the goods were stolen.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: receiving stolen property requires knowing or believing the property was stolen at receipt, and knowledge may be shown circumstantially, including by deliberate avoidance of obvious facts. The federal stolen-goods statute, 18 U.S.C. 2315, uses the phrase "knowing the same to have been stolen" for receipt, possession, concealment, sale, or disposal of covered stolen goods. Global-Tech Appliances, Inc. v. SEB S.A., 563 U.S. 754 (2011), describes willful blindness as requiring a subjective belief in a high probability that a fact exists plus deliberate actions to avoid learning that fact. Matter of Deang, 27 I&N Dec. 57 (BIA 2017), cites generic receipt-of-stolen-property authority, Model Penal Code section 223.6(1), and the criminal-law willful-blindness line to distinguish knowledge or belief from mere reason-to-believe negligence.
