## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Tested rule:** A state constitution may provide broader criminal-procedure protections than the federal Constitution. If state high-court precedent clearly establishes that the police conduct violated the defendant's state constitutional rights and that suppression is the proper remedy, a state court should suppress the evidence even if federal law would not require suppression.

**Federal recording baseline:** In *United States v. White*, 401 U.S. 745 (1971), the Supreme Court held that the government's use of an informant who may reveal or electronically transmit conversations with the accused does not violate the Fourth Amendment merely because the informant uses recording or transmitting equipment.

**State constitutional floor-and-ceiling rule:** In *Oregon v. Hass*, 420 U.S. 714 (1975), the Court recognized that a state is free, as a matter of its own law, to impose greater restrictions on police activity than federal constitutional standards require. In *California v. Greenwood*, 486 U.S. 35 (1988), the Court likewise recognized that states may construe their own constitutions to impose more stringent constraints on police conduct than the federal Constitution imposes.

**Wrong-party federal-rights point:** *Rakas v. Illinois*, 439 U.S. 128 (1978), confirms that Fourth Amendment rights are personal and may not be asserted vicariously. That supports rejecting the distractor that tries to make Lydia's federal rights the basis for Daniel's suppression motion.

**Application:** Federal law alone would not necessarily suppress Daniel's recorded admission. But the stem adds a clear state constitutional rule from the state's highest court and says exclusion is the proper remedy. The state court therefore should grant Daniel's motion under state constitutional law. The tested rule, legal issue, trap structure, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row.
