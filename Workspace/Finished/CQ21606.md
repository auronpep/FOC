### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**Gold Key:** Under the majority common-law rule (and the MPC for non-felonies), once a bilateral agreement to commit a crime is formed, an overt act by *any one* conspirator in furtherance of the agreement satisfies the overt-act element for *every* member of the conspiracy. The act need not be criminal, need not be close to the completed offense, and need not be known to or authorized by the other conspirators. The agreement itself is what binds each member to every co-conspirator's qualifying act.

Timothy and Lydia formed a genuine bilateral agreement to steal the fixture — that satisfies the agreement element. Lydia's reconnaissance drive past the shuttered church to check the lock was an outward act done in furtherance of the planned theft — that satisfies the overt-act element. It doesn't matter that Timothy never left the reunion and never knew about the drive. The conspiracy agreement is what makes him liable for Lydia's act. He is guilty of conspiracy.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation only):** Model Penal Code § 5.03(1), (5); *United States v. Shabani*, 513 U.S. 10 (1995) (federal conspiracy statute 18 U.S.C. § 371 requires overt act); Wex, Cornell LII, "overt act" (conspiracy context); *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640 (1946) (co-conspirator liability for acts of others in furtherance of conspiracy).

**Governed rule:** Conspiracy — overt-act requirement under the majority rule.

**Elements of conspiracy (majority rule):**
1. Bilateral agreement between two or more persons to commit a crime
2. Specific intent to agree + specific intent to accomplish the criminal objective
3. Overt act in furtherance of the conspiracy by *any one* conspirator

**Authority:**

- **18 U.S.C. § 371** (federal conspiracy): requires an overt act. *United States v. Shabani*, 513 U.S. 10 (1995) (confirming overt-act requirement in § 371).
- **Wex (Cornell LII), "overt act":** "In the context of conspiracy charges, an overt act is an action taken by one or more individuals to further the illegal agreement … It is important to note that the overt act doesn't have to be the actual commission of the crime; it can be any act that demonstrates the individuals' commitment to the conspiracy's objectives."

- **USLegal, "Overt Act – Conspiracy":** "A single overt act before termination of the conspiracy committed by one of the co-conspirators is sufficient."

The item is anchor-assisted: the student needs a Gold Key to override the powerful fairness intuition that personal action is required for criminal liability.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Timothy and Lydia are both deacons at a rural church. They agree to steal a baptistry fixture from a shuttered church across town and resell it for cash. The next day, Lydia drives past the shuttered church twice to check whether the side door lock is rusted. Neither Timothy nor Lydia does anything else before they are arrested three days later. The jurisdiction requires an overt act for conspiracy and applies the majority rule. Timothy was at a family reunion the entire week and had no contact with Lydia after their agreement.

**Call:** Is Timothy guilty of conspiracy to commit theft of the fixture?

**Choices:**
- A) No, because Lydia's drive-by was only preparation and cannot satisfy the overt-act requirement
- B) No, because Timothy personally took no overt act in furtherance of the conspiracy
- C) Yes, but only if the prosecution can prove Timothy knew about Lydia's reconnaissance drive
- D) Yes, because Lydia's act of reconnaissance satisfied the overt-act requirement for the entire conspiracy

#### Distilled Core Question

Two people agree to commit a theft. One co-conspirator does a surveillance act. The other is absent and never learns of the act. Is the absent co-conspirator guilty of conspiracy under the majority rule?

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks whether Timothy is guilty of conspiracy. The key facts are: (1) bilateral agreement to steal, (2) Lydia performed a reconnaissance act, (3) Timothy was absent and never knew of the act, (4) majority rule applies. #### Wrong-Answer Recovery Paths

**If a student picked A (preparation trap):**
- Misconception: Conspiracy requires the same "more than preparation" standard as attempt.
- Recovery: Teach the distinction — conspiracy's overt act can be purely preparatory. Attempt requires a substantial step. The conspiracy bar is deliberately lower because the agreement itself is the dangerous act.
- Drill: Give the student three acts (phone call, drive-by, buying supplies) and ask which qualify as overt acts for conspiracy. All three do.

**If a student picked B (dominant trap — fairness trap):**
- Misconception: Each conspirator must personally commit an overt act.
- Recovery: Teach the co-conspirator binding principle — the agreement is what makes each member liable for the acts of the others. Use an analogy: when you join a team, you're bound by what the team does, even if you're not the one doing it.
- Drill: Give the student a scenario where three people agree to commit a crime and only one acts. Ask if all three are guilty of conspiracy. Yes.

**If a student picked C (knowledge trap):**
- Misconception: Each conspirator must know about the specific overt act.
- Recovery: Teach that the overt act element is about proving the conspiracy is real (not just talk), not about giving notice to each member. The agreement is the basis of liability, not knowledge of each act.
- Drill: Ask the student to identify which element each fact in the stem relates to. The "Timothy never knew" fact is a distractor — it doesn't relate to any element of conspiracy.
