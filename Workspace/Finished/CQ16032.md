## 7. Right-Answer Explanation

**B — No, because the ordinary person would not have been violently enraged** is correct.

Voluntary manslaughter requires that the killing resulted from heat of passion produced by adequate provocation. The provocation must be of a kind that would arouse sudden and intense passion in a **person of ordinary temperament** — this is an objective standard. A defendant's individual peculiarities (a hot temper, extreme emotional sensitivity, obsessive attachment to a role) are legally irrelevant to whether the provocation was adequate.

Losing a church treasurer vote is simply not the kind of provocation that would drive an ordinary person to fatal violence. The adequacy of the provocation is tested against the hypothetical ordinary person, not against Solomon's unusual emotional state. The heat-of-passion theory therefore fails the objective prong, and the charge cannot be reduced to voluntary manslaughter.

**Gold Key:** Voluntary manslaughter's heat-of-passion standard is OBJECTIVE. The defendant's own peculiar temperament or emotional makeup is legally irrelevant. If an ordinary person would not have been fatally enraged by this provocation, the theory fails — no matter how intensely the actual defendant felt.

**Silver Key:** When the defense argues heat of passion, ask: "Would an ordinary person — not this particular defendant — have been provoked to fatal violence by this specific event?" Losing a vote, seeing a dance, receiving a public criticism — these are inadequate provocations under the objective standard, regardless of how extreme the defendant's personal reaction was.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Tested rule:** Common law voluntary manslaughter — heat of passion. To reduce murder to voluntary manslaughter on a heat-of-passion theory, the defendant must: (1) have been provoked; (2) the provocation must have been of a kind that would arouse sudden and intense passion in a person of ORDINARY temperament (the objective provocation standard); (3) there must have been insufficient time to cool off; and (4) the passion must have actually caused the killing.

**Objective standard:** The adequacy of provocation is measured against the reasonable or ordinary person, not against the particular defendant. See Wayne R. LaFave, *Substantive Criminal Law* § 15.2(b) (3d ed.) ("Most courts have held that the law will not recognize an unusual mental condition of the defendant as making adequate what would otherwise be inadequate provocation."). The recognized categories of adequate provocation at common law include: being subjected to mutual combat, a serious battery, unlawful arrest, witnessing a close relative being assaulted, and discovery of a spouse in adultery. Losing an organizational vote does not fall within any recognized category of adequate provocation.

**Application:** Solomon had an intense personal attachment to the treasurer role — a purely idiosyncratic emotional condition. A person of ordinary temperament, even one who cared about their position, would not be provoked to fatal violence by being voted out in a business meeting. The objective standard is not met, and the heat-of-passion theory therefore fails. The murder charge is not reducible to voluntary manslaughter on this theory.

**Distractor A (deliberate provocation theory):** No such doctrine exists in common law manslaughter. The phrase "deliberate provocation" appears in some discussions of comparative fault or contributory negligence, but there is no recognized criminal law doctrine by this name that would reduce murder to manslaughter from the provoked party's perspective. This is a fabricated_rule distractor.

**Distractor C (mistaken justification):** This is a real doctrine: a defendant who kills under a sincere but mistaken belief that the killing was legally justified (e.g., mistaken self-defense) may have the charge reduced from murder to manslaughter (imperfect self-defense, or "unreasonable belief" in the necessity of defensive force). Nothing in the stem indicates Solomon believed he was justified. This is a bait_doctrine distractor — real rule, wrong facts.

**MPC note (lawyer-confirmation only):** The MPC uses a different standard under § 210.3(1)(b) — "extreme mental or emotional disturbance for which there is a reasonable explanation or excuse, the reasonableness of which is to be determined from the viewpoint of a person in the actor's situation." The MPC standard gives somewhat more weight to the actor's situation but still requires an objective reasonableness component. The MBE tests common law unless the question specifies otherwise; this question uses the common law standard.

**Authority:**
- LaFave, *Substantive Criminal Law* § 15.2(b) (3d ed.) (objective provocation standard).
- MPC § 210.3(1)(b) (extreme emotional disturbance — distinct from common law, not applicable here).
- *People v. Berry*, 18 Cal. 3d 509 (1976) (discussing adequate provocation); *Girouard v. State*, 321 Md. 532 (1991) (verbal provocation alone insufficient at common law).

**Choice B — No, because the ordinary person would not have been violently enraged** ← *CORRECT*

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:**

**Gold Key:** Objective standard. Ordinary person. Solomon's feelings don't change the legal test.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:**

Losing an organizational vote falls outside all recognized adequate-provocation categories (mutual combat, serious battery, unlawful arrest, discovering adultery, assault of a close relative). LaFave § 15.2.

**Choice C — No, on the theory of mistaken justification**

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:**
Mistaken justification requires the defendant to have sincerely believed the killing was legally justified (e.g., mistaken self-defense). Solomon didn't believe he was justified — he acted in blind, uncontrolled rage. The doctrine's own condition is absent from the facts. Right area (manslaughter doctrines), wrong specific variant.

**Gold Key:** The applicable theory here is the objective provocation standard, not mistaken justification. Mistaken justification requires evidence of a mistaken belief in justification — which this stem doesn't supply.

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like:**

ISSUE_SENSE / bait_doctrine. Mistaken justification (imperfect self-defense): defendant kills sincerely but mistakenly believing the killing was legally authorized — may reduce to manslaughter. No such belief in the stem. The doctrine is real; the application to these facts is wrong.

**Choice D — Yes, on the theory of deliberate provocation**

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:**
"Deliberate provocation" is not a recognized legal doctrine in manslaughter law. The phrase does not appear in any codified common law rule or MPC provision as a theory that would reduce murder to manslaughter from the provoked party's perspective. The theory does not exist; cut immediately.

**(2) Student label:** CUT — fabricated_rule: no such doctrine exists

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like:**
For D to be true, there would need to be a recognized manslaughter doctrine that reduces a killing's charge specifically because the victim deliberately provoked the defendant. No such doctrine exists. Even when a victim deliberately provokes, the legal effect runs to comparative fault or contributory negligence concepts in civil law, not to manslaughter reduction in criminal law — and those doctrines operate differently.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:**
EAR_OVERCLAIM / fabricated_rule. "Deliberate provocation" is not a doctrine in common law manslaughter or MPC homicide. No authority supports this theory as a basis for reduction to manslaughter. Purely invented.
