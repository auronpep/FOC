### 6. Student black-letter-law answer explanation
C is correct. Receiving stolen property requires property that is actually stolen when the defendant receives it. Timothy believed he was buying a stolen keyboard, but the police had already recovered the keyboard from Esther and notified the owner. That gives Timothy his strongest defense: the keyboard had lost its stolen-property status before Timothy bought it.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. The charge is receiving stolen property, so the most direct defense attacks an element of that offense: the property must be stolen when the defendant receives it. The keyboard had been stolen when Esther first brought it to Barnabas, but the police then recovered it, arrested Esther, and notified the owner. By the time Timothy bought it the next day, the keyboard was in lawful police-controlled custody and was being used as a decoy. Timothy's belief that it was stolen is damaging, but belief alone is not the same thing as receiving property that is still stolen.

**Gold Key:** For receiving stolen property, ask whether the property is actually still stolen at the moment of receipt. Once the owner or police have recovered it and use it as a decoy, the strongest defense is that the stolen-property element is missing.

**Silver Key:** On a "most effective argument" call, start with the answer that attacks an element of the charged crime before reaching for affirmative defenses or third-party-rights arguments.

The argument is not guaranteed in every modern jurisdiction because some statutes and attempt doctrines focus on the defendant's belief. But on the common-law MBE framing, C is the only answer that directly undermines the receiving-stolen-property charge.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
**A is attractive** because Barnabas cooperated only after he was arrested and promised reduced charges. The breaker is that this is Barnabas's pressure point, not Timothy's defense. Timothy generally cannot defend his own charge by asserting that a third person's cooperation agreement was coerced, and the answer does not attack the stolen-property element or Timothy's mens rea.

**B is attractive** because the final sale happened at the direction of an undercover officer through Barnabas, a police helper. The breaker is Timothy's own opening move: he came to the booth and said he wanted to buy a stolen keyboard. **Gold Key:** the missing stolen-property element is the stronger defense; the entrapment theory fails because the facts show predisposition rather than police inducement of an otherwise unwilling person. **Silver Key:** do not let police involvement alone pull you off the charged-crime element.

**D is attractive** because it sounds like a rights-protection answer: the police made a deal with Barnabas, so maybe Timothy's rights were traded away. The breaker is that no right of Timothy's was bargained away. The agreement reduced Barnabas's charges in exchange for cooperation; it did not waive Timothy's constitutional rights, trial rights, or defense rights.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. The item tests receipt of stolen property under the common-law/Jaffe recovered-property problem: the defendant's best argument is that the property was no longer stolen when he received it because it had already been recovered by the police and held for the owner.

Authority floor: Model Penal Code section 223.6 identifies receiving stolen property as receiving, retaining, or disposing of stolen movable property with the required culpable mental state. The common-law recovered-property rule is illustrated by *People v. Jaffe*, 185 N.Y. 497 (1906), where the New York Court of Appeals treated goods restored to the owner and delivered as a decoy as having lost their stolen character. Source: https://www.nycourts.gov/reporter/archives/p_jaffe.htm

The modern caveat is real but does not change the MBE answer. In *People v. Rojas*, 55 Cal.2d 252 (1961), the California Supreme Court recognized that goods recovered by police no longer had stolen status for the completed receiving offense, but it modified the conviction to attempted receiving stolen property. Source: https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/people-v-rojas-24303

Those authorities confirm the source key as the most effective defense to the charged completed offense. Timothy's belief and conduct may support attempt liability in some jurisdictions, but he was charged with receiving stolen property. The best listed defense is therefore that the keyboard was no longer stolen property when Timothy received it.
