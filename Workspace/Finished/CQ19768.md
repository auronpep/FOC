# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question

Barnabas Printworks sent Lydia, who owned a private Christian retreat-supply shop, a signed letter stating: "We will sell you Scripture-memory card decks at $2 per deck for as many decks as you would like to purchase, up to 40,000 decks per quarter." Lydia did not stop negotiating with other printers or otherwise change her position because of the letter. She did, however, sign and return a note stating: "I accept your offer to sell any card decks we need to me for $2 per deck. We will probably order some decks before the fall retreat season."

Six months later, Lydia ordered 18,000 card decks at $2 per deck. Barnabas replied that, because paper and freight costs had risen outside its control, the price was now $2.75 per deck. Lydia sued Barnabas for breach of contract.

May Lydia recover?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A seller signs a goods price quote for as many units as the buyer wants, and the buyer signs a response saying it will probably order some if needed. Six months later, can the buyer enforce the old price after placing an order?

**D.** Yes, because Barnabas's price quote was in writing and signed by the seller.

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Lydia's response did not bind her to buy any card decks from Barnabas. She said she accepted the price for any decks she needed and would probably order some, but she never promised to buy all of her requirements from Barnabas, any minimum quantity, or even one deck. That made her apparent promise illusory.

**Gold Key:** A buyer's "I will buy what I need if I choose to order" language is not a requirements contract unless the buyer commits to buy its requirements from that seller. Without that commitment, the buyer's words do not supply consideration for the seller's price promise.

**Silver Key:** When the answer set points to signatures and later price changes, ask the quantity-commitment question first: what did the buyer actually promise to buy? If the answer is "nothing definite and not all requirements," the signature traps cannot create consideration.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A.** This choice sells the ordinary-acceptance instinct: Lydia signed a note saying "I accept," so a contract must have formed. The breaker is that the signed note still left Lydia free to buy nothing from Barnabas. **Gold Key:** acceptance language does not matter unless the buyer's return promise is real consideration, and this response did not commit Lydia to any quantity or to her actual requirements.

**B.** This choice borrows the supplier's business reason and makes the case about excuse. That feels practical because the stem gives a real cost increase, but the price-change reason does not decide the formation problem. Lydia loses before excuse: without consideration from her side, there was no enforceable contract fixing the $2 price.

**D.** This is the dominant trap. It notices the signed seller writing and reaches for the UCC firm-offer idea. **Gold Key:** UCC 2-205 requires a signed merchant writing that gives assurance the offer will be held open, and any no-time-stated irrevocability period cannot exceed three months. Barnabas's letter gave no hold-open assurance, and Lydia ordered six months later.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule is unchanged from the source item: an illusory promise does not supply consideration, and a buyer's statement that it will buy goods only if it chooses to order them is not a binding return promise. Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 77 is the authority floor for the illusory-promise rule. The requirements-contract contrast is UCC 2-306: a true requirements term measures quantity by the buyer's actual good-faith requirements. The firm-offer contrast is UCC 2-205: a merchant's signed goods offer is irrevocable without consideration only when the writing gives assurance it will be held open, and if no time is stated the irrevocability period cannot exceed three months.

That confirms the credited outcome. Lydia did not promise to buy all card decks she required from Barnabas, did not promise any minimum quantity, and did not rely on the letter. Her signed response therefore did not provide consideration for Barnabas's price quote. UCC 2-306 does not convert this into a requirements contract, and UCC 2-205 does not preserve the quote for a six-month-later order.
