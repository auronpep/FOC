## 1. Final Question

Lydia is a student at a private Christian academy. She asked her Sunday school teacher, Deacon Paul, whether it would be a crime to log into the academy's grading portal using her own credentials and change her own math grade. Deacon Paul told her that "unauthorized access to any computer system is a crime under the computer-trespass statute" and that it was a serious offense. In fact, Deacon Paul was incorrect: the applicable statute in the jurisdiction defines computer trespass as "the unauthorized access of a computer system **belonging to another person**." Believing what Deacon Paul told her, Lydia logged into her own academy account and changed her math grade from a C to an A, thinking she was committing a crime so that she could later be caught and face legal consequences as a form of self-sacrifice. ## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

A person is guilty of criminal attempt when, with the specific intent to bring about a result that is criminally prohibited, she comes substantially close to bringing about that result. Here, the applicable statute defines computer trespass as unauthorized access of a computer system **belonging to another person**. Lydia accessed only her own account. Because the result she specifically intended to bring about — accessing her own account — is not criminally prohibited by the computer-trespass statute, she cannot be guilty of attempted computer trespass. **Gold Key:** Attempt requires specific intent to accomplish a result that is *actually* a crime; intending to do something that is not a crime, no matter how firmly you believe it is, cannot support an attempt conviction. C is correct.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap):** This choice states a true rule — a mistake of fact is generally not a defense to attempt — and that rule sounds like it should resolve the question. The attraction is that students hear "mistake + attempt" and lock onto the familiar principle without noticing that Lydia's error was a mistake of *law* (she misunderstood the scope of the statute), not a mistake of *fact* (she knew she was accessing her own account). The breaker: the rule this choice states is accurate for mistake-of-fact problems, but it does not apply to a defendant whose intended conduct is simply not criminal. **Gold Key:** The mistake-of-fact rule only applies when the defendant's factual misunderstanding would still result in a crime if the facts were as the defendant believed — here, even if the law were as Lydia believed, her *intended conduct* (accessing her own account) would still not be a crime under the statute as written.

**B:** This choice appeals to common sense: Lydia relied on a trusted authority figure, so she should be excused. The attraction is the moral intuition that someone who sought guidance and got bad advice deserves lenity. The breaker: reasonable reliance on an advisor's legal opinion — even an attorney's — is not a defense to attempt. What matters is whether the defendant specifically intended to bring about a criminally prohibited result, not whether she believed her conduct was prohibited. **Gold Key:** Attempt turns on the objective criminality of the intended result, not the defendant's subjective belief that the result was criminal.

**D:** This choice invents a rule that attempt is "subsumed" in a related substantive crime (academic fraud). The attraction is that students may reason Lydia's real goal was academic fraud, so the attempt charge should merge. The breaker: there is no such subsumption doctrine. A defendant can be convicted of attempt even when a related substantive crime exists, and the charge here is attempted computer trespass, not attempted academic fraud. **Gold Key:** The existence of a different potential crime does not eliminate liability for attempt on the charged offense — and in any event, Lydia's intended conduct was not criminal at all.

## 8. Black-Letter Legal Verification

**Governing rule:** Criminal attempt requires the specific intent to commit the target offense — that is, the specific intent to bring about a result that is criminally prohibited. See Model Penal Code § 5.01(1)(a) (person is guilty of attempt if, acting with the kind of culpability required for the crime, she "purposely engages in conduct which would constitute the crime if the attendant circumstances were as she believes them to be"); *People v. Rizzo*, 246 N.Y. 334, 158 N.E. 888 (N.Y. 1927) (attempt requires specific intent to commit the target crime plus acts dangerously near its accomplishment).

**Application to this item:** Lydia's intended conduct — accessing her own account — is not prohibited by the computer-trespass statute, which requires access to a system "belonging to another person." Because the result she intended to bring about was not criminally prohibited, she lacked the specific intent required for attempt. The outcome is unchanged from the original: the defendant cannot be guilty of attempt.

**Mistake of law is no defense to attempt:** It is well established that a defendant's mistaken belief that her conduct is criminal — even if that belief was formed reasonably or on the advice of counsel — does not supply the specific intent required for attempt. The specific-intent requirement looks to whether the *intended result* is actually criminal, not whether the defendant believed it was. See MPC § 5.01; *People v. Rizzo*, supra.

#### Distilled Core Question

A defendant asked a trusted advisor whether her planned conduct was a crime. The advisor incorrectly said it was. The applicable statute criminalizes only conduct directed at property belonging to another person, but the defendant's planned conduct involved only her own property. She then performed the conduct, believing it was a crime. Charged with attempt. Guilty?

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks whether Lydia should be found guilty of attempted computer trespass. The call is binary (guilty / not guilty) with a "should" framing. The correct prediction: **not guilty**, because Lydia lacked specific intent to bring about a criminally prohibited result. The statute requires access to a system belonging to another person; Lydia accessed her own account.

4. Lydia accessed her *own* account and changed her *own* grade. (Establishes she acted on her own property.)
5. Lydia believed she was committing a crime. (Establishes subjective belief — but belief alone is insufficient.)
