### 1. Final question
A valid warrant was issued for Ruth's arrest. Police learned that a person with Ruth's name and description owned a small cottage near an old hymn-singing retreat road. When officers arrived, the cottage looked empty: plywood covered the windows, the mailbox was stuffed, and weeds filled the path. A neighbor said the cottage was Ruth's but that Ruth had not been seen there for months. Officers knocked and shouted, "Police, open up." No one answered. They pried away the plywood, broke the door, and entered. They found no person inside, but they did find an illegal short-barreled shotgun. When Ruth returned weeks later, she was charged with unlawful possession of the shotgun.

Ruth moves to suppress the shotgun. Should the court grant the motion?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
Choice C is correct. The arrest warrant did not give officers a roving license to enter when the visible facts and neighbor information gave them no reason to think Ruth was inside. Silver Key: Do not stop at valid warrant; ask whether the entry facts satisfy the warrant limit. Gold Key: An arrest warrant carries limited authority to enter the suspect home only when officers have reason to believe the suspect is within.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
- A: The broken-door fact draws attention because the entry was forceful. The breaker is that force would not be the decisive problem if the officers had reason to believe Ruth was inside.
- B: The valid-warrant answer is the dominant trap because it starts with a real warrant. The breaker is the Gold Key: a valid arrest warrant still needs reason to believe the suspect is inside before home entry.
- D: The abandonment answer uses the neglected cottage facts to make the search feel harmless. The breaker is that boarded windows and weeds do not by themselves erase Ruth's privacy interest.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
For Fourth Amendment purposes, an arrest warrant founded on probable cause gives limited authority to enter the suspect dwelling when there is reason to believe the suspect is within. Without that reason, the forced entry is unlawful and the discovered evidence is suppressible. Authority: Payton v. New York, 445 U.S. 573 (1980). The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged.
