## 1. Final question
At a private lakeside retreat cabin, Martha owned a hand-carved cedar canoe used by weekend guests. She contracted with Paul, a Christian woodworker, to refinish the canoe for $4,000. Before finishing the job, Paul used the wrong varnish, permanently dulling the cedar and lowering the canoe's market value. Martha sued Paul in federal court, alleging breach of contract and seeking an injunction to stop Paul from applying any more varnish and money damages for the diminution in value that had already resulted. The suit was properly based on diversity jurisdiction. Paul made a timely request for a jury trial. Does Paul have a Seventh Amendment right to a jury trial on the claims?

A: No, Paul does not have a right to a jury trial as to any claim because the case is essentially equitable because of the request for injunctive relief.
B: Yes, Paul has a right to a jury trial as to every claim, regardless of whether they are equitable in nature.
C: Yes, Paul has a right to a jury trial as to every claim because the issues are common to both the legal and equitable claims.
D: No, Paul does not have a right to a jury trial as to any claim because none of them bear exclusively upon equitable relief.

## 6. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)
The credited answer is New C. Martha seeks money damages for the canoe's already-decreased market value, which is a legal remedy, and she also seeks an injunction to stop further varnish work, which is equitable relief. The same facts about Paul's work, the wrong varnish, and the resulting damage matter to both forms of relief. Because Paul timely requested a jury, the common factual issues must be tried to a jury.

**Gold Key:** In a federal case with both legal damages and equitable relief, common factual issues are tried to the jury when a jury has been timely and properly requested; purely equitable issues remain for the judge.

**Silver Key:** Sort the remedies first: money damages = legal, injunction = equitable. Then ask whether the same facts decide both. If they do, the common facts go to the jury before the judge handles any purely equitable relief.

New B is too broad because it says Paul gets a jury on every claim regardless of equitable nature. The correct rule is narrower: the common issues shared by the legal and equitable claims go to the jury. New C is correct because it ties the jury right to the common issues, not to a blanket rule for every claim.
## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations
**A (New A — dominant trap):** This choice is attractive because an injunction is equitable, and students often remember that equity belongs to the judge. **B (New B):** This choice is attractive because it notices the legal damages claim and swings to the opposite extreme. It is defeated because it says Paul gets a jury on every claim regardless of equitable nature. The rule is not that equitable issues become legal just because they are joined with legal damages; only the issues common to both legal and equitable claims go to the jury.

**D (New D):** This choice is attractive because it uses technical language about claims bearing exclusively upon equitable relief. It is defeated because the reason points the wrong way. The fact that the issues are not purely equitable is exactly why the jury right survives for the common issues. The choice denies the jury right for the reason that should preserve it.
## 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The credited answer is correct and the tested rule, legal issue, and outcome are identical to the source question. The original source key was New D's mechanic; the post-shuffle key is New C.

Governing authority: U.S. Const. amend. VII preserves the right to jury trial in suits at common law. The Supreme Court's law/equity merger line protects that right when legal and equitable claims are joined. In Beacon Theatres, Inc. v. Westover, 359 U.S. 500 (1959), the Court held that legal issues cannot be lost through prior judge-only determination of equitable claims except in the most imperative circumstances. In Dairy Queen, Inc. v. Wood, 369 U.S. 469 (1962), the Court held that a claim for money damages required jury trial even though joined with equitable relief, because the legal claim could not be dismissed as merely incidental. Ross v. Bernhard, 396 U.S. 531 (1970), further confirms that the jury right turns on the nature of the issues to be tried, not the procedural label of the overall case.
