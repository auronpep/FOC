## 7. Right-Answer Explanation

Officers watched Timothy commit an armed felony (assault with a deadly weapon) and immediately gave chase. Timothy fled continuously into the cabin and closed the door — but the chase never paused.

**Gold Key GK-CRIMINAL-HOT-PURSUIT-01:** When police are in immediate, continuous hot pursuit of a fleeing felon, the exigency justifies warrantless entry into a dwelling. The suspect's act of closing a door does not reinstate the warrant requirement — allowing any fleeing felon to defeat hot pursuit by stepping inside would gut the doctrine.

**Silver Key SK-CRIMINAL-HOT-PURSUIT-SCOPE-01:** The scope of hot pursuit is limited to locating and arresting the suspect. Officers entered the cabin, went directly to the bathroom, and arrested Timothy without searching cabinets, drawers, or unrelated rooms. That limited scope confirms the entry is justified.

Choice C states both the justification and the scope limitation correctly. It is the only choice that is true and responsive.

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A — "Yes, because hot pursuit also allowed officers to search every cabinet in the cabin for evidence."**

The cognitive move this choice sells: the student gets to "Yes" correctly — hot pursuit justified the entry — and then reasons that if they can enter at all, the whole cabin is fair game. An "everything is justified" extension feels logical once you've accepted the entry was valid.

The breaker: hot pursuit justifies entry to locate and apprehend the fleeing suspect, not a general evidence search. Scope is the dispositive limitation. Extending entry rights to "every cabinet" is an overclaim of the doctrine — a false absolute. **Silver Key SK-CRIMINAL-HOT-PURSUIT-SCOPE-01:** cut any "Yes" choice that adds "also allowed full search" — hot pursuit scope = capture only.

**Choice B — "No, because officers were required to pause outside and seek a warrant once Timothy pulled the cabin door shut." (DOMINANT TRAP)**

The cognitive move: students know homes enjoy the highest Fourth Amendment protection and that officers generally may not cross a threshold without a warrant. When Timothy shut the door, he seemingly "locked in" his protected zone — and a student who respects the home-as-castle idea stops the officers at the door.

The breaker: the door-closing does not interrupt an active, continuous hot pursuit. The hot pursuit began the moment Timothy fled the market; it was unbroken when he entered the cabin. Pausing to secure a warrant would enable any fleeing felon to defeat exigent circumstances simply by stepping inside. **Gold Key GK-CRIMINAL-HOT-PURSUIT-01** kills this trap: the closed door does not reinstate the warrant requirement during immediate pursuit.

**Choice D — "No, because police may never enter a home without a warrant."**

The cognitive move: students who learned the black-letter rule that homes are the most protected Fourth Amendment space may overstate it as absolute — "police can NEVER enter without a warrant." This is the rule stripped of its exceptions.

The breaker: exigent circumstances — including hot pursuit — are well-established exceptions to the warrant requirement. "Never" is the false absolute here. The rule is: police generally need a warrant to enter a home, *unless* an exception applies. Hot pursuit is one.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Tested rule:** Police in immediate hot pursuit of a fleeing felon may enter a dwelling without a warrant to prevent escape. The scope of the warrantless entry is limited to locating and arresting the suspect; it does not authorize a general evidentiary search. Closing a door does not defeat a continuous hot pursuit exigency.

**Credited outcome:** The warrantless entry was justified. Unchanged from the original.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer):**
- *Warden v. Hayden*, 387 U.S. 294 (1967): established hot pursuit as a recognized exigent circumstance justifying warrantless entry to arrest a fleeing felon.
- *United States v. Santana*, 427 U.S. 38 (1976): police chased Santana from a public place into her home; Supreme Court held hot pursuit justified the warrantless entry; "a suspect may not defeat an arrest which has been set in motion in a public place . . . by the expedient of escaping to a private place."
- *Minnesota v. Olson*, 495 U.S. 91 (1990): confirmed home entry requires exigency; hot pursuit satisfies the exigency requirement for a serious offense.
- Scope limitation: See *Mincey v. Arizona*, 437 U.S. 385 (1978) (exigency does not authorize general "murder scene" evidence search; scope tied to the emergency justifying entry).

**No doctrinal-currency hotlist item triggered.** Hot pursuit exigency doctrine from *Warden* and *Santana* remains current law. *Caniglia v. Strom*, 593 U.S. 194 (2021) (community caretaking does not apply to home entry) is inapplicable here because the entry rested on hot pursuit, not community caretaking.
