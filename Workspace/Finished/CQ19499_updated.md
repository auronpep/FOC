### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct. Gold Key:** Wharton’s Rule is narrow; the extra-participant exception takes the case outside the merger idea when people beyond those necessary for the target offense join the agreement. **Silver Key:** count the people the target offense needs, then count the people who joined the plan. ### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** The attraction is the absolute-sounding shortcut: once an offense involves an agreement, the conspiracy charge supposedly disappears. The breaker is the word **always**; Wharton’s Rule is a narrow doctrine with exceptions, and the extra-participant setup defeats this broad version.

**B.** The attraction is that the organizers’ hoped-for profit sounds like the reason the case feels more serious. The breaker is the axis: payment is not the Wharton’s Rule trigger; the deciding fact is that extra people joined beyond the two racers needed for the offense.

**D.** The attraction is the base Wharton’s Rule idea: the target offense itself requires two people. **Gold Key:** the base rule does not finish the analysis when the plan includes unnecessary extra participants; the extra-party exception is what steers the student off this trap.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: Wharton’s Rule may bar conspiracy when the target offense necessarily requires concerted participation by the same minimum parties, but the rule does not bar conspiracy when the agreement includes more participants than are required to commit the substantive offense. The Supreme Court’s discussion in *Iannelli v. United States* describes Wharton’s Rule as an exception to the ordinary rule that conspiracy and the completed offense do not merge, and it identifies the recognized third-party exception when the conspiracy includes more persons than the substantive offense requires. ([Justia Law][1]) ([Justia Law][1])
