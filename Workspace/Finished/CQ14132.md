### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. Once Barnabas testified that both parties were citizens of State A, the case no longer had complete diversity. The dispute was a private state-law contract dispute, and no federal-question basis appeared in the facts. Subject-matter jurisdiction is different from trial-stage devices like JMOL or summary judgment: if the court discovers that it lacks subject-matter jurisdiction, it must dismiss the action even during trial. Gold Key: A federal court must dismiss whenever it discovers it lacks subject-matter jurisdiction, even after trial has begun. That is why Lydia's lawyer can still prevent the case from going to the jury.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is the dominant trap. It is attractive because Lydia's lawyer cannot get JMOL while Barnabas is still presenting evidence, so the answer feels procedurally disciplined. The breaker is that the call asks whether Lydia has any way to stop jury submission, not whether JMOL is ripe; the revealed lack of complete diversity gives her a subject-matter-jurisdiction path. Gold Key: subject-matter jurisdiction can be raised even after trial has begun, so a true JMOL-timing point does not answer the question.

C is attractive because it sounds like the practical trial answer: once the jury is hearing evidence, maybe the lawyer has to keep going. The breaker is that a federal court cannot ignore a subject-matter-jurisdiction defect; the “no option but continue” rule is false when the court lacks power to hear the case.

D is attractive because summary judgment is a familiar way to stop a case before fact-finding. The breaker is timing: summary judgment is a pretrial/discovery-stage motion, and this case is already in the middle of trial after Barnabas has begun presenting evidence.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested black-letter rule is unchanged: a federal court must dismiss whenever it determines that it lacks subject-matter jurisdiction, and subject-matter jurisdiction cannot be cured by waiting until a more convenient trial stage. Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(h)(3) provides: “If the court determines at any time that it lacks subject-matter jurisdiction, the court must dismiss the action.”

Research confirmation (current as of 2026): diversity jurisdiction requires the matter in controversy to exceed $75,000 and the parties to be citizens of different states. 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a)(1); see also Cornell LII’s diversity-jurisdiction summary, which states that complete diversity means no plaintiff and defendant are domiciled in the same state (https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/diversity_jurisdiction). Federal-question jurisdiction is separately governed by 28 U.S.C. § 1331, and no federal question is indicated by the private contract facts. Summary judgment is governed by Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(b), which places the motion before the post-discovery deadline, not in the middle of trial. Judgment as a matter of law is governed by Fed. R. Civ. P. 50(a)(1)-(2), which requires that the party be fully heard on the issue and allows the motion before the case is submitted to the jury. No doctrinal shift affects the credited outcome. The transform preserves the legal substance, the subject-matter-jurisdiction timing rule, the trial-stage distractors, and the original credited answer.
