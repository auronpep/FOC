## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** Even assuming the locker search violated the Fourth Amendment, the exclusionary rule generally does not require suppression of illegally obtained evidence in parole revocation proceedings. In *Pennsylvania Board of Probation and Parole v. Scott*, 524 U.S. 357 (1998), the Supreme Court held that the exclusionary rule does not apply in state parole revocation proceedings. The costs of excluding reliable evidence outweigh the marginal deterrent benefit, given the administrative nature of revocation hearings and the board's need to have access to all relevant information. The Fourth Amendment right still exists; the *remedy* of exclusion is what's limited in this context.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. "No, because parolees have no Fourth Amendment rights at all."**
Right conclusion, wrong reason. Flat misstatement: parolees do retain some Fourth Amendment protections. The issue isn't whether Timothy has Fourth Amendment rights — he does. The issue is whether the *exclusionary remedy* applies in parole revocation proceedings. It generally doesn't, but that's not because parolees lack rights altogether.

**B. "Yes, because every Fourth Amendment violation triggers exclusion in every proceeding."** ⚠️ DOMINANT TRAP
Overclaim trap. The word "every" is the structural red flag. The exclusionary rule is limited by proceeding type — it applies in criminal trials but generally not in parole revocation, civil proceedings, grand jury proceedings, or sentencing. Students who know the exclusionary rule is important overgeneralize it to all contexts.

**C. "Yes, because parole revocation is more serious than a criminal trial."**
Distortion trap. Consequence severity doesn't determine whether the exclusionary rule applies. And the premise is backwards — a criminal trial (with potential imprisonment) is more serious than a parole revocation hearing. The exclusionary rule applies in the more serious proceeding (criminal trial), not the less serious one.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Governing rule:** The exclusionary rule does not generally require suppression of illegally obtained evidence in parole revocation proceedings. *Pennsylvania Board of Probation and Parole v. Scott*, 524 U.S. 357 (1998).

**Authority:** *Pennsylvania Board of Probation and Parole v. Scott*, 524 U.S. 357 (1998) — the Court held that the exclusionary rule does not apply in state parole revocation proceedings, weighing the costs of excluding reliable evidence against the marginal deterrent benefit. *See also* *United States v.
