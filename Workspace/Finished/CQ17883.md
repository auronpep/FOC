### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is the best answer. Nathan intentionally lit fuel on a shared wooden stair landing inside an apartment building. That created an obvious risk that the dwelling would burn, and the stair tread actually charred. Gold Key: common-law arson is a malice crime, not a specific-purpose-to-burn crime; reckless disregard of an obvious fire risk can satisfy malice. Silver Key: when the call asks whether a missing purpose is a complete defense, test malice before accepting the defendant's motive label.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A has a strong pull because the question uses purpose language and the defendant says he did not want the building burned. The breaker is that common-law arson does not require conscious purpose to burn the structure when malicious fire-setting shows reckless disregard of an obvious risk. Gold Key: arson malice can be reckless disregard, so a purpose-required answer overclaims the mens rea.

C notices a serious fact: someone may be in the building when a stairwell fire starts. The breaker is that the arson issue turns on malicious burning of the protected dwelling structure, not on a separate requirement that a person be inside at the moment of the fire.

D captures Nathan's stated motive to scare people rather than destroy property. The breaker is that motive does not erase malice when he intentionally lit fuel in a place where the building obviously could burn.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested issue and credited outcome are unchanged: whether lack of a specific purpose to burn the building is a complete defense to common-law arson when the defendant intentionally creates an obvious fire risk to a dwelling and the structure chars.

Common-law arson was the malicious burning of the dwelling of another, and modern formulations preserve malice while expanding covered structures. Cornell Wex summarizes the common-law core of arson as malicious burning of a dwelling: https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/arson. The malice floor is supported by DeBettencourt v. State, 428 A.2d 479 (Md. Ct. Spec. App. 1981), which treats malice for arson as including a dangerous fire act done with reckless and wanton disregard of the consequences: https://law.justia.com/cases/maryland/court-of-special-appeals/1981/1112-september-term-1980-0.html. Model Penal Code section 2.02(2)(c) defines recklessness as conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk, and section 220.1 covers arson and reckless burning/fire-related offenses: https://www.criminallawweb.net/web1/MPC/PART1/snippets/220_1.htm.

Under that rule, Nathan's lack of a purpose to burn the building is not a complete defense. He deliberately lit fuel on a wooden stair landing in an apartment building. The risk to the protected dwelling was obvious, and the stair tread was actually charred. Those facts support malice through reckless disregard even if his immediate motive was intimidation.
