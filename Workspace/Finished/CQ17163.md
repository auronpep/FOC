### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Paul's claim fails. The Due Process Clause targets deprivations accomplished without constitutionally adequate process or by deliberate or reckless state action; it is not a font of tort law for run-of-the-mill negligence. The fact pattern alleges only an accidental loss of a $75 fee by a single clerk — a paradigm of carelessness, not an abuse of state power. **Gold Key:** negligent loss by a state official is not a substantive due process violation. The proper remedy for a misplaced fee is a state-law claim against the clerk or the county, not a federal § 1983 suit.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**D. Every property loss by a state employee is a due process violation.**

This is the dominant trap. The overclaim is seductive because the Due Process Clause protects property, and the citizen did lose $75. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: the clause targets deprivations by wrongful state action, not every careless loss; a federal due process claim is not a make-tort-whole theory.

**A. Strict scrutiny applies, because permit fees burden travel within the state.**

The wrong-frame trap. Strict scrutiny is reserved for fundamental rights and suspect classifications; a $75 application fee for a concealed-carry permit burdens the right to keep and bear arms only at the margins and is not even a constitutional burden for these purposes. The Due Process Clause's standard analysis on a negligent-loss claim is rational-basis review at most, and the claim still fails because the deprivation is not constitutionally cognizable on negligent facts.

**B. The clerk must receive a hearing before the applicant can sue.**

The state-employee-hearing trap. The question asks about the applicant's due process rights, not the clerk's employment process. The applicant's claim fails on the merits because negligence is not a due process violation, not because the clerk needs a hearing.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17163, a Constitutional Law item on whether mere negligence by a state official can support a substantive due process claim. The official key is B: the Due Process Clause is not ordinarily violated by mere negligence.

Substantive due process protects against arbitrary or wrongful government action, not against negligent mistakes. The Supreme Court's leading teaching is that a negligent act by a state official causing unintended loss of property does not amount to a deprivation of property under the Due Process Clause in the constitutional sense; only deliberate or reckless deprivations, or deprivations accomplished by constitutionally inadequate procedure, are actionable under § 1983. The Court restated the principle in County of Sacramento v. Lewis, 523 U.S. 833 (1998) (paradigmatic substantive due process claim requires deliberate government action; negligent action is not the kind of governmental abuse the Clause was designed to control). For procedural due process, the Court in Parratt v. Taylor, 451 U.S. 527 (1981), and Hudson v. Palmer, 468 U.S. 517 (1984), held that negligent loss of property by a state actor does not state a due process claim if a meaningful postdeprivation remedy (e.g., a state tort suit) is available. Because the facts allege only negligence, the claim fails on the merits; the proper avenue is a state tort or contract claim.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: a negligent loss of property by a state official is not a due process violation; the correct answer is the no-negligence-violation rule.
