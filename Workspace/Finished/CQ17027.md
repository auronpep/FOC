### 1. Final question

Mary, a heritage farmer in State X, agreed to buy a hand-forged breaking plow from Daniel, a blacksmith in State Y, for $18,000. Daniel wrote on the invoice that the plow was "high-carbon, work-hardened steel, hand-forged by a fourth-generation smith." Mary paid in full and took delivery. ### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The court will apply the **State X clear-and-convincing-evidence standard** to reliance. The claim sounds in State X common-law fraud, and the State X forum has attached a heightened burden to one of the claim's elements. Under Erie, a federal court sitting in diversity must apply state substantive law, and the Supreme Court has treated the burden of proof on a state-law claim element (or on a state-law defense) as a substantive ingredient of the state right. The federal procedural-rules track does not displace that allocation. Mary has therefore not stated a basis for a federal preponderance standard, and the in limine motion should be denied. **Gold Key:** in a diversity case the burden of proof on a state-law claim element follows the state right into federal court. **Silver Key:** lock the call to "which burden applies to reliance on a state-law fraud claim in diversity," not to abstract labels about burdens in general.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. A federal preponderance-of-the-evidence standard, because all burdens of proof in federal court are governed by federal procedural rules.**

This is the dominant trap and the classic Erie misconception. The student hears "federal court" and reaches for a federal procedural rule. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: under Erie and the outcome-determinative line of cases, the burden of proof on a state-law claim element is treated as substantive, and the federal court applies the state burden. Federal procedural rules do not override a state allocation that is bound up with the substantive right.

**B. Whatever standard the defendant prefers, because Erie exists to prevent forum shopping between state and federal court.**

This is the "Erie is about forum shopping and party preferences" trap. The student has half-remembered the policy rationale for Erie and converts it into a rule that lets a party pick the burden. The breaker is the rule itself: Erie requires the federal court to apply state law where state law is substantive, not to defer to the defendant's litigation preference. Erie's purpose is to make the federal court apply the same substantive law a state court would, not to let the defendant pick.

**D. No burden of proof at all, because the jury evaluates the evidence freely and reaches its own conclusion.**

This is the "no burden, jury decides" trap. The student reads "burden of proof" and pictures the jury as a free evaluator. The breaker is structural: every claim in every American court has a burden of proof; the question is whose burden, and on which element. A "no burden" answer is not a legal standard — it is a misframing of what a burden is.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17027, a Civil Procedure item involving a state-law fraud claim brought in federal diversity court, where the forum state requires clear and convincing evidence of reliance and the plaintiff argues the federal preponderance standard should control because federal procedure governs trial conduct. The official key is A: the state clear-and-convincing burden applies because it is tied to the state-law fraud claim.

Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), held that federal courts sitting in diversity apply state substantive law and federal procedural law, abandoning the older Swift v. Tyson regime. The Supreme Court then developed a series of decisions clarifying which "procedure-substance" allocations the federal court must respect. In Guaranty Trust Co. v. York, 326 U.S. 99 (1945), the Court treated the burden of proof on a state-law claim element as outcome-determinative in the Erie sense and required the federal court to apply the state burden. The principle is that a state allocation of the burden of proof on a state-law claim or defense is bound up with the substantive state right and travels with that right into federal court. The same logic appears in later decisions addressing pleading, statute-of-limitations, and choice-of-law allocations; the basic Erie instruction is that a federal court may not give a state-law claim more (or less) procedural advantage than the state would give it in its own courts. The result is that the State X clear-and-convincing standard governs Mary's reliance element in the federal diversity action.

The Hanna v. Plumer, 380 U.S. 460 (1965), two-track analysis is not triggered here. The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure do not contain a uniform "preponderance" allocation that collides with the State X burden on this element; the question is which body of law supplies the burden, and Erie's substantive/procedural allocation points to State X. (Hanna addresses the situation where a valid Federal Rule of Civil Procedure is on point and "argued" to cover the disputed point; it does not authorize a federal court to displace a substantive state burden of proof on a state-law claim element.)

The transformed outcome is identical to the original. Mary is not entitled to a federal preponderance standard on the reliance element of her State X fraud claim; the State X clear-and-convincing standard applies.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. A federal preponderance-of-the-evidence standard, because all burdens of proof in federal court are governed by federal procedural rules.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice asserts a flat rule that all burdens of proof in federal court are procedural. The overclaim is the doctrinal assertion itself; without the Gold Key, a student may not see why the rule is wrong as applied to a state-law claim element.
2. **Student label:** Federal-procedural / "all burdens are procedural" trap.
3. **True/responsive version:** "A federal preponderance standard might apply if the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure contained a uniform burden allocation covering this point and there were no state substantive right at stake, but on a state-law claim element the Erie allocation points to the state burden."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Under Erie and the outcome-determinative line, the burden of proof on a state-law claim element is treated as substantive, and the federal court applies the state burden. Guaranty Trust Co. v. York, 326 U.S. 99 (1945), established the outcome-determinative analysis that places the burden of proof on a state-law claim element within the state substantive law for Erie purposes.

### B. Whatever standard the defendant prefers, because Erie exists to prevent forum shopping between state and federal court.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice recharacterizes Erie as a party-preference or forum-shopping rule. Erie's actual function is to require the federal court to apply state substantive law; it is not a party-preference rule.
2. **Student label:** Erie-as-party-preference misframe.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Erie's purpose is to ensure that the federal court applies the same substantive law the state court would; it does not let the defendant pick the burden."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Erie requires the federal court to apply the state substantive right, not to defer to a party's litigation preference. Erie R.R. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938).

### C. The State X clear-and-convincing-evidence standard, because the burden is bound up with the substantive State X fraud claim.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the residual after the federal-procedural and misfit traps are cut. It maps the Erie allocation to the specific burden on a state-law claim element.
2. **Student label:** Residual answer.
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The State X clear-and-convincing standard applies because the burden is bound up with the substantive State X fraud claim; the federal court in diversity applies the state burden on a state-law claim element.

### D. No burden of proof at all, because the jury evaluates the evidence freely and reaches its own conclusion.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** "No burden of proof at all" is not a recognized standard. The answer text itself overclaims; the cut is structural.
2. **Student label:** No-burden / jury-decides-freely misframe.
3. **True/responsive version:** "A 'no burden' answer is not a legal standard. The Due Process Clause does not eliminate the burden of proof; it requires the state to provide adequate procedures for its allocation."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Every civil claim has a burden of proof on each element. The question is which party bears the burden and on what standard. The Due Process Clause requires procedures, not the elimination of the burden.
