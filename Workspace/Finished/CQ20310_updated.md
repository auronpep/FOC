### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**A is correct.** The later wire guard does not answer the breach question by itself. The breach question still asks whether reasonable care required the guard before Ruth was burned. **Gold Key:** a later safety change is not proof that the earlier condition was negligent; its limited use is a non-breach purpose such as feasibility, and only if that purpose is genuinely disputed. **Silver Key:** lock the call to “best use for breach.” Do not let the later fix become a shortcut for deciding whether the earlier setup was unreasonable.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B.** The pull is that the answer sounds like it uses the later installation to say something about fault. The breaker is that Ruth’s care is not the breach issue, and Lydia’s later guard says nothing reliable about whether Ruth failed to use care.

**C.** The pull is the common “they fixed it, so they must have been wrong before” move. The breaker is the word **conclusively**: the later guard cannot replace the reasonable-care analysis that belongs to the conditions before the burn. **Gold Key:** later safety changes do not prove negligence; at most they may serve a limited non-breach purpose such as feasibility if disputed.

**D.** The pull is the clean timing rule: later means too late. The breaker is the overclaim **always irrelevant to every issue**; later safety measures can have limited uses, so the absolute version says too much.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

Legal confirmation only: Federal Rule of Evidence 407 states that later measures that would have made an earlier injury less likely are not admissible to prove negligence or culpable conduct, but may be admitted for another purpose such as feasibility if disputed. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) The breach side remains a negligence inquiry: Restatement (Third) of Torts §3 frames negligence as failure to exercise reasonable care under all circumstances, considering foreseeable likelihood of harm, severity, and burden of precautions. ([Open Casebook][2])

The original legal issue and outcome are unchanged: a later safety measure is not itself proof of breach; its limited possible use is feasibility if genuinely disputed. Here, as in the source row, feasibility is admitted, so the later installation does not substitute for proving that reasonable care required the precaution before the injury.
