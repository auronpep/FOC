### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Emma Rail's negligence was a but-for cause of Lydia's loss because the shop would not have flooded if Emma Rail had maintained its culvert. The city's negligence also mattered, but that does not erase Emma Rail's causal role. When two negligent actors combine to create one indivisible loss, the plaintiff may recover the whole loss from one jointly liable tortfeasor, leaving allocation or contribution issues for the tortfeasors.

**Gold Key:** In a concurrent-cause negligence item, ask whether the defendant's negligence was necessary to the harm and whether the harm is one indivisible loss. If both are true, the plaintiff can recover the whole loss from that defendant even though another negligent actor also contributed.

**Silver Key:** Do not let apportionment language flip the plaintiff's burden. The plaintiff proves causation and loss; the defendant worries about contribution or allocation against the other wrongdoer.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This is the dominant trap. It sounds like a careful courtroom answer because no one has supplied a clean percentage split between the city and Emma Rail. The breaker is that indivisible concurrent-tortfeasor liability does not make the plaintiff lose because she failed to prove a fault split. **Silver Key:** apportionment problems travel to contribution or allocation; they do not defeat the plaintiff's full recovery from a jointly liable tortfeasor.

**C.** This choice sells the fairness instinct: two negligent actors, so divide the loss in half. The breaker is that the loss is indivisible and Emma Rail's negligence was a but-for cause of all of it. **Gold Key:** when one indivisible injury is caused by multiple negligent actors, the plaintiff's recovery from a jointly liable actor is not automatically cut to a neat fraction.

**D.** This choice treats the missing city as if it were a required defendant. The city may be a target for impleader, contribution, or allocation, but Lydia does not have to join it before recovering from Emma Rail. The absent negligent actor does not erase Emma Rail's but-for causal role.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are preserved. The source item tests actual cause when two negligent failures combine to produce one indivisible loss, and neither failure alone would have produced the flood. The credited answer remains full recovery from the sued tortfeasor because the sued tortfeasor's negligence was a but-for cause of the whole loss.

The authority floor is satisfied. Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical and Emotional Harm section 26 states the ordinary factual-cause rule: conduct is a factual cause when the harm would not have occurred absent that conduct. The ALI's Restatement (Third) of Torts: Apportionment of Liability addresses multiple tortfeasors and indivisible harm, including joint-and-several-liability tracks for a single injury. [1] Cornell's Wex summary of joint and several liability states the standard effect: when multiple parties are jointly and severally liable for a tortious act, each is liable for the full extent of the injury, and the paying party may seek contribution. That rule confirms the credited answer. If Emma Rail had maintained its culvert, Lydia's shop would not have flooded. Emma Rail is therefore a factual cause under the but-for test. The city's negligence is a concurrent cause, not a defense to Emma Rail's liability. Because the harm is one indivisible flood loss and no special rule shields the city, Lydia may recover the whole loss from Emma Rail, subject to whatever contribution or allocation rights Emma Rail may have against the city.
