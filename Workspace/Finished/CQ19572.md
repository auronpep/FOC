### 6. Right-Answer Explanation

Gideon drove two oxen into a stampede across a packed threshing floor. He knew the crowd was there — he shouted that everyone would step aside. That shout is the key: it shows conscious awareness of the danger. He proceeded anyway, driving powerful animals at full run through a space packed with people. The risk of death was extreme and obvious.

That is depraved-heart murder. No intent to kill is required. The defining elements are conscious disregard of an extreme risk with extreme indifference to human life — both present here.

Involuntary manslaughter requires only criminal negligence (gross deviation from reasonable care). What Gideon did was far worse: he was aware of the extreme risk and ignored it. That elevates the offense to murder.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIMINAL-DEPRAVED-HEART-01):** Depraved-heart murder requires conscious disregard of an extremely high risk of death — extreme indifference to human life. No intent to kill is required. A defendant who knowingly creates a very high risk of death and proceeds anyway commits murder, even without aiming to hurt anyone.

**Silver Key (SK-CRIMINAL-DEPRAVED-HEART-01):** In a "most serious homicide offense" question, check two depraved-heart signals: (1) Did the defendant know people were at risk? (shout shows yes); (2) Was the risk extremely high? (stampeding oxen in a packed crowd — yes). Both present → murder, not manslaughter.

### 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A — "First-degree murder, because intentionally startling animals into a crowd constitutes felony murder"**

The appeal is that Gideon did something intentional — he deliberately goaded the oxen — and it resulted in death, so perhaps this triggers felony murder. But felony murder requires an underlying predicate felony from a recognized list. Intentionally startling animals does not constitute a qualifying felony murder predicate. The clearer and better-supported charge here is depraved-heart murder, which does not require any predicate felony at all.

**Choice B — "Involuntary manslaughter only, because Gideon did not intend to hit anyone" (dominant trap)**

The draw is real: Gideon never aimed for the worker, and without intent to kill, "murder" sounds like too strong a label. But the "no intent" framing focuses on the wrong element. Murder via depraved heart does not require intent to kill. What it requires — conscious awareness of an extreme risk and indifference to it — is exactly what the facts show. Gideon knew the threshing floor was packed; he shouted about it; and he drove the oxen in anyway. That conscious disregard elevates the offense above manslaughter to murder.

**Gold Key:** Depraved-heart murder does not require intent to harm anyone. Conscious disregard of an extreme risk with extreme indifference is enough. "No intent = only manslaughter" is the classic trap this doctrine is tested against.

**Silver Key:** Check the two signals before accepting "only manslaughter." Gideon knew people were there (signal 1 — shouted about it). The risk was extreme (signal 2 — stampeding oxen in a packed space). Both present → murder.

**Choice C — "No homicide liability, because the workers could have stepped aside"**

The pull is that the workers had warning — Gideon shouted — and perhaps they shared responsibility for being hit. But a victim's opportunity to avoid harm does not sever the defendant's causal liability. Gideon created the extreme risk; the worker's failure to escape in time is not a superseding cause that breaks the causal chain between Gideon's act and her death.

### 8. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Rule:** Depraved-heart (or depraved-indifference) murder requires: (1) reckless conduct; (2) creating a very high (not merely ordinary) risk of death or serious bodily harm; (3) manifesting extreme indifference to human life. No purpose to kill is required. This contrasts with involuntary manslaughter, which requires only criminal negligence (gross deviation from reasonable care, without the conscious-disregard / extreme-indifference elevation).

**Application:** Gideon (1) deliberately goaded oxen into a stampede — a conscious volitional act, not mere carelessness; (2) on a packed threshing floor — creating an extreme, not merely elevated, risk of death; (3) while demonstrating awareness of the danger ("everyone would step aside" shows he knew the crowd was present and at risk). He proceeded regardless. Those facts satisfy depraved-heart murder.

**Legal reasoning — why D is correct:** The call asks for the "most serious" offense. Depraved-heart murder is more serious than involuntary manslaughter and is clearly supported. Felony murder (choice A) fails because goading animals is not a BARRK predicate felony. Choice C fails because the victim's inability to escape is not a superseding cause. Choice B (the dominant trap) fails because it misidentifies intent-to-kill as a required element of murder.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer):** MPC § 210.2(1)(b): criminal homicide constitutes murder when "it is committed recklessly under circumstances manifesting extreme indifference to the value of human life." Common law depraved-heart murder similarly requires malice aforethought expressed through depraved indifference — neither approach requires intent to kill. Involuntary manslaughter (MPC § 210.3(1)(a)) requires recklessness but not the extreme-indifference elevation. The distinction between the two (extreme indifference vs. ordinary recklessness) is the central MBE-tested issue here. Felony murder predicates under the common law are typically limited to BARRK offenses (Burglary, Arson, Rape, Robbery, Kidnapping) and do not include merely reckless or tortious animal-related acts. See LaFave, Criminal Law §§ 14.4, 15.5 (5th ed. 2010).

**Tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome unchanged from original:** Original tested depraved-heart murder as the most serious supported offense when the defendant consciously disregarded extreme risk without intending to kill. The variation tests the identical rule and issue with the same structural elements (crowded setting, conscious awareness shown by shout, extreme risk, no intent to kill). Credited outcome: murder. ✓
