### 6. Right-Answer Explanation

Naomi's letter delivered a wrongful future threat — she promised to scatter Bartholomew's flock "before the shearing season," a harm that had not yet occurred and was not immediate. Bartholomew placed the silver in response to that threat while Naomi was miles away. She collected the silver later.

These facts match extortion precisely: a wrongful threat of future harm that coerces a later property transfer. The transfer is the product of the threat.

Robbery is out for two reasons: Naomi was not present when Bartholomew gave up the silver, and the threat was of future harm rather than immediate force or intimidation. The call asks for the "best" offense — extortion captures the full crime.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIMINAL-EXTORTION-01):** Extortion requires only a wrongful threat — including a threat of future harm — that coerces a property transfer. Robbery requires immediate force or intimidation in the victim's presence during the taking. When the threat is future and the defendant is absent at the moment of transfer, the correct offense is extortion.

**Silver Key (SK-CRIMINAL-EXTORTION-01):** In a "best offense" question involving a threat and a later property transfer, check two things: (1) Was the defendant present when the victim gave up the property? If no — not robbery. (2) Was the threat of future harm? If yes — extortion.

### 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice B — "No offense, because Bartholomew voluntarily placed the silver"**

The draw is that no one physically compelled Bartholomew at the moment he placed the silver — he chose to walk to the stump and leave the coins. But the law does not treat a transfer made in response to a wrongful threat as voluntary consent. The threatening letter stripped the transfer of legal voluntariness. "Voluntarily" in the common sense ("no one was holding a sword to his neck") does not mean "voluntarily" in the legal sense that would negate an offense.

**Choice C — "Robbery, because Bartholomew acted out of fear" (dominant trap)**

This is the most seductive wrong answer: Bartholomew DID act out of fear, and robbery does involve threatening someone to obtain property. But robbery requires the defendant to be present — using immediate force or intimidation during the taking, in the victim's immediate presence. Naomi was on her farm several miles away when Bartholomew placed the silver. The threat was of future harm, not imminent force.

**Silver Key:** The stem tells you Naomi was "several miles away." That detail is the signal: she was not in Bartholomew's presence. If the defendant is not present during the transfer, it cannot be robbery — reach for extortion.

**Gold Key:** Robbery requires immediate force or intimidation in the victim's presence. Extortion governs when a future threat coerces a later transfer with the defendant absent.

**Choice D — "Larceny only, because Naomi physically collected the silver from the stump"**

The pull is that Naomi did physically pick up property she had no right to, which looks like a trespassory taking. But the physical collection is not the defining element — the wrongful coercive demand is. Reducing the offense to larceny ignores the threatening letter that caused Bartholomew to place the silver in the first place. The "best offense" for this fact pattern is the one that captures the coercive mechanism, not just the physical act at the end.

### 8. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Rule:** Robbery requires a taking of property from the person or immediate presence of another by force or intimidation. Extortion is obtaining property through a wrongful threat — which may be a threat of future harm — that causes the victim to transfer property. Extortion does not require the defendant's presence during the transfer; the wrongful threat is the gravamen.

**Application:** Naomi's letter threatened property damage to Bartholomew's flock at a future time ("before the shearing season"). The threat was future, not immediate. Bartholomew transferred silver in response to that threat while Naomi was miles away — not in his presence. Naomi collected the silver later. All elements of extortion are satisfied; none of robbery's required elements (immediate force/presence) are present.

**Legal reasoning — why A is correct:** The extortion elements are met — wrongful threat of property harm that coerces a transfer. Robbery fails because (1) Naomi was not present during the transfer and (2) the threat was not immediate. The "best offense" call requires the charge that captures the full wrongdoing; extortion does; larceny and robbery do not.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer):** MPC § 223.4 (theft by extortion): a person is guilty of theft if they purposely obtain property of another by threatening to inflict harm that would not benefit the actor, including threatened damage to property. MPC § 222.1 (robbery): requires that force or threat be used against the victim's person in the course of committing theft — the immediacy and presence requirements distinguish it from extortion. The robbery/extortion distinction on these facts (future threat + absent defendant) is a foundational MBE-tested rule. See LaFave, Criminal Law § 20.3 (5th ed. 2010); Perkins & Boyce, Criminal Law 428–32 (3d ed. 1982).

**Tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome unchanged from original:** The original tested the robbery/extortion distinction — future threat + absent defendant = extortion, not robbery. The variation tests the same distinction with identical structural elements. Credited outcome: extortion. ✓
