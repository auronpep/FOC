## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Ruth told Silas, who was selling a passenger van for his youth ministry, that her cashier's check was "drawn on the local credit union and immediately redeemable for the full amount." Ruth knew the check was a counterfeit she had printed at home. Relying on Ruth’s statement, Silas signed over the legal title to the van and handed Ruth the keys. Ruth drove away and later sold the van for parts.

What is Ruth's best theft offense?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A defendant uses a counterfeit check and a lie about its validity to induce a seller to sign over the legal title to a vehicle. Is this larceny by trick or false pretenses?

### 3. Final Answer Choices

(A) False pretenses, because Silas intended to transfer legal ownership based on a false statement of present fact.

(B) Larceny by trick, because Ruth obtained possession of the van through a fraudulent misrepresentation.

(C) Embezzlement, because Ruth converted the van to her own use after Silas gave her the keys.

(D) No offense, because Silas voluntarily signed the title and handed over the keys.

### 5. Correct Answer

A (original key A).

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

(A) is correct. False pretenses is the obtaining of title to the personal property of another by an intentional false statement of past or present material fact, with the intent to defraud the other. The critical distinction between false pretenses and larceny by trick is the victim's intent: if the victim intends to transfer title (ownership), it is false pretenses. Here, Silas signed over the legal title to the van because he relied on Ruth's false statement that the check was genuine. Since title passed, the offense is false pretenses. Gold Key: False pretenses requires the transfer of title, whereas larceny by trick requires only the transfer of possession.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

(B) This choice is attractive because it correctly identifies that fraud was used to obtain the property. However, it is incorrect because larceny by trick is the correct label only when the victim intends to transfer possession only. Because Silas signed over the title, title passed, and the offense escalated to false pretenses.

(C) This choice is attractive because Ruth did "convert" the van to her own use. However, it is incorrect because embezzlement requires that the defendant first receive lawful possession of the property through a relationship of trust (like a bailee or agent). Ruth obtained the van through fraud from the start, which is a trespassory-style taking (vitiated consent), not a trust-based entrustment.

(D) This choice is attractive because it appeals to the idea that Silas made a choice to sign. However, it is incorrect because consent obtained by fraud as to a material fact is not valid legal consent and does not prevent a theft conviction.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: False Pretenses vs. Larceny by Trick (Title vs. Possession). The distinction depends on the victim's intent. If the victim intends to part with title, the crime is false pretenses. Using a counterfeit check to "buy" an item induces the seller to transfer ownership (title). Silas's act of signing the title document is the definitive fact that title passed. Authority: People v. Williams, 57 Cal.4th 776 (2013). The outcome (False Pretenses) is consistent with the source item and the Williams logic that a fraudulent commercial exchange where title passes is false pretenses.

### 10. Rigor and Difficulty Preservation

The variation preserves the high-rigor distinction between the two fraud-based theft crimes. Students often struggle with the "title vs. possession" line, especially in transactions involving vehicles where both possession (keys) and title (document) are transferred.

### 11. C3 Elimination Walkthrough

CUT (C): Embezzlement is a misfit because there was no prior entrustment; it was an arm's length "sale" induced by fraud.

CUT (D): Fraudulent inducement vitiates consent, making the "voluntary" argument a failure of logic.

CLASH (A) vs. (B): The axis is Title vs. Possession. (B) sells the broader "fraud handover" idea, while (A) narrows it to the specific "transfer of ownership" fact.

CALL (A): The signing of the title document is the definitive "title-passing" event.
