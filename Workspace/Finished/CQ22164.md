## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** When a plaintiff's attorney creates a false impression that the defendant carries liability insurance — even indirectly, such as by asking jurors about their own connections to insurance companies — the defendant may offer evidence that he lacked insurance to counter that false suggestion. This is not an admission of fault; it is a curative response to the plaintiff's improper conduct.

Ruth's attorney asked each prospective juror whether he or she held shares in any agricultural liability insurance company. A reasonable juror could conclude from that line of questioning that David was insured and that any verdict would be paid by an insurance company rather than by David's estate. That is a false suggestion. David's estate may therefore offer the conviction judgment to show that David was, in fact, not insured. The evidence is not being offered to prove fault or freedom from fault — it is being offered to counter the false impression created by the plaintiff's voir dire. While it is not certain that a court would admit it, C is the only option that could possibly be correct.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (originally B) — Dominant Trap**
*What's attractive:* FRE 411 really does say that evidence of liability insurance (or lack of it) is inadmissible to prove whether a person acted negligently. A student who remembers this rule will feel confident picking this answer.
*The breaker:* The rule is a general one. The question is whether the evidence is being offered to prove fault — it isn't. It's being offered to counter the false suggestion of insurance created by the plaintiff's voir dire. The general rule does not block the evidence when offered for this curative purpose. **Silver Key:** Before locking in the general rule, check what the evidence is actually being offered for. If the purpose is something other than proving fault — like countering a false impression — the general bar does not apply.

**B (originally D)**
*What's attractive:* A "judgment of conviction" sounds like an out-of-court statement being offered for its truth, which triggers the hearsay instinct.
*The breaker:* A judgment of conviction is a legal record, not hearsay in the ordinary sense. It is a court's official act. The Federal Rules specifically authorize the admission of judgments of prior conviction when relevant (see FRE 803(22) for judgments of previous convictions as a hearsay exception). Calling it "hearsay not within any exception" is simply wrong — there is an exception that covers it.

**D (originally C)**
*What's attractive:* The evidence does benefit the defendant's side, so "self-serving" feels like a real objection.
*The breaker:* There is no rule of evidence that excludes material simply because it is self-serving. Nearly every piece of evidence a party offers benefits that party. If "self-serving" were a valid objection, most trials would grind to a halt. This is not a recognized ground for exclusion.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule:** Federal Rule of Evidence 411 — Liability Insurance.

> "Evidence that a person was or not insured against liability is not admissible upon the issue whether the person acted negligently or otherwise wrongfully. This rule does not require the exclusion of evidence of insurance against liability when offered for another purpose, such as proof of agency, ownership, or control, or bias or prejudice of a witness."

**Text from US Code (28 U.S.C. App. Fed. R. Evid. 411):**
> "Evidence that a person was or was not insured against liability is not admissible upon the issue whether the person acted negligently or otherwise wrongfully. This rule does not require the exclusion of evidence of insurance against liability when offered for another purpose, such as proof of agency, ownership, or control, or bias or prejudice of a witness."

**Advisory Committee Notes:** "The courts have with substantial unanimity rejected evidence of liability insurance for the purpose of proving fault, and absence of liability insurance as proof of lack of fault."

**The false-suggestion exception:** Courts have recognized that when a plaintiff's attorney creates a false impression that the defendant is insured — whether through argument, questions to witnesses, or questions during voir dire — the defendant may be permitted to rebut that false impression by showing lack of insurance. This is not an exception to Rule 411 per se; rather, it follows from the principle that Rule 411 bars insurance evidence only when offered to prove fault. When offered to counter a false suggestion, the evidence serves a curative purpose, not a fault-proving one.

**Hearsay point:** A judgment of conviction is admissible under FRE 803(22) (judgment of previous conviction) as a hearsay exception. It is also admissible as a legal record of a court's official act. Choice B's hearsay objection fails.

**Verification of credited outcome:** The original credited answer (A) states that the judgment is admissible because the plaintiff's attorney falsely suggested the defendant was insured. This is consistent with the case law on the false-suggestion exception. The legal substance, rule, and credited outcome are unchanged.

**Authority:** FRE 411; Advisory Committee Notes to FRE 411; McCormick on Evidence §168; Annot., 4 A.L.R.2d 761.
