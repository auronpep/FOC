## 1. Final Question
A church safety monitor was assigned to work from 5 P.M. to 10 P.M. every night at a large indoor baptismal pool operated by the Tabernacle of Grace. When she arrived at work Wednesday evening, she asked her supervisor whether she could leave early to attend a mission trip preparation meeting. Since there were only a few people at the pool area, the supervisor said that the monitor could leave at 8 P.M. At 8 P.M., the monitor told the supervisor she was departing and left, although the area had become quite crowded with adults and young children attending a fellowship event.

At 9 P.M., a nine-year-old girl fell into the baptismal pool, striking her head against its marble edge. One of the adults attending the event saw the girl fall and realized that the child would drown if someone did not rescue her. The adult had seen the safety monitor leave and knew that there was no monitor present, but she made no effort to rescue the girl, although the adult was a strong swimmer and could easily have done so with no risk to herself. The girl drowned. 

If the safety monitor is charged with criminal homicide in the death of the girl, which of the following would be her most effective argument in defense?

(A) The girl's death resulted from the adult's failure to render aid.
(B) She did not intend the girl's death.
(C) Her duty to assist people in the pool area terminated when the supervisor permitted her to leave at 8 P.M.
(D) She was not present at the time of the drowning.

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation
The most effective defense is that the monitor no longer had a legal duty to act at the time of the drowning. Criminal liability for an omission (a failure to act) requires that the defendant had a legal duty to act. One source of such a duty is a contract, such as an employment agreement. Here, the monitor’s duty to supervise the pool area arose from her employment. Because her supervisor—acting as the agent of the employer—explicitly permitted her to leave at 8 P.M., her contractual duty ended at that time. Since the accident occurred at 9 P.M., she was no longer under a legal obligation to provide aid, and thus her omission cannot form the basis of a homicide charge.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations
**(A) [Original C]** This argument is ineffective because criminal law allows for multiple proximate causes of death. The fact that another person (the adult bystander) also failed to act does not absolve a defendant who had a legal duty to act. Furthermore, under common law, a mere bystander generally has no legal duty to rescue a stranger in peril, whereas the safety monitor's liability hinges on her own duty, not the bystander's inaction.

**(B) [Original D] [DOMINANT TRAP]** This is ineffective because homicide does not always require a specific intent to kill. Forms of homicide such as involuntary manslaughter can be based on criminal negligence or recklessness, which involve a failure to perceive or a conscious disregard of a substantial risk, rather than a specific intent to cause death. Thus, "lack of intent" is not a complete defense to a homicide charge.

**(D) [Original A]** Physical absence is not a defense if a legal duty to act exists. If the monitor had been required to be present and her absence constituted a breach of that duty, she could still be held liable for the resulting death. The critical issue is the existence of the duty, not the defendant's location at the time of the incident.

## 8. Black-Letter Verification
**Tested Rule:** Liability for an omission requires a legal duty to act.
**Authority:** Model Penal Code § 2.01(3); *State v. Miranda*, 715 A.2d 680 (Conn. 1998) (outlining sources of legal duty).
**Reasoning:** Legal duties arise from (1) statute, (2) contract, (3) status relationship, (4) voluntary assumption of care, or (5) creation of peril. The safety monitor’s duty was contractual. An employer or their authorized agent (the supervisor) can modify or terminate the scope of employment. Once the monitor was released at 8 P.M., her contractual duty to the patrons of the pool ceased.

## 7. governing_c3_lane

## 8. choice_by_choice_walkthrough
- **Choice A (The adult's failure):** This is a **Causation Trap**. Students often think that if someone else *could* have helped and didn't, it lets the defendant off the hook. But unless that adult had a duty, their inaction is legally "nothing," and even if they had a duty, criminal law allows for multiple causes.
- **Choice B (No intent):** This is a **Mens Rea Trap**. Students forget that homicide doesn't just mean "murder." It includes manslaughter, which can be based on negligence or recklessness. "I didn't mean to" is not a defense to negligence.
- **Choice C (Duty terminated):** This is the **Correct Answer**. It attacks the very first requirement of omission liability: the existence of a legal duty at the time of the omission. 
- **Choice D (Not present):** This is **Factual Noise**. Absence is not a defense; it is the *content* of the omission. If you have a duty to be there, not being there is the crime.

## 9. residual_answer
Choice C survives because it identifies the missing element (legal duty) that prevents the actus reus from being criminal.

## 10. legal_leak_audit
The law correctly distinguishes between a moral obligation and a legal duty. Employment is a contract, and contracts can be modified by the parties (or their agents, like a supervisor).
