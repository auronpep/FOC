# BarMatrix CQ 14916 — Sonoma Vineyard Waiver (Pass-1 Transform Report)

## 1. Final Question

A valid written contract executed in January requires Ruth, the seller, to sell her late husband's 22-acre vineyard in the Sonoma Valley to Daniel, the buyer. The contract states, "The parties agree that closing will occur on March 1 at 2 p.m." There is no other reference to closing. The contract is silent as to quality of title. On February 23, Ruth notifies Daniel that she has discovered that the land is subject to a long-standing recorded easement in favor of the Cedar Creek Wine Cooperative for a wine-press road and grape-haul route, should the cooperative ever resume pressing operations at its old facility. Daniel concludes that a resumption of cooperative pressing is so unlikely that closing should occur notwithstanding the easement. Therefore, on February 24, Daniel notifies Ruth that he will expect to close on March 1. When Ruth later refuses to close, Daniel sues Ruth for specific performance. Is Daniel likely to prevail?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (A)

The implied covenant of marketable title is read into every contract for the sale of real estate, even when the contract is silent on the subject. The covenant runs to the **buyer**; it is the buyer's protection, not the seller's. An easement held by a third party is an encumbrance and therefore renders the seller's title unmarketable as a matter of black-letter property law. The twist that defeats choice **C** is that the covenant is waivable. The buyer may affirm the contract and accept the land subject to a known defect. That is exactly what Daniel did on February 24, when he wrote to Ruth that he "will expect to close on March 1" notwithstanding the easement. Once Daniel waived, Ruth had no remaining ground to refuse to convey. Because the legal and equitable remedy for seller's wrongful refusal to close in a land-sale contract is specific performance, Daniel will likely prevail.

**Silver Key:** when the buyer's response to a discovered title defect is "I'll close anyway," the question is no longer whether title was marketable — it is whether the buyer still has a marketable-title defense. He doesn't, so choice **B**'s notice-and-cure reasoning doesn't even reach the analysis.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### B. "Yes, because the seller did not give notice of the easement a reasonable time before the closing date."

This choice gets the bottom line right but misstates the reasoning. The "reasonable time to cure" timing rule operates when a buyer *objects* to a discovered title defect: the buyer must notify the seller with specificity and allow the seller a reasonable opportunity to remove the defect before the closing date. Here, Daniel did not object. He expressly told Ruth he would close "notwithstanding" the easement. The notice-and-cure clock never started because Daniel waived the very right the rule exists to protect. The right answer is A, not B — and B's reasoning collapses once you identify that Daniel's response was waiver, not objection. (This is the Silver Key.) Lawyer confirmation: the notice-and-cure rule is a corollary of the buyer's right to insist on marketable title, not a free-standing duty of the seller.

### C. "No, because the easement renders the seller's title unmarketable." (Dominant trap — 18% inherited.)

This is the most attractive wrong answer. The premise is true: the easement is an encumbrance, and encumbrances render title unmarketable. The trap is the leap from a true premise to a false conclusion. Because the implied covenant of marketable title is for the **buyer's** benefit, only the buyer can invoke it as a ground to avoid the contract. The seller cannot weaponize the buyer's marketable-title right to escape a deal the seller no longer wants. Once Daniel affirmed the contract and said he would close, the covenant was waived, and the unmarketable-title premise no longer decides the outcome. C is a classic half-truth: a correct sub-rule (easement = encumbrance = unmarketable) coupled with a missing element (the buyer's waiver right).

### D. "No, because rights of third parties are unresolved."

This choice is a misfit. The third party's rights are perfectly resolved: the Cedar Creek Wine Cooperative holds a recorded easement for a wine-press road and grape-haul route. That easement is not contingent, in litigation, or in dispute. The cooperative has whatever rights the recorded instrument gives it, full stop, before and after the sale. The question on the exam is whether the **buyer** can be forced to take land subject to a known easement, and that question is answered by the buyer's waiver, not by the status of the cooperative's rights. D invites the student to chase a non-issue.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

**The rule.** Every contract for the sale of real estate contains an implied covenant that the seller will deliver marketable title at closing, even where the contract is silent on the question of title quality. *Marketable title* is title of such character as would reasonably be accepted by a prudent, educated buyer in the ordinary course of business; an easement held by a third party is an encumbrance that renders title unmarketable. The covenant runs to the **buyer**, not the seller, and the buyer may waive it — expressly or by conduct — and proceed to closing. Once the buyer has waived, the seller is not entitled to refuse to close on the ground that title is unmarketable.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer).**

- The implied covenant of marketable title is a universally recognized black-letter rule in U.S. real-property law. See, e.g., Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School, *Implied warranty* (defining marketable title as the warranty of good title against infringement, implying no liens or encumbrances), and *Marketable title* (Cornell Wex) (last reviewed March 2022). Source: [https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/implied_warranty](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/implied_warranty).
- *Staley v. Stephens*, 404 N.E.2d 633 (Ind. Ct. App. 1980) — confirmed real authority holding that any infringement rendering title unmarketable gives the buyer a ground to refuse, absent waiver, and that the buyer is the party who decides whether to invoke that ground. Source: [https://lawschoolers.com/marketable-title-and-deeds/](https://lawschoolers.com/marketable-title-and-deeds/) (case summary).
- The Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes §§ 2.1, 2.4 (Am. Law Inst. 2000) frames the easement-as-encumbrance rule that drives the unmarketability premise in choices A and C; the marketable-title rule itself is contract-law-rooted and articulated in real-property casebooks as black-letter.

**CLASH phase.** A and B both conclude "Yes," so they survive CUT and fight on a single axis: **Is the buyer's correct affirmative ground "waiver" or "notice-and-cure timing"?**

- **A (correct).** Waiver: the buyer is the holder of the marketable-title right, and Daniel's February 24 letter is a clear waiver by conduct of any objection to the easement.
- **B (distractor).** Notice-and-cure timing: the seller didn't give Daniel enough time to address the defect before closing. This is a real doctrine, but it applies when the buyer *objects*. Daniel did not object; he waived.

**Splitting fact.** Did the buyer object, or did the buyer affirm? The stem is explicit: "notwithstanding" the easement, "he will expect to close." That's affirmation, not objection. B's reason cannot reach the outcome on these facts.

**CALL.** A. The buyer's affirmation is the affirmative ground; the seller must perform.

### 2. The 17-section student case study

#### Program frame

Real Property — Marketable Title (Contracts for Land). The implied covenant of marketable title is read into every land-sale contract, even a silent one. The covenant runs to the buyer; the buyer alone may waive it; the seller's refusal to close must rest on something the seller holds, not on something the buyer holds and has released.

#### Distilled Core Question

Land-sale contract silent on title. Seller discovers a long-standing easement and notifies buyer. Buyer replies he will close anyway. Seller refuses. Will the buyer win specific performance?

#### Call and prediction

The call is a likelihood-of-prevailing question on specific performance. The C3 prediction is "Yes" — the buyer holds the only right that could excuse closing, and the buyer has expressly given it up.

#### Trigger facts

1. The contract is silent as to quality of title.
2. A long-standing recorded easement in favor of the Cedar Creek Wine Cooperative burdens the land.
3. Daniel, on February 24, tells Ruth he will close on March 1 "notwithstanding" the easement.
4. Ruth later refuses to close.
5. Daniel sues for specific performance.

#### Governing C3 lane

The lane is Marketable Title — implied covenant. The deciding phase is **CLASH** because the two "Yes" answers fight on a single axis (waiver vs. notice-and-cure timing), and the answer is reached by resolving the axis, not by adding doctrine.

#### Choice-by-choice walkthrough

**A (correct) — 4-part block**

1. **C3 signal (student-accessible).** The contract says nothing about title. The implied covenant that fills the silence is the buyer's protection — not the seller's. Daniel's February 24 letter is an unmistakable affirmation: "I will expect to close on March 1" *notwithstanding* the easement. Once the holder of a right says they don't need it, the right is gone.
2. **Student label.** "Buyer's right, buyer's release."
3. **True/responsive version.** A true/responsive version of A would also work as "Yes, because the buyer waived the implied covenant of marketable title by affirming the contract after learning of the easement."
4. **Lawyer confirmation.** Black-letter rule: the implied covenant of marketable title is implied in every land-sale contract; the covenant runs to the buyer; the buyer may waive it expressly or by conduct; waiver leaves the seller with no marketable-title ground to refuse to close. Source: Cornell LII, *Implied warranty* ([https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/implied_warranty](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/implied_warranty)).

**B (distractor) — 4-part block**

1. **C3 signal.** The bottom line (Yes) is right, but the *reason* targets the wrong doctrine. The "reasonable time to cure" rule is a buyer's corollary, not a seller's duty. It kicks in only when the buyer *objects* to a title defect. Daniel did not object. He waived. The corollary never started.
2. **Student label.** "Right answer for the wrong reason — notice-and-cure doesn't apply when the buyer has waived."
3. **True/responsive version.** B would be true and responsive if Daniel had objected to the easement and demanded cure, and the seller had failed to cure within a reasonable time.
4. **Lawyer confirmation.** The notice-and-cure rule is the buyer's remedy for preserving the right to insist on marketable title; it presupposes an objection. Waiver extinguishes the right; the corollary is inoperative.

**C (distractor — dominant trap) — 4-part block**

1. **C3 signal.** The first half of C is true: easement = encumbrance = unmarketable. The second half is wrong: it treats the seller's refusal as if the seller could invoke the buyer's right. The covenant is for the buyer's benefit, and the buyer has waived. The seller's refusal is not grounded in any surviving right.
2. **Student label.** "True sub-rule, false conclusion. The seller's hand is empty once the buyer has waived."
3. **True/responsive version.** C would be true and responsive if Daniel had *objected* to the easement, refused to close, and Ruth had tried to force the sale.
4. **Lawyer confirmation.** *Staley v. Stephens*, 404 N.E.2d 633 (Ind. Ct. App. 1980) (encumbrance that renders title unmarketable gives the buyer a ground to refuse, absent waiver; the buyer — not the seller — is the party who decides whether to invoke the right).

**D (distractor) — 4-part block**

1. **C3 signal.** The cooperative's rights are not in litigation, not contingent, not in dispute. The recorded easement is the full answer to "what rights does the cooperative hold?" The exam question is the buyer's remedy against the seller, not the third party's standing.
2. **Student label.** "Misfit — the third-party question is already answered by the recorded easement."
3. **True/responsive version.** D would be true and responsive if the easement were *in litigation* or *contested* between the parties to the contract.
4. **Lawyer confirmation.** An easement that is recorded and unchallenged is, by definition, a resolved bundle of rights. Sale of the servient estate does not affect the easement's continuing validity.

#### Residual answer

**A.** Yes, because the decision to terminate the contract for title not being marketable belongs only to the buyer. Daniel holds the only right that could excuse closing, and he has expressly released it.

#### Legal-leak audit

- Implied covenant of marketable title runs to the buyer → student-accessible (the choice text says "belongs only to the buyer"; the stem shows the buyer affirming).
- Easement = encumbrance = unmarketable → student-accessible (the only place this doctrine is needed is in the half-truth mechanic for C, and C is wrong on the conclusion, not on the premise).
- Notice-and-cure applies only when the buyer objects → student-accessible (visible from the stem: Daniel affirmed, did not object).
- Third-party rights are resolved by the recorded easement → student-accessible (visible from the stem; no need to bring in easement-validity doctrine).
- Drift audit: all four choice walks are student-accessible. No hidden doctrinal unlock required. Drift clean.

#### Final student script

> Two "No" choices hit the table first. **C** (the dominant trap) is true on the sub-rule (easement = unmarketable) but wrong on the conclusion (the seller can't use the buyer's right as a defense). **D** is a misfit (the cooperative's rights are resolved by the recorded easement). CUT both. The two "Yes" choices fight on one axis: is this an *objection* case (B's notice-and-cure logic) or a *waiver* case (A)? Daniel said "notwithstanding the easement, I will close" — that's waiver, not objection. B's doctrine doesn't reach the outcome on these facts. CALL A.
