Daniel writes a note asking Stephen to burn down a private Bible-map storage loft. Daniel seals the note in a greeting-card envelope and drops it in the outgoing courier box at a Christian craft fair. ### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is the best argument because completed solicitation turns on a communicated request. Daniel's note shows what he intended, and dropping it in the courier box shows a step toward communicating it, but Stephen never received or learned of the request. Gold Key: for completed solicitation under the communication-required formulation, the request must reach another person; an unreceived message may show intent or attempt, but it does not complete solicitation. Silver Key: separate "prepared/sent" from "communicated"; the call asks completed solicitation, not attempt.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is the strongest trap because the courier-box drop feels like a meaningful act. The breaker is the charge: completed solicitation is not proved by an overt act if the request never reaches the solicited person.

B is attractive because Daniel's criminal purpose is obvious. The breaker is that solicitation needs more than intent; the request must be communicated to another person under the tested rule.

D notices that arson targets property. The breaker is that the property-crime label does not answer the solicitation element; the missing piece is communication.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The credited answer remains unchanged in substance. Solicitation is an inchoate offense based on intentionally asking, commanding, encouraging, or requesting another person to commit a crime. Under the communication-required formulation tested here, completed solicitation requires the request to be communicated to the solicited person. A letter or note that is intercepted before receipt may show intent and may support an attempt theory in some jurisdictions, but it does not complete solicitation under that formulation.

Authority floor: California Penal Code section 653f defines solicitation in terms of soliciting another person to commit specified crimes, and People v. Saephanh, 80 Cal. App. 4th 451 (2000), held that an intercepted, unreceived letter did not support completed solicitation because the solicitous communication was not received by the intended recipient. Source checked: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/4th/80/451.html. I also checked Justia's solicitation overview for the same communication-required formulation: https://www.justia.com/criminal/offenses/inchoate-crimes/solicitation/.
