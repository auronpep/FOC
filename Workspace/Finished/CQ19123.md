# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question — stem + call

Stephen agreed to build a stone wall around Martha's olive grove for $4,200. After he had
set the gateposts and laid the first long section, a master builder in a distant city offered
Stephen a place on a large project that he had to accept right away or lose. Stephen asked
Martha to release him from their agreement, and Martha, seeing the opportunity he had,
agreed. Stephen then asked to be paid for the stonework he had already completed. Martha
refused. Can Stephen sue to recover the value of the partial work he performed?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**D is correct.** Neither party had fully performed — the wall was only partly built — so the
contract was still executory on both sides, and parties to a wholly executory contract may
agree to cancel it. That agreement is a mutual rescission; each party's surrender of its
remaining rights is the consideration for the other's. Once they rescind, the deal is off,
and absent any agreement to settle up, neither party owes the other for benefits already
conferred — so Stephen cannot recover for the stonework he had laid. **Gold Key:** a mutual
rescission of a still-executory contract discharges all remaining duties AND, unless the
parties agree otherwise, leaves no obligation to pay for part performance already rendered.
**Silver Key:** two "Yes" choices just frame the same restitution instinct two ways
(entitlement / benefit received) — both fall to the rescission rule, so the "No, mutual
rescission" answer beats them both.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A — "entitled to be paid for the part of the work." (dominant trap)** It sells the
strongest fairness instinct on the page: Stephen did real work, so surely he is entitled to
be paid for it — and the choice tracks his exact request word for word. The breaker: a
part-performance recovery right exists in other settings (breach, unjust enrichment), but a
mutual rescission discharges it; the missing element is that both parties agreed to cancel.
**Gold Key:** after a mutual rescission neither party owes for benefits already received
unless they agreed otherwise.

**B — "Martha received the benefit of the stonework."** It sells common-sense fairness: she
got value, so she ought to pay for it. The breaker: "received a benefit" is the fairness
appeal, not the legal test; once the parties rescind the contract, the benefit already
conferred creates no obligation. **Silver Key:** when two choices reach the same "Yes" through
benefit-received and entitlement framings, treat them as one trap and look for the rule that
defeats both.

**C — "Stephen was the one who asked to end the agreement."** It sells a fault-flavored
intuition: he walked away, so he forfeits any claim — and it even lands on the correct "No."
The breaker: who proposed the rescission is a non-dispositive fact; the outcome turns on the
mutual rescission itself, not on which party raised it first. **Silver Key:** cut any choice
whose reason is the identity of who asked to cancel — it answers a question the call never
posed.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

**Tested rule (unchanged).** A contract that remains executory on both sides may be
discharged by the parties' mutual agreement to cancel it — a mutual rescission — with the
discharge of each side's remaining duties serving as consideration for the other's
(Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 283, Agreement of Rescission: an agreement of rescission
discharges all remaining duties of performance of both parties). Where the rescission
agreement is silent on benefits already exchanged, the majority/bar-tested view treats the
rescission as calling the whole deal off, leaving neither party obligated to pay for part
performance already rendered.

**Application.** Stephen had built only part of the wall, so the contract was still executory
when he and Martha agreed to release each other; their agreement is a valid mutual rescission.
Nothing in their agreement provided for paying Stephen for the completed stonework, so he has
no claim for the value of his partial performance. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited
outcome (No recovery) are **unchanged** from the source item (original key B). *Lawyer note:*
a minority restitution/status-quo-ante line would allow recovery to prevent unjust enrichment;
the official key follows the majority "leave things where they lie" view, so the key stands —
`agrees_with_official_key: true`, no key disagreement.

**review_truth.** When both parties agree to call off a contract that neither has fully
performed, the mutual rescission discharges the deal and — absent a contrary agreement —
neither side can recover for work or benefits already provided.

## Gold Key(s)

- **GK-CONTRACTS-RESCISSION-01 (rule).** A mutual rescission of a still-executory contract
  discharges all remaining duties AND, unless the parties agree otherwise, leaves no
  obligation to pay for part performance already rendered. *Unlocks* the fairness "Yes"
  choices (A, B). *Authority (lawyer layer):* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 283.

## Silver Key(s)

- **SK-CONTRACTS-SAMEOUTCOME-01 (answer_array).** Two "Yes" choices frame the same restitution
  instinct two ways (entitlement / benefit received) — both fall to the rescission rule, so the
  "No, mutual rescission" answer beats both.
- **SK-CONTRACTS-WHOASKED-02 (cut).** Cut any choice whose reason is the identity of who asked
  to cancel the deal — that is non-dispositive to recovery for part performance.
