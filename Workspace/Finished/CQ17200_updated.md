# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

Peter and three others agree to steal wine barrels from a vineyard after the harvest. Peter draws a map of the cellar layout and lends them his duplicate key to the storage gate. Two weeks before the planned night, Peter privately decides he cannot go through with it. He stops coming to the evening planning sessions at the vineyard's edge. He does not tell the others about his change of heart, does not ask for his key back, and does not warn the vineyard owner. On the planned night, the others use Peter's key and map to take the barrels. Peter later claims he withdrew from the conspiracy. Is Peter's withdrawal claim likely effective?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A conspirator privately regrets joining, stops attending meetings, and does nothing to communicate withdrawal or retrieve the aid he provided. Is his silent withdrawal effective? No—withdrawal requires an affirmative act.

## 3. Final answer choices (post-shuffle)

**A.** Yes, because withdrawal is automatic once a conspirator misses a meeting

**B.** No, but only because wine barrels are valuable

**C.** Yes, because he privately changed his mind before the theft

**D.** No, because mere absence and private regret do not effectively withdraw from the conspiracy

## 4. Correct answer

**D** (original key was B)

## 6. Full right-answer explanation

D is correct. Withdrawal from a conspiracy is not automatic and cannot be accomplished by private regret alone. The law requires an affirmative act—either communicating withdrawal to the co-conspirators or neutralizing the aid one has provided, such as retrieving tools or alerting authorities. Peter did neither. He never told the others he was out, never asked for his key back, and never warned the vineyard owner. His mere absence and silent regret are legally insufficient to sever his connection to the conspiracy.

**Gold Key:** Conspiracy withdrawal requires an affirmative act—communicating withdrawal to co-conspirators or neutralizing the aid provided. Mere silence, absence, or private regret is never sufficient.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice invents an automatic rule. The word "automatic" is a structural tell; no legal doctrine makes withdrawal effective merely because a conspirator misses a meeting. Absence may be evidence of intent in some contexts, but it is never dispositive. A student should cut any choice that uses absolute language like "automatic" without a named exception.

**C.** This choice sells the dominant trap: the emotional narrative that a private change of heart should be enough. It is an autonomy appeal—Peter's personal choice to leave is treated as legally operative. But criminal liability is not erased by regret. The law requires an affirmative act to communicate or neutralize.

**B.** This choice reaches the right conclusion ("No") but for the wrong reason. It introduces a non-dispositive element—property value—that is never mentioned in the stem as a factor in withdrawal analysis. The "only because" overclaims, making value the sole determinant. When a choice cites a factor the stem never tied to the call, it is not responsive.

## 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited answer is legally correct. Under the majority rule, withdrawal from a conspiracy requires an affirmative act that is reasonably calculated to communicate the defendant's departure to co-conspirators or to defeat the conspiracy's purpose. Mere cessation of activity, private regret, or silent absence is insufficient. The Federal Law Enforcement Training Centers (FLETC) teaches that a co-conspirator must perform an affirmative act inconsistent with the goals of the conspiracy and must communicate that withdrawal to a fellow co-conspirator or law enforcement. The Model Penal Code § 5.03(6) likewise requires that the defendant "thwarted the success of the conspiracy, under circumstances manifesting a complete and voluntary renunciation of his criminal purpose." People v. Denio, 454 Mich. 691, 710 (1997), quoting United States v. Castro, 972 F.2d 1107, 1112 (9th Cir. 1992), notes that the crime of conspiracy is a continuing offense "presumed to continue until there is affirmative evidence of abandonment, withdrawal, disavowal, or defeat of the object of the conspiracy." Peter's conduct—stopping attendance, keeping his key with the group, and never warning anyone—does not meet this standard. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original item.
