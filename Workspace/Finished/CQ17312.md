# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question
After a midweek Bible-study supply pickup, officers stopped a volunteer van for a broken taillight. While speaking with Daniel, the driver, an officer saw a syringe tucked in Daniel's jacket pocket. Daniel admitted that he used the syringe for illegal drug use and said he had been using drugs during the trip. Officers searched the van for drugs.

On the rear bench, officers found Lydia's zippered tote bag. Lydia was a passenger in the van, had not been arrested, and was not separately suspected of any crime. The officers opened the tote and found methamphetamine.

Lydia moves to suppress the methamphetamine. Was the search of Lydia's tote constitutional?

## 2. Distilled Core Question
When police have probable cause to search a vehicle for drugs, may they open a passenger's personal bag inside the vehicle if the bag could contain drugs?

## 3. Final Answer Choices
A: No, because Lydia was not arrested, and the police therefore could not search any property associated with her.

B: Yes, because probable cause to search the van for drugs extended to containers in the van, including a passenger's tote, where drugs could be hidden.

C: Yes, but only because Lydia assumed the risk of a search by riding in a van driven by someone using illegal drugs.

D: No, because Lydia's ownership of the tote gave her a stronger Fourth Amendment interest than Daniel had in the van, and ownership controls the searchability of a container.

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation
(B) is correct. The officer had probable cause to believe drugs were in the van after seeing the syringe and hearing Daniel admit illegal drug use during the trip. Under the automobile exception, probable cause to search a vehicle for contraband permits officers to search containers in the vehicle that could conceal the object of the search. That authority is not limited by the fact that the container belongs to a passenger who has not been arrested or independently suspected.

Lydia's tote was inside the van and could hold drugs. Because the object of the search was drugs, the tote fell within the permissible scope of the vehicle search. The methamphetamine is admissible.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations
(A) is incorrect because the search was not justified as a search incident to Lydia's arrest. The automobile exception turns on probable cause to search the vehicle for contraband, not on whether the passenger herself has been arrested.

(C) is incorrect because the rule is not based on implied consent or assumption of risk. Lydia did not lose Fourth Amendment protection by choosing to ride in the van. The search is valid only because the automobile exception permitted a container search within the scope of the probable cause.

(D) is the dominant trap. It starts from a real intuition: a purse, tote, or personal bag carries privacy significance. But Wyoming v. Houghton rejects a passenger-property carveout when officers have probable cause to search the vehicle for contraband and the container could conceal the object of the search. Ownership does not control the container's searchability.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification
Primary authority: Wyoming v. Houghton, 526 U.S. 295 (1999), holds that officers with probable cause to search a car may inspect passengers' belongings found in the car when those belongings could conceal the object of the search.

Supporting scope authority: United States v. Ross, 456 U.S. 798 (1982), permits a vehicle search extending to containers that may conceal the object of the search. California v. Acevedo, 500 U.S. 565 (1991), confirms the automobile-exception container-search framework.
