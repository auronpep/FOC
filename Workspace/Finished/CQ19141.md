## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question
Ruth owned a rare hand-illuminated Psalms folio. She gave the folio to Stephen, who ran a private Christian bookshop, as collateral for a short-term loan. Eight months later, after Ruth paid the loan in full, she demanded the folio back. Stephen refused because his assistant mistakenly believed that one payment was still unpaid. Stephen did not check the shop ledger until seven weeks later, when he discovered the error and offered to return the folio. Ruth refused to take it back. The folio was worth $8,000 when Stephen received it, $24,000 when Ruth paid the loan and demanded its return, and $6,000 when Stephen later offered to return it.

If Ruth brings an action against Stephen based on conversion, how much, if anything, should Ruth recover?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
When a lawful holder refuses a rightful demand to return collateral after the debt is paid, what value date controls conversion damages?

### 3. Final answer choices
A. $6,000, because that was the value of the folio when Ruth declined Stephen's later offer to return it.

B. $24,000, because that was the value of the folio when Ruth was entitled to its return and Stephen refused her demand.

C. Nothing, because Stephen originally received the folio under a lawful collateral arrangement.

D. $8,000, because that was the value of the folio when Stephen first took possession.

### 5. Correct answer
B (original key: D)

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. Stephen's first possession was lawful, but the conversion occurred later, when Ruth had paid the debt, had the immediate right to the folio, demanded it back, and Stephen refused. Gold Key: lawful possession at the start does not protect a holder who later refuses a rightful demand to return chattel; the conversion date is the refusal date. Silver Key: when the choices line up three dollar amounts with three dates, label the legal event first, then choose the amount attached to that event. The operative legal event is Stephen's refusal when Ruth was entitled to return, so the recovery is $24,000.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because it makes Ruth's later refusal to accept the folio look like the event that should limit damages. The breaker is that Stephen had already completed the conversion when he refused Ruth's rightful demand, and the later attempt to undo the problem does not move the damages date. Gold Key: conversion damages are measured at the refusal date, not at a later repair offer. Silver Key: the lowest number belongs to the remedy-cleanup date, not the conversion date.

C is attractive because Stephen did not steal the folio at the beginning and held it under a real collateral arrangement. The breaker is that lawful possession can turn into conversion when the holder refuses to surrender the chattel after the owner is entitled to immediate possession. Gold Key: the initial lawful receipt is not immunity from a later conversion.

D is attractive because it picks a real value from the stem and anchors on the first transfer of possession. The breaker is that Stephen's first possession was authorized; the conversion did not occur until Ruth paid the loan, demanded return, and Stephen refused.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue is Torts, Intentional Torts to Property, Conversion, and the verified outline code is `64010203`. The source row's listed negligence/custom code does not match the tested doctrine, so the conversion code is used.

The governing rule is unchanged: conversion is a serious intentional exercise of dominion or control over chattel that justifies requiring the defendant to pay the chattel's full value. A person who lawfully received a chattel can still commit conversion by refusing, on demand, to surrender it to the person then entitled to immediate possession. The ordinary damages measure is the value at the time and place of conversion. Restatement (Second) of Torts sections 222A, 237, and 927 support those propositions. Accessible research checks: Restatement section 222A was checked at `https://www.justsecurity.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/222A-What-Constitutes-Conversion-1.pdf`; a court discussion of Restatement section 237 was checked at `https://www.courts.wa.gov/opinions/pdf/D2%2048507-9-II%20Unpublished%20Opinion.pdf`; Restatement section 927's value-at-time-and-place measure was checked through court and jury-instruction sources including `https://www.mass.gov/doc/superior-court-model-civil-jury-instructions-conversion-pdf/download`.

Stephen's mistaken ledger belief does not change the conversion date. Ruth had fully repaid the loan and was entitled to immediate possession. Stephen refused her demand. At that moment the folio was worth $24,000, so the credited outcome remains $24,000.
