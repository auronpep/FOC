7) Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

A is correct. Under traditional common-law homicide doctrine, a defendant who intentionally uses force likely to cause serious bodily injury (even if not intending death) that in fact causes another's death may be convicted of murder. Here, Peter picked up a hammer and struck Matthew on the head; a jury could find that Peter intended to inflict serious bodily harm, and that the resulting death satisfies the elements of murder under common law. Although the defendant may argue provocation and heat of passion to mitigate to voluntary manslaughter, mere destruction of property (the wooden cross) is ordinarily insufficient to constitute legally adequate provocation. Gold Key: property damage (even personal, nontrivial damage) usually does not meet the objective standard for adequate provocation that reduces murder to voluntary manslaughter; classic examples of adequate provocation are violent assault or discovering a spouse in bed with another. Therefore, murder is the most serious crime of which Peter could properly be convicted.

Breaker: Heat-of-passion reduction to voluntary manslaughter requires legally adequate provocation measured by objective standards (an ordinary person would lose self-control). Destruction of property, even valuable or sentimental property, ordinarily does not constitute the kind of extreme provocation that the common law recognizes (examples include discovery of adultery or a serious assault). The prosecution could therefore prove murder by showing intent to inflict serious bodily harm and causation, defeating this mitigation. C (Involuntary manslaughter) — why attractive: Students may think the killing was accidental or reckless without the required intent for murder, reducing liability to involuntary manslaughter. Breaker: The facts show an intentional striking with a hammer (an intentional use of force capable of causing serious bodily injury). In common-law doctrine, an intentional act intended to cause serious bodily harm that results in death supports murder rather than mere involuntary manslaughter.

D (Assault) — why attractive: This choice downgrades the conduct to a simple assault (the application of force) rather than a homicide offense. Breaker: Assault is a less serious offense than homicide; because the act resulted in death, the prosecution can pursue homicide charges (murder or manslaughter) that encompass the assaultive conduct and the resulting fatality. 9) Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

Black-letter rule (student version): Under common law, murder includes (1) an unlawful killing of another (2) with malice aforethought, which includes an intent to kill or an intent to inflict serious bodily harm; voluntary manslaughter is a killing that would otherwise be murder but is committed in the heat of passion caused by adequate provocation.

Authoritative grounding and notes: The common-law distinction between murder and manslaughter — murder for intentional killing or intent-to-inflict-serious-bodily-harm and voluntary manslaughter for heat-of-passion killings provoked by adequate provocation — is a longstanding principle found in classic criminal-law sources (see Blackstone, Commentaries on the Laws of England (Book IV) and standard criminal law treatises). Jurisdictions vary on what counts as adequate provocation; traditionally, mere property damage is unlikely to suffice.
