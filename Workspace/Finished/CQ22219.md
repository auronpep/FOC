### 7. Full right-answer explanation
Choice C is the one answer that keys admissibility to the right relationship: the statement was made by Lydia, and Mary is offering it against Lydia in the same lawsuit. That is the opposing-party-statement lane. Gold Key: a party's own statement, when offered against that party, is not hearsay under Rule 801(d)(2)(A), so the deputy may repeat Lydia's words for their truth. Silver Key: when one answer choice turns on who said the words and who is offering them, lock onto that axis before drifting into public-record or impeachment labels.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
**A.** This choice sells the familiar out-of-court-statement reflex: officer heard it outside court, so call it hearsay and stop. The breaker is that the rule has a specific carveout for an opposing party's own statement when offered against that party. Gold Key: once that lane is open, the generic hearsay label does not defeat admissibility.

**B.** This choice sounds lawyerly because a deputy and a scene investigation make students think "public record." The breaker is that the question is not asking whether a government record can come in; it is asking why Lydia's own statement can be used against her, and the public-record label does not solve that declarant-level hearsay problem. Silver Key: keep your eyes on who made the words and who is offering them, because that is the axis this choice dodges.

**D.** This choice pulls students toward prior-statement impeachment doctrine because the proof comes through another witness. The breaker is that Mary is not forced to use Lydia's words only to attack credibility; she can offer them substantively as an opposing-party statement, and impeachment is beside the point here. Silver Key: do not let a prior-statement answer drag you into Rule 613 when the cleaner path is Rule 801(d)(2)(A).

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. The question asks whether one litigant may offer the opposing litigant's own out-of-court statement through an officer who heard it at the scene. Under Fed. R. Evid. 801(d)(2)(A), a statement is not hearsay when it is offered against an opposing party and was made by that party in an individual capacity. That is exactly this setup: Lydia made the statement, and Mary offers it against Lydia. See Fed. R. Evid. 801(d)(2)(A): https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=2023&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-2023-title28a-node230-article8-rule801

Choice A is therefore legally wrong because the statement is not excluded by Rule 802 once Rule 801(d)(2)(A) applies. Choice B is legally wrong because "public record" is the wrong evidentiary path; Rule 803(8) concerns a public office's record or statement, and even if a report were involved, that label would not by itself supply admissibility for the private declarant's embedded assertion. See Fed. R. Evid. 803(8): https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title28a-node232-article8-rule803 Choice D is legally wrong because substantive use does not depend on impeachment, and Rule 613(b) expressly says its prior-inconsistent-statement foundation rule does not apply to an opposing party's statement under Rule 801(d)(2). See Fed. R. Evid. 613(b): https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title28a-node232-article6-rule613
