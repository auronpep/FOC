# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question (revised stem + call)

Naomi owned a wooded ridge of about fifty acres. Complying with the state's subdivision law, she subdivided it into eighteen building lots together with the required public areas, retaining for herself a separate parcel on which she built a wood-fired heating plant. From that plant she ran insulated steam pipes to every lot in the development, supplying heat to the cottages. Every conveyance of a lot contained a restriction requiring the grantee to purchase from Naomi all heat used on the lot. Paul contracted to purchase a lot from Naomi by a written agreement in which Paul promised that, after the closing of title, he would purchase from Naomi all heat used on the lot at a specified price for a period of twelve years. At the closing, Naomi delivered a deed containing the heat-purchase restriction, but Paul did not sign the deed. Four years after closing, Paul installed his own furnace and stopped purchasing heat from Naomi.

**If Naomi asserts a claim for damages against Paul, should the court find in Naomi's favor?**

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A buyer signs a written contract promising to buy all of a resource from the seller for a fixed term, then stops before the term ends. In the seller's damages suit, is the buyer liable on the contract regardless of whether the promise runs with the land?

## 3. Final answer choices (A–D, post-shuffle)

- **A.** No, because the agreement to purchase heat did not touch and concern Naomi's land.
- **B.** No, because Paul did not sign the deed.
- **C.** Yes, because Paul contracted to purchase heat from Naomi for a period of twelve years.
- **D.** Yes, because a discrepancy between a contract for the sale of realty and a conveyance is resolved by looking to the contract.

## 4. Correct answer

**C** (original key letter: A).

## 6. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

The call asks whether the court should find for Naomi on a damages claim. **C** is correct. Paul personally signed a written agreement to buy all his heat from Naomi for twelve years and breached it after four. **Gold Key:** the original promisor is bound by privity of contract on a covenant he made, *whether or not the covenant runs with the land* — "running" doctrine governs only whether **successors** are bound, never the person who made the promise. Naomi sues the original promisor, so she recovers on the contract; nothing in the facts makes it unenforceable. **Silver Key:** when the defendant is the very party who signed the promise, you don't need the covenant to run — sue on the contract and cut every choice arguing about running, touch-and-concern, or successor liability.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A — did not touch and concern (dominant trap).** It invites the student to run the prestige analysis — touch and concern, privity of estate, notice — and feel rewarded for spotting a "covenants" issue. But touch and concern decides only whether a covenant binds *successors*; against the original promisor the claim rests on his own contract, so the doctrine is the wrong question. **Gold Key:** running-covenant requirements never reach the person who personally made the promise. *(Lawyer layer: the covenant did touch and concern in any event — only the heating-plant owner benefits, only a lot owner can comply.)*

**B — Paul did not sign the deed.** It trades on the intuition that you're bound only by what you sign. Paul signed the written purchase agreement, and a grantee who accepts a deed is bound by its restrictions even without signing it. **Gold Key:** acceptance of a deed — here, taking the lot — supplies the grantee's assent; signature is not required.

**D — discrepancy resolved by looking to the contract.** It sounds lawyerly and gestures at a real area (contract-versus-deed conflicts). The rule is backwards and the premise is missing: under the merger-by-deed doctrine the deed controls, not the contract, and here the contract and deed say the same thing — there is no discrepancy. **Gold Key:** at closing the contract merges into the deed; the deed governs the estate conveyed.

## 8. Black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

**Tested rule (unchanged):** an original covenantor is personally liable on his contractual promise by **privity of contract**, independent of whether the covenant "runs with the land." The running requirements (intent, notice, privity of estate, touch and concern) determine only whether a **successor** to the burdened or benefited parcel is bound; they do not insulate the original promisor, who is liable in contract for his own breach. Naomi sues Paul — the original promisor — so she prevails on the contract (**C**). Choice **A** misapplies running doctrine to the wrong defendant (and is factually wrong: the heat covenant benefits the retained plant parcel and burdens the lot, so it does touch and concern). Choice **B** invents a signature requirement: a grantee is bound by deed restrictions upon **acceptance** of the deed, without signing. Choice **D** reverses the **merger-by-deed** rule (the deed, not the contract, controls a discrepancy as to the estate conveyed) and assumes a discrepancy that the facts do not contain. Tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are **unchanged** from the original.

*Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):* Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes (running of covenants; §3.2 reframes the traditional touch-and-concern requirement, which the bar still tests; the contract claim does not depend on running, so the framing change is immaterial to the outcome); merger-by-deed doctrine; deed-poll / grantee-acceptance doctrine. Doctrine/Restatement-class authority; no case name asserted. Not on the doctrinal-currency hotlist. ([Real covenants & privity](https://jdadvising.com/real-covenants-and-the-privity-requirement/), [Merger doctrine (property law)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merger_doctrine_(property_law)))
