## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**B is correct.** The rule of larceny applies to "the personal property
of another." Once olives are picked from the trees, they are no longer
part of the realty — severance converts them into personal property
(chattels). Ruth, the owner, had already done that work: she picked the
olives and laid them in baskets to take to market the next day. Stephen
thereafter trespassorily took those baskets, carried them away on a
donkey, and did so with the intent to sell them as his own — i.e., to
permanently deprive Ruth of them. All four common-law elements of
larceny are satisfied, and **Gold Key:** larceny of trees, crops, and
other products of the land turns on the *moment of severance* — once
severed, the item is personal property regardless of where it sits
(beside a wall, beside a barn, on the back of a truck, anywhere).

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations (one per distractor)

### Choice A — "No, because produce from a grove can never be personal property for larceny purposes."

The cognitive move this sells is the categorical "never" — the trap is
that the student reads "produce" and "real property" as a single
category and concludes it can never become personalty. The breaker is
the **severance rule**: standing crops or uncut trees are real
property, but the moment they are picked, felled, or otherwise detached
from the soil, they become personal property. A "never" rule that
ignores severance is not the law. C3 cut: the choice uses an absolute
("never") that contradicts the visible state of the olives in the
stem — already picked and laid in baskets beside a wall, i.e.,
*already* converted to personalty. Filter broken: **NOT_TRUE**.

### Choice C — "Yes, but only if Stephen personally picked the olives from the trees himself."

The cognitive move this sells is the "only if" — it sounds cautious and
rule-like, but it injects a fabricated extra element: that the
defendant must have been the one who severed the property from the
land. The breaker is that the larceny rule asks whether the defendant
took **personal property of another** with intent to steal. It says
nothing about who performed the prior severance. The owner (Ruth)
already did; the thief's contribution was the trespassory taking. The
"only if personally picked" qualifier is invented. C3 cut: the answer
is in the stem, not in the thief's résumé. Filter broken: **NOT_TRUE**.

### Choice D (dominant trap) — "No, because Stephen had ridden the donkey only a short way down the road before the household woke and stopped him."

This is the highest-pull wrong answer on the mechanic, and the only
distractor that disguises a true rule behind a wrong threshold. The
cognitive move it sells is the **asportation myth** — that the thief
must have moved the property a "meaningful" distance before the
taking is complete. The breaker is the actual asportation rule: any
movement of the property, however slight, satisfies asportation. Once
Stephen loaded the baskets and began riding the donkey, the asportation
was complete; the subsequent stop did not undo the taking. C3 cut: the
movement element of larceny is satisfied by the *slightest* carrying
away — short distance is the rule's friend, not its enemy. Filter
broken: **NOT_TRUE**.

> **Gold Key:** larceny requires (i) trespassory taking (caption), (ii)
> carrying away (asportation — any movement suffices), (iii) of personal
> property of another, (iv) with intent to permanently deprive. Of those,
> (ii) is the one that fools students into "short-distance" denials.

## 9. Black-letter verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**The rule.** Common-law larceny is the trespassory taking (caption)
and carrying away (asportation) of the **personal property of another**
with intent to permanently deprive the owner of its possession. The
Rule applies to timber, crops, fixtures, or other products of the land
*once those items are severed from the realty*; before severance they
are part of the realty and cannot be the subject of larceny. Severance
— by cutting, picking, digging, or otherwise detaching the item from
the soil — converts the item into personal property (a chattel).

**Application to Stephen's conduct.** Ruth had already severed the
olives: she picked them from the trees and laid them in baskets beside
her courtyard wall, plainly in preparation for market. The olives were
therefore personal property at the moment Stephen took them. Stephen's
loading of the baskets onto a donkey and riding off is a trespassory
taking and asportation (any movement, however slight, satisfies
asportation). His stated intent — to sell the olives as his own — is
intent to permanently deprive. Every element of common-law larceny is
satisfied.

**Authority floor (lawyer confirmation).**

- *Common-law larceny elements:* caption + asportation + personal
  property of another + intent to permanently deprive. This is the
  canonical articulation retained in the Model Penal Code and in U.S.
  larceny codifications descended from the common law.
- *Authority:* **Model Penal Code § 223.2** ("Theft by Unlawful Taking
  or Disposition") — the modern codification of the trespassory
  taking/asportation framework; movables are the analog of "personal
  property of another" and the section expressly reaches tangible
  movable property taken from the owner without consent. (Authority
  class: rule-number / model code. Low fabrication risk; widely
  cited.)
- *Severance principle:* the doctrine that products of the land become
  personal property upon severance is a foundational real-property
  rule. I do not cite a specific case for the severance sub-rule to
  avoid citation risk; the rule is well settled and a treatise citation
  would not raise the primary-law floor under the QA gate. Flag for
  attorney review if a specific case anchor is needed downstream.

**Doctrinal-currency check.** The severance + larceny rule is not on
the doctrinal-currency hotlist (it does not implicate FRE 702, the
Confrontation Clause, Kennedy v. Bremerton, Bruen, Dobbs, Mallory,
Cedar Point, Ramos, Erlinger, or Counterman). No currency concerns.
