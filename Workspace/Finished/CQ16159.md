## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question
Lydia testified as a witness in a civil trial about a private Christian bookstore's inventory dispute. On cross-examination, she was asked whether she had been convicted in circuit court of stealing $320 from a former employer's cash drawer on March 3, 2019. Lydia answered, "No, I have never been convicted of any crime." In fact, Lydia had pleaded guilty to that charge and had been placed on probation. Lydia was later charged with perjury on the ground that her denial of a conviction was false. A statute in the jurisdiction defines perjury as knowingly making a false statement while under oath. At Lydia's perjury trial, the state proved her sworn statement and the prior conviction. Lydia testified that Paul, the lawyer who represented her in the theft case, had told her that because she had received probation and completed it successfully, she had not been convicted of a crime. Paul's advice was incorrect. If the jury believes Lydia, it should find her:

### 2. Distilled Core Question
If a perjury statute requires a knowingly false statement, is a witness guilty for denying a prior conviction when she honestly believed probation meant there was no conviction?

### 3. Final answer choices
A. Guilty, because relying on a lawyer's incorrect advice is not a defense.
B. Not guilty, because she lacked the necessary mental state.
C. Not guilty only if the jury also finds that her reliance on the lawyer's advice was reasonable.
D. Guilty, because her misunderstanding was a mistake of law.

### 4. Correct answer
B (source official key: D)

### 6. Student black-letter-law answer explanation
B is correct. The statute does not punish every false statement under oath. It punishes a knowingly false statement under oath. If the jury believes Lydia, she thought she had not been convicted because her lawyer told her probation meant no conviction. That belief may be wrong, but it prevents the state from proving that she knew her denial was false. Gold Key: lawyer advice is not a freestanding defense, but the belief produced by the advice can still defeat a required mental state. Silver Key: when the statute gives you the mental word, test the element before testing an excuse.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct because the perjury statute requires knowledge that the sworn statement is false. Lydia's answer was objectively false because a guilty plea followed by probation counted as a conviction in the jurisdiction. But the jury is told to believe Lydia's testimony about what Paul told her. If she honestly believed she had not been convicted, she did not knowingly make a false statement when she denied having a conviction. The legal advice does not excuse completed perjury. It matters as evidence that the knowledge element was missing.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because it states a familiar criminal-law rule: relying on a lawyer's mistaken advice ordinarily does not excuse a crime. The breaker is that Lydia does not need an excuse if the state cannot prove the required knowledge. Gold Key: separate "lawyer advice as a defense" from "lawyer advice as evidence that the defendant lacked the required mens rea."

C is attractive because it tries to make the lawyer-advice fact feel safer by adding a reasonableness screen. The breaker is that knowledge is subjective here. If Lydia did not know her statement was false, the perjury element fails even if her reliance on Paul was careless or unreasonable.

D is attractive because Lydia misunderstood the legal effect of probation, so the label "mistake of law" sounds decisive. The breaker is the statute's exact mental-state requirement. This is not a person who knowingly violated the perjury statute and then says she did not know perjury was illegal; it is a person who, if believed, did not know the statement was false.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law and Procedure, General Principles, Mens rea, and the verified outline code is `72010302`. The supplied statute requires that the defendant knowingly make a false statement while under oath. Federal false-declaration law uses the same core mens rea idea: 18 U.S.C. § 1623(a) punishes a person who, under oath in a court or grand jury proceeding, "knowingly makes any false material declaration." Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1623

Model Penal Code § 2.04(1)(a) states the general mistake principle: ignorance or mistake as to a matter of fact or law is a defense when it negatives the culpability required for a material element of the offense. Source: https://www.criminallawweb.net/web1/MPC/PART1/snippets/2_04%2B2_06%285%29.htm

Those authorities confirm the official key. The prosecution proved Lydia's statement and the prior conviction, but the question asks what happens if the jury believes Lydia's testimony about her state of mind. Because she believed probation meant she had not been convicted, she lacked knowledge that her denial was false. The attorney's incorrect advice is not itself a defense; it is the factual source of the belief that defeats the knowledge element. The tested rule, issue, trap structure, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row.
