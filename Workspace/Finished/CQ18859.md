## 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is the best answer. The problem is not that the state is forbidden to criminalize counterfeit sales to minors. The problem is the conclusive presumption. The judge told the jury that once it found the trigger fact -- more than 45 minutes in the same closed vendor tent -- it had to find the sale element.

**Gold Key:** A mandatory or conclusive presumption that forces the jury to find an element of a crime violates due process because the prosecution must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt.

**Silver Key:** lock the verb "must." A jury instruction that says the jury must find an element after a trigger fact is not merely allowing an inference; it removes the element from ordinary jury proof.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Substantive due process requirements.**
This is the dominant trap. It sells the broad-rights instinct: a statute feels overreaching, so the answer must be about liberty. The breaker is the call/fact match. The challenge is to a conviction based on a compelled factual finding, not to a fundamental right to sell counterfeit wristbands to minors.

**C. The prohibition against bills of attainder.**
This sells a punishment-by-statute story. A criminal statute punishes conduct, so the label sounds close. The breaker is that a bill of attainder punishes identified persons or a defined class without the benefit of a judicial trial. Paul was prosecuted and tried; the defect is the jury instruction's mandatory fact finding.

**D. The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.**
This sells the classification instinct. The statute mentions age and relationship status, so a student may reach for equal protection. The breaker is that the best attack is not unequal treatment. The operative defect is that the jury was required to find an element from a conclusive presumption.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

**Pass-1 Step 1 - tested black-letter rule.** In a criminal case, due process requires the prosecution to prove every fact necessary to constitute the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt. A mandatory or conclusive presumption in a jury instruction violates that requirement if a reasonable juror could understand it as requiring a finding on an element once a predicate fact is found.

Legal research confirms the source key. In *Sandstrom v. Montana*, 442 U.S. 510 (1979), the Supreme Court held unconstitutional an instruction that a reasonable jury could understand as conclusive or as shifting the burden of persuasion on an element. In *Francis v. Franklin*, 471 U.S. 307 (1985), the Court applied the same principle to mandatory presumptions in criminal jury instructions. The Constitution Annotated summary of the Fourteenth Amendment reasonable-doubt doctrine also grounds the rule in *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970): absent a guilty plea, due process requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt before conviction.
