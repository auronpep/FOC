### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** When a contract is discharged by impossibility, restitution for benefit conferred survives discharge. The performing party may recover in quasi-contract the reasonable value of the work already done, measured by the benefit to the other party — not the contract rate.

C is correct. The flood destroyed the subject matter of the contract (the bookshelf site and materials in Lydia's basement) through no fault of either party. This is a classic case of discharge by impossibility: the supervening event made further performance literally impossible. Both Timothy's duty to finish the bookshelf and Lydia's duty to pay the $800 are discharged going forward. However, Timothy conferred a real benefit on Lydia — half a built bookshelf frame and three installed shelves in her home. Quasi-contract (legal restitution) requires Lydia to pay the reasonable value of the benefit she received, to avoid unjust enrichment.

---

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations (Diagnostic Register)

**D (dominant trap — original A, inherited 17%):** The attraction here is simplicity: if the contract is dead, everyone walks away. It *feels* fair — the house flooded, nobody's at fault, tough luck all around. But this ignores the restitution principle. The contract is discharged only as to *future* performance. The benefit Timothy already conferred on Lydia (half a built bookshelf) is a real gain she received. Letting her keep it for free is unjust enrichment. **Gold Key:** Restitution for benefit conferred survives discharge by impossibility.

**B (original D, inherited 19%):** This answer correctly identifies discharge on one side but then reaches for the wrong remedy — reliance damages. Reliance damages compensate a party for expenses incurred in preparing to perform, and they are generally *not* available when the contract was discharged by impossibility (as opposed to breach). The right remedy is restitution for the *benefit conferred* on Lydia, out-of-pocket expenditures are reliance; quasi-contract measures the value received by the defendant. **Silver Key:** When the call asks about remedies after impossibility discharge, look for restitution (benefit conferred), not reliance (costs incurred).

**A (original C, inherited 10%):** This treats Lydia's promise to pay $800 as still enforceable because "cash isn't impossible to hand over." But it misstates the law: impossibility of the *subject matter* discharges *both* sides. Lydia doesn't owe the contract price because the contract is fully discharged. The performing party's recovery is in quasi-contract, not under the contract.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Governing rule — Doctrine of Impossibility (Discharge by Supervening Impossibility of Performance):**

When the existence of a particular thing is essential to a contract, and that thing is destroyed without the fault of either party, the contract is discharged. *Taylor v. Caldwell* (1863) 122 Eng. Rep. 309 (K.B.) (destruction of music hall excused performance). Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 261 (discharge by supervening impracticability); § 265 (discharge by supervening frustration of purpose). Under the Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment § 38 (2011), a party who has conferred a benefit on the other party in the course of performance under a contract that is subsequently discharged for impracticability or frustration may recover in restitution for the benefit conferred, measured by the value of the benefit to the recipient.

**Restitution / Quasi-Contract:**

The elements of quasi-contract (unjust enrichment / restitution) are: (1) a benefit conferred by the plaintiff on the defendant; (2) the defendant's knowledge or appreciation of the benefit; and (3) acceptance or retention of the benefit under circumstances that make it inequitable for the defendant to retain it without payment. *See* Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment §§ 1–2 (2011).

**Application to the facts:**

The flood (water main break, no fault of either party) destroyed the construction site and all materials, making further performance literally impossible. This triggers discharge by impossibility under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 261. Both Timothy's and Lydia's forward-looking duties are discharged. However, Timothy already built half a bookshelf — a tangible benefit Lydia received. Restitution survives independently of contract discharge. *See* Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment § 38 (restitution for benefit conferred under a contract subsequently discharged).

**Unchanged from original:** The tested rule (impossibility discharge + restitution for partial performance), the legal issue, and the credited outcome are identical.
