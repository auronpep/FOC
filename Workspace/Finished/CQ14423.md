## 1. Final Question

A missionary bought on credit and took delivery of a rugged satellite phone and solar charging kit from a Christian missions supply ministry for an agreed price of $875. The missionary promised to pay the supplier, "as soon as I am able" after the next quarterly support update. The missionary subsequently repudiated this promise and refused to pay the supplier anything. What effect does this quoted language have on enforceability of the promise?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

## 7. Right-answer explanation

B is correct. The quoted language creates a condition precedent to the missionary's duty to pay the fixed price. The supplier (the party for whose benefit the condition operates) bears the burden of proving that the condition has occurred — that the missionary is now able to pay the $875. Once that is shown, the duty to pay arises. The promise is not illusory because it imposes a real obligation once the ability exists.

## 8. Wrong-answer explanations

A is incorrect. Nothing in the facts gives the missionary (the promisor) the right or power to avoid the contract on grounds such as duress, undue influence, or misrepresentation. The language only delays when the duty to pay arises; it does not make the contract voidable at the buyer's option.

C is incorrect. The seller/supplier is the party who will receive the payment once the condition is satisfied. The burden of proving a condition precedent has occurred rests on the party for whose benefit the condition exists, not on the party whose performance is conditioned.

D is incorrect. The missionary's promise to pay a definite sum once able is real consideration that makes the contract enforceable. The open-ended timing does not render the promise illusory; it merely postpones the moment the duty matures.

## 9. Black-letter verification with authority

The language "as soon as I am able" creates an express condition precedent. Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 224, a condition is "an event, not certain to occur, which must occur, unless its non-occurrence is excused, before performance under a contract becomes due." Section 225 provides that the non-occurrence of a condition discharges the duty. The burden of proving that a condition precedent has occurred is on the party for whose benefit the condition was inserted — here the supplier who is to receive payment. See also common-law authorities allocating the burden to the promisee asserting satisfaction of a condition precedent to the promisor's duty (e.g., the rule reflected in cases such as those collected under Corbin on Contracts and standard MBE materials on conditions). The original credited outcome and tested rule are unchanged.

The variation preserves the original rigor: the item still turns on recognizing a condition precedent and correctly allocating the burden of proof, with the same three classic distractors (voidable, illusory, reversed burden).
