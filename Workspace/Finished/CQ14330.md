## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

A federal statute provides that all high-capacity rechargeable batteries discarded in this country must be processed only at facilities licensed by the federal Environmental Protection Agency. Acting under the statute and all required federal procedures, the agency has adopted very strict standards for licensing those battery-processing facilities. The cost of using licensed facilities is substantial.

The State of New Zion, through Commissioner Lydia's facilities office, owns thousands of these batteries. The barn is not federally licensed, although in daily practice it satisfies most of the standards the Environmental Protection Agency imposes on licensed battery-processing facilities.

Consistent with United States Supreme Court precedent, may the state continue to process its discarded batteries in this manner?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. The federal law directly regulates the disposal of discarded high-capacity batteries, a class of goods moving in and affecting interstate commerce. Once that federal rule is validly enacted, the state cannot keep using its own unlicensed facility simply because the facility belongs to the state. Gold Key: a state is not exempt from a valid federal commerce regulation merely because the regulated state activity supports traditional governmental functions; the anti-commandeering limit blocks federal commands that make states regulate private parties, not ordinary compliance by the state as a regulated actor. Silver Key: the winning answer says no and gives the federal command as the reason, instead of sorting the batteries by commercial use or police use.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because it treats practical safety compliance as the real target of the federal scheme. The breaker is that the federal law requires processing at a licensed facility, and this state barn has no license. Meeting most standards is not the same thing as satisfying a licensing requirement.

B is attractive because it reaches the correct no result and uses the commercial-equipment fact that sounds easiest to fit under the Commerce Clause. The breaker is responsiveness. The state must comply with the valid federal rule for all discarded batteries covered by that rule, not only the batteries from revenue-producing state operations. Silver Key: in this answer array, do not let the correct no outcome rescue a reason that turns on the wrong fact split.

D is the dominant trap. It sells the familiar state-sovereignty instinct: police equipment feels like the kind of core governmental function that should sit outside federal control. The breaker is the Gold Key: Supreme Court doctrine does not give a state a traditional-government-function exemption from a valid commerce regulation that directly regulates state activity. The state is being regulated as a battery processor, not conscripted to regulate private citizens.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. A valid federal law regulating commerce binds states as well as private actors under the Supremacy Clause. U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2 makes valid federal law supreme, and U.S. Const. art. I, sec. 8, cl. 3 gives Congress power to regulate interstate commerce. Under Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority, 469 U.S. 528 (1985), the Court rejected an immunity test based on traditional governmental functions and held that applying federal commerce legislation to a state transit authority did not exceed Congress's commerce power. Under Reno v. Condon, 528 U.S. 141 (2000), Congress may regulate states directly as owners or participants in regulated activity without impermissibly commandeering them to regulate private parties.

The transformed federal statute, like the source tire-disposal statute, regulates a class of discarded commercial articles through a licensing requirement. The state owns an unlicensed processing facility and uses it for covered items. Because the federal law is valid and directly regulates disposal activity, the state may not continue using the unlicensed facility. The fact that some batteries came from police equipment does not create state immunity. The fact that some batteries came from commercial equipment is not the exclusive reason for the no result. The fact that the barn satisfies most standards does not satisfy the license command.
