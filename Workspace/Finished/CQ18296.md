### 1. Final question
During a week of Christian youth camp in the mountains, Stephen served as the lead counselor for a group of high-school boys. On the final night he led the boys on a short hike to a clearing for a star-gazing devotional. The trail was slick from earlier rain. Timothy, one of the campers, kept stepping off the marked path to look at wildlife. After the third warning, Stephen reached out, grabbed the strap of Timothy's backpack, and pulled him firmly back onto the trail. Timothy's boot slipped on wet leaves; he fell sideways down a rocky embankment and struck his head. He died from the head injury before the group could get help.

At Stephen's trial for second-degree murder under a state statute that defines the crime as "knowingly causing the death of another person" and further provides: "The mental state of the defendant at the time of the killing is presumed from the act of killing. The defendant bears the burden of producing evidence that the killing was accidental," the trial court instructed the jury in the exact words of the statute. Stephen's counsel presented no evidence and did not call Stephen to testify. The jury returned a guilty verdict. On appeal, Stephen argues that the jury instruction violated due process. Is Stephen's due process claim correct?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
After a youth camp counselor grabs a camper's backpack to pull him back onto a slick trail during a night devotional hike and the camper slips and dies, and the trial court instructs the jury that the mental state for second-degree murder is presumed from the act of killing and the defendant must produce evidence it was accidental, does the instruction violate due process by shifting the burden on the "knowingly" element?

### 3. Final answer choices
A: No, because the instruction only required Stephen to produce some evidence that the fall was accidental, and shifting the burden of production on the mental-state element is always permissible under the Due Process Clause.

B: No, because the state legislature has the authority to define the elements of crimes, including by setting presumptions that accompany the act of causing death on a camp trail.

C: Yes, because any presumption regarding a defendant's mental state in a criminal case is unconstitutional under the Due Process Clause.

D: Yes, because the instruction places the burden of producing evidence on an element of the offense on the defendant, relieving the prosecution of its constitutional duty to prove knowledge beyond a reasonable doubt.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
D is correct. The instruction told the jury to presume the mental state "knowingly" (an element of the charged second-degree murder) from the act of causing death and required Stephen to produce evidence that the killing was accidental. Gold Key: a jury instruction that creates a mandatory presumption shifting the burden of production or persuasion on an element of the crime to the defendant violates the Due Process Clause. Silver Key: when the call asks whether the due-process claim is correct, stay with the precise constitutional defect (burden shift on an element) rather than a blanket attack on all presumptions or a general defense of legislative power. The credited answer therefore survives as the residual.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because students commonly remember that the burden of production can shift for affirmative defenses and assume the same rule applies to elements of the crime itself. The breaker is that once the presumption is mandatory and shifts any burden on an element (here the mental state "knowingly"), it violates due process regardless of whether the shift is labeled "production" or "persuasion."

B is attractive because it appeals to the general principle that legislatures have wide latitude to define crimes. The breaker is that even though states may define the elements of offenses, they may not relieve the prosecution of its constitutional duty to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt through a mandatory presumption that shifts the burden to the defendant.

C is attractive because it sounds like a strong pro-defendant rule and many students over-generalize the ban on presumptions. The breaker is that permissive inferences (the jury may but need not infer the mental state) are constitutional when rationally connected to the proven facts; only mandatory presumptions that shift the burden on an element are forbidden.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law (Homicide / Murder) and the verified outline code is 74020101. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), holds that the Due Process Clause requires the prosecution to prove every element of a criminal offense beyond a reasonable doubt. Sandstrom v. Montana, 442 U.S. 510 (1979), holds that a jury instruction creating a mandatory presumption (even a rebuttable one) that shifts the burden of production or persuasion on an element such as intent or knowledge to the defendant is unconstitutional. Francis v. Franklin, 471 U.S. 307 (1985), confirms that the same rule applies when the instruction shifts only the burden of production on an element. Here "knowingly" is an element of the second-degree murder defined by the statute. The instruction told the jury to presume that element from the act of killing and required the defendant to produce evidence to rebut it. This unconstitutionally shifts the burden on an element to the defendant and violates due process. The credited outcome is unchanged from the source row.
