### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. Ruth committed larceny when she picked up Daniel's pin, put it in her pocket, and did so with the intent to keep it. Gold Key: larceny is complete once there is a wrongful taking plus even slight movement with intent to steal; later guilt and return do not downgrade the completed offense. Silver Key: in a "most serious crime" call, freeze the elements at the moment the offense became complete before giving later repentance any weight.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because Ruth returned the pin quickly, so the episode feels unfinished. Gold Key: the taking was already complete when she pocketed the pin with intent to steal; the later return may matter morally, but it does not turn completed larceny into only an attempt.

B sells the clean-ending story. Ruth gave the pin back, so a student may want the law to treat the guilt and return as wiping the slate clean. The breaker is the earlier snapshot: she had already taken and moved Daniel's property with intent to steal.

D is attractive because Ruth was helping her employer at the registration table. The breaker is custody. Daniel's personal pin did not come lawfully into Ruth's possession through her job; she acquired possession only by picking it up and pocketing it without permission.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested issue is unchanged: whether a defendant who takes and slightly moves another person's personal property with intent to steal commits larceny even though the defendant quickly returns it. The credited outcome is unchanged: larceny is the most serious proper conviction.

For the lawyer-confirmation layer, DOJ Criminal Resource Manual section 1639 describes larceny under 18 U.S.C. 641 as requiring wrongful taking and carrying away, personal property of another, lack of consent, and intent to deprive, citing United States v. Barlow, 480 F.2d 1245, 1251 (D.C. Cir. 1972): https://www.justice.gov/archives/usam/criminal-resource-manual-1639-definition-steal-or-purloin. People v. Shannon, 66 Cal.App.4th 649 (1998), states the common larceny asportation point directly: slight movement with intent to deprive completes theft, and later return does not erase the completed offense: https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/ca-court-of-appeal/1064481.html. DOJ Criminal Resource Manual section 1005, quoting Moore v. United States, 160 U.S. 268 (1895), distinguishes embezzlement as appropriation of property entrusted to the defendant or lawfully in the defendant's hands: https://www.justice.gov/archives/usam/criminal-resource-manual-1005-embezzlement.

Applied here, Ruth knew the pin belonged to Daniel, picked it up without permission, moved it into her pocket, and at that moment intended to keep it. That satisfies larceny. Returning it a few minutes later does not undo the completed offense. Embezzlement does not fit because Ruth's employment did not give her lawful possession of Daniel's personal pin before the conversion.
