### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The amount in controversy **is** satisfied. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a), the amount in controversy must exceed $75,000. A single plaintiff **may aggregate all claims — related or unrelated — against a single defendant** to meet the threshold. Mary has two claims against Paul totaling $85,000 ($50,000 for the botched car repair and $35,000 for the unpaid tutoring); aggregated, the amount exceeds $75,000. Diversity is satisfied because the parties are citizens of different states. **Silver Key:** the aggregation rule is a single-plaintiff-vs-single-defendant rule; relatedness is irrelevant when one plaintiff is suing one defendant. The "unrelated" qualifier is a red herring.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. No, because unrelated claims can never be aggregated.**

This is the dominant trap. The student assumes the rule requires the claims to be related. The breaker is the actual rule: a single plaintiff CAN aggregate unrelated claims against a single defendant. The "never" is a `tiered_absolute` overclaim — relatedness is NOT a requirement for a single plaintiff aggregating against a single defendant. Relatedness IS a requirement for **multiple plaintiffs** aggregating against a single defendant, but the present case has one plaintiff. **Silver Key:** run the "one plaintiff vs one defendant" check first; if yes, the claims aggregate whether or not they are related.

**B. No, because each individual claim must exceed $75,000.**

This is the "single-claim" trap. The student applies a non-existent requirement that each individual claim independently exceed $75,000. The breaker is the aggregation rule: a single plaintiff can aggregate to meet the threshold, so the $50,000 claim and the $35,000 claim together satisfy it. The "each individual claim must exceed" condition is a `fabricated_rule`. The rule that each claim must independently exceed $75,000 is the **supplemental-jurisdiction** rule for some post-Exxon Mobil contexts, not the general aggregation rule.

**C. Yes, but only because the claims arise under federal law.**

This is the "basis" trap. The student gets the answer right (yes) but for the wrong reason (federal question). The breaker is the basis: this is a **diversity** action under § 1332(a), not a federal-question action under § 1331. The aggregation rule applies to diversity cases (and to federal-question cases too), but the basis for jurisdiction here is diversity of citizenship plus the amount in controversy. The "but only because" condition is a `wrong_element` cut — it identifies a non-dispositive element (federal question) as if it were the dispositive one.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17709, a Civil Procedure item on aggregation of claims for the amount in controversy. The official key is A (yes, one plaintiff may aggregate multiple claims against one defendant to exceed $75,000).

The controlling rule sits in 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a), which grants district courts original jurisdiction over civil actions between citizens of different states where the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000. The aggregation rule is well-settled: a single plaintiff may aggregate all of its claims against a single defendant, whether the claims are related or unrelated, to meet the amount in controversy. See, e.g., the Restatement-style aggregation discussion in Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc., 545 U.S. 546 (2005), and the leading Civil Procedure treatises (e.g., Wright & Miller, Federal Practice and Procedure §§ 3701-3704). The rule contrasts with the rule for multiple plaintiffs (cannot aggregate unrelated claims against a single defendant) and for a single plaintiff against multiple defendants (cannot aggregate unless joint liability).

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: Mary has two claims against Paul (a botched auto repair and a separate tutoring engagement) totaling $85,000. Mary (State A) and Paul (State B) are citizens of different states, satisfying diversity. The aggregated amount exceeds $75,000, so the amount-in-controversy requirement is met. The asset (a contract + a property-damage claim) and the cast (Jon and Kara → Mary and Paul) and the specific dollar amounts ($50K + $35K) have been re-skinned, but the doctrinal anchor (single-plaintiff aggregation) is unchanged.

## program_frame

This is a Civil Procedure question on aggregation of claims for the amount in controversy. The legal issue is narrow but classic: when a single plaintiff sues a single defendant in federal court on multiple claims, may the plaintiff aggregate the claims to meet the $75,000 threshold under § 1332? The trap structure is three classic wrong-direction statements of the aggregation rule (unrelated-cannot, each-must-exceed, and federal-question-basis) versus the correct statement (single-plaintiff aggregation). The discrimination between the correct answer and the dominant trap requires the single-plaintiff-vs-single-defendant anchor.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. No, because unrelated claims can never be aggregated.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The "never" is a `tiered_absolute` overclaim. The aggregation rule does NOT require relatedness when one plaintiff sues one defendant. The "relatedness required" rule applies to multiple plaintiffs, not to one plaintiff.
2. **Student label:** Unrelated-cannot-aggregate trap (dominant).
3. **True/responsive version:** "A single plaintiff may aggregate all claims, related or unrelated, against a single defendant. Relatedness IS required for multiple plaintiffs aggregating against a single defendant, but the present case has one plaintiff."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Single-plaintiff aggregation rule under 28 U.S.C. § 1332. A single plaintiff can aggregate all claims, related or unrelated, against a single defendant. See Wright & Miller, Federal Practice and Procedure §§ 3701-3704.

### B. No, because each individual claim must exceed $75,000.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The "each individual claim must exceed $75,000" condition is a `fabricated_rule`. A single plaintiff can aggregate to meet the threshold; the rule that each claim must independently exceed $75,000 is the multi-plaintiff rule, not the single-plaintiff rule.
2. **Student label:** Each-claim-must-exceed trap.
3. **True/responsive version:** "A single plaintiff can aggregate to meet the threshold. The $50,000 claim and the $35,000 claim together exceed $75,000."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Single-plaintiff aggregation rule under 28 U.S.C. § 1332. The "each claim must independently satisfy" rule applies to multiple plaintiffs aggregating unrelated claims, not to a single plaintiff.

### C. Yes, but only because the claims arise under federal law.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The student gets the answer right (yes) but for the wrong reason (federal question). The basis here is diversity, not federal question, and the "but only because" condition makes the federal-question basis dispositive when it is not.
2. **Student label:** Basis trap (federal-question mischaracterization).
3. **True/responsive version:** "Yes, the amount is satisfied — but because a single plaintiff can aggregate all claims against a single defendant, not because the claims arise under federal law. The basis for jurisdiction is diversity of citizenship plus the aggregated amount."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a) (diversity jurisdiction) is the basis. The aggregation rule applies to both diversity and federal-question cases, but the present case is diversity.

### D. Yes, because one plaintiff may aggregate multiple claims against one defendant to exceed $75,000.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the residual answer. The Silver Key tells the student that the single-plaintiff-vs-single-defendant rule makes relatedness irrelevant, so the unrelated-claims-cannot-aggregate choice is the dominant trap.
2. **Student label:** Residual answer (single-plaintiff aggregation).
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a); single-plaintiff aggregation rule. See Wright & Miller, Federal Practice and Procedure §§ 3701-3704.
