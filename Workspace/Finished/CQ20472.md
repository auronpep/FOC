## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The buyer (Timothy) transferred both possession and title to his money to Barnabas when he paid cash for the generator and took it away. Barnabas obtained title to that money by knowingly false statements of past and existing fact (the hour meter reading and the "brand new / no flood damage" condition of the unit). This is false pretenses.

**Gold Key:** False pretenses requires the victim to transfer *title* to the property (here, the purchase money), not mere possession or custody. Larceny by trick applies only when the defendant obtains possession/custody while title remains with the victim.

Barnabas's two knowing material lies about existing facts induced the completed sale and payment. The crime is complete when title to the money passes.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A (Embezzlement):** Attractive because the facts involve a trusted church member and a transfer of money that later turns out bad; students reach for "he had the money and then kept it wrongfully." Breaker: Embezzlement requires the defendant to have been in *lawful possession* of the property *before* the conversion. Barnabas had no possession of Timothy's money until the sale; the transfer itself was induced by the lies. No prior entrustment existed.

**B (No crime):** Attractive because the transaction looks like an ordinary private sale between church members and the lies feel like "just puffery" or a civil warranty issue. Breaker: The knowing false statements of past/existing fact (usage history and flood concealment) that induced the victim to part with title to his money satisfy the elements of false pretenses. It is not mere opinion or future promise.

**D (Larceny by trick — dominant trap):** Attractive because the seller used a trick (fake meter + concealment) to get the buyer's money; students stop at "fraud + property transferred = larceny by trick." Breaker (Gold Key): Larceny by trick requires that the victim transfer only *custody/possession*, not title. In a sale where the buyer pays and receives the item, title to the purchase money passes to the seller. The title passage moves this out of larceny by trick and into false pretenses.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested black-letter rule is the classic common-law distinction among theft crimes based on whether and when title or possession passes:

- False pretenses: Defendant obtains *title and possession* of the victim's property by a false representation of a past or existing material fact, knowing it is false, with intent to defraud, and the victim relies on the representation.

- Larceny by trick: Defendant obtains *possession/custody only* (title does not pass) by trick or deception.

- Embezzlement: Defendant is already in lawful possession of the property (entrusted), then converts it to his own use.

- No crime (on these facts): incorrect; the elements of false pretenses are met.

Research confirmation (lawyer-confirmation layer only): Standard MBE bar-prep sources and hornbooks draw the title-versus-possession line exactly as the original explanation states. See, e.g., the distinctions summarized in bar-review materials contrasting larceny (including by trick), false pretenses, and embezzlement: title passage to the defendant on a sale induced by existing-fact fraud = false pretenses; only custody = larceny by trick; prior lawful possession + later conversion = embezzlement. The two knowing lies here (rolled-back hour meter as a representation of existing usage; concealment of flood submersion as a representation of existing condition) are classic existing-fact misrepresentations. The completed cash sale and delivery of the generator confirm title to the money passed. The credited outcome (false pretenses) and the legal issue (which theft crime fits when title passes via fraud in a sale) are identical to the source row. No drift.

Authority floor: The distinctions are black-letter common-law rules preserved for MBE testing of these specifically named crimes (many jurisdictions retain the labels even after consolidation statutes). No leading case citation is required for the core distinction; the rule statement itself is the governing authority for exam purposes.
