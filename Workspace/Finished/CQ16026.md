## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

The correct answer is C — neither conspiracy.

Conspiracy, at common law, is an agreement between two or more persons to commit a crime, plus (in most modern statutes) an overt act in furtherance of the agreement. Whether the charged conspiracy exists therefore turns on whether the target offense — the crime the parties agreed to commit — is actually a crime under the governing law. Here, the plan had two candidate "targets": entering the study (potentially burglary) and copying the manuscript (potentially larceny). Neither one is a common-law crime on these facts.

**Burglary (common law)** is the breaking and entering of the **dwelling** of another **at night** with intent to commit a felony therein. The study is part of a church, not a dwelling; and Sunday afternoon is not nighttime. Two of the four core elements fail, so the agreement was not to commit common-law burglary. (A modern statutory breaking-and-entering charge may exist in many jurisdictions, but the question tests the common-law definition.) **Gold Key: Common-Law Burglary — the dwelling + nighttime requirements are non-negotiable. An office entered during the day is not common-law burglary.**

**Larceny (common law)** is the trespassory **taking and carrying away (asportation)** of the personal property of another with intent to **permanently deprive** the owner. The plan was to *copy* the manuscript and leave the original in the drawer. There is no taking, no asportation, and no intent to permanently deprive — the property returns to the owner untouched. The agreement was not to commit common-law larceny. (A separate unauthorized-copying statute may apply in some jurisdictions, but the question tests the common-law definition.) **Gold Key: Common-Law Larceny — copying is not taking. Asportation and the intent to permanently deprive are both missing.**

Because the agreement was to commit no common-law crime, there is no conspiracy to commit either. **Silver Key: When a "Both X and Y" choice is on the table, test each prong independently. If either prong fails on its own facts, the conjunction collapses — both prongs must be true, not just one.**

## 8. Black-Letter Verification (Lawyer-Confirmation Layer)

**Tested rule.** Conspiracy liability attaches only when the parties agree to commit a *crime* as defined by the controlling law. The target offense is measured against its own elements.

**Common-law burglary.** Defined as the breaking and entering of the *dwelling* of another *at night* with the intent to commit a felony therein. Blackstonian formulation, preserved by modern courts describing the historical common-law rule. *See, e.g., Cornell LII, Wex "Burglary"* (defining common-law burglary as "the breaking and entering of the dwelling house of another in the nighttime with the intent to commit a felony") [https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/burglary]. Modern statutes have often dropped the dwelling or nighttime element (or both), but the question tests the common-law definition.

**Common-law larceny.** Defined as the trespassory *taking and carrying away* (caption + asportation) of the personal property of another with the *intent to permanently deprive* the owner. *See, e.g., Cornell LII / Wex-style sources and standard criminal-law treatises* (Blackstone's "wrongful or fraudulent taking and carrying away, by any man, of the personal goods of another"). *Copying* does not satisfy asportation because the property is not moved; it does not satisfy the intent to permanently deprive because the owner is left in possession of the original.

**Conspiracy (common law).** An agreement between two or more persons to commit a crime, with the bilateral approach requiring at least two genuinely agreeing minds. *See, e.g., Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640 (1946), for the federal framework and scope of conspiratorial liability.

**Reasoning applied to this case.** The plan was to enter a church study (not a dwelling) on a Sunday afternoon (not night) and copy a manuscript (no taking or permanent deprivation). Because the planned conduct does not constitute common-law burglary or common-law larceny, the agreement was not to commit either crime. Conspiracy requires an agreement to commit a *crime*; the agreement here is to commit no common-law crime. **Outcome confirmed: no conspiracy to commit burglary; no conspiracy to commit larceny; the original credited answer (D — neither) is preserved unchanged.**

### 2. The 17-section student case study

## 1. program_frame

In the CRIMINAL lane, we test inchoate-crimes liability — specifically, whether an *agreement* to do something becomes a *conspiracy* to commit a crime. The decision rule for conspiracy is strict: the agreement must be to commit a *crime* as the law defines that crime. If the planned conduct is not a crime, the conspiracy charge for that target fails.

## 2. student_access_test

The plan has *two* candidate target crimes visually embedded in it: a breaking-and-entering frame and a property-interference frame. A student who skims will spot both shapes and reach for the most "obviously" matching conspiracy. The test is to *run the element list* of each candidate target crime against the planned facts. If either candidate target fails on its own elements, the conspiracy charge for that target fails — and the "Both" choice cannot survive.

## 3. question_data

Lydia and Priscilla agree to photograph (not remove) the senior pastor's sermon manuscript from his locked study at their community church on a Sunday afternoon. Lydia buys the camera; Priscilla opens the door with a plastic card; Lydia finds and photographs the manuscript, leaving the original in the drawer.

## 4. distilled_core_question

Two volunteers agree to enter a church pastor's locked study on a Sunday afternoon and photograph his sermon manuscript without taking it. May either be convicted of conspiracy to commit common-law burglary or conspiracy to commit common-law larceny?

## 5. call_and_prediction

The call is "of which of the following crimes may Lydia properly be convicted." Prediction: the agreement is not to commit common-law burglary (no dwelling; not night) and not to commit common-law larceny (no taking; no asportation; no intent to permanently deprive). Therefore: neither conspiracy fits. The correct answer is C.

## 6. trigger_facts

- **Location:** Pastor's study inside a community church (a non-dwelling, commercial-type space). [Kills the burglary dwelling element.]
- **Timing:** Sunday afternoon, after the morning service (clearly daytime). [Kills the burglary nighttime element.]
- **Conduct toward the manuscript:** Photograph every page, leave the original in the drawer. [Kills the larceny taking/asportation + intent-to-permanently-deprive elements.]
- **Two-person plan:** Lydia buys the camera, Priscilla pays half and opens the door. [Bilateral agreement + overt acts — this part *is* present, so conspiracy is not categorically unavailable; it just has no crime to target.]

## 7. governing_c3_lane

Lane: CRIMINAL > Inchoate Crimes > Conspiracy. We route to the *target-offense* sub-lane: the conspiracy charge lives or dies by whether the *target offense* fits the facts.

## 8. choice_by_choice_walkthrough

- **Choice A (Both conspiracies).** This is the "all-of-the-above" compounding trap. It looks attractive because the plan *visually* has both a breaking-in shape and a property shape. **Cut:** A conjunction requires *both* prongs to be true. Each prong fails on its own facts (burglary: not a dwelling, not night; larceny: not a taking, not an asportation, not a permanent deprivation). Two independent failures do not add up to one success.

- **Choice B (Larceny only).** This is the "larceny-by-photographing" trap. It sells the cognitive move "they took the manuscript." **Cut:** Common-law larceny is the trespassory *taking and carrying away* of personal property with *intent to permanently deprive*. The plan copies the manuscript and leaves the original in the drawer. No taking, no asportation, no permanent deprivation. The "larceny by copying" intuition is a common student myth; modern unauthorized-copying statutes may reach the conduct, but they are a different question.

- **Choice C (Neither).** This is the **correct answer**. It identifies that the agreement is not to commit a common-law crime, so there is no conspiracy for either target. The reasoning is symmetric: each candidate target crime fails its own element list on the planned facts. The "Neither" answer is the only one that is true and responsive.

- **Choice D (Burglary only) [DOMINANT TRAP].** This is the iconic "breaking-and-entering = burglary" trap. It is the most attractive single-misconception because the "breaking and entering" frame is the most recognizable burglary shape in MBE culture. **Cut:** Common-law burglary requires breaking + entering + **dwelling of another** + **at night** + intent to commit a felony therein. The pastor's study is not a dwelling (it is a room inside a church, a non-residential space), and Sunday afternoon is not night. Two of the four core elements fail. *Silver Key move:* when the call is "conspiracy to commit [property crime]," run the full element list of that crime against the planned facts. The most-forgotten elements are the technical ones (dwelling for burglary; nighttime for burglary; asportation and intent to permanently deprive for larceny). *Gold Key:* the dwelling + nighttime elements are the most-forgotten common-law burglary requirements.

## 9. residual_answer

Choice C survives because it is the only true and responsive answer. The agreement is not to commit common-law burglary; the agreement is not to commit common-law larceny; therefore, no conspiracy attaches to either target.

## 11. final_student_script

"To find Lydia guilty of conspiracy to commit burglary, the prosecution must prove she agreed to commit common-law burglary. Common-law burglary requires breaking, entering, a *dwelling*, *nighttime*, and intent to commit a felony inside. The pastor's study is not a dwelling; Sunday afternoon is not night. Two elements fail — no conspiracy to commit burglary. To find her guilty of conspiracy to commit larceny, the prosecution must prove she agreed to commit common-law larceny. Common-law larceny requires trespassory taking, asportation, and intent to permanently deprive. The plan was to *copy* the manuscript and leave it in the drawer. No taking, no asportation, no permanent deprivation — no conspiracy to commit larceny. The 'Both' choice fails because each prong fails independently. The correct answer is C — neither."

## 17. review_truth

Conspiracy requires an agreement to commit a crime as defined by the controlling law; a plan to enter an office in the afternoon and copy documents is neither conspiracy to commit common-law burglary nor conspiracy to commit common-law larceny.

## Gold Key(s)

**Gold Key: GK-CRIM-BURGLARY-COMMONLAW-01 — Common-law burglary elements**

Common-law burglary is the breaking and entering of the *dwelling* of another *at night* with the intent to commit a felony therein. The dwelling + nighttime elements are non-negotiable. An office or commercial space entered during the day is not common-law burglary. Authority: Cornell LII Wex, "Burglary"; CALI Criminal Law treatise, Chapter 5 (Property Crimes); Blackstonian formulation preserved by modern courts describing the historical common-law rule.

**Gold Key: GK-CRIM-LARCENY-COMMONLAW-01 — Common-law larceny elements**

Common-law larceny is the trespassory *taking and carrying away* (caption + asportation) of the personal property of another with the *intent to permanently deprive* the owner. Copying is not taking; the original is left in place. Without taking, asportation, and intent to permanently deprive, the conduct is not common-law larceny. Authority: Cornell LII / standard criminal-law treatises (Blackstone's "wrongful or fraudulent taking and carrying away, by any man, of the personal goods of another"); CALI Criminal Law treatise, Chapter 5 (Property Crimes).

## Silver Key(s)

**Silver Key: SK-CRIM-CONSPIRACY-INDEPENDENT-PRONG-01 — Test each "Both" prong independently**

When a "Both X and Y" choice is on the table, test each prong independently. If *either* prong fails on its own facts, the conjunction collapses. Both prongs must be independently true; two independent failures do not add up to one success.

**Silver Key: SK-CRIM-CONSPIRACY-TARGET-01 — Run the target crime's element list**

When the call is "conspiracy to commit [property crime]," run the full element list of the candidate target crime against the planned facts. The most-forgotten elements are the technical ones: dwelling for burglary, nighttime for burglary, asportation for larceny, intent to permanently deprive for larceny. If any element is missing, the conspiracy charge for that target fails.
