## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

Generally, a landlord is not liable for injuries caused by the condition of the premises. But when the landlord makes a covenant (a contractual promise) to repair, the landlord becomes liable for injuries that result from breaching that covenant. Here, Timothy agreed in the lease to make necessary repairs within a reasonable time. The storm damaged the railing. Hannah notified Timothy. Three weeks passed with no repairs. Hannah was injured as a direct result of the unrepaired condition. Timothy's breach of the covenant to repair makes him liable for Hannah's injuries. It doesn't matter that Timothy didn't cause the storm, and it doesn't matter that Hannah chose to use the porch — the landlord's contractual duty controls.

**Gold Key:** A landlord who covenants to repair is liable for injuries resulting from breach of that covenant, regardless of whether the landlord caused the dangerous condition and regardless of assumption of risk by the tenant.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (originally C) — DOMINANT TRAP:** This says Hannah assumed the risk by choosing to use the porch. That sounds right — she knew the railing was damaged, and she chose to use it anyway. Assumption of risk is a real doctrine. But the landlord's covenant to repair defeats it. When the landlord promises to fix something within a reasonable time and doesn't, the tenant isn't required to avoid the area entirely. The Gold Key makes this clear: the landlord's contractual duty overrides assumption of risk.

**B (originally D):** This says Timothy isn't liable because he didn't cause the storm. That's true — he didn't cause the storm. But the covenant to repair doesn't require the landlord to have caused the damage. The covenant creates an independent duty to fix the problem and liability for injuries from failing to do so. The landlord's liability flows from the broken promise, not from causing the broken railing.

**D (originally B):** This says "Yes, but only because the railing was damaged by an act of nature." The "but only" is wrong. The act of nature is irrelevant to the analysis. Timothy's liability comes from his covenant to repair, not from the cause of the damage. A storm, a vandal, or normal wear and tear — it doesn't matter. The covenant covers all necessary repairs.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Governing rule:** A landlord is generally not liable for injuries caused by the condition of leased premises. However, an exception exists when the landlord has covenanted (contractually agreed) to make repairs. In that case, the landlord is liable for injuries resulting from the landlord's breach of the repair covenant. *See* Restatement (Second) of Property: Landlord and Tenant § 17.3; *Kine v. Zuckerman*, 49 Cal. App. 3d 201 (1975); *Blyth v. Birmingham Waterworks Co.*, 11 Ex. 781 (1856) (general principle that contractual duty creates liability for breach).

Application: Timothy's lease expressly required him to make necessary repairs within a reasonable time. The railing was damaged. Hannah notified him. Three weeks passed — well beyond a reasonable time. Hannah was injured because the railing was unrepaired. Timothy is liable under the covenant to repair.

## Wrong-Answer Recovery Paths

**If a student picked A (assumption of risk — dominant trap):** The tenant DID know about the danger. But the landlord promised to fix it. When the landlord breaks that promise, the tenant isn't required to avoid the area entirely. The covenant overrides assumption of risk.

**If a student picked B (no causation):** The landlord's liability doesn't come from causing the damage. It comes from the promise to fix it. The covenant creates an independent duty.

**If a student picked D (act of nature):** The cause of damage doesn't matter. The covenant covers all necessary repairs — storm, vandalism, wear and tear. The liability comes from the broken promise.
