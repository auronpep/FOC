### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Stephen's claim is weak. The choice is **D**. The rim of a steel campfire ring getting hot during use is exactly the kind of danger that an ordinary user of an ordinary product already understands and contemplates. **Gold Key:** a manufacturer has no duty to warn of dangers that are open, obvious, and commonly understood by ordinary users. The doctrine operates as a non-duty rule, not as an affirmative defense: the duty to warn never arises for risks a reasonable user can plainly perceive. **Silver Key:** run the "open and obvious" check on the specific risk the plaintiff claims was undisclosed; the specific risk here ("the rim can burn skin") is the very risk any user of a hot campfire ring would plainly perceive.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Yes, because the user suffered physical injury.**

This distractor reframes the failure-to-warn analysis as if injury alone is enough. The breaker is doctrinal: physical injury does not create a warning defect. The duty to warn must be grounded in a non-obvious product risk; the user's injury is the consequence of the risk, not the trigger for the duty. Cut on a missing-element test — the choice supplies the consequence (injury) but skips the antecedent duty question.

**B. No, because fire rings are never products for products-liability purposes.**

This is a flat misstatement of products-liability doctrine. A campfire ring is a manufactured item sold in the commercial chain; it is a "product" for § 402A and Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability purposes. Cut on a misstated-rule test — the choice invents a categorical exclusion that the doctrine does not contain.

**C. Yes, because every dangerous product needs a warning for every possible injury.**

This is the dominant trap. The "every dangerous product needs a warning" claim is a tiered absolute: it asserts an always/only rule that has a doctrinal exception. The breaker is the **Gold Key** — the obvious-danger non-duty rule. A manufacturer has no duty to warn of risks that are open, obvious, and commonly understood; the rim getting hot during a campfire burn is exactly such a risk. The trap is engineered to look like the safe, all-purpose default ("of course you warn"), but the doctrine carves out the obvious risks.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18179, a Torts (Products Liability) item involving an ordinary product (a kitchen knife), an obvious cutting risk, and a failure-to-warn claim. The official key is C: the cutting risk of an ordinary sharp knife is obvious.

The black-letter rule sits at the intersection of strict products liability and the duty to warn. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 402A imposes strict liability for "defective conditions" that make a product "dangerous to an extent beyond that which would be contemplated by the ordinary consumer." Comment j further provides that a seller is "required to give such warning as is feasible" of "dangers which are not in the first instance obvious." Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability § 2, cmt. f, makes the same point: a product is not defective for failure to warn of risks that are "open and obvious" or generally known. The "obvious danger" doctrine is therefore a non-duty rule, not an affirmative defense: the duty to warn never arises for risks that a reasonable ordinary user can plainly perceive and understand.

The transformation preserves the identical legal substance. The steel campfire ring is the analog of the kitchen knife: an ordinary product whose ordinary-use risk is visible and commonly understood. The "rim can burn skin" claim is the analog of the "blade can cut skin" claim. The dominant misconception (tiered absolute) and the structural distractors (injury-claim misframing, flat misstatement) are inherited with the same legal character. The transformed outcome is identical to the original: Stephen's failure-to-warn claim is weak because the burning risk of an ordinary steel campfire ring rim is obvious.
