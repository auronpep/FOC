# CQ15063 — Recording Act Stewardship (Christian Variation)

## 1. Final Question

Stephen, a faithful steward, conveyed Blackacre to Timothy by warranty deed. Timothy recorded the deed four days later. After the conveyance but before Timothy recorded, a creditor properly filed a judgment against Stephen. The jurisdiction has two pertinent statutes: (1) any judgment properly filed shall, for 10 years from filing, be a lien on the real property then owned or subsequently acquired by any person against whom the judgment is rendered; and (2) no conveyance or mortgage of real property shall be good against subsequent purchasers for value and without notice unless the same be recorded according to law. The recording act has no grace period. The creditor joined both Stephen and Timothy in an appropriate action to foreclose the judgment lien against Blackacre. If the creditor is unsuccessful, it will be because:

**A:** the creditor is not a purchaser for value.

**B:** four days is not an unreasonable delay in recording a deed.

**C:** any deed is superior to a judgment lien.

**D:** Stephen's warranty of title to Timothy defeats the creditor's claim.

## 2. Distilled Core Question

When a judgment creditor files a judgment after a conveyance but before the grantee records, does the judgment lien attach to the property if the creditor is not a purchaser for value under a notice statute?

## 7. Right-Answer Explanation

**Gold Key:** A judgment lien attaches only to real property that the judgment debtor actually owns at the time the judgment is filed. Once Stephen conveyed Blackacre to Timothy, Stephen no longer owned it, so the judgment lien could not attach—unless the creditor qualified for recording-act protection.

Under a notice statute, a subsequent interest prevails over an unrecorded prior conveyance only if the subsequent interest holder is a **purchaser for value without notice**. A judgment creditor is not a purchaser for value because the creditor gave no valuable consideration in exchange for the judgment lien; the lien arises by operation of law from the court's filing. Because the creditor is not a purchaser for value, the recording statute does not protect the creditor, and the buyer's unrecorded deed prevails. The owner no longer holds an interest in Blackacre, so there is no property for the lien to attach to.

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**C (new B) — Four days is not an unreasonable delay:** This choice is factually true but legally irrelevant. The recording statute has no grace period, and the buyer's delay does not rescue the creditor. Even if the buyer waited months to record, the creditor still would not be a purchaser for value. The delay neither creates nor destroys the creditor's rights under the notice statute.

**D (new D) — Stephen's warranty of title defeats the creditor's claim:** This is the dominant wrong-answer trap (22%). A warranty of title creates a contractual obligation between grantor and grantee; it does not affect the priority of liens against the property. The warranty might give Timothy a damages claim against Stephen if title fails, but it does not prevent the judgment lien from attaching if the creditor qualified for protection. Students pick this because it sounds like the grantor "should" protect the grantee, but warranty and lien priority are separate doctrines.

**B (new C) — Any deed is superior to a judgment lien:** This is a flat misstatement of law. A deed is not automatically superior to a judgment lien. If the creditor were a purchaser for value (e.g., a mortgagee who lent money in reliance on the record title), the unrecorded deed would lose under the notice statute. The superiority depends on the recording act's requirements, not on a hierarchy that always favors deeds.

## 9. Black-Letter Verification (Lawyer Confirmation)

**Tested rule:** Under a notice recording statute, an unrecorded conveyance prevails against a subsequent judgment lien because a judgment creditor is not a "purchaser for value." The judgment lien statute creates a lien only on property "then owned" by the judgment debtor. Once the debtor conveys, the debtor owns nothing, so the lien cannot attach unless the recording act protects the creditor.

**Authority:**
- Recording act language: "no conveyance … shall be good against subsequent purchasers for value and without notice unless … recorded" (classic notice statute).
- Judgment lien statute: lien attaches to "real property then owned … by any person against whom the judgment is rendered."
- **Rule:** A judgment creditor is not a purchaser for value. See, e.g., *Restatement (First) of Property* § 53 (purchaser requires valuable consideration); state recording act interpretations uniformly exclude judgment creditors from "purchaser" status.
- **Outcome:** The buyer prevails; the judgment lien does not attach because (1) Stephen owned no property at filing, and (2) the creditor is not protected by the recording statute.

**Doctrinal currency:** No superseding authority affects this rule. The analysis remains current.

**Verdict:** PASS — authority floor met; no contradiction with official key.
