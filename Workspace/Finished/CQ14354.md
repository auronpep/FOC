### 1. Final Question

After a series of hurricanes, Congress enacts the Safe Wells Prize Act. The Act sets aside $8.4 million for a nationwide contest inviting United States residents to submit low-cost designs for emergency water-purification kits and delivery plans. Its stated purpose is to collect practical designs that could help the United States deliver safe drinking water after disasters.

The Act directs a panel appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate to score entries for ingenuity, suitability to disaster relief, and operational feasibility. It authorizes one $800,000 grand prize, 80 $75,000 regional prizes, and 160 $10,000 honorable-mention prizes. Federal employees may not compete, but all other United States residents may. The Act also states that any federal taxpayer may sue to challenge its constitutionality. Ruth, a federal taxpayer, sues under that provision.

### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is the keyed merits answer. Congress is spending federal money for a public purpose: practical disaster-water ideas. The Act does not attempt to regulate private conduct under a free-floating general-welfare power. It appropriates money and directs how a prize program will be run. It also gives the prize panel workable standards: ingenuity, suitability to disaster relief, and operational feasibility.

Gold Key: Congress may spend for the general welfare even when the program is not tied to another enumerated regulatory power, so long as the spending serves a public purpose and violates no independent constitutional limit.

Gold Key: A delegation survives when Congress supplies an intelligible principle. Broad scoring words can be enough when they tell the decision-makers what to evaluate.

Validation flag: the stem's taxpayer plaintiff creates a separate standing issue. Bare federal taxpayer status ordinarily does not support Article III standing to challenge federal spending, outside narrow exceptions. Because no answer choice captures that threshold defect, the bank validation is `NEEDS_HUMAN`.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because it sees real discretion in the prize panel. The breaker is the statutory criteria. The panel is not told to give away money however it wants; it must judge entries by ingenuity, suitability to disaster relief, and operational feasibility. Gold Key: the intelligible-principle standard tolerates broad criteria when Congress gives a usable channel for discretion.

C is attractive because the Necessary and Proper Clause sounds like the catchall test for federal means. The breaker is the public-purpose fit. Disaster drinking-water planning is not a speculative private hobby; it is a national public-welfare objective connected to Congress's spending power.

D is attractive because the stem involves policy choices and a taxpayer plaintiff. The breaker is the specific doctrine named. A constitutional challenge to a spending statute has judicially manageable legal standards; it is not a political question merely because the program concerns public policy. Silver Key: do not let a courthouse-access instinct turn into the wrong justiciability label.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

Merits rule preserved. Article I, Section 8, Clause 1 gives Congress power to tax and spend to pay debts and provide for the common defense and general welfare. The Supreme Court's Spending Clause cases recognize that spending for the general welfare is a separate spending power, not limited to the later enumerated regulatory powers, though it is limited to public welfare purposes and independent constitutional constraints. United States v. Butler, 297 U.S. 1 (1936), is the classic authority for that framing. The statute here funds a public-purpose contest for emergency water delivery after disasters, so the merits fit the spending power.

Delegation rule preserved. Congress may delegate implementation authority if it gives an intelligible principle to guide the delegee. J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394 (1928), supplies the standard. The Act's scoring criteria give a workable principle for awarding prizes.

Political-question rule preserved. The political-question doctrine applies when an issue is textually committed to another branch or lacks judicially manageable standards. A court can apply Spending Clause and nondelegation standards here, so the political-question answer is not the merits winner.

Bank-validation issue. Current taxpayer-standing doctrine creates a serious threshold problem. Constitution Annotated summarizes that a litigant generally may not rely solely on federal taxpayer status to challenge government spending decisions. Flast v. Cohen, 392 U.S. 83 (1968), creates only a narrow taxpayer-standing path for specific constitutional limits on taxing and spending, classically Establishment Clause claims. This source stem says Congress authorized any taxpayer to sue, but Congress cannot eliminate Article III case-or-controversy limits by label alone. Because the answer array does not include a standing dismissal, the clean bank verdict is `NEEDS_HUMAN`, not `PASS`.
