### 2. Distilled Core Question

In an attempted-murder question, which fact leads to acquittal: failed means that leave intent to kill intact, or a belief that the victim is already dead and therefore cannot be killed?

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.** Attempted murder requires a specific intent to kill. This choice is the only one that destroys that intent. If Ruth believed Naomi had already died that morning, Ruth could not be acting with the conscious objective of killing Naomi when she handled the loaf afterward. That belief removes the mens rea required for attempted murder, so acquittal is most likely.

**Gold Key:** Attempt is a specific-intent crime. Failed means do not help a defendant if the intent to kill is still present, but a belief that the victim is already dead eliminates the intent to kill.

**Silver Key:** In an attempt-acquittal array, sort the choices into two piles: failed method with intent still intact, and no intent to kill at all. The acquittal answer comes from the second pile.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A is incorrect.** This answer sells the instinct that a harmless powder means no crime because the plan could never work. That instinct confuses impossibility with acquittal. Ruth still believed she was using lethal arsenic and still took a substantial step toward killing Naomi. The means failed, but the murderous intent remained. **Gold Key:** factual impossibility is not a defense to attempt.

**B is incorrect.** This is the classic harmless-substance trap. A student sees baking soda and wants to say the law backs away because no one could die from it. But attempt liability focuses on the defendant's intent and conduct as she believed the facts to be. Ruth believed she was using poison and acted on that belief. The pharmacy's mistake does not create an acquittal.

**D is incorrect.** This is the most attractive wrong answer because it feels impossible in the strongest way: Naomi was already dead. But Ruth believed Naomi was alive and used real poison to kill her. That preserves the specific intent to kill and preserves attempt liability even though completion was factually impossible. **Silver Key:** when one answer erases intent and another only shows a failed result, the failed-result choice is still wrong on an acquittal call.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited answer remains correct. The tested rule is unchanged: attempt requires intent to commit the target offense, and attempted murder requires a specific intent to kill. The official outcome also remains unchanged: acquittal is most likely only when the defendant's own belief prevents proof of an intent to kill.

Authority floor:

- Model Penal Code section 5.01 treats attempt liability by looking to the circumstances as the actor believes them to be and rejects impossibility when the actor still has the criminal purpose.
- *Braxton v. United States*, 500 U.S. 344, 351 n.* (1991), states that although murder may be committed without an intent to kill, attempted murder requires a specific intent to kill.
- *People v. Dlugash*, 41 N.Y.2d 725 (1977), explains that factual impossibility is no defense where the actor believes the victim is alive and acts to kill.

From those authorities, the acquittal logic for choice C is a direct inference: if Ruth believed Naomi was already dead, Ruth was not acting with the purpose of killing a living victim. By contrast, choices A, B, and D all leave the intent to kill intact and only make success impossible. That is why C, and only C, supports acquittal.
