Esther, a Christian cookie-cart owner, arranged Scripture-verse placards on her rolling display cart while the cart stood in a downtown curb lane, in violation of a city ordinance that prohibits setting up vendor carts in curb lanes between 4 p.m. and 6 p.m. The ordinance was enacted only to keep evening automobile traffic moving. ### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. Daniel offered only one negligence fact: Esther admitted violating the curb-lane vendor-cart ordinance. Gold Key: an ordinance violation supports negligence per se only when the ordinance was designed to protect this plaintiff against this type of accident or harm. This ordinance was enacted only to keep automobile traffic moving, not to protect pedestrians from a placard blown by sudden wind. Silver Key: before giving legal force to a rule violation, ask what danger the rule was built to prevent. Because the protected-risk link is missing and Daniel offered no other evidence that Esther was negligent, the judge should grant Esther's directed-verdict motion.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

The breaker is that the violation does not match the ordinance's protected risk: traffic delay is not the same danger as a windblown placard. D is attractive because a placard flying into a crosswalk feels like something careless happened. The breaker is the evidence limit in the stem. Daniel's only negligence evidence was the ordinance violation, and the sudden unexpected gust does not itself supply a reasonable negligence inference.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested black-letter rule is unchanged: negligence per se requires more than proof that the defendant violated a statute or ordinance. The law must be designed to protect a class that includes the plaintiff and to protect against the kind of accident or harm that occurred. Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical and Emotional Harm § 14 states the modern statutory-purpose rule; Restatement (Second) of Torts § 286 states the same protected-class/protected-risk idea. Public summaries confirm that negligence per se turns on whether the law was designed to protect against the type of accident or harm and whether the plaintiff is within the protected class (https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/negligence_per_se; https://saidtorts.lawbooks.cali.org/chapter/negligence-per-se-and-res-ipsa-loquitur/). The American Law Institute publication page confirms the Restatement (Third) Torts work covering negligence-causing-physical-harm provisions (https://www.ali.org/publications/restatement-law-third/torts-liability-physical-and-emotional-harm).

The directed-verdict/JMOL layer is also consistent with the credited answer. Under the modern federal formulation, a court may grant judgment as a matter of law when a party has been fully heard and no reasonable jury would have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis to find for that party on the issue. Fed. R. Civ. P. 50(a)(1)-(2), available at https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_50. Here, Daniel was fully heard on negligence, but his only negligence evidence was an ordinance violation that does not fit the protected purpose of the ordinance. The transform preserves the source rule, the legal issue, the defendant-directed-verdict outcome, the three original distractor mechanics, and the dominance of the ordinance-violation trap.
