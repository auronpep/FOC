# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question

Lydia is indicted for embezzling silver from the trading house where she kept the accounts. While released on bail awaiting trial, she confides to Naomi — an acquaintance who runs a neighboring market stall — exactly where she buried the skimmed coins. At the time of that conversation, Naomi had never been contacted by the prosecutor's office, had received no instructions from anyone in law enforcement, and had been promised nothing. Weeks later, hoping the prosecutor will go easy on her own unrelated pending charge, Naomi volunteers to prosecutors what Lydia told her. Does admitting Lydia's statement violate the Sixth Amendment?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam register)

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel had attached — Lydia was indicted, so adversarial proceedings had begun. But attachment alone does not suppress anything. The Massiah rule reaches only statements **deliberately elicited by a government agent** after the right attaches. Naomi was a private acquaintance acting on her own. No one in law enforcement had contacted her, instructed her, or promised her anything when Lydia spoke. A private person who later decides to trade information for her own advantage was not a government agent at the time of the statement.

**Gold Key:** A post-charge statement is suppressible under the Sixth Amendment only if the listener was a government agent who deliberately elicited it — a private person acting on his own, even one who later trades the information for leniency, is not an agent.

**Silver Key:** When two answers split on the listener's *motive* versus the *government's conduct*, suppression turns on government action, not the informant's private hope — lock the agency answer.

C is correct: the deciding element is agency, and Naomi had none.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (Yes, because Naomi hoped to receive a benefit).** Naomi's self-interested hope for leniency feels like it should taint the statement — a witness angling for a deal looks like a government plant. But a unilateral hope is the listener's private state of mind; it answers "was Naomi self-interested," not "was Naomi a government agent." Agency requires the government to use, direct, or agree with the listener at the time of elicitation. **Gold Key:** a listener's private hope for a benefit, without prior government direction or agreement, is not agency. Naomi's hope, formed on her own and acted on weeks later, never made her the State's instrument.

**B (No, because the Sixth Amendment does not apply between indictment and trial).** This reaches the right outcome and sounds like a tidy timing rule, which tempts a student who senses the answer is "No." But the timing claim is simply false: the right to counsel attaches *at* the first formal charge and runs continuously through trial — it is not dormant in the interval. B is right by accident and wrong on the law; the real reason is missing agency, not a gap in coverage.

**C — credited.**

**D (Yes, because any statement after indictment is treated as police interrogation).** The word "any" sells a clean, memorable rule, and post-charge informant facts do feel interrogation-like. But "any" is an absolute over a rule riddled with exceptions — spontaneous statements to true private parties are not interrogation at all. The overclaim collapses the moment you test it against a single counterexample: a defendant who blurts a confession to a friend has not been interrogated by the State.

## 9. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule (unchanged from original):** Under the Massiah doctrine, once the Sixth Amendment right to counsel has attached, the government may not deliberately elicit incriminating statements from the accused absent counsel — but the prohibition runs only against **government agents**. A statement to a private person who is not acting under government direction or agreement at the time is not suppressible, even if that person later reports it.

**Authority:**
- *Massiah v. United States*, 377 U.S. 201 (1964) — Sixth Amendment violated where government agents deliberately elicited post-indictment statements without counsel.
- *United States v. Henry*, 447 U.S. 264 (1980) — paid informant placed in the cell and instructed to report, who stimulated conversation, *was* a government agent (violation).
- *Kuhlmann v. Wilson*, 477 U.S. 436 (1986) — the defendant must show the informant took action **beyond merely listening** designed to deliberately elicit; spontaneous statements to a "listening post" are admissible. A fortiori, a person under **no** government instruction at all is not an agent.

**Affirmative reasoning for the credited answer:** The right attached at indictment (attachment satisfied). The dispositive variable is agency. Naomi received no prior contact, instruction, agreement, or promise; she was a wholly private party when Lydia spoke. Under *Massiah/Henry/Kuhlmann*, no state actor deliberately elicited the statement, so admission does not violate the Sixth Amendment. Credited outcome ("No," for absence of agency) is **unchanged** from the source item.

**Doctrinal currency:** Not on the hotlist; the Massiah/Henry/Kuhlmann agency framework is stable controlling law. **Key agreement:** research confirms the official key (original A → new C). No disagreement → PASS, not NEEDS_HUMAN.
