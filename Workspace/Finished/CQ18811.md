### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Rule 23(b)(2) is the most natural fit. The university has allegedly **acted or refused to act on grounds generally applicable to the class** (a uniform failure to repair a single elevator serving the entire dormitory, affecting every wheelchair-using resident in the same way). The remedy the class seeks is a **single injunction** of a kind that, if granted, would provide **classwide relief**: either fix the elevator or provide accessible alternative housing. The students do not ask for individualized damages, so the predominance-and-superiority inquiry that drives Rule 23(b)(3) is not the right lens. Rule 23(b)(1) (mandatory classes) does not fit because there is no risk of inconsistent adjudications or impairment of absent members' interests from separate suits. The class is the textbook Rule 23(b)(2) injunctive class.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. No class can be certified, because each student uses the elevator at different times of day.**

This is the broad-absolute "no class can be certified" trap. The student who picks A treats individualized use patterns as a class-certification killer, when in fact Rule 23(a)'s commonality requirement asks only whether there are common *questions* of law or fact — not whether all class members behave identically. The student also forgets that Rule 23(b)(2) does not require predominance at all; the question is whether a uniform injunction can resolve the class's claims. The breaker: a single uniform policy (broken elevator, no repair) that affects every class member in the same way is precisely the kind of "conduct generally applicable to the class" that Rule 23(b)(2) was written for. Different times of use do not defeat class certification when the conduct and the relief are classwide.

**B. Rule 23(b)(1) only, because a judgment about one student could influence the university's conduct toward the others.**

This is the 23(b)(1)-only misframe. The student who picks B treats 23(b)(1) as the catch-all for "any class where individual actions could affect others." The breaker: Rule 23(b)(1) covers **mandatory** classes — situations where separate actions would risk (i) inconsistent adjudications that would impose incompatible standards on the defendant, or (ii) adjudications that as a practical matter would impair absent members' ability to protect their interests (e.g., a limited fund). The students' dormitory-access claim fits neither: the university can repair the elevator for one student without "impairing" anyone else's interest, and there is no inconsistent-adjudication risk. 23(b)(1) is not the right category; 23(b)(2) is. The choice is **NOT_RESPONSIVE** to the right rule (it points to the wrong subsection).

**D. Rule 23(b)(3), because every class action involving many people must satisfy predominance and superiority.**

This is the **dominant trap**. The cognitive move it sells is the "23(b)(3) is the default class action" shortcut — a student who half-remembers that class actions typically involve predominance and superiority picks D and walks into the broad-absolute overclaim. The breaker: Rule 23(b)(3) is **not** the only class category. Rule 23(b)(2) is a separate, narrower category that does **not** require predominance or superiority; it requires only that the defendant has acted on grounds generally applicable to the class and that final injunctive or declaratory relief is appropriate for the class as a whole. A single injunction against a single uniform policy is the textbook Rule 23(b)(2) case, and the choice's "every class action" framing is a `tiered_absolute` overclaim that loses to that counterexample. **Silver Key:** when a class action call has a single uniform defendant-conduct pattern and a single injunctive remedy, ask "is this a 23(b)(2) case?" first, before defaulting to 23(b)(3).

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18811, a Civil Procedure item on the Rule 23(b) categories. The official key is A: Rule 23(b)(2), because the requested injunction would provide classwide relief from conduct generally applicable to the class.

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23(b) sets out three categories of class actions. Rule 23(b)(1) covers mandatory classes where separate actions would risk inconsistent adjudications or impairment of absent members' interests. Rule 23(b)(2) covers classes where "the party opposing the class has acted or refused to act on grounds that apply generally to the class, making final injunctive or declaratory relief appropriate with respect to the class as a whole." Rule 23(b)(3) covers classes where common questions predominate and a class action is superior to other available methods.

The Supreme Court confirmed the structure of Rule 23(b)(2) in Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338 (2011), holding that a (b)(2) class is "entirely cohesive" when "a single injunction can provide relief to each member of the class." Although Dukes involved a Title VII pattern-or-practice claim, its articulation of when injunctive relief is "appropriate with respect to the class as a whole" is the standard Rule 23(b)(2) test. The Rule 23(b)(2) class does not require predominance, superiority, or opt-out; it is designed for civil-rights-style injunctive suits against a uniform policy.

The transformed fact pattern preserves the legal substance exactly: a single defendant (a public state university), a single uniform policy or course of conduct (failure to repair a dormitory elevator over three months), a class of more than forty similarly situated plaintiffs (wheelchair-using dormitory residents), and a single injunctive remedy (repair the elevator or provide accessible alternative housing). The students do not seek individualized damages. The only changes are surface story elements: the disability context (campus shuttle accessibility → dormitory elevator accessibility), the class size descriptor ("students with mobility impairments" → "more than forty similarly situated dormitory residents who use wheelchairs"), the class representatives (unnamed → Hannah, Stephen, Lydia, all popular Christian first-names), and the setting (a generic public university → a public state university with dormitories). The legal issue, the credited outcome, the distractor mechanics, and the dominant trap are all unchanged.

Choice C is correct because the university's alleged refusal to repair the elevator is precisely the kind of "refus[al] to act on grounds that apply generally to the class" that Rule 23(b)(2) was written for. A single injunction requiring repair or accessible alternative housing is the kind of "final injunctive or declaratory relief appropriate . . . with respect to the class as a whole" that the rule contemplates. Wal-Mart v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338 (2011), confirms the cohesion test is satisfied.
