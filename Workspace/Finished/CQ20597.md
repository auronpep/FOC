At a community Advent craft swap, **Stephen** sees a hand-carved olivewood nativity figurine on a table beside small giveaway cards printed with Bible verses. ## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.** **Silver Key:** read the array as two switches: Stephen’s mistake is about a fact; Mary’s mistake is about the law. The credited answer must split them.

**Gold Key:** for a specific intent crime, an honest mistake of fact can negate the required specific intent even if the mistake is unreasonable. Stephen honestly thought the figurine was part of the giveaway, so he did not intend to take property he knew belonged to another. Mary’s mistake is different. She knew the property belonged to someone at the event, but misunderstood whether the law allowed her to keep it. That is a mistake of law. Mistake of law is generally no defense.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because it treats sincerity as the only thing that matters. The breaker is the Gold Key distinction: a genuine factual mistake can negate specific intent, but a genuine legal misunderstanding generally does not.

**B.** This choice is attractive because “reasonable mistake” sounds like a familiar criminal-law standard. The breaker is the Gold Key for specific intent: Stephen’s fact mistake does not need to be reasonable if it honestly negates the required intent; the Silver Key would also prevent choosing a “both guilty” answer when the two scenarios are split.

**D.** This choice is attractive because it punishes Stephen’s careless-looking assumption and tries to reward Mary’s stated legal belief. The breaker is direction: it reverses both sides of the fact/law split.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged from the supplied row: larceny is a specific intent crime, an honest mistake of fact may negate the required specific intent, and mistake of law is generally no defense. The Department of Justice’s larceny formulation identifies intent to deprive the owner as an element, and Cornell’s criminal-law summary states that a mistake of fact may negate mens rea, including that even an unreasonable mistake may matter for a specific-intent offense. ([Department of Justice][1])

The Stephen facts track the intent problem in *Morissette v. United States*: a defendant’s belief that property was abandoned can matter because it bears on whether he had the required wrongful intent. The Mary facts track the ordinary mistake-of-law rule; *Cheek* states the general rule that ignorance of the law is no defense, while recognizing a narrow tax-specific exception not present here. ([Justia Law][2])
