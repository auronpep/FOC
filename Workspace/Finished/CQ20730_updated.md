## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**D is correct.** The strongest liability argument is that Peter intended the bodily contact itself: he meant to slam his shoulder into Lydia. **Gold Key:** Battery does not require intent to cause injury, appreciation of risk, or bad motive; the key mental-state target is harmful/offensive contact or apprehension of that contact. **Silver Key:** When the options offer different mental states, map each one to the tort named in the call before choosing. ## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells the negligence-style thought: a seven-year-old who understands risk should be answerable for risky conduct. The breaker is the call: battery is not asking whether Peter appreciated risk; **Gold Key:** the required mental-state target is contact, not riskiness.

**B.** This choice is attractive because it sounds more serious: Peter may have understood that a fall into a table could cause injury. The breaker is that injury appreciation is not the battery element; the strongest battery argument is that Peter meant to make the contact.

**C.** This choice uses moral common sense: Peter was angry about the ribbon decision, and the anger was not justified. The breaker is that motive is not the liability hook; **Silver Key:** do not choose the answer that explains why the child was mad when another answer identifies the intentional contact.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row: the original question tests battery liability for a child and credits the answer identifying the child’s intent to make bodily contact, not intent to injure, risk appreciation, or justified anger. The uploaded row states QID 20730, Torts, Intentional Torts, official key A, and explains that the child’s intent to make harmful or offensive bodily contact is sufficient while injury intent, risk appreciation, and anger are not controlling. 

Lawyer confirmation: Restatement (Second) of Torts § 13, as quoted in *Garratt v. Dailey*, frames battery around an act intended to bring about harmful or offensive contact or apprehension of such contact, with harmful contact resulting. ([Justia][1]) *Garratt* also confirms that substantial certainty of contact can satisfy intent, that lack of intent to injure does not defeat battery if the required contact intent is present, and that a child’s age matters to what the child knew, not as a categorical immunity. ([Justia][1]) On remand, the Washington court entered judgment after finding the five-year-old knew with substantial certainty that the plaintiff would attempt to sit where the chair had been.
