## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Day Register)

The credited answer is **A: Yes, for murder, because intent to inflict grievous bodily harm is a recognized form of malice aforethought.**

Peter's stated intent — "I just wanted to hurt him" — combined with his actions (striking a fallen man repeatedly with a heavy cast-iron candlestick, continuing after the victim was down) demonstrates intent to inflict grievous bodily harm. At common law, malice aforethought includes four states of mind: (1) intent to kill, (2) intent to inflict grievous bodily harm, (3) depraved-heart recklessness, and (4) intent to commit a felony (felony murder). Peter's conduct satisfies category (2).

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### B — Yes, for first-degree murder (repeated striking shows premeditation)

**What makes it attractive:** The student sees a prolonged, repeated attack — multiple strikes even after the victim falls — and infers that the defendant must have thought about killing during the beating. The word "premeditation" sounds lawyerly and fits the brutality.

**The breaker:** Premeditation at common law requires a prior decision to kill formed through cool reflection — deliberation about the *killing itself*, not just about hurting. Continued assault during a fight does not become premeditated murder just because the attacker keeps swinging. There is zero evidence Peter thought about killing Stephen before or during the attack. The proper charge is murder (second-degree) under the grievous bodily harm theory. **Silver Key:** The 3-vs-1 array shows three "Yes" answers — B is asking you to pick the *wrong degree* of murder, not the wrong crime. `SK-CRIM-THREE-V-ONE-01`

### C — Yes, for involuntary manslaughter (Peter did not intend to kill)

**What makes it attractive:** The student hears "didn't want to kill him" and defaults to the catch-all unintended-killing charge. It feels intuitively proportional — a man who didn't mean to kill shouldn't be called a murderer.

**The breaker:** Involuntary manslaughter covers unintended killings caused by criminal negligence or during an unlawful act (misdemeanor-manslaughter). It does **not** apply when the defendant intentionally inflicts grievous bodily harm that results in death. Intent to cause serious injury is a higher mens rea — it elevates the homicide to murder, not manslaughter. The label "manslaughter" understates Peter's culpability for choosing to inflict severe harm.

### D — No, because Peter lacked intent to kill — **Dominant Trap**

**What makes it attractive:** This is the most natural, intuitive answer. Most people (and many first-year law students) believe "murder = intended to kill." The stem feeds this: Peter says "I didn't want to kill him," and the facts support that — no prior relationship, no planning, just a burst of anger. The student stops at the surface and picks the common-sense answer.

**The breaker:** The law does not require intent to kill for murder. Malice aforethought — the mental element for murder — can be satisfied by intent to inflict grievous bodily harm, even without any intent to kill. Peter's statement "I just wanted to hurt him" and his use of a heavy candlestick on a fallen victim demonstrate precisely the kind of serious-injury intent that supports murder. **Gold Key:** Intent to inflict grievous bodily harm = malice aforethought = murder. `GK-CRIM-GBH-MALICE-01`

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Authority:** At common law, murder is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought. Malice aforethought includes four distinct mental states: (1) intent to kill; (2) intent to inflict grievous bodily harm; (3) depraved-heart recklessness (extreme indifference to human life); and (4) intent to commit a felony (felony murder). See, e.g., Louisiana Revised Statutes § 14:30.1 (second-degree murder includes "specific intent to kill or to inflict great bodily harm"); Model Penal Code § 210.2 (murder includes "causing death intentionally or knowingly" or "causing death recklessly under circumstances manifesting extreme indifference to human life," with such recklessness presumed when causing serious bodily injury). The common-law grievous-bodily-harm form of malice is widely recognized: "intent to cause serious bodily injury that results in death is murder."

**Application:** Peter struck Stephen repeatedly with a heavy cast-iron candlestick, continued after Stephen fell to the ground, and stated he wanted to "hurt him." These facts demonstrate intent to inflict grievous bodily harm. Stephen died from the injuries. The killing is therefore murder (second-degree at common law, absent premeditation).

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### Choice A — Yes, for murder, because intent to inflict grievous bodily harm is a recognized form of malice aforethought. **(CORRECT)**

**Student-accessible C3 signal:** Peter said "hurt," not "kill." The law says serious-injury intent is enough for murder. The Gold Key makes this explicit.

**What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true and responsive version. It correctly answers the call (Yes, murder) with the correct legal basis (GBH intent = malice).

**Lawyer confirmation:** At common law, murder is the unlawful killing with malice aforethought. Malice includes (1) intent to kill, (2) intent to inflict grievous bodily harm, (3) depraved-heart recklessness, and (4) felony-murder intent. Peter's conduct and statement satisfy (2). See Louisiana RS 14:30.1; Model Penal Code § 210.2.

### Choice B — Yes, for first-degree murder, because the repeated striking shows premeditation.

**Student-accessible C3 signal:** The stem says nothing about Peter thinking about killing before he hit. Premeditation = thought about killing beforehand. Repeated hitting during a fight ≠ premeditation. The overclaim is visible from the text.

**Student label:** "Yes — but the wrong degree. Multiple hits = continued anger, not prior planning to kill."

**What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would need evidence of cool reflection — e.g., "Peter planned to confront Stephen with the candlestick and waited for him behind the donation table." The stem lacks this.

**Lawyer confirmation:** Premeditation requires a prior formed design to kill — a decision reached through cool reflection, not formed during the heat of the ongoing attack. See, e.g., State v. Brown, 836 S.E.2d 337 (N.C. 2019) (multiple blows in continuous assault insufficient for premeditation). The proper charge is second-degree murder via grievous bodily harm theory.

---

### Choice C — Yes, for involuntary manslaughter, because Peter did not intend to kill.

**Student-accessible C3 signal:** The call asks "Can Peter be convicted of **murder**?" Involuntary manslaughter is not murder — it's a different crime entirely. Non-responsive is the cleanest CUT.

**Student label:** "Yes — but the wrong charge. 'Murder?' is the question; 'manslaughter' doesn't answer it."

**What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would say "No, but he could be convicted of involuntary manslaughter." But the call asks about murder only.

**Lawyer confirmation:** Involuntary manslaughter applies to unintended killings caused by criminal negligence or during an unlawful act (misdemeanor-manslaughter). When the defendant intentionally inflicts grievous bodily harm that results in death, the mens rea is too high for manslaughter — it is murder. See, e.g., People v. Taylor, 150 Cal. Rptr. 3d 128 (Cal. Ct. App. 2013).

### Choice D — No, because Peter lacked intent to kill, which is required for murder. **(Dominant Trap)**

**Lawyer confirmation:** Malice aforethought does NOT require intent to kill. Intent to inflict grievous bodily harm is sufficient. State v. Harris, 817 N.W.2d 409 (Wis. 2012) (intent to cause great bodily harm that results in death constitutes murder).
