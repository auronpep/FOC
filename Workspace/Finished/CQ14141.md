Lydia Sound Systems, a company that sells portable speakers for Christian conferences and Bible-study events, filed suit in federal district court against Mary, a reporter for *The Berean Review*, a consumer-product testing website. The suit arose after Mary published a review of Lydia Sound Systems’ “DoveSound” speakers.

The court ruled that Lydia Sound Systems was a “public figure” and thus had to prove by clear and convincing evidence that Mary made a false and disparaging statement about the speakers with “actual malice.” “Actual malice” requires knowledge that one is making a false statement or reckless disregard of the truthfulness of one’s statement.

### 5. Full right-answer explanation

**A is correct.** This is a summary-judgment burden question. Mary supported her motion with her own affidavit denying the state of mind required for actual malice. Lydia Sound Systems had the trial burden on actual malice and offered no contrary evidence. On this record, Lydia Sound Systems has not created a genuine dispute on the element the court identified as necessary.

**Gold Key:** On summary judgment, when the nonmovant bears the trial burden on an essential element, the nonmovant must point to record evidence creating a genuine dispute; silence or unsupported allegations do not defeat a properly supported motion.

**Silver Key:** Do not convert “no response” into “bad faith.” On a summary-judgment call, the clean issue is record evidence, not blame.

### 6. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B is incorrect.** The stem says Lydia Sound Systems provided no contrary evidence. It does not say Lydia acted in bad faith. The answer reaches the same “Yes” result but gives a fact-not-in-evidence reason. **Silver Key:** when the call is summary judgment, track the evidentiary burden; do not add blame words the stem did not give.

**C is incorrect.** It invents a categorical affidavit ban. A party’s own affidavit may be used at summary judgment if it satisfies the affidavit/declaration requirements. **Gold Key:** Rule 56 permits affidavits or declarations made on personal knowledge, setting out admissible facts, and showing competence.

**D is incorrect.** It invents a mandatory first-step rule. A court is not required to order a nonmovant to respond before ruling on a properly supported motion. Current Rule 56 allows several responses to a failure to address facts, including considering facts undisputed or granting summary judgment if the materials show entitlement.

### 7. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

Rule 56(a) provides that summary judgment must be granted when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law; Rule 56(c) allows factual positions to be supported by affidavits or declarations, and Rule 56(c)(4) states the requirements for affidavits/declarations. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) Rule 56(e) permits the court, when a party fails to address a fact properly, to consider the fact undisputed or grant summary judgment if the motion and supporting materials show entitlement. ([Legal Information Institute][1])

The Supreme Court’s summary-judgment framework also supports the answer. *Celotex* states that when the nonmoving party bears the trial burden, the nonmovant must go beyond the pleadings and designate specific facts showing a genuine issue for trial. ([Justia Law][2]) *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby* confirms that in public-figure defamation cases, actual malice must be shown with convincing clarity and that the clear-and-convincing standard is considered at summary judgment. ([Legal Information Institute][3]) ([Legal Information Institute][3])

Legal-audit note: the supplied official explanation for D says the FRCP do not give authority to order a response. The safer current formulation is that Rule 56 does not require the court to order a response before ruling. That does not change the official key.
