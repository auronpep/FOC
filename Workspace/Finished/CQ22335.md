## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**B is correct.** Barnabas formed the intent to kill when he pointed the revolver at the deputy's head and pulled the trigger rather than refuse the order. Under the traditional common-law rule tested on the MBE, duress is not a defense to intentional murder. One person's life is not more valuable than another's; the defendant must endure the threatened harm rather than transfer it to an innocent third party by committing murder. 

**Gold Key:** Duress never justifies the intentional killing of an innocent person. The defendant must suffer the threatened harm himself rather than kill an innocent to escape it. (This is the specific limitation that defeats both "No" choices.)

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A (involuntary manslaughter — original B mechanic):** Attractive because the defendant was "forced," so students may think the act was not truly voluntary or that lack of personal motive reduces it to manslaughter. Breaker: Involuntary manslaughter requires an unintentional killing (reckless, negligent, or during an unlawful act without intent to kill or cause serious bodily harm). Here Barnabas was handed a loaded gun, ordered to shoot the deputy in the head at close range, aimed, and fired. The facts show purposeful, intentional killing. This choice mischaracterizes the mens rea that is visible on the face of the stem.

**C (necessity — original D mechanic):** Attractive because it sounds like a "choice of evils" or "I had to save myself" fairness argument, and students often conflate duress with necessity. Breaker: Necessity (choice of evils) traditionally applies when natural forces or circumstances create the pressure, not when a third-party human threat creates the coercion. Even if the labels were swapped, neither duress nor necessity excuses the intentional killing of an innocent third party under the rule that one life is not to be preferred over another. The Gold Key directly defeats this.

**D (duress — original C mechanic, dominant trap):** Attractive because the sympathy pull is powerful ("he would have been killed if he refused"; "anyone would have done the same"; "his life was on the line"). ## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row.

**Governing rule (traditional common law, MBE-tested):** Duress is not available as a defense to the crime of murder. A defendant who intentionally kills an innocent person to avoid being killed himself is guilty of murder. The rationale is that the law does not permit one innocent life to be sacrificed to save another; the threatened person "ought rather to die himself than escape by the murder of an innocent." 4 W. Blackstone, Commentaries *30 (1902); see also Arp v. State, 12 So. 301, 303 (Ala. 1893) (coerced defendant cannot plead compulsion to excuse killing an innocent). Most American jurisdictions follow this common-law prohibition for intentional murder (some states make limited exceptions for felony murder where the defendant did not participate in the killing itself, but that is not this case). The Model Penal Code § 2.09 takes the minority view and would allow duress for murder if a person of reasonable firmness could not have resisted, but MBE questions on this topic test the traditional majority/common-law bar rule.

**Application here:** Barnabas was ordered to shoot the deputy in the head and complied by aiming and firing a loaded revolver at close range. This is purposeful, intentional killing (express malice). The fact that he acted under an immediate threat to his own life supplies the classic duress facts, but the law withholds the defense precisely because the act was the intentional murder of an innocent. Necessity is a distinct doctrine (natural forces/choice of evils) and does not apply to human coercion or to intentional homicide on these facts. Involuntary manslaughter is unavailable because the killing was not unintentional.

The source row's explanation ("Duress is not a defense to murder because one person's life is not more important than another person's life. Here, the hostage clearly intended to kill... C and D are incorrect because they would not excuse taking the life of another") is an accurate statement of the tested rule and outcome. Research confirms no doctrinal drift that would change the credited answer for MBE purposes.

**Lawyer confirmation (authority layer only):** The above rests on Blackstone, the traditional common-law rule, and standard MBE bar-review framing. No recent U.S. Supreme Court decision or hotlist item alters the traditional rule tested here. If a specific jurisdiction's statute or modern case were at issue, that would be outside the four corners of this MBE-style item.
