### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Evidence of prior transactions is relevant in contract interpretation only when it tends to show what the parties intended when they formed their agreement. Prior dealings between *the same parties* are probative of their shared understanding. But transactions with third parties — even similar ones — do not tend to show what *this* defendant intended or expected unless the defendant was aware of those transactions. Here, Paul shipped Bibles in cardboard boxes to 15 other churches. Timothy was not a party to those transactions and there is no showing Timothy knew about them. The 15 transactions therefore have no tendency to prove Timothy's state of mind when the Paul-Timothy contract was formed. The objection should have been sustained.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A** is attractive because students know the "opened the door" principle — a party who introduces evidence on a topic may not object to the other side's rebuttal on the same topic. But the warehouse supervisor testified about how Paul's Bibles arrived at Timothy's warehouse. The shipping coordinator's testimony about Paul's shipments to *other* churches is not the same topic. The supervisor's testimony said nothing about Paul's outbound shipping practices, so the door was not opened to evidence of Paul's dealings with third parties. The "opened the door" doctrine requires the rebuttal to be responsive to the specific subject the opponent raised, and it was not.

**B** is the dominant trap. It states a true general principle — evidence of previous dealings *is* admissible to show the parties' state of mind at the time of contract formation. But the principle applies only to dealings *between the same parties*. Here, the 15 transactions involved Paul and other churches, not Paul and Timothy. The choice deliberately omits the same-party limitation, making an overbroad statement that sounds right but doesn't fit the facts. A student who recognizes the "previous dealings" language but doesn't check whether the transactions involved the counterparty will pick this one.

**D** is attractive because it tries to make the third-party transactions seem relevant by pointing out that the 15 recipients were similar to Timothy. But similarity of the other customers doesn't change the fundamental problem: these transactions didn't involve Timothy, and there's no evidence Timothy knew about them. Even if the 15 recipients were identical in every way to Timothy, their acceptance of cardboard boxes says nothing about what Timothy intended or expected when he contracted with Paul. The "same size and type" fact is a red herring.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** Under FRE 401, evidence is relevant if it has any tendency to make a fact of consequence more or less probable. In contract interpretation, the fact of consequence is the parties' intent at the time of formation. Prior transactions between the same parties are relevant to show course of dealing and mutual understanding. However, transactions between one party and third parties are not relevant to show the other party's intent unless that party was aware of those transactions.

**Authority:** FRE 401 (test for relevant evidence); FRE 402 (irrelevant evidence not admissible). The distinction between same-party dealings and third-party dealings in contract interpretation is a foundational relevance principle. See 2 McCormick on Evidence § 219 (8th ed.) (course of dealing between the parties admissible to interpret terms; transactions with outsiders not probative of mutual intent). See also Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 202(4) (course of dealing between the parties is considered in interpreting an agreement).

**Verification:** The tested rule, credited answer, and legal issue are unchanged from the original. The original key (D) is correct. The transform preserves the identical black-letter principle: third-party transactions are not relevant to a counterparty's intent. Outcome confirmed.
