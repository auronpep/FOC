## 9. Black-letter-law verification + right-answer legal reasoning

The tested rule is unchanged: burglary requires the intent to commit a crime or felony inside at the time of entry, while larceny can be supplied by a wrongful taking followed by later intent to permanently deprive under continuing trespass. Cornell Wex states common-law burglary as breaking and entering with intent to commit a felony, and notes that without that intent the entry may be only trespass. Cornell also describes modern burglary as entering a building or structure with intent to commit a crime inside. ([Legal Information Institute][1])

Larceny requires a wrongful taking and carrying away, without consent, with intent to deprive the owner of property. The DOJ Criminal Resource Manual states the larceny elements in those terms and identifies larceny as a specific-intent crime. ([Department of Justice][2])

Continuing trespass supplies the concurrence bridge. In *State v. Brasslett*, the Maine Supreme Judicial Court quoted *State v. Coombs* for the rule that when a wrongful taking is continuous, the felonious intent need not exist at the original taking if it is later added while the wrongful taking continues. That confirms the official outcome: larceny, but not burglary. ([Justia Law][3])
