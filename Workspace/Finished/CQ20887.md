## 7. Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**The credited answer is A: The evidence should be suppressed, because Timothy had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the room where he was staying.**

Timothy was staying in Paul's room for 10 days — he was an overnight guest with a legitimate expectation of privacy in the space. Under *Minnesota v. Olson* (1990), an overnight guest has a reasonable expectation of privacy in the host's home, and society recognizes that expectation as reasonable. Timothy therefore has **standing** to challenge the search.

The police entered without a warrant. No exception applies:
- **Plain view (Choice C):** The plain view doctrine requires that the officer be **lawfully present** at the vantage point. Here, the police pushed past Timothy and entered unlawfully. The marijuana was only visible AFTER the unlawful entry. Plain view does not validate an entry that preceded the observation.
- **Exigent circumstances (Choice D):** The odor of marijuana alone does not create exigent circumstances. There was no evidence of imminent destruction of evidence, no emergency, and no hot pursuit. Smell alone ≠ exigency.
- **Ownership (Choice D→B):** Standing to challenge a search depends on reasonable expectation of privacy, not ownership. A guest staying for 10 days has standing; an owner who is not present may not. The Fourth Amendment protects people, not property.

The evidence must be suppressed under the exclusionary rule.

**Gold Key:** GK-CRIMPROC-STANDING-GUEST-01 — An overnight guest (or anyone staying in another's home for an extended period) has a reasonable expectation of privacy and standing to challenge a warrantless search. Standing ≠ ownership.

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice B — "The evidence should not be suppressed, because Timothy did not own the room."**

*What made it attractive:* Ownership feels like it should matter. Students who conflate property rights with constitutional rights think that only the person whose name is on the lease can challenge a search. The Fourth Amendment says "the right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects" — and "houses" includes any place where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy.

*The breaker:* Standing under the Fourth Amendment depends on whether the defendant had a **reasonable expectation of privacy** in the place searched, not whether they owned it. *Minnesota v. Olson*, 495 U.S. 91 (1990) (overnight guest has standing); *Jones v. United States*, 362 U.S. 257 (1960) (person legitimately on premises has standing). Timothy was staying in the room for 10 days — he had standing regardless of ownership.

**Choice C (dominant trap) — "The evidence should not be suppressed, because the police officers saw the marijuana in plain view."**

*What made it attractive (dominant trap):* The marijuana was sitting on the desk — literally in plain view. "Plain view" is one of the most tested warrant exceptions, and students have it memorized. The facts scream "plain view!" — drugs on a table, clearly visible. It takes a disciplined student to pause and ask: "Were the officers lawfully present when they saw it?"

*The breaker:* The plain view doctrine has three requirements: (1) the officer must be **lawfully present** at the vantage point, (2) the officer must have lawful access to the object, and (3) the incriminating nature of the object must be immediately apparent. Here, requirement (1) fails. The police pushed past Timothy and entered the room without a warrant and without consent. They were NOT lawfully present. The marijuana was only visible because of the unlawful entry. Plain view cannot bootstrap an unlawful entry into a lawful search.

*Gold Key applied:* The GK-CRIMPROC-PLAIN-VIEW-01 Gold Key confirms: plain view requires a lawful vantage point. If the officer is unlawfully present, plain view does not apply.

**Choice D — "The evidence should not be suppressed, because the neighbors smelled the marijuana."**

*What made it attractive:* The neighbors' complaint is what brought the police there, and the smell is real evidence of criminal activity. Students may think that the smell creates an emergency — maybe the evidence is being destroyed, or maybe the smell itself is probable cause for entry.

*The breaker:* The odor of marijuana alone does not create exigent circumstances. Exigent circumstances require an imminent threat of evidence destruction, danger to life, or hot pursuit. *Kentucky v. King*, 563 U.S. 452 (2011) (odor of marijuana does not, by itself, create exigent circumstances justifying warrantless entry). The police had time to get a warrant. Smell ≠ emergency.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rules:**

1. **Standing — Reasonable expectation of privacy:** A person has standing to challenge a search under the Fourth Amendment if they had a legitimate expectation of privacy in the place searched. *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967). An overnight guest has a reasonable expectation of privacy in the host's home. *Minnesota v. Olson*, 495 U.S. 91 (1990). A person staying in another's room for 10 days is functionally an overnight guest with standing.

2. **Plain view doctrine:** Three requirements: (a) officer lawfully present at vantage point, (b) lawful access to the object, (c) incriminating nature immediately apparent. *Horton v. California*, 496 U.S. 128 (1990). The lawful-vantage-point requirement is dispositive — if the officer is unlawfully present, plain view does not apply.

3. **Exigent circumstances:** Warrantless entry is permitted only when there is an imminent threat of evidence destruction, danger to life, or hot pursuit. *Kentucky v. King*, 563 U.S. 452 (2011). Odor of marijuana alone does not create exigent circumstances.

4. **Ownership ≠ standing:** Fourth Amendment rights are personal and depend on reasonable expectation of privacy, not property ownership. *Jones v. United States*, 362 U.S. 257 (1960).

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

**program_frame:** This case study analyzes a Criminal Procedure Fourth Amendment suppression motion. The student must identify that a long-term guest has standing to challenge a warrantless search, that plain view requires a lawful vantage point, that odor alone does not create exigent circumstances, and that ownership is irrelevant to standing.

**call_and_prediction:** "How should the court rule?" — standard MBE suppression motion call. Prediction: The evidence should be suppressed. Timothy had standing (10-day stay = reasonable expectation of privacy), police entered without a warrant, and no exception applies.

**choice_by_choice_walkthrough (4-part blocks):**

**Choice A — "The evidence should be suppressed, because Timothy had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the room where he was staying." ← CREDITED ANSWER**

(1) Student-accessible C3 signal: Timothy was staying in the room for 10 days — that's not a brief visit. He was living there. Society recognizes that a person staying in someone else's home for an extended period has a reasonable expectation of privacy. Standing ≠ ownership.

(2) Student label: "Correct — standing + no valid exception"

(3) What a true/responsive version would look like: This IS the true/responsive version.

(4) Lawyer confirmation: *Minnesota v. Olson*, 495 U.S. 91 (1990) (overnight guest has standing — society recognizes the expectation of privacy in a host's home). *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967) (Fourth Amendment protects people, not places — reasonable expectation of privacy test). Timothy's 10-day stay gives him standing. No warrant exception applies. Evidence suppressed.

**Choice B — "The evidence should not be suppressed, because Timothy did not own the room."**

(1) Student-accessible C3 signal: "Did not own" — the Fourth Amendment doesn't require ownership. It protects people with a reasonable expectation of privacy. A guest, a house-sitter, a long-term visitor — all can have standing. The test is privacy, not property.

(2) Student label: "Ownership ≠ standing — flat misstatement"

(3) What a true/responsive version would look like: "The evidence should be suppressed, because Timothy had a reasonable expectation of privacy regardless of ownership."

(4) Lawyer confirmation: *Jones v. United States*, 362 U.S. 257 (1960) (person legitimately on premises has standing). *Rakas v. Illinois*, 439 U.S. 128 (1978) (standing requires legitimate expectation of privacy, not property interest). Timothy doesn't need to own the room — he just needs to have been staying there, which he was.

**Choice C (dominant trap) — "The evidence should not be suppressed, because the police officers saw the marijuana in plain view."**

(1) Student-accessible C3 signal: The marijuana was on the desk — visible. But the police only saw it AFTER pushing past Timothy and entering the room. Plain view has three requirements, and the first one — lawful vantage point — is missing. An unlawful entry cannot be cured by what the officer sees inside.

(2) Student label: "Dominant trap — plain view requires lawful vantage point"

(3) What a true/responsive version would look like: "The evidence should be suppressed, because the officers were not lawfully present when they observed the marijuana, so the plain view doctrine does not apply."

(4) Lawyer confirmation: *Horton v. California*, 496 U.S. 128 (1990) (plain view requires: (1) lawful vantage point, (2) lawful access, (3) immediately apparent incriminating nature). The officers pushed past Timothy without a warrant — they were NOT lawfully present. The plain view doctrine cannot bootstrap an unlawful entry. This is the classic MBE plain-view trap: the right exception, missing one element.

**Choice D — "The evidence should not be suppressed, because the neighbors smelled the marijuana."**

(1) Student-accessible C3 signal: The neighbors' complaint brought the police there, but smell alone doesn't create an emergency. Exigent circumstances require imminent evidence destruction, danger, or hot pursuit. Marijuana smell ≠ emergency.

(2) Student label: "Odor alone ≠ exigent circumstances"

(3) What a true/responsive version would look like: "The evidence should be suppressed, because the odor of marijuana alone does not create exigent circumstances justifying warrantless entry."

(4) Lawyer confirmation: *Kentucky v. King*, 563 U.S. 452 (2011) (odor of marijuana does not, by itself, create exigent circumstances). The police had time to obtain a warrant. No evidence was being destroyed. No one was in danger. Smell ≠ exigency.
