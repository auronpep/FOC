## 6. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.**

**Gold Key:** When two defendants are convicted from one regulatory sale, ask what each conviction needs. The clerk’s conviction despite a convincing fake ID requires **strict liability**. The owner’s conviction for the clerk’s sale requires **vicarious liability**.

**Silver Key:** Do not pick the best-sounding single doctrine. Map each actor to the liability theory needed to convict that actor. Timothy needs strict liability. Lydia needs vicarious liability. The only answer that covers both boxes is C.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Strict liability only — wrong.**
This explains Timothy’s conviction. It does not explain Lydia’s conviction for a sale made by Timothy.
**Gold Key:** Strict liability removes the mental-state problem; it does not automatically make one person liable for another person’s act.

**B. Vicarious liability only — wrong.**
This explains Lydia’s conviction. It does not explain Timothy’s conviction despite the fake ID and lack of knowing sale to a minor.
**Gold Key:** Vicarious liability solves the “owner for clerk” problem; it does not solve the “clerk lacked fault” problem.

**D. Either strict or vicarious liability — wrong.**
“Either” is too loose. The result needs both: strict liability for Timothy and vicarious liability for Lydia.
**Silver Key:** On a two-actor liability-standard question, “either” is a warning sign. The exam is asking for the theories required to support the actual double conviction.

## 8. Black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

The original rule and outcome are unchanged: the tested issue is the interaction of **strict liability** and **vicarious liability** in a regulatory misdemeanor involving sale of alcohol to a minor. The official source explanation for Q14631 states that C is correct because the clerk conviction requires strict liability and the owner conviction requires vicarious liability. 

For lawyer-confirmation only: public welfare or regulatory offenses may be construed to dispense with ordinary mens rea requirements. *People v. Farley* describes public welfare offenses as regulatory offenses where convictions may be permitted without proof of criminal intent or criminal negligence. ([Justia Law][1]) In *Commonwealth v. Koczwara*, the court recognized that liquor-code regulation can eliminate the common-law mens rea requirement and place high responsibility on the license holder, while also recognizing due-process limits on imprisonment for purely vicarious liability. ([Justia Law][2]) The item only asks what interpretation the court must have used after convicting both actors; it does not ask whether every punishment would be constitutionally permissible.
