### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**Gold Key:** Manifest necessity is a *high degree* of necessity — not just any fairness concern. Before declaring a mistrial over the defendant's objection, the court must seriously consider all reasonable alternatives. A viable alternative existed here: excusing the juror and proceeding with eleven jurors, which state law expressly permitted. The judge's stated reason — that retrial was "simpler" — does not meet the demanding standard. Because the court failed to adequately consider that alternative, the mistrial was not manifestly necessary, and retrial is barred by the Double Jeopardy Clause.

Retrial is barred. Jeopardy attached when the jury was sworn. Once jeopardy attaches, a mistrial declared over the defendant's objection bars retrial unless the prosecution demonstrates manifest necessity. The prosecution bears a heavy burden to show a high degree of necessity. Here, the judge did not seriously consider the reasonable alternative of proceeding with eleven jurors — a path state law expressly allowed. The judge's reasoning that a retrial was "simpler" falls far short of the manifest necessity standard.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap):** This choice sounds like a clean, authoritative rule — juror misconduct threatens fairness, so mistrial is manifestly necessary. That is exactly the kind of overbroad statement that feels right on first read. But manifest necessity is a demanding standard, not a rubber stamp. Even when juror misconduct occurs, the court must first seriously consider all reasonable alternatives before aborting the trial. Here, a viable alternative existed: excusing the juror and proceeding with eleven jurors, which state law permitted. If the court failed to adequately consider that alternative, the mistrial was not manifestly necessary, and retrial is barred. **Gold Key:** Manifest necessity requires a *high degree* of necessity and serious consideration of all reasonable alternatives — not just any fairness concern.

**B:** This choice plays on a timing misconception — the idea that jeopardy attaches when evidence begins, not when the jury is sworn. It feels plausible if a student confuses the attachment trigger with the start of proof. But in a jury trial, jeopardy attaches when the jury is sworn, not when witnesses begin testifying. The stem states the jury was sworn before the misconduct arose. Jeopardy had already attached. The issue is whether the mistrial was supported by manifest necessity — and it was not.

**D:** This choice overstates the rule in the opposite direction — it claims that *any* mistrial over the defendant's objection automatically bars retrial. That absolute framing is attractive because it seems to give defendants total veto power. But the law is narrower: a mistrial over objection bars retrial only when manifest necessity is *absent*. If genuine manifest necessity existed (e.g., a juror became permanently incapacitated with no alternatives), retrial would be permitted even over the defendant's objection. The result here is correct (retrial is barred), but the reasoning is wrong — it would produce incorrect outcomes on different facts.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule:** The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment (*Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784 (1969)). Once jeopardy attaches, a mistrial declared over the defendant's objection bars retrial unless the prosecution demonstrates "manifest necessity" — a high degree of necessity (*Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505 (1978)). The trial judge must seriously consider all reasonable alternatives before declaring a mistrial (*United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. at 480). The prosecution bears the heavy burden of justifying the mistrial (*Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 505).
