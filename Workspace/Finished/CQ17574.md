### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The order is a **prior restraint** on speech. A prior restraint is a content-based restraint imposed before publication — typically a government requirement that the speaker obtain advance approval from a government official before speaking. Prior restraints carry a "heavy presumption against [their] constitutional validity" and are the most heavily disfavored form of speech regulation. New York Times Co. v. United States, 403 U.S. 713 (1971) (Pentagon Papers); Near v. Minnesota, 283 U.S. 697 (1931). The order here is a textbook prior restraint: it is a government requirement that Mary obtain Paul's approval before publishing any future podcast episodes about him, and it was entered before any final adjudication on the defamation claim.

**Gold Key:** a court order that requires a speaker to obtain advance approval from a government official (or a private party acting under government order) before publication is a prior restraint, regardless of the speaker's medium or whether the speech generates ad revenue. The medium and the revenue model are not the constitutional test; the test is whether the government is conditioning publication on prior approval. **Silver Key:** ad revenue does NOT convert political or civic commentary about public officials into commercial speech; commercial speech is speech that does no more than propose a commercial transaction. **Silver Key:** the First Amendment protects all speakers, not just institutional newspapers; the medium (podcast, blog, newsletter) does not determine whether the prior-restraint doctrine applies.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Rational basis, because defamation is a tort.**

This is the weakest trap. It imports a completely unrelated level-of-scrutiny framework (rational basis) that does not apply to a First Amendment prior-restraint question. Rational basis is the deferential review used for economic and social-welfare legislation; speech restrictions are reviewed under strict scrutiny (content-based) or intermediate scrutiny (content-neutral). The choice fails the issue-frame check: a prepublication injunction is not a rational-basis question. Visible from the stem: nothing in the facts supports a rational-basis analysis; the order restricts speech before it occurs, which is the prior-restraint frame.

**C. Commercial speech, because the podcaster earns ad revenue.**

This is the dominant trap. The stem contains a salient "ad revenue" fact that is designed to lure students toward the commercial-speech doctrine. Commercial speech is speech that does no more than propose a commercial transaction (e.g., advertising for a product or service). Political or civic commentary about public officials — even when monetized through ads — is not commercial speech. The choice fails the commercial-speech test: Mary's podcast is civic commentary about a school-board member, not an advertisement for a product. The breaker is the **Silver Key**: ad revenue does not convert political or civic commentary about public officials into commercial speech. Visible from the stem: the subject matter is "commentary about a local school-board member," not a commercial transaction; the ad revenue is incidental monetization, not the speech's character.

**D. No First Amendment doctrine, because podcasts are not newspapers.**

This is the second-strongest trap. It tests the misconception that the First Amendment's "freedom of the press" clause protects only institutional newspapers. The First Amendment protects all speakers, regardless of medium: bloggers, podcasters, YouTubers, newsletter writers, and self-publishers are all entitled to the same First Amendment protection as institutional newspapers. The choice fails the scope-of-speech test: the medium does not determine whether the prior-restraint doctrine applies. The breaker is the **Silver Key**: the First Amendment protects all speakers, not just institutional newspapers.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17574, a Constitutional Law item on the First Amendment prior-restraint doctrine. The official key is A, and the official explanation is that the order "suppresses speech before it occurs and is presumptively invalid."

The black-letter rule is the **prior-restraint doctrine**: a prior restraint is a content-based restraint on speech imposed before publication, and it carries a heavy presumption against constitutional validity. The leading cases are Near v. Minnesota, 283 U.S. 697 (1931) (prior restraints are the most extreme form of speech restriction and bear a heavy presumption against constitutional validity), and New York Times Co. v. United States, 403 U.S. 713 (1971) (the government "carries a heavy burden" of showing justification for a prior restraint). The modern doctrine applies the prior-restraint analysis to any government requirement of advance approval for speech, regardless of the speaker's medium. The First Amendment's "freedom of the press" clause protects all speakers, not just institutional newspapers; this is a settled modern principle.

The "commercial speech" frame in choice D is governed by Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission, 447 U.S. 557 (1980), and its modern refinements. Commercial speech is defined as speech that does no more than propose a commercial transaction. Political or civic commentary about public officials — even when monetized through ads — is not commercial speech; it is fully protected by the First Amendment under the prior-restraint and content-based-speech doctrines.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: a prior restraint is the most directly implicated First Amendment doctrine; the medium and the ad-revenue fact are red herrings designed to lure students toward the commercial-speech and "not the institutional press" distractors.
