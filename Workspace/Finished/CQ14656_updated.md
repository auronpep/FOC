## 5. Correct answer

## 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** The court should deny acquittal because John’s refusal to seek treatment does not erase Peter’s stabbing as a legally sufficient cause of death. The court should still instruct on voluntary manslaughter because John slapped Peter immediately before the stabbing, giving the jury some evidence from which it could find heat-of-passion/adequate provocation.

**Gold Key: Victim-refusal causation.** A victim’s foreseeable refusal to seek medical treatment after being wounded usually does not break the causal chain.

**Gold Key: Lesser-instruction threshold.** If the evidence gives the jury a reasonable path to voluntary manslaughter, the defendant gets the instruction even though the prosecution also has enough evidence for murder.

**Silver Key:** The call has two requested rulings. A complete answer must handle both: acquittal and the manslaughter instruction.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A is wrong.** It uses a true fact: John would have survived with treatment. But that fact does not, by itself, force acquittal. The missing piece is the causal-chain anchor. **Gold Key:** refusal of treatment after a serious wound usually does not break causation.

**C is wrong.** It points at the insult, but the stem gives more than words: John slapped Peter. The answer addresses the wrong provocation fact. A true/responsive version would say there was no adequate provocation if the facts showed only insulting words and no physical blow.

**D is wrong.** This is the dominant trap. It gives a real murder-sufficiency reason: knife + stomach can support malice. But the call also asks for a manslaughter instruction. A reason that supports denying acquittal does not automatically support denying the lesser instruction. **Silver Key:** do not let the murder-sufficiency answer swallow the alternative instruction question.

## 8. Black-letter verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule and outcome are unchanged from the original row: deny acquittal, but give the voluntary manslaughter instruction. The official explanation says the victim’s refusal to seek medical treatment is not enough to break criminal causation and that the slap can support a heat-of-passion manslaughter instruction. 

For lawyer confirmation only: Cornell’s Wex entry describes an intervening cause as a later event that can break the chain of causation, and gives a homicide example in which a victim’s later medical-related failures did not break causation. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) Justia’s CALCRIM 570 states that a killing that would otherwise be murder is reduced to voluntary manslaughter if committed because of sudden quarrel or heat of passion, with provocation, intense emotion, and an objective-average-person component. ([Justia][2]) CALCRIM’s bench notes also state that a voluntary manslaughter instruction is required when the evidence is substantial enough for jury consideration. ([Justia][2])
