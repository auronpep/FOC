### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B** is the answer. Lydia filed a timely notice of appeal, but timeliness does not create appellate jurisdiction. The district court ordered a new trial. That means the case is still alive in the trial court; there is no final decision for the court of appeals to review under the ordinary final-judgment route.

**Gold Key:** an order granting a new trial is interlocutory, not a final decision under 28 U.S.C. Section 1291, and Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 48(c) expressly allows the judge to poll jurors on the judge's own initiative and then either direct further deliberations or order a new trial if unanimity fails.

**Silver Key:** lock the call first. The question asks whether the court of appeals is likely to hear the appeal, so the first filter is appellate jurisdiction, not whether the trial judge picked the better post-poll option.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Yes, because contributory negligence is not a defense in a federal negligence action.**

This answer is attractive because contributory negligence is the disputed trial issue and sounds like a reason the plaintiff should keep the verdict. The breaker is call discipline: the appeal is from a new-trial order, so the first question is whether there is an appealable final decision. A merits argument about contributory negligence does not supply appellate jurisdiction, and in a diversity negligence action state substantive law would normally govern the defense.

**C. No, because a federal judge may not poll the jurors on the judge's own initiative.**

This answer notices that the judge acted without a party's request, so it feels like a clean procedural defect. The breaker is the Rule 48(c) anchor: a federal civil judge may poll jurors individually on the judge's own initiative after the verdict and before discharge. The statement attacks the polling power, but that attack is legally false.

**D. Yes, because the judge was required to send the jurors back for further deliberations once the poll showed no unanimity.**

This is the dominant trap. It is attractive because further deliberation is one real option after a failed poll, so the answer feels like it identifies a trial-court error that would justify immediate review. The breaker is the **Gold Key:** Rule 48(c) gives the judge a choice between directing further deliberations and ordering a new trial. The **Silver Key** also steers away from it: even a plausible complaint about the judge's choice does not answer the threshold question whether a new-trial order is immediately appealable.

### 8. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested issue remains Civil Procedure, Appealability and Review, Final judgment rule. The verified outline code is `93110101`, which appears in `OUTLINE_CODES_COMPLETE.md` as `Appeal and Review > Interlocutory Review > Final judgment rule`.

The governing appellate-jurisdiction rule is 28 U.S.C. Section 1291, which gives federal courts of appeals jurisdiction over appeals from final decisions of the district courts. A new-trial order does not end the litigation; it returns the case for further proceedings. The Supreme Court's decision in *Allied Chemical Corp. v. Daiflon, Inc.*, 449 U.S. 33 (1980), confirms that an order granting a new trial is interlocutory in nature and not immediately appealable, with review normally available after final judgment.

The jury-polling rule is Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 48(c). It provides that after a verdict is returned but before the jury is discharged, the court may on its own poll the jurors individually; if the poll reveals a lack of unanimity or the required assent, the court may direct further deliberations or order a new trial. That rule confirms that the judge's sua sponte poll and new-trial order are both authorized options.

The Erie point confirms why the merits-defense distractor does not help Lydia. In a federal diversity negligence case, substantive state law generally supplies the negligence defenses; *Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), rejects a general federal common-law rule displacing state substantive law. But even if Lydia had a strong merits argument, that would not create appellate jurisdiction over a nonfinal new-trial order.
