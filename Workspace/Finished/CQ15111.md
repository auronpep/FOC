### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** When one tortfeasor's liability is merely negligent and the co-tortfeasor committed an intentional tort, the negligent tortfeasor who discharges the joint judgment is entitled to **full indemnity**—not mere contribution—from the intentional tortfeasor. The intentional tortfeasor bears the entire loss because intentional wrongdoing is regarded as more culpable, and public policy forbids shifting any portion of the intentional wrongdoer's responsibility back to the negligent party through contribution.

**Silver Key:** When the call asks what a tortfeasor can recover *from a co-tortfeasor* after paying a joint judgment, lock the call: this is a **between-tortfeasor** question, not a plaintiff-vs.-defendant question. The first move is to classify each tortfeasor's culpability level. If one is intentional and the other negligent, the indemnity rule—not the contribution statute—governs.

C is correct. Peter was merely negligent (he left the nail gun accessible), while Daniel committed an intentional tort (deliberately using the nail gun to injure the shopkeeper). Under traditional common-law indemnity principles, a negligent tortfeasor who pays the full joint judgment is entitled to full indemnity from an intentional tortfeasor. Daniel, as the intentional tortfeasor, is liable for all consequences of his wrongful act—including the negligent co-tortfeasor's share. Peter can recover the entire amount he paid.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**(A) Contribution only, based on comparative fault, because Peter himself was negligent.**

*What makes it attractive:* This feels right because both Peter and Daniel were at fault, and the jurisdiction has a comparative-fault contribution statute. Students who default to the "normal" contribution rule will pick this without considering the intentional-tort exception.

*The breaker:* **Gold Key:** The contribution statute applies when both tortfeasors are negligent (or at least neither committed an intentional tort). When one tortfeasor committed an intentional tort, the negligent co-tortfeasor's remedy is not contribution—it is **full indemnity**. The intentional tortfeasor cannot benefit from having the loss shared. The contribution statute does not override the common-law indemnity rule in this situation.

**(B) Nothing, because Peter's negligence was a substantial proximate cause of the injury.**

*What makes it attractive:* Peter's negligence really did contribute to the injury—had he locked the shed, Daniel might not have gotten the gun. Students conflate Peter's liability *to the plaintiff* with the allocation of loss *between the tortfeasors*.

*The breaker:* The question is not whether Peter is liable to the shopkeeper (he is—that's already been established). The question is whether Peter can recover from Daniel. Daniel's intentional tort supersedes Peter's negligence in the between-tortfeasor allocation. Peter's proximate cause of the original injury is irrelevant to his indemnity claim against Daniel.

**(D) One-half of the amount of the judgment.**

*What makes it attractive:* Equal splitting feels fair and is a common default when two parties share fault. Students who know contribution exists but don't work through the specifics may guess at a 50/50 split.

*The breaker:* There is no rule that joint tortfeasors split liability equally. Contribution is based on proportionate fault, not equal shares—and more importantly, this isn't a contribution question at all. The intentional-tortfeasor rule means Daniel bears the entire loss, not half.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** When two or more tortfeasors are jointly and severally liable for an indivisible injury, one who pays the entire judgment has a right of recovery against the other(s). The *nature* of that recovery depends on the relative culpability of the tortfeasors.

**Contribution** is a sharing mechanism: a tortfeasor who paid more than their proportionate share can recover the excess from other jointly liable tortfeasors, apportioned by relative fault. However, **an intentional tortfeasor has no right of contribution**—and conversely, a negligent tortfeasor is **not limited to contribution** when the co-tortfeasor acted intentionally.

**Indemnity** shifts the *entire* loss from one tortfeasor to another. Under traditional common-law rules, a negligent tortfeasor is entitled to **full indemnity** from an intentional tortfeasor for the common liability. The rationale: the intentional wrongdoer is more culpable, and public policy prevents the intentional tortfeasor from shifting any portion of the loss to the negligent party. See Restatement (Second) of Torts § 886B (indemnity based on unjust enrichment; the party whose culpability is greater bears the ultimate burden); Restatement (Third) of Torts: Apportionment of Liability § 22 & cmt. (addressing intentional tortfeasors and the limitation on contribution rights).

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 886B; Restatement (Third) of Torts: Apportionment of Liability §§ 1, 22. The majority rule on the MBE is that a negligent tortfeasor may obtain full indemnity from an intentional tortfeasor for the entire joint liability.
