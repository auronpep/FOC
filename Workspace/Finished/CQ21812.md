### 7. Full right-answer explanation
A is correct. The statement was not merely taken in violation of an interrogation warning rule; it was produced by police violence and threats. Gold Key: the impeachment exception for some suppressed statements does not apply to an involuntary coerced confession. Silver Key: when the stem gives both "barred from the case-in-chief" and "only to impeach," lock onto the reason for suppression before using the impeachment shortcut. Because due process bars use of a coerced involuntary confession against the defendant for any purpose at trial, the prosecutor may not use the statement even to attack Timothy's credibility.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
B is attractive because it grabs the familiar impeachment shortcut: a defendant should not be able to testify falsely and hide behind suppression. The breaker is the word "always." Gold Key: the shortcut can apply to voluntary statements suppressed for Miranda-type reasons, but it does not apply to a confession beaten or threatened out of the defendant.

C is attractive because it reaches the same "No" result and uses a criminal-procedure phrase students recognize. The breaker is the timing and the defect. The statement is barred because it was coerced by force and threats; warnings given after the statement would not make that coerced confession usable.

D is attractive because it treats coercion as a credibility problem, as if jurors can discount a rough interrogation when deciding weight. The breaker is that involuntariness is an admissibility bar, not merely a weight issue. The judge cannot send a coerced confession to the jury for impeachment weight.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue is criminal procedure, statements and confessions, voluntary statements, and the verified outline code is `75080101`. The source row lists `73030400` for robbery, but the robbery is only the underlying charge; the rule being tested is the constitutional admissibility of a coerced confession. The credited answer remains unchanged.

The governing due-process rule is that an involuntary confession extracted by physical coercion or threats may not be used against the defendant in a criminal trial for any purpose. In *Mincey v. Arizona*, 437 U.S. 385 (1978), the Supreme Court stated that any criminal-trial use of an involuntary statement is a denial of due process. That rule controls the impeachment attempt here. By contrast, *Harris v. New York*, 401 U.S. 222 (1971), permits impeachment use of certain voluntary statements taken without full Miranda compliance, but that exception does not extend to statements that are actually involuntary. The official key is therefore consistent with current controlling law: the coerced confession cannot be used even for impeachment.
