## 1. Final question (revised stem + call)

Peter had a life insurance policy that would not pay benefits if Peter died by suicide. One winter morning, Peter's wife, Mary, drove to Peter's small retreat cabin where he often went to read and pray. She found Peter dead inside the cabin from carbon monoxide poisoning, with a portable heater still sitting near the doorway.

The cabin was deep in the hills, far from the nearest occupied house, and no one else was anywhere near Peter when Mary found him. A county fire investigator determined that it was unlikely the heater's fumes entered the cabin by accident, although there was evidence that the heater had leaked carbon monoxide during prior use. The county medical examiner listed the manner of death as "suicide."

Mary made a claim for life insurance benefits, and the life insurance company filed an action in federal court, claiming that she was not entitled to benefits because Peter had committed suicide. In determining whether Peter committed suicide, which of the following statements is correct?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**C is correct.** This is a civil case about insurance benefits. When the cause of death is disputed, the law recognizes a rebuttable presumption against suicide. That means the starting presumption favors non-suicide, and the insurer must come forward with evidence to rebut it. **Gold Key:** in a civil disputed-death insurance case, official suicide language may be evidence, but it does not create the presumption; the presumption runs against suicide. **Silver Key:** do not let an official label flip the direction of the presumption. The call asks what presumption applies, not which side has stronger evidence.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A - There is a rebuttable presumption Peter did commit suicide because the medical examiner listed the manner of death as "suicide."** This is the dominant trap because it uses a real official label and chooses the softer word "rebuttable" instead of "conclusive." The breaker is direction. **Gold Key:** where suicide is disputed in a civil insurance case, the presumption runs against suicide, not toward the medical examiner's label.

**B - There is a conclusive presumption that the heater leaked by accident based on the county fire investigator's statement.** The fire investigator's statement matters because it bears on the factual dispute. But it does not create a conclusive presumption. A conclusive presumption cannot be rebutted, and nothing in this fact pattern makes the investigator's view legally unanswerable.

**D - There is a conclusive presumption Peter did commit suicide because the medical examiner listed his manner of death as "suicide."** The medical examiner's conclusion is strong evidence for the insurer, and that is why this answer has pull. But an official report does not become a conclusive presumption of suicide. The civil presumption starts the other way and can be rebutted by evidence.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule:** Federal Rule of Evidence 301 states the general effect of presumptions in civil cases: unless a federal statute or rule provides otherwise, the party against whom a presumption is directed has the burden of producing evidence to rebut it, while the burden of persuasion remains where it was originally placed. A recognized civil presumption in disputed death cases is the presumption against suicide. In a civil life-insurance dispute where suicide would defeat coverage and the cause or manner of death is disputed, the law presumes the insured did not commit suicide unless the opposing party produces evidence sufficient to rebut that presumption.

**Authority:** FRE 301, in the current Federal Rules of Evidence, supplies the burden-of-production effect for civil presumptions. The current rule text is available in the United States Courts rules publication at https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2025-02/federal-rules-of-evidence-dec-1-2024_0.pdf. The House codification notes for FRE Article III discuss `Dick v. New York Life Ins. Co.`, 359 U.S. 437 (1959), as an example applying a state-law presumption against suicide in an insurance case involving death by gunshot wound. Those notes quote the Supreme Court's statement that proof of coverage and death by gunshot shifted the burden to the insurer to establish suicide. See https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&path=%2Fprelim%40title28%2Ftitle28a%2Fnode232%2Farticle3.

**Confirmation:** The credited outcome is unchanged from the source: there is a rebuttable presumption that the insured did not commit suicide because the cause of death is disputed. The legal issue is unchanged: civil presumptions and the presumption against suicide in a life-insurance dispute. The dominant trap is unchanged: treating an official suicide listing as creating a presumption of suicide. Not on the doctrinal-currency hotlist. Authority floor satisfied by FRE 301 and `Dick v. New York Life Ins. Co.` as discussed in the federal codification notes.
