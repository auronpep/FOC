## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key: The preexisting duty rule says that a promise to do what you are already contractually obligated to do is not valid consideration for a new agreement.**

The tutors had a binding contract to tutor through December at a fixed rate. When they demanded more money mid-semester, they were merely promising to do exactly what they were already obligated to do -- complete the remaining tutoring sessions. Under the preexisting duty rule, performance of an existing contractual obligation does not constitute fresh consideration for a modified agreement. *Stilk v Myrick* (1809) 2 Camp 317 is the foundational case: sailors who demanded extra pay for duties they were already bound to perform gave no consideration for the captain's promise of additional wages. The same principle applies here. The modification is unenforceable for lack of consideration, and Barnabas is not bound to pay the increased rate.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B (new D) -- Yes, because Barnabas voluntarily agreed to the higher rate and received the benefit of their continued tutoring.**

**D (new B) -- Yes, because the parties are free to renegotiate a contract when circumstances change.**

*The breaker that defeats it:* Freedom to renegotiate does not eliminate the consideration requirement. A modification still needs new consideration to be enforceable at common law. The tutors did not promise anything beyond their original duty -- no additional sessions, no new services, no forbearance of a right. **Gold Key:** The preexisting duty rule says that a promise to do what you are already contractually obligated to do is not valid consideration for a new agreement.

**A (new C) -- No, because Barnabas agreed while under economic duress.**

*What makes it attractive:* The facts genuinely describe coercive pressure -- the tutors threatened to stop tutoring mid-semester with no replacements available, and Barnabas had no real choice but to agree. Students who recognize the duress dynamic may gravitate to this answer.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule -- Preexisting Duty Rule (Common Law):**

Under the common law preexisting duty rule, a promise to perform an act that one is already legally obligated to perform under an existing contract does not constitute valid consideration for a new or modified agreement. *Stilk v Myrick* (1809) 2 Camp 317, 170 ER 1168 (Lord Ellenborough: "There was no consideration for the ulterior pay promised to the mariners who remained with the captain"). The Restatement (Second) of Contracts Sec. 73 provides that performance of a legal duty owed to the promisor that is neither doubtful nor the subject of honest dispute is not consideration.

**Application to this question:**

Peter and Timothy were contractually obligated to tutor through December at a fixed rate. Their promise to continue tutoring was a promise to perform a preexisting contractual duty. They undertook no additional services, forbore no right, and incurred no new detriment. Under the preexisting duty rule, their continued performance was not valid consideration for Barnabas's promise of a higher rate. The modification is unenforceable.

**Why duress is not the best answer:**

While the facts do describe economic pressure, duress renders a contract *voidable*, not void, and requires a showing that the victim had no reasonable alternative. The preexisting duty rule is a formation-level defense -- there was never a valid modification in the first place because consideration was lacking. On the MBE, the preexisting duty rule is the more precise and complete answer. See *Alaska Packers' Ass'n v. Domenico* (1902) (applying preexisting duty rule to similar facts).

**Authority:** *Stilk v Myrick* (1809) 2 Camp 317; Restatement (Second) of Contracts Sec. 73; *Alaska Packers' Ass'n v. Domenico*, 117 F. 99 (9th Cir. 1902).
