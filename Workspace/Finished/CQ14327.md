## 1. Final question

A federally regulated interstate ferry company operates scheduled passenger service along the U.S. Atlantic coast between three states. A federal statute authorizes the Federal Maritime Commission (FMC) to permit interstate ferry operators to discontinue entirely any unprofitable route. The ferry company applied to the FMC for permission to drop a deeply unprofitable route serving the Hatteras Banks islands off the coast of North Carolina. The FMC granted that permission even though the ferry company provided the only public transportation to the islands. Lydia is the owner of a small fishing lodge on the Hatteras Banks, whose customers typically arrived on the ferry. After exhausting all available federal administrative remedies, Lydia filed suit against the ferry company in the trial court of North Carolina to enjoin the discontinuance by the ferry company of its service to the islands. Lydia alleged that the discontinuance of service by the ferry company would violate a state statute of North Carolina prohibiting common carriers of persons from abandoning service to communities having no alternate form of public transportation. B: hear the case on its merits and decide for Lydia because, on these facts, a federal agency is interfering with essential state functions.

C: hear the case on its merits and decide for the ferry company, because a valid federal law preempts the state statute on which Lydia relies.

D: dismiss the action, because Lydia lacks standing to sue.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** Lydia is suing under a North Carolina statute that forbids carriers from discontinuing routes to communities with no alternative public transportation. That state prohibition directly conflicts with a federal statute that authorizes the FMC to let interstate ferry operators discontinue any unprofitable route. Under the Supremacy Clause (U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2), a state law that conflicts with a validly enacted federal law is preempted. The state court therefore has authority to decide the case (state courts share concurrent jurisdiction over federal preemption questions, *Tafflin v. Levitt*, 493 U.S. 455 (1990); *Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 453 U.S. 470 (1981)), and on the merits the federal statute prevails. *Gold Key:* There is no "essential state functions" exception to Supremacy Clause preemption. Regulation of interstate common carriers is a federal function under the Commerce Clause, and any state law in direct conflict with the federal regulatory scheme is invalid. *Silver Key:* The procedural posture here (state court should "hear on merits") is shared by B and C, so the deciding axis is the SUBSTANTIVE rule — apply Supremacy Clause preemption and pick the choice that gets the outcome right.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

### A (original B) — Remove for substantial federal question

The "substantial federal question" hook sounds like the path to federal court, but it actually misapplies two doctrines at once. First, under the well-pleaded complaint rule, federal question jurisdiction turns on the face of the plaintiff's complaint; a federal DEFENSE (here, preemption) does not create it. *Franchise Tax Bd. v. Constr. Laborers Vacation Trust*, 463 U.S. 1 (1983). Lydia's complaint pleads only a state-law claim. Second, even if a federal question were presented, state courts share concurrent jurisdiction over federal claims absent congressional exclusivity, *Tafflin v. Levitt*, 493 U.S. 455 (1990). The state trial court is a court of general jurisdiction and can decide the preemption question. Removal is improper; the choice does not address the question actually being asked of the state court. **Filter: NOT_RESPONSIVE. Mold: bait_doctrine.**

### B (original C) — Hear on merits + decide for Lydia on "essential state functions" — DOMINANT TRAP

The "hear on merits" half of this choice is correct, which is exactly what makes the distractor attractive. The "essential state functions" reasoning is the bait. It evokes Tenth Amendment / anti-commandeering language and reads as a constitutional check on federal overreach, but it does not apply here. The federal statute at issue regulates INTERSTATE common carriers — a quintessentially federal function under the Commerce Clause — and the Supremacy Clause preempts any state law that directly conflicts with a federal regulatory scheme in that field. There is no "essential state functions" exception that rescues a directly conflicting state law. *Gold Key:* regulation of interstate common carriers is a federal function, not a state function, and the Supremacy Clause preempts directly conflicting state law in that field. The choice gets the outcome wrong. **Filter: NOT_TRUE. Mold: half_truth.**

### D (original A) — Dismiss for lack of standing

This choice sounds plausible because Lydia is one private business owner, not the entire affected public — but that framing inverts Lujan's standing test. Standing requires (i) a concrete and particularized "injury in fact," (ii) caused by the conduct complained of, and (iii) likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Lydia satisfies all three: she has a direct economic injury (loss of customers when the ferry discontinues), the injury is caused by the discontinuance, and an injunction against the discontinuance would remedy it. The choice treats individualized economic harm as if it defeated standing; in fact, individualized economic harm is the threshold showing Lujan requires. **Filter: NOT_TRUE. Mold: backwards.**

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**Governing rule.** The Supremacy Clause provides: "This Constitution, and the Laws of the United States which shall be made in Pursuance thereof … shall be the supreme Law of the Land … any Thing in the Constitution or Laws of any State to the Contrary notwithstanding." U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2. Under the Supremacy Clause, federal law preempts state law in three principal ways: (i) express preemption (the federal statute says so); (ii) field preemption (federal regulation is so pervasive it occupies the field); and (iii) conflict preemption (compliance with both is impossible or the state law stands as an obstacle to federal objectives). *Altria Group, Inc. v. Good*, 555 U.S. 70, 76–77 (2008); *Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc.*, 505 U.S. 504, 516 (1992). Here, the conflict is direct on its face: the federal statute authorizes the FMC to permit discontinuance of an unprofitable route, and the state statute forbids common carriers from abandoning service to communities with no alternate public transportation. The two cannot be reconciled. The federal statute prevails.

**Confirming that the legal issue and credited outcome are unchanged.** The original Q tests (i) standing, (ii) state-court concurrent jurisdiction over a federal preemption question, and (iii) Supremacy Clause conflict preemption between a federal common-carrier statute and a state anti-abandonment statute. The transformed item tests the same three sub-issues, with the only changes being the surface-level asset (ferry vs. bus), the geography (Hatteras Banks islands vs. Shaley Mountains), the cast (Lydia, fishing-lodge owner, vs. an unnamed mountain-resort owner), the regulator (FMC vs. ICC), and the reach (three-state Atlantic coast vs. five-state area).
