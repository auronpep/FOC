### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. The cross-examination question is proper because it points to a concrete reason Hannah might have been angry at Timothy and therefore might have a motive to accuse him falsely. Gold Key: bias and motive to lie are independent credibility grounds; the fact used to show bias does not need to be a truthfulness act under Rule 608. Silver Key: ask what the question is offered to show. The laptop incident is not offered to prove Hannah is the kind of person who lies; it is offered to show a reason she might slant this accusation against Timothy.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because it reaches the correct "proper" result and sounds like standard witness-impeachment language. The breaker is the Gold Key: cracking a laptop is not a truthfulness act, so the question is not proper for character-for-truthfulness impeachment; it is proper only because it shows possible bias or motive.

B is attractive because it treats cross-examination as locked to the exact story told on direct. The breaker is that credibility is always a proper cross-examination lane, and bias or motive to lie is a credibility issue even if the direct examination did not discuss the punishment.

D is the dominant trap because its first sentence feels clean: laptop damage does not prove truthfulness or untruthfulness. The breaker is the Silver Key: purpose controls. The question is offered for bias or motive to accuse falsely, not for character for truthfulness.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, answer-array mechanics, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. The rule is that a witness may be impeached by showing bias, interest, hostility, or motive to testify falsely. The punishment shortly before the accusation gives Hannah a possible motive to be angry with Timothy and to slant or fabricate testimony against him, so defense counsel may ask about it on cross-examination.

Authority layer: In United States v. Abel, 469 U.S. 45 (1984), the Supreme Court held that bias impeachment is permitted under the Federal Rules of Evidence and explained that bias evidence is generally relevant because it bears on the accuracy of witness testimony: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/469/45/. Federal Rule of Evidence 611(b) permits cross-examination into "matters affecting the witness's credibility," which includes bias and motive: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_611. Federal Rule of Evidence 608(b) separately limits specific-act inquiry for character for truthfulness to acts probative of truthfulness or untruthfulness, which confirms why the laptop-damage fact is not a truthfulness act even though it remains usable for bias: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_608.

The official key is consistent with those authorities. The question is proper not because property damage reveals Hannah's character for veracity, and not because cross-examination may roam anywhere. It is proper because the recent punishment gives the jury a legally relevant reason to evaluate whether Hannah had a motive to accuse Timothy falsely.
