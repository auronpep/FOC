# BarMatrix CQ14908 — Risk of Loss and Equitable Conversion

## Source Metadata

- **BarMatrix Q#:** 14908
- **MBE subject:** Real Property
- **Subtopic:** Contracts for Land
- **Outline code:** 85030400
- **Outline:** Risk of Loss and Equitable Conversion
- **Correct answer:** **D**

## Condensed Question

A buyer validly contracted in writing to buy improved land. The contract had **no contingencies** and was **silent** about risk of loss and insurance duties. After signing, the seller canceled her insurance; the buyer did not know and did not buy insurance. Three weeks before closing, lightning burned the building to the ground. No applicable statute governs. Buyer seeks to cancel and recover earnest money. Seller says risk passed to buyer.

**If seller prevails, what is the most likely explanation?**

## Answer

**D. Upon execution of the contract, the buyer became the equitable owner of the land under the doctrine of equitable conversion.**

## Issue

Who bears the risk of casualty loss to improvements on land between a valid land-sale contract and closing when the contract is silent, no statute applies, and neither party has a contractual duty to carry insurance?

## Rule

Under the traditional **equitable conversion** rule for land-sale contracts:

- Once the parties sign a valid, enforceable contract for sale of real property, the buyer is treated in equity as the owner of the land.
- The seller retains legal title mainly as security for payment of the purchase price.
- Because the buyer is treated as the equitable owner, the buyer bears the risk of loss if the property is damaged or destroyed before closing, unless the contract provides otherwise or a statute changes the default rule.

Important limits:

- Jurisdictions differ. Some statutes, including Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act-style rules, place risk on the seller until legal title or possession passes to the buyer.
- This question says **there is no applicable statute**, so the traditional equitable-conversion rule controls.
- The seller’s failure to maintain insurance does not itself shift risk.
- Neither party has a duty to carry insurance unless the contract or statute imposes one.
- Both buyer and seller may have insurable interests after signing: the seller retains legal title/security interest, and the buyer holds equitable title.

## Application

The buyer had a valid written contract with no contingencies. The contract did not allocate risk of loss and did not require either party to maintain insurance. The building burned after signing but before closing, and no statute applies.

Because the jurisdiction in this hypothetical is one that lets the seller prevail, it is applying the traditional equitable-conversion rule: the buyer became the equitable owner at contract formation and therefore bore the risk of fire loss. The buyer must perform and cannot cancel solely because the building was destroyed.

The seller’s insurance cancellation is not the legal basis for shifting risk. The key event is contract formation, which triggered equitable conversion.

## Why D Is Correct

**D** correctly identifies the doctrine that explains the seller’s victory: **equitable conversion**. The buyer became the equitable owner when the contract was executed, so the buyer bore the risk of loss before closing under the traditional rule.

## Wrong Answer Forensics

### A — Insurable interest

**Wrong.** After a land-sale contract is signed, both parties can have insurable interests. The seller still holds legal title/security for the price, and the buyer holds equitable title. The buyer’s ability to insure is not the reason the buyer bears the loss.

**Trap:** Confuses “insurable interest” with “risk of loss.” Insurance availability does not decide who bears casualty risk unless the contract or statute says so.

### B — Constructive possession / duty to protect

**Wrong.** The buyer’s equitable ownership does not necessarily mean the buyer had constructive possession, and there is no affirmative duty to protect against fire absent a contract term or statute. The contract was silent on insurance and risk.

**Trap:** Adds a possession/duty fact that the question does not support.

### C — Seller’s insurance cancellation

**Wrong.** The seller’s cancellation of casualty insurance does not transfer risk of loss to the buyer. Risk allocation comes from the contract, statute, or equitable-conversion doctrine, not from the seller’s insurance decision.

**Trap:** Treats insurance as risk allocation. Insurance is a practical protection, but it is not the default legal rule for land casualty loss.

### D — Equitable conversion

**Correct.** The buyer became the equitable owner upon execution of the contract. In a jurisdiction following the traditional rule and with no statute to the contrary, the buyer bears the risk of loss before closing.

## Research Notes

- Quimbee’s equitable-conversion definition states that after an enforceable real-property sale contract is executed, the buyer is regarded as the equitable owner and the seller has the equitable right to be paid the purchase price.
- New York’s Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act, N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 5-1311, illustrates the statutory counter-rule: if neither legal title nor possession has transferred, the vendor generally cannot enforce the contract after material destruction without purchaser fault. This question excludes that statutory regime by saying there is no applicable statute.
- The traditional common-law rule is often traced to **Paine v. Meller**, 6 Ves. Jun. 349, 31 Eng. Rep. 1088 (Ch. 1801), under which the purchaser bore the risk after an enforceable land-sale contract because equitable conversion had occurred.
