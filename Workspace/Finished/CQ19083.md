## 6. Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** The good-faith exception ( *Leon*) has four exceptions. One: the affidavit is "bare bones" — so lacking in probable cause facts that no reasonable officer would rely on the warrant. Here, the affidavit said only "I believe stolen tools are inside based on training and experience." No informant details. No surveillance. No controlled buy. No dates. No facts connecting stolen goods to the workshop. That's the definition of bare bones. No reasonable officer could rely on this warrant. Good faith fails → suppress.

## 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**New A (original D): "No, because the good-faith exception never applies to property crimes"** — Fabricated rule. Good faith applies regardless of the offense category. It depends on the quality of the affidavit and the reasonableness of reliance, not whether the case involves drugs, property crimes, or anything else.

**New B (original A — DOMINANT TRAP): "Yes, because a magistrate issued the warrant"** — Half-truth. A magistrate's issuance is generally the trigger for good-faith protection. But it's not absolute. The magistrate's signature is necessary but not sufficient.

**New C (original B): "Yes, because the officer subjectively believed stolen tools were inside"** — Flat misstatement. Good faith is an *objective* standard. The question is whether a *reasonable* officer could rely on the warrant, not whether this particular officer subjectively believed. A conclusory belief without facts doesn't meet the objective standard.

**New D (original C — CORRECT):** Explained above.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule:** Under *United States v. Leon*, 468 U.S. 897 (1984), the exclusionary rule's good-faith exception does not apply when the warrant affidavit is "bare bones" — that is, when the affidavit is "so lacking in indicia of probable cause as to render official belief in its existence entirely unreasonable." A conclusory affidavit that states only the officer's belief without providing underlying facts (informant details, surveillance, controlled buys, dates, or connection to the place to be searched) is bare bones. No reasonable officer could rely on such a warrant, and the good-faith exception fails.

**Authority:**
- **United States v. Leon, 468 U.S. 897 (1984):** Established the good-faith exception to the exclusionary rule. Identified four exceptions, including when "the magistrate or judge in issuing a warrant was misled by information in an affidavit that the affiant knew was false or would have known was false except for his reckless disregard of the truth" and when "the warrant [is] based on an affidavit 'so lacking in indicia of probable cause as to render official belief in its existence entirely unreasonable'" (bare-bones exception).
- **Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 (1983):** Established the totality-of-circumstances test for probable cause. A bare-bones affidavit fails this test because there are no circumstances to weigh.
