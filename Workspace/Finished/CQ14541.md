# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question

Mary ran a private Bible-study retreat center that offered weekly livestream classes. She contracted with Peter's Audio Shop to maintain the center's cameras, microphones, and soundboard for one year at a fixed monthly fee. The contract said nothing about assignment or delegation by either side.

Three months later, Peter sold the maintenance side of his business to Lydia Signal Care, an experienced and well-financed audiovisual service company. The sale agreement's only provision about Mary's contract stated that Peter "hereby assigns all audiovisual maintenance contracts to Lydia Signal Care." Lydia Signal Care then performed the monthly maintenance required by Mary's contract, but did the work defectively and disrupted several paid online classes.

Whom can Mary sue for damages arising from Lydia Signal Care's defective performance?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

After a service provider transfers a service contract to a new provider who performs badly, and the customer never releases the first provider, may the customer sue either provider?

## 3. Final Answer Choices

A. Either Peter's Audio Shop or Lydia Signal Care, because each can enforce Mary's monthly payment promise, so mutuality of remedy makes each answerable for the defective work.

B. Lydia Signal Care only, because it is experienced and financially responsible enough to provide audiovisual service.

C. Either Peter's Audio Shop or Lydia Signal Care, because Mary did not release Peter's Audio Shop and Mary is an intended beneficiary of Lydia Signal Care's promise to take over Peter's maintenance contracts.

D. Peter's Audio Shop only, because Lydia Signal Care made no promise directly to Mary.

## 5. Correct Answer

**C** (original key: B).

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Peter's Audio Shop delegated the maintenance work, but Mary never agreed to release Peter from the original service contract. A delegation does not discharge the delegating obligor unless the obligee agrees otherwise. Lydia Signal Care also stepped into the performance role by accepting and performing the transferred maintenance contracts, and Mary was the person the transferred performance was meant to benefit. Mary can therefore sue Peter on the original duty and Lydia Signal Care as an intended beneficiary of Lydia's assumed performance duty.

Gold Key: Delegation moves performance; it does not erase the delegator unless there is a novation. When the delegatee assumes the duty, the obligee can enforce that promise as an intended beneficiary.

Silver Key: When two choices name the same defendants, do not stop at the party list. The because-clause still has to use the correct legal bridge.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the most attractive move: it names the same two defendants as the credited answer. The breaker is the reason. Mutuality of remedy is not why Mary can sue both. The right bridge is no novation for Peter and intended-beneficiary enforcement against Lydia Signal Care. Silver Key: same defendant list is not enough when the answer choice includes a legal reason.

B makes Lydia Signal Care's competence feel like a clean substitution. The breaker is that being qualified and well financed does not release Peter. Only Mary's agreement to a novation or other release would discharge Peter's original duty.

D leans on the lack of a direct promise from Lydia Signal Care to Mary. The breaker is the Gold Key: an intended beneficiary does not need a direct promise made to her. Lydia's promise to Peter to take over the maintenance contracts was aimed at performance for Mary.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The governing law is common-law contracts, specifically delegation of duties, novation, and intended-beneficiary enforcement. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: the original service provider remains liable because there was no novation, and the customer can also sue the assuming delegatee as an intended beneficiary of the assumption agreement.

Authority check:

- Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 318(3) provides that delegation or an assumption contract does not discharge the delegating obligor unless the obligee agrees otherwise. Source checked: https://advance.lexis.com/open/document/openwebdocview/-318-Delegation-of-Performance-of-Duty/
- Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 302 defines intended beneficiaries, and section 304 states that a promise creates a duty to an intended beneficiary who may enforce it. Sources checked: https://advance.lexis.com/open/document/openwebdocview/-302-Intended-and-Incidental-Beneficiaries/ and https://matthewminer.name/law/outlines/1L/2nd%2BSemester/LAW%2B506-002%2B%E2%80%93%2BContracts%2BII/R2C%2B%C2%A7%2B304
- Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 279 explains that a substituted contract discharges the original duty, and when a new party is brought in the transaction is a novation under section 280. Source checked: https://advance.lexis.com/open/document/openwebdocview/-279-Substituted-Contract/

No researched authority contradicted the supplied key. The original contract was silent on delegation, the work was routine equipment maintenance, and Mary did not release Peter. Lydia Signal Care's actual performance confirms the assumption of the maintenance duty. That supports the same answer: either party may be sued.
