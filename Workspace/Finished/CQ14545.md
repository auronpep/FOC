Professor Lydia teaches a senior seminar called "Coins of Scripture" at Covenant Christian College. The seminar uses authentic ancient coins as teaching tools for understanding economic imagery in the Gospels, including the temple tax and the thirty pieces of silver. On December 1, she contracted with Silas, a curriculum resources dealer who also privately sources rare biblical-era artifacts for Christian educators and small colleges, to sell her one authentic Tyrian shekel of the type used in the first century for the temple tax for $12,000, delivery and payment to occur on the next March 1.

To fulfill that contract, and without Professor Lydia's knowledge, Silas contracted on January 1 to purchase for $10,000 a specimen of that type of coin from Barnabas, a retired missionary and private collector of first-century Judean coins who occasionally sells individual pieces from his personal collection to support short-term mission teams, delivery and payment to occur on February 1. The market price of such coins had unexpectedly fallen to $8,000 by February 1, when Barnabas tendered the coin and Silas repudiated. On February 25, the market in such coins suddenly reversed and had stabilized at $12,000 on March 1. Silas, however, had failed to obtain a specimen of the coin and repudiated his agreement with Professor Lydia when she tendered the $12,000 agreed price on March 1.

Later that day, after learning by chance of Silas's dealing with Professor Lydia, Barnabas telephoned Lydia and said: "Listen, Silas probably owes me at least $2,000 in damages for refusing wrongfully to buy my coin for $10,000 on February 1 when the market was down to $8,000. But I'm in good shape in view of the market's recovery since then, and I think you ought to get after the so-and-so."

If Professor Lydia immediately sues Silas for his breach of the Silas-Barnabas contract, which of the following will the court probably decide?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is the best answer. Silas wins. **Gold Key:** Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 302, a third party is an intended beneficiary with enforcement rights only if recognition of a right to performance in the beneficiary is appropriate to effectuate the intention of the parties and either (a) performance will satisfy an obligation of the promisee to pay money to the beneficiary or (b) the circumstances indicate that the promisee intends to give the beneficiary the benefit of the promised performance. An incidental beneficiary has no rights. **Silver Key:** The Silas-Barnabas sourcing contract was made solely to allow Silas to fulfill his separate obligation to Lydia; nothing in the facts shows that Barnabas and Silas manifested an intention to give Lydia direct enforcement rights against Barnabas or to treat her as anything other than an incidental beneficiary of their private deal.

The contract between Barnabas and Silas had nothing to do with or anticipate Lydia's interest in the specific coin for her seminar; any future benefit she would have received was only incidental, preventing her from having contractual rights to enforce that sourcing agreement.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Professor Lydia wins, because she was an intended beneficiary of the Silas-Barnabas contract, under which damages for Silas's repudiation became fixed on February 1.**

This is the dominant trap. It sells the attractive story that the ultimate "end user" (the Christian college professor building a teaching collection) must have been intended to benefit. **Gold Key:** The sourcing contract was a private commercial arrangement between Silas and Barnabas to allow Silas to acquire inventory for resale or fulfillment. The facts contain no manifestation by the promisee (Silas) of an intention to confer direct enforcement rights on Lydia against Barnabas. The call asks about enforcement of the Silas-Barnabas contract; merely being the person who ultimately wanted the coin does not convert an incidental beneficiary into an intended one. The timing language is a secondary distractor.

**B. Silas wins, because the telephone suggestion from Barnabas to Professor Lydia constituted an effective assignment of Barnabas's claim for damages against Silas.**

This sells the "the guy on the phone said you should sue him" fairness intuition. **Gold Key:** An effective assignment requires a manifested intention by the assignor to transfer the right immediately and completely. Barnabas's casual, after-the-fact suggestion ("I think you ought to get after the so-and-so") is not a clear present transfer of his claim. It is at most an invitation or encouragement, not an assignment.

**C. Silas wins, because as of March 1 neither Barnabas nor Professor Lydia had sustained any damage from Silas's repudiation of both contracts.**

This sells a timing-and-recovery story. **Gold Key:** Even assuming for argument that Lydia had rights (she does not), under UCC § 2-713 the buyer's damages for non-delivery or repudiation are measured by the difference between the market price at the time the buyer learned of the breach and the contract price (plus incidental and consequential damages, less expenses saved). The market drop and repudiation on February 1 would fix any damages then; later recovery does not erase the breach or the damage measure. More fundamentally, Lydia has no rights under the sourcing contract at all.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

**Pass-1 Step 1 — tested black-letter rule.** A third party is an intended beneficiary with the right to enforce a contract only when the promisee manifests an intention, judged by the Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 302 test, to give that third party direct rights against the promisor. Performance of the promise must satisfy an obligation of the promisee to the beneficiary or the circumstances must indicate that the promisee intends to give the beneficiary the benefit of the promised performance. An incidental beneficiary — one who merely stands to benefit as a side effect of the contract — has no enforcement rights.

The source row’s official explanation states the same rule: the contract between the hoarder and the broker had nothing to do with or anticipate the collector's interest; any benefit was incidental only.

Legal research confirms the rule. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 302 (Intended and Incidental Beneficiaries) is the controlling modern statement. The classic archetype of an intended creditor beneficiary is Lawrence v. Fox, 20 N.Y. 268 (1859), where the promisee owed a debt to the third party and the contract was made to satisfy that debt. Here, Silas owed Lydia a coin under a separate contract; the Barnabas-Silas contract was not made to satisfy any obligation Silas owed to Barnabas or to confer enforcement rights on Lydia. Modern cases and hornbooks emphasize that intent must be manifested in the contract or circumstances; the mere fact that the ultimate purchaser or end user will benefit is insufficient for intended-beneficiary status. See also Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 304 (Creation of Duty to Beneficiary).

For the damages-timing distractor (choice C in the variant), UCC § 2-713(1) is clear: the measure of damages for non-delivery or repudiation by the seller is the difference between the market price at the time when the buyer learned of the breach and the contract price (plus incidental and consequential damages, less expenses saved). Market recovery after the buyer learns of the breach does not eliminate the damage claim.

**Pass-1 Step 2 — invariant layer preserved.** The legal issue remains third-party beneficiary status (intended vs incidental) in a sourcing contract made to fulfill a separate obligation to the ultimate purchaser. The credited legal substance remains that the ultimate purchaser is only an incidental beneficiary with no enforcement rights against the remote supplier. The dominant trap remains the "intended beneficiary" classification on these facts. The array remains a 2×2 outcome-and-reason set: buyer wins on intended-beneficiary-plus-timing reasons versus dealer wins on incidental-beneficiary or assignment or no-damage reasons.

## distilled_core_question

A buyer contracts with a dealer to purchase a specific rare coin for a Christian college teaching collection. The dealer, to fulfill, contracts with a private collector to source the coin. The dealer repudiates both contracts. The private collector later suggests the buyer "get after" the dealer. Does the buyer have standing to sue the dealer for breach of the dealer-collector sourcing contract?

## call_and_prediction

**Call:** Can Professor Lydia immediately sue Silas for breach of the Silas-Barnabas sourcing contract?

**Prediction:** Lydia loses unless she is an intended beneficiary or assignee of that contract. The facts show a private sourcing deal made so Silas could fulfill his separate obligation to Lydia. They do not show manifestation of intent to give Lydia direct rights against Barnabas.

## governing_c3_lane

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. Professor Lydia wins, because she was an intended beneficiary of the Silas-Barnabas contract, under which damages for Silas's repudiation became fixed on February 1.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice treats the fact that Lydia ultimately wanted the coin for her seminar as proof that she was an intended beneficiary of the remote sourcing contract.
2. **Student label:** End-user benefit treated as intended-beneficiary status.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Lydia wins only if Silas and Barnabas manifested an intention to give her direct enforcement rights against Barnabas under their sourcing contract."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 302, the mere fact that performance of a contract will benefit a third party does not make that third party an intended beneficiary with enforcement rights. The promisee (Silas) must have manifested an intention to confer direct rights on Lydia against the promisor (Barnabas). The sourcing contract was made to allow Silas to fulfill his own separate obligation to Lydia; that is the classic incidental-beneficiary pattern.

### B. Silas wins, because the telephone suggestion from Barnabas to Professor Lydia constituted an effective assignment of Barnabas's claim for damages against Silas.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice takes the after-the-fact phone encouragement and treats it as a completed present transfer of the claim.
2. **Student label:** Casual suggestion treated as effective assignment.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Barnabas's suggestion might be encouragement or an invitation to negotiate, but it is not a manifested present intention to transfer the entire claim immediately and completely."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** An effective assignment requires a clear manifestation by the assignor of an intention to make a present transfer of the right. Barnabas's casual, post-repudiation suggestion ("I think you ought to get after the so-and-so") lacks the necessary present-transfer language and completeness.

### C. Silas wins, because as of March 1 neither Barnabas nor Professor Lydia had sustained any damage from Silas's repudiation of both contracts.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice focuses on the later market recovery and concludes there is "no damage."
2. **Student label:** Market recovery erases damages.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Even if Lydia had rights under the sourcing contract (she does not), damages would be measured at the time she learned of the breach under UCC § 2-713, not at the original delivery date."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** UCC § 2-713(1) measures the buyer's damages for non-delivery or repudiation by the difference between the market price at the time the buyer learned of the breach and the contract price. The February 1 repudiation when the market was $8,000 would fix any damages claim then. Later recovery does not erase the breach. More fundamentally, Lydia has no rights under the sourcing contract at all.

### D. Silas wins, because Professor Lydia, if a beneficiary at all of the Silas-Barnabas contract, was only an incidental beneficiary.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This answer directly addresses the enforcement call and applies the correct classification.
2. **Student label:** True and responsive.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This is already true and responsive.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** This is the credited rule and result under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 302 on these facts.
