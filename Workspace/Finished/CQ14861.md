### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Attorney-client privilege normally protects confidential legal consultations, but the crime-fraud exception removes protection when the client seeks legal services to help commit or plan a future crime or fraud. Lydia had already been served with a subpoena for the ledgers, and the question to the lawyer concerned moving those ledgers to a foreign vault. Gold Key: the exception tracks the client's purpose, not the lawyer's awareness. Silver Key: once A and B both say not privileged, the clash is only whether attorney knowledge is required; the stronger answer rejects that added condition.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is the dominant trap because it reaches the right result and adds a familiar knowledge condition. The breaker is that the crime-fraud exception does not require the attorney to know the client's illegal purpose. Gold Key: client-purpose controls; lawyer ignorance does not restore privilege.

C is attractive because Luxembourg looks like a jurisdictional hook. The privilege question does not turn on where the records would be stored. The location fact may matter to the story of concealment, but it does not make a communication privileged.

D is attractive because lawyers ordinarily must keep client confidences. That general confidentiality instinct is not the evidentiary privilege answer when the client seeks help planning or carrying out a crime or fraud. The exception defeats the privilege for the communication tied to the planned wrongdoing.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is unchanged: in a federal criminal case, attorney-client privilege is governed by federal common law under FRE 501, and the common-law crime-fraud exception removes protection for communications made to obtain legal assistance in committing, planning, or concealing a crime or fraud. FRE 501 provides that common law, as interpreted by federal courts in light of reason and experience, governs privilege unless a higher source provides otherwise: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_501.

United States v. Zolin, 491 U.S. 554 (1989), recognizes the attorney-client privilege under federal law but confirms that the crime-fraud exception prevents the seal of secrecy from extending to communications made for advice in committing a crime or fraud: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/491/554.

The attorney-knowledge point is also supported by federal appellate authority. In re Grand Jury Proceedings, 87 F.3d 377 (9th Cir. 1996), states that the crime-fraud exception can apply even if the attorney is unaware of the crime and takes no step that actually furthers it; the relevant focus is the client's purpose and the communication's relationship to the illegality: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/87/377/610776/.

Because Lydia sought advice about moving subpoenaed ledgers after the subpoena was served, the communication was sought to aid the charged concealment. The testimony is therefore not privileged whether or not the attorney knew Lydia's illegal purpose. The supplied official key is consistent with the researched rule.
