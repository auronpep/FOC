### 1. Final Question

Mary owned a privately held hill-country retreat property used for small Christian study weekends. Her title papers made it appear that the property included an 18-acre tract called Mercy Field and an adjoining 4-acre tract called Bell Meadow.

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** Stephen executed a general warranty deed to Hannah that purported to convey Bell Meadow even though Stephen did not yet hold title to Bell Meadow. When Paul later deeded Bell Meadow to Stephen, Stephen acquired the very title he had previously purported to convey. Under after-acquired title, also called estoppel by deed, Stephen is estopped from asserting that newly acquired title against Hannah. The title passes to Hannah by operation of law.

**Gold Key:** When a grantor purports by warranty deed to convey land he does not own and later acquires that title, the later-acquired title inures to the benefit of the grantee. The grantor cannot keep the title against the person to whom he already gave the warranty deed.

**Silver Key:** In a deed-chain item, find the last defective grantor who deeded the disputed parcel to the claimant. Upstream warranties and repair efforts may explain the story, but the claimant's ownership argument must run through that grantor's own deed.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A** is attractive because Stephen may well have a warranty-based claim against Mary. Mary purported to convey Bell Meadow to Stephen when she did not hold title, so Stephen's rights against Mary look legally important. The breaker is the call: Hannah needs an argument that she owns Bell Meadow as against Stephen. Stephen's possible damages or warranty remedy against Mary does not by itself transfer title to Hannah.

**C** is the dominant trap. It sounds strong because Mary's payment to Paul is the event that fixed the missing title in Stephen's hands, and it was connected to Mary's covenant problem. But Mary's motive for curing the defect is not Hannah's title theory. Hannah wins because Stephen's own prior warranty deed to Hannah estops Stephen from keeping the title after he later receives it.

**D** is attractive because Stephen's deed described Bell Meadow, so his intent to include Bell Meadow is easy to infer. The breaker is that intent alone did not convey Bell Meadow on the day Stephen signed the deed, because Stephen did not then own Bell Meadow. The operative rule is not bare intent; it is the later operation of after-acquired title once Stephen received title from Paul.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule is after-acquired title through estoppel by deed. When a grantor gives a deed, especially a warranty deed, purporting to convey an estate the grantor does not own, and the grantor later acquires that estate, the grantor is estopped from denying the earlier conveyance as against the grantee and those in privity. The later-acquired title passes to the grantee by operation of law.

The governing authority floor is satisfied by **Van Rensselaer v. Kearney**, 52 U.S. 297 (1850). The Supreme Court describes the common-law rule this way: "The estoppel works upon the estate and binds an after-acquired title as between parties and privies." Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/52/297/

Applied here, Stephen is the grantor whose warranty deed to Hannah purported to convey Bell Meadow before Stephen owned it. Paul then conveyed Bell Meadow to Stephen. That later-acquired title is the exact title Stephen had already purported to convey to Hannah. Stephen is estopped from asserting it against Hannah, so Hannah's most effective argument is choice B.

The legal issue, tested rule, credited outcome, and trap structure are unchanged. The only changes are surface facts, names, setting, numbers, and choice positions.

## crossover_intersection_map

- Civil procedure crossover: declaratory relief posture does not change the deed rule.
- Evidence crossover: none.

## review_truth
