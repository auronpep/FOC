## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Martha ended a private Christmas hymn-recording project with Peter but kept a carved olivewood music box that Peter had given her during the project. Peter insisted the box should come back to him. After midnight, while Martha was away, Peter entered Martha's townhouse to look for the box. A neighbor saw Peter go inside and called the police. Which of the following, if proved, would serve as Peter's best defense to the burglary charge?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

After entering another person's dwelling at night to recover disputed personal property, what fact best defeats common-law burglary?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is the best defense. Common-law burglary requires a breaking and entering of another person's dwelling at night with intent to commit a felony inside, usually larceny. Gold Key: if the intended felony is larceny, a good-faith claim of right to the property defeats the felonious taking intent, even if the belief is unreasonable. Peter's belief that the music box was legally his blocks the larceny intent that burglary needs.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells the idea that an empty house makes the entry less burglarious. Occupancy is not the hinge; common-law burglary focuses on a dwelling at night, not whether the resident was home at that moment.

B is the dominant trap because it makes the entry look orderly instead of forceful. Silver Key: do not let a neat entry method answer the wrong question; a spare key does not supply consent or defeat the intent-to-commit-felony element.

C is attractive because Peter left with nothing, so the event feels incomplete. Burglary is complete at entry with the required intent; success at taking the property is not required.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. At common law, burglary is breaking and entering the dwelling of another at night with intent to commit a felony inside. Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575 (1990), identifies the traditional common-law definition as breaking and entering a dwelling at night with intent to commit a felony, and also treats intent to commit a crime as a core burglary element. https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-supreme-court/495/575.html

The intended felony here is larceny. Larceny requires a felonious intent to steal, so a bona fide claim of right negates that intent. People v. Navarro, 99 Cal. App. 3d Supp. 1 (1979), explains that a person who takes property in a good-faith belief of legal entitlement lacks larceny intent even if the belief is unreasonable. https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/99/supp1.html

Because D negates the intent for the underlying larceny, it is the best defense to burglary. The other facts do not defeat a required element.
