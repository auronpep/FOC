## 1. Final Question

Peter and his older brother Daniel grew up together in the town of Cedarville, where Peter owned a small cottage near the lake. On April 1, Peter called his sister Ruth on the phone and told her that he had been thinking about the future. Ruth promised to do so, and on April 14, Peter deeded the cottage to Ruth. The cottage was worth $120,000.

On April 12, Ruth called Daniel and told him that Peter had deeded her the cottage, but she did not mention her promise to let him live there.

On April 14, Aunt Lydia wrote to Daniel: "I don't think your brother or I have much longer, and we both miss you. If you come live here in Cedarville for as long as either of us is alive, I will leave you everything I own."

On April 16, Daniel called Ruth about Lydia's letter. He said he was thinking of moving back to Cedarville, and that if Ruth promised to let him stay in the cottage until Lydia died, he would give Ruth half of whatever he inherited from Lydia. Ruth agreed. Daniel immediately wrote to Lydia saying he was coming. Lydia received the letter on April 19.

Daniel arranged his move at a cost of $1,200. Peter and Lydia both died on April 21. Daniel didn't learn of their deaths until he arrived in Cedarville on April 23. Ruth then refused to let him move into the cottage.

Daniel sued Ruth for damages from her refusal. Ruth's only defense was that her promises were not in writing.

**Which of the following is Ruth's most effective argument in support of her position?**

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**C is correct.** The Statute of Frauds requires that any promise creating an interest in land be in writing and signed by the party to be charged. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 125. A promise to allow someone to live in a house for life — or for as long as they wish — creates a leasehold or life-estate interest in land. Both of Ruth's promises (to Lydia for life, to Daniel conditionally) are promises to create interests in land. Neither was in writing. This is Ruth's strongest defense because the interest-in-land provision of the Statute of Frauds is absolute on its face — if the promise creates an interest in land, it must be in writing, full stop.

**Gold Key:** A leasehold interest — including a right to occupy property for life or at will — is an "interest in land" under the Statute of Frauds. Any promise to create such an interest must be in writing, regardless of whether the promise could theoretically be performed within one year.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original B):** This choice invokes the one-year rule of the Statute of Frauds, which requires a writing when a contract by its terms cannot be performed within one year from formation. The attraction is that the one-year rule *sounds* like a classic SoF trap. But both of Ruth's promises *could* have been performed within a year — if Daniel or Lydia had died within that period, the promises would have been fully performed. Because performance within a year was possible, the one-year rule does not apply. This choice overstates the reach of the one-year provision.

**B (original D — DOMINANT TRAP):** This is the most tempting wrong answer. It argues that Ruth received no consideration from her brother. Students are drawn to this because they associate unenforceability with lack of consideration. But consideration is not relevant to the Statute of Frauds analysis. Moreover, the choice is factually wrong: Peter's conveyance of the cottage to Ruth was consideration for her promise to him, and Daniel's promise to share his inheritance was consideration for Ruth's promise to him. Even if there were no consideration, that would not support a Statute of Frauds defense — it would be a formation defense, which Ruth did not raise. **Gold Key:** The Statute of Frauds is a writing requirement, not a consideration requirement. Consideration and the Statute of Frauds are independent doctrines.

**D (original C):** This choice invokes the UCC's $500 threshold for contracts for the sale of goods. Students who have the UCC rule memorized may reflexively apply it here. But a cottage is real property, not a "good," and the UCC Statute of Frauds (UCC § 2-201) applies only to sales of goods. The common-law Statute of Frauds governs interests in real property. The value of the property is irrelevant under the real-property provision — any interest in land, regardless of value, triggers the writing requirement.

## 9. Black-Letter Legal Verification

**Governing rule:** The Statute of Frauds requires that certain categories of contracts be evidenced by a writing signed by the party to be charged. One such category is "a contract for the sale of an interest in land." Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 125(1) ("A promise to transfer to any person any interest in land is within the Statute of Frauds."). This includes leaseholds and life estates — any possessory interest in real property. *See also* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 125 cmt. a (the term "interest in land" includes "a lease, a life estate, a remainder, or any other interest in real property").

**Application:** Ruth's promise to let Lydia live in the cottage for life is a promise to create a life-estate interest in land. Ruth's promise to let Daniel live there if he moved back is a promise to create a leasehold or possessory interest. Both fall squarely within the interest-in-land provision. Neither was in writing. Ruth's defense under this provision is strong.

**One-year rule (why B/A is wrong):** The one-year rule applies only when the contract *by its terms* cannot be performed within one year. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 130. If performance within a year is theoretically possible, the rule does not apply. Here, both promises could have been fully performed within a year if the relevant person died during that period.

**Consideration (why D/B is wrong):** The Statute of Frauds is independent of consideration. A promise can be supported by consideration and still fail for lack of writing, or lack consideration and still be enforceable if the writing requirement is satisfied. Moreover, consideration *was* present here.

**UCC $500 (why C/D is wrong):** UCC § 2-201 applies to sales of goods, not real property. A house or cottage is realty.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 90, 125, 130 (American Law Institute, 1981). The original credited answer (A → now C) is confirmed correct. No doctrinal-currency issues. No key disagreement. Verdict: PASS.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "It was possible that the promise... would not be performed within a year."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The one-year rule only applies when the contract *cannot possibly* be performed within one year. Here, both promises *could* have been performed within a year — if Daniel or Lydia had died, the promises would be fully performed. So the one-year rule doesn't apply.
2. **Student label:** One-year rule — doesn't apply because performance within a year was possible.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "The promises by their terms could not be performed within one year because Lydia's life expectancy exceeded one year." (But this isn't in the facts.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 130. The one-year provision requires that performance within a year be impossible. Not the case here.

**Choice B: "She received no consideration from her brother for either promise."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Two problems. First, consideration *was* present — Peter's deed was consideration for Ruth's promise to him, and Daniel's promise to share his inheritance was consideration to Ruth. Second, the Statute of Frauds is about *writing*, not consideration. Even if there were no consideration, that wouldn't support a SoF defense.
2. **Student label:** Consideration argument — factually wrong and doctrinally irrelevant to SoF.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "The promises were not supported by consideration, and therefore no contract was formed." (But this isn't the defense Ruth raised, and consideration was present.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Consideration and the Statute of Frauds are independent doctrines. Consideration was present here. **Gold Key:** The Statute of Frauds is a writing requirement, not a consideration requirement.

**Choice C: "Her promises... were promises to create an interest in land."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** A promise to let someone live in a house for life creates a life estate or leasehold — that's an interest in land. The Statute of Frauds says interests in land must be in writing. Neither promise was written. This is a direct hit.
2. **Student label:** Interest in land — the strongest SoF argument.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** Exactly this. The promise to create a life-estate/leasehold interest in land must be in writing.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 125(1). **Gold Key:** A leasehold interest — including a right to occupy property for life or at will — is an "interest in land" under the Statute of Frauds.

**Choice D: "The cottage had a value in excess of $500."**
