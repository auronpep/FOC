### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Mary owed only limited duties to Stephen. Under traditional entrant categories, an unknown trespasser — one whose presence the landowner has not discovered — is owed a duty only to refrain from willful and wanton conduct (and, in some modern formulations, to warn of known artificial conditions that create an unreasonable risk of death or serious bodily injury). The duty does not include the invitee inspection duty (which requires reasonable inspections and remediation of known or discoverable hazards), nor a broad duty to warn of every natural condition on remote land, nor strict liability. The injury here came from a naturally fallen branch — a paradigmatic natural condition — and Mary had no knowledge of Stephen's presence. **Gold Key:** the traditional categories track what the landowner *knows* and what the hazard *is*; an unknown trespasser injured by a natural condition receives only the floor-level duty.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. The full invitee inspection duty for all hazards on the land.**

This is the dominant trap. The student overgeneralizes: "someone came onto the land, so the owner owes the highest duty." The breaker is the **Gold Key**: the invitee inspection duty runs only to invitees, defined as entrants whom the owner invites or holds out the land to as a member of the public. No-entry signs are precisely the inverse — the owner has affirmatively excluded the public. Stephen is a trespasser, not an invitee, and the limited-trespasser duty applies, not the invitee inspection duty.

**C. A duty to warn of every natural condition on remote land.**

This trap conflates the artificial/natural condition distinction with a broad warn-of-everything rule. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: the traditional duty to unknown trespassers is narrow and does not include a duty to warn of every natural condition on remote land. (Even under the modern artificial-condition rule that may require warnings to discovered trespassers, "every natural condition" is not the standard, and Stephen was unknown.)

**D. Strict liability because no-entry signs prove the owner expected injury.**

This is the strict-liability mislabel. The student reads "no-entry signs" as evidence of expected injury and infers strict liability. The breaker is the foundational rule: premises liability to trespassers is a fault-based regime, not strict liability. The presence of warning signs is evidence of the owner's reasonable care, not a basis for strict liability.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17264, a Torts item involving a hiker who ignored no-entry signs, crossed onto a remote parcel, and was injured by a natural condition (a naturally fallen branch on an unmaintained footpath). The official key is C: only limited duties, because the hiker was an unknown trespasser injured by a natural condition.

The traditional entrant categories in American premises liability are: invitee (highest duty — reasonable inspections and warnings of known or discoverable hazards), licensee (intermediate duty — warn of known dangerous conditions that the licensee is unlikely to discover), and trespasser (lowest duty — refrain from willful and wanton conduct). A small but well-established subset of jurisdictions applies a "discovered-trespasser" rule that requires the landowner to warn of known artificial conditions that create an unreasonable risk of death or serious bodily harm. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 333–339 sets out the basic structure; Prosser & Keeton, *Torts* § 57–60, surveys the variations across states. The Restatement (Third) of Torts: Premises Liability (2010, last revised 2023) collapses the categories into a single reasonable-care standard under the circumstances, but the question is explicitly framed under the "traditional entrant categories" test, so the older rule applies for purposes of this exam item.

On these facts, Stephen ignored no-entry signs and entered the parcel without Mary's knowledge. No-entry signs are affirmative evidence that Mary did not invite or hold out the parcel to the public; Stephen is therefore a trespasser, not an invitee or licensee. Mary did not know of Stephen's presence, so the discovered-trespasser rule is not triggered. The hazard — a naturally fallen branch on an unmaintained footpath — is a paradigmatic natural condition, not an artificial condition, and natural conditions on remote land do not give rise to a duty to warn under the traditional rule. The duty Mary owed is the limited one owed to unknown trespassers.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: only limited duties, because the hiker was an unknown trespasser injured by a natural condition.

**CUT**

- **B (full invitee inspection duty)** is cut on the entrant-category mislabel. **CLASH**

A and C survive to the final axis: is the duty limited (because of the unknown-trespasser + natural-condition combination) or is it broader (a duty to warn of every natural condition on remote land)? The single axis they fight on is the *scope* of any duty owed to an unknown trespasser.

**CALL**

The **Gold Key** resolves the axis. The traditional categories are narrow; an unknown trespasser is owed only the floor-level duty (refrain from willful and wanton conduct), and even the modern artificial-condition rule does not impose a duty to warn of every natural condition on remote land. Stephen was unknown; the branch was natural; the duty was limited. Residual answer: **A**. A student who picks C is over-extending the artificial/natural condition distinction; a student who picks B is misclassifying the entrant; a student who picks D is misstating the fault-based nature of the regime.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. Only limited duties, because the hiker was an unknown trespasser injured by a natural condition.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** No-entry signs make Stephen a trespasser; Mary did not know; the hazard was natural. Limited duty is the doctrinal answer.
2. **Student label:** True and responsive.
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Under Restatement (Second) of Torts § 333–339, an unknown trespasser is owed only the floor-level duty. The natural-condition and remote-land facts confirm the limited-duty rule.

### B. The full invitee inspection duty for all hazards on the land.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The student overgeneralizes "someone came onto the land, so the highest duty runs." The Gold Key flips: the invitee inspection duty runs only to invitees; no-entry signs are affirmative evidence the owner did not invite the public.
2. **Student label:** Hiker = invitee mislabel.
3. **True/responsive version:** "The full invitee inspection duty might run if the owner had held the land out to the public. No-entry signs are the inverse."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** An invitee is a member of the public invited onto the land or held out as such. No-entry signs show the owner did not invite the public. The entrant is a trespasser, not an invitee.

### C. A duty to warn of every natural condition on remote land.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The student over-extends the artificial/natural condition distinction into a warn-of-everything rule.
2. **Student label:** Warn-every-natural-condition mislabel.
3. **True/responsive version:** "A duty to warn of every natural condition on remote land is the broad version. The traditional duty is narrower; even the modern artificial-condition rule does not require warnings to unknown trespassers for every natural condition."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The traditional duty to unknown trespassers is narrow; "every natural condition" is not the standard, and Stephen was an unknown trespasser. The limited-duty rule applies.

### D. Strict liability because no-entry signs prove the owner expected injury.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The student reads warning signs as evidence of expected injury and infers strict liability. The Silver Key flips: warning signs are evidence of reasonable care, not a basis for strict liability.
2. **Student label:** Warning signs create strict liability.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Strict liability might apply in some other contexts (e.g., wild animals, ultrahazardous activities), but premises liability to trespassers is fault-based."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The traditional premises-liability regime imposes negligence-based duties, not strict liability. Warning signs are consistent with reasonable care, not a basis for strict liability.
