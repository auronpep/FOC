## 6. Right-Answer Explanation

**Gold Key: functional-equivalent interrogation.** Miranda interrogation is not limited to question marks. It includes police words or actions, other than normal arrest or custody words, that police should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response.

Lydia was under arrest and in custody, and the officers gave no Miranda warnings. The officer did not ask, "Where is the knife?" but he staged a warning about Ruth, a relative Lydia was known to worry about. Because the officers had already secured and searched the area, the safety comment was not a real emergency measure. It was a pressure move reasonably likely to make Lydia reveal the knife's location. Her statement is excluded from the prosecution's case-in-chief.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Tested rule:** Miranda bars the prosecution from using a suspect's statement in its case-in-chief when the statement stems from custodial interrogation without required warnings. Interrogation includes express questioning and its functional equivalent: police words or actions, apart from routine arrest/custody conduct, that police should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response.

**Authority checked:** Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), requires safeguards before custodial interrogation statements may be used in the prosecution's case-in-chief. Rhode Island v. Innis, 446 U.S. 291 (1980), supplies the functional-equivalent interrogation test and recognizes that known susceptibility can matter. New York v. Quarles, 467 U.S. 649 (1984), recognizes a narrow public-safety exception for questions reasonably prompted by a real safety concern. United States v. Patane, 542 U.S. 630 (2004), confirms that physical fruits of an unwarned but voluntary statement are a separate issue from suppressing the statement itself.

**Research sources checked:** Library of Congress U.S. Reports PDFs for Miranda, Innis, and Patane: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep384/usrep384436/usrep384436.pdf ; https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep446/usrep446291/usrep446291.pdf ; https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep542/usrep542630/usrep542630.pdf . Quarles checked at Justia Supreme Court text: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/467/649/ .

**Credited outcome:** The credited answer remains the same in legal substance. Lydia's statement is suppressed because the officer's staged safety comment was the functional equivalent of interrogation while she was in custody and unwarned. The false safety premise also keeps Quarles from changing the outcome because the officers knew the knife was not accessible to anyone.
