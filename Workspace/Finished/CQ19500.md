Peter was angry enough at Deacon John to want him dead. On Sunday morning, before the service, Peter crept up to the bell tower above the church entrance. ### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** The transferred-intent doctrine applies only when the defendant intends one victim and the *same crime* happens to a different victim. Peter intended to kill John — a crime against a person. What actually happened was destruction of property — a categorically different offense. The homicidal intent cannot supply the mental state for malicious destruction of property. The doctrine transfers intent from Victim A to Victim B within the *same* crime, not from one crime to an entirely different one.

**Gold Key:** Transferred intent is same-crime-only: the intended crime and the actual crime must be the *same offense* for the doctrine to transfer the defendant's mens rea from the intended victim to the unintended victim.

---

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap):** This is attractive because Peter clearly intended harm and harm did result — the window was destroyed. The trap is extending the transferred-intent doctrine beyond its limit. The doctrine does not say "any intent to harm transfers to whatever harm results." It transfers intent only when the *same crime* is committed against an unintended victim. Intent to kill (a crime against a person) does not become intent to destroy property (a crime against property). **Gold Key:** Transferred intent is same-crime-only — the doctrine would have killed this trap because the intended crime (murder) and the actual crime (property destruction) are different offenses.

**B:** This choice states something that is likely true — Peter probably can be charged with attempted murder of John for cutting the rope with intent to kill. But the question asks whether the *transferred-intent doctrine* supports a charge of *malicious destruction of property*. This choice answers a completely different legal question. It is factually plausible but not responsive to the call.

**D:** This is attractive to students who conflate "no completed harm to the intended victim" with "no crime at all." Peter fired a bullet — or here, cut a rope — at a specific person with intent to kill. That is attempted murder. The absence of a completed homicide does not eliminate criminal liability; attempt is a separate offense. This choice ignores attempt liability entirely.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Authority

**Rule:** The transferred-intent doctrine (also called "transferred malice" at common law) operates only when the defendant's intended crime and the actual crime are the *same offense*. The defendant's mens rea transfers from the intended victim to the actual victim within that single crime. It does not transfer across different crime categories (e.g., from a crime against a person to a crime against property).

**Authority:**
- *Wex Legal Encyclopedia*, "Transferred Intent," Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School: "Transferred intent is used when a defendant intends to harm one victim, but then unintentionally harms a second victim instead. In this case, the defendant's intent transfers from the intended victim to the actual victim and can be used to satisfy the mens rea element of the crime that the defendant is being charged with." (https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/transferred_intent)
- The doctrine is classically illustrated by *People v. Birger*, 25 Cal. App. 3d 984 (1972), and the classic hypothetical: D aims at A, misses, and hits B — D is guilty of battery against B under transferred intent because the *same crime* (battery) occurred against an unintended victim.
- The limitation is well-established: intent to commit Crime X does not transfer to supply mens rea for Crime Y. See *Rethinking the Transferred Intent Doctrine*, 30 Am. J. Crim. L. 149 (2003) (discussing the same-crime limitation as the majority rule).

**Verification:** The credited answer (B in original, C in variant) is correct. Transferred intent does not operate across different crime types. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original.

#### Remediation Card

**Card ID:** RC-19500-01
**Title:** Transferred Intent — Same Crime Only
**Signal:** Question involves intent to commit one crime that results in a different crime
**Student move:** Ask: Is the actual crime the SAME offense as the intended crime? If different categories, transferred intent does NOT apply.
**Tiny rule:** Transferred intent = same crime, different victim. Not: any intent → any harm.
**Trap:** Overextending transferred intent across crime-type boundaries
**Confidence:** ANCHOR_ASSISTED

---

#### Gold Key(s)

**GK-CRIM-TRANSINTENT-01**
> **Transferred intent is same-crime-only:** the defendant's mens rea transfers from the intended victim to the actual victim of the *same offense* — it does not bridge different crime categories (e.g., intent to kill a person does not become intent to destroy property).
>
> **Trigger:** Any transferred-intent question where the intended crime and the actual result are different categories (person vs. property, or different person-crimes).
> **Defeats:** The dominant trap that transferred intent applies whenever harm results from an intent to harm.
> **Authority:** Wex, Transferred Intent, Cornell LII (https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/transferred_intent).
