## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Ruth was injured when a small sailboat capsized during a lesson at a sailing school operated by Daniel. Ruth sued the school for negligence, claiming that the boat used for the lesson was unstable and prone to capsizing. On the defense case, Daniel's attorney called a marine-safety expert. After establishing that the witness was an expert in small-craft stability, Daniel's attorney asked whether, in the expert's opinion, it was possible for a person to sustain Ruth's type of injuries from a sailboat capsize in calm water. The expert answered that, in his opinion, such injuries could not result from a simple capsize in calm conditions. On cross-examination, Ruth's attorney asked whether the expert had ever examined or treated Ruth. The expert answered, "I have never even met the plaintiff." Ruth's attorney then moved to strike the expert's direct testimony. Should the motion be granted?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

An expert answered a hypothetical question and then admitted he never examined the plaintiff. Must the court strike the opinion?

### 3. Final Answer Choices

**A.** No, since the expert was testifying in response to a hypothetical question.

**B.** Yes, since the expert's opinion is not based on matters within his personal knowledge.

**C.** No, since an expert's testimony may be based on observations made in the courtroom.

**D.** Yes, since the expert's opinion is based on matters not in evidence.

### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

The motion should not be granted. The defense expert was asked a hypothetical question about whether Ruth's injuries could result from a calm-water capsize, and experts may answer that kind of question without first having examined the plaintiff. **Gold Key:** Rule 602's personal-knowledge requirement does not govern expert testimony under Rule 703, and an expert may base an opinion on facts or data the expert has been made aware of. **Gold Key:** A hypothetical question can supply those assumed facts even before they are formally admitted, so long as the opinion rests on facts or data experts may reasonably rely on in the field. **Silver Key:** When two choices give the same outcome, keep only the one whose stated reason matches the actual basis of admissibility. Here, that makes A the only answer that is both true and responsive.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B. Yes, since the expert's opinion is not based on matters within his personal knowledge.**

This choice is attractive because the cross-examination fact is vivid: the expert never met Ruth. The breaker is that students are supposed to separate lay-witness personal knowledge from expert opinion testimony. **Gold Key:** Rule 602 expressly does not apply to expert testimony under Rule 703, so the lack of firsthand contact with Ruth does not require exclusion.

**C. No, since an expert's testimony may be based on observations made in the courtroom.**

This choice is attractive because it reaches the right bottom-line outcome: the motion should be denied. The breaker is the reason. **Silver Key:** A correct outcome with a mismatched reason is still a wrong answer. Nothing in the stem says this expert based his opinion on courtroom observations; his opinion came from the hypothetical question about the accident mechanics.

**D. Yes, since the expert's opinion is based on matters not in evidence.**

This is the dominant trap. Students know opinions usually need a foundation, so an answer complaining that the assumed facts are "not in evidence" feels lawyerly and safe. The breaker is the expert-hypothetical rule. **Gold Key:** Experts may answer hypotheticals based on facts or data made known to them, and those facts need not already be admitted when the opinion is given.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The credited answer remains the same in legal substance. Current federal evidence law still supports it.

- **Rule 602:** A witness generally must have personal knowledge, but the rule expressly says it "does not apply to a witness's expert testimony under Rule 703."
- **Rule 703:** An expert may base an opinion on facts or data in the case that the expert has been made aware of or personally observed, and if experts in the field would reasonably rely on those kinds of facts or data, the facts or data need not themselves be admissible for the opinion to come in.
- **Rule 705:** An expert may state an opinion without first testifying to the underlying facts or data, and the advisory note explains that the rule reduced the old insistence on preliminary disclosure and discusses hypothetical questions directly.
- **Rule 702 (as amended effective December 1, 2023):** The current reliability gate requires the proponent to show admissibility by a more-likely-than-not standard, but that amendment does not change the specific point tested here: lack of personal examination of the plaintiff does not itself defeat a properly framed expert hypothetical.

Applied here, the defense did not offer the marine-safety expert as a fact witness to what happened to Ruth. The expert was asked a classic hypothetical: whether injuries of that sort could result from a capsize in calm water. Because that opinion could be based on facts or data made known to the expert rather than firsthand observation, Ruth's motion to strike should be denied. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item.
