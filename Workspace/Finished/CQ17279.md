## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**A is correct.** Ruth's conduct amounted to **criminal negligence**:
a gross deviation from the standard of care a reasonable operator
would have observed in her position. She drove a tractor at dusk
across a marked field path, saw a dark shape near the path, and
drove over it without confirming what it was. She thereby ignored a
workplace safety rule that exists precisely to prevent this kind of
death. A reasonable operator would have slowed, confirmed the
target, and avoided driving over an unidentified object on a marked
path at dusk. The field worker's death was a foreseeable consequence
of Ruth's deviation. That is the textbook definition of involuntary
manslaughter.

> **Gold Key:** homicide classification tracks **mental state**, not
> the fact of death. Murder requires **malice** (intent to kill,
> intent to cause serious bodily harm, **depraved-heart
> recklessness** (extreme conscious disregard), or **felony murder**).
> Criminal negligence is *less* than depraved-heart recklessness — it
> is a gross deviation from the standard of care, not an extreme
> conscious disregard. That gap is the entire distance between
> murder and involuntary manslaughter on these facts.

> **Silver Key:** the four choices span four different mental
> states. Match the mental state to the category, not the death to
> the most serious-sounding label. Death + intent (or extreme
> indifference) = murder. Death + heat of passion from adequate
> provocation = voluntary manslaughter. Death + criminal negligence
> = involuntary manslaughter. Death + reasonable mistake = no
> liability. The variable is the *mental state*, not the death.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations (one per distractor)

### Choice C (dominant trap) — "Murder, because a person died from Ruth running them over."

This is the highest-pull wrong answer on the mechanic, and the only

between murder and involuntary manslaughter. C3 cut: the variable
on a homicide-classification question is the **mental state**, not
the death.

> **Gold Key:** death + negligence = involuntary manslaughter. Death
> alone, even death caused by the defendant's own act, is **not**
> murder. Murder requires the malice element; without malice, the
> classification drops down the spectrum.

### Choice D — "No homicide liability, because Ruth honestly believed she was driving over a hay bale."

The cognitive move this sells is the **mistake-of-fact defense**:
yes, she had a genuine belief about the target, and the
mistake-of-fact defense can defeat specific-intent crimes when the
mistake is reasonable. The breaker is that the **mistake must be
reasonable** to defeat liability. A belief formed by driving a
tractor at dusk over an unidentified object on a marked path,
against a workplace safety rule requiring target confirmation, is
**not a reasonable belief** — it is itself a criminally negligent
belief. C3 cut: the mistake defense requires reasonableness; a
criminally negligent belief fails the reasonableness element and
does not defeat liability.

## 9. Black-letter verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**The rule.** Common-law homicide is classified by the defendant's
mental state. **Murder** requires malice aforethought: (i) intent to
kill, (ii) intent to cause serious bodily harm, (iii) **depraved-
heart recklessness** (extreme conscious disregard for human life), or
(iv) felony murder. **Voluntary manslaughter** is a heat-of-passion
killing in response to adequate provocation, judged under a
reasonable-person standard, with no cooling time. **Involuntary
manslaughter** is a killing by **criminal negligence** — a gross
deviation from the standard of care a reasonable person would have
observed in the defendant's position. The Model Penal Code § 210.3
codifies the homicide framework; § 210.3(1)(b) preserves the
depraved-heart murder category as recklessness "under circumstances
manifesting extreme indifference to the value of human life," and
§ 210.4 captures the manslaughter categories. The critical doctrinal
distinction is between **depraved-heart recklessness** (extreme
conscious disregard) and **criminal negligence** (gross deviation
from the standard of care). The former is murder; the latter is
involuntary manslaughter. The mistake-of-fact defense is unavailable
when the underlying belief is itself unreasonable — i.e., formed
through criminal negligence.

**Application to Ruth's conduct.** Ruth drove a tractor at dusk
across a marked field path, saw a dark shape near the path, and
drove over it without confirming what it was, in violation of a
workplace safety rule requiring operators to confirm visible objects
before driving over them. A reasonable operator would have slowed
and confirmed the target; Ruth did not. Her deviation from the
standard of care was a **gross deviation** — not a slight
inattention, not a momentary lapse, but a categorical failure to
observe a basic safety rule. The field worker's death was a
foreseeable consequence of that deviation. The mental state is
**criminal negligence**, not malice, not depraved-heart
recklessness, not heat of passion, and not a reasonable mistake. The
classification is **involuntary manslaughter**. Each other
classification is wrong on these facts:

- **Murder** fails because no malice is present — Ruth did not
  intend to kill, did not intend to cause serious bodily harm, did
  not consciously disregard human life to an extreme degree, and
  was not engaged in a felony.
- **Voluntary manslaughter** fails because there is no adequate
  provocation, no heat of passion, and no reasonable-person cooling
  inquiry. Ruth was careless, not provoked.
- **No liability** fails because the honest belief defense requires
  a **reasonable** belief. A belief formed by failing to observe a
  basic safety rule at dusk is not reasonable; it is itself
  criminally negligent.

**Authority floor (lawyer confirmation).**

- *Common-law framework:* murder = malice aforethought (four types:
  intent to kill, intent to cause serious bodily harm, depraved-heart
  recklessness, felony murder); voluntary manslaughter = heat of
  passion from adequate provocation; involuntary manslaughter =
  criminal negligence (gross deviation from the standard of care).
- *Authority:* **Model Penal Code § 210.3** (Homicide) — the modern
  codification preserving the depraved-heart murder category in
  § 210.3(1)(b) as recklessness "under circumstances manifesting
  extreme indifference to the value of human life"; **MPC § 210.4**
  (Manslaughter) — covers both voluntary and involuntary manslaughter
  in the modern framework. (Authority class: rule-number / model
  code. Low fabrication risk; widely cited.)
- *Distinction between depraved-heart and criminal negligence:* the
  doctrinal hinge of this question. The common-law formulations
  treat depraved-heart as a subjective, conscious extreme
  disregard; criminal negligence as an objective, gross deviation
  from the standard of care. I do not cite a specific case for
  this distinction to avoid citation risk; the principle is well
  settled and a treatise citation would not raise the primary-law
  floor under the QA gate. Flag for attorney review if a specific
  case anchor is needed downstream.

  provocation) and apply the test (heat of passion judged
  under a reasonable-person standard, with no cooling time).
- **C recovery (dominant trap):** the "murder, because a person
  died" choice is killed by the malice element. Murder requires
  malice (intent, intent to cause serious bodily harm,
  depraved-heart recklessness, or felony murder); "a person
  died" alone is not malice. A student who picks C has
  applied the death-equals-murder fallacy. Recovery: ask
  whether the defendant's mental state satisfies any of the
  four malice types. If not, the classification drops down
  the spectrum.
- **D recovery:** the "no liability, honest belief" choice is
  killed by the reasonableness element. The mistake-of-fact
  defense requires a reasonable belief; a belief formed by
  criminal negligence is not reasonable. A student who picks D
  has confused honest belief with reasonable belief. Recovery:
  ask whether the belief was reasonable — i.e., whether a
  reasonable person in the defendant's position would have
  formed the same belief through the same conduct. If the
  conduct itself was a gross deviation from the standard of
  care, the belief was not reasonable.
