### 5. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Answer C / Battery)

**Gold Key:** Battery is a general-intent crime. It is complete the moment the defendant's intentional, reckless, or criminally negligent conduct causes harmful or offensive contact with another person. No intent to cause the specific injury is required — only that the defendant's conduct was at least criminally negligent with respect to the contact.

Ruth fired a loaded rifle toward a deck she knew was occupied by people. That act — discharging a firearm in the direction of a space where people were present — is at minimum criminally negligent with respect to the risk of harmful contact. The bullet struck and wounded a person. That is battery. It does not matter that Ruth's purpose was only to frighten. The contact was the direct result of her criminally negligent act, and battery does not require intent to injure — only that the force application was intentional, reckless, or criminally negligent. Ruth intentionally fired the gun; the reckless/negligent character of the act with respect to contact is sufficient.

### 6. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**D (Attempted involuntary manslaughter) — why attractive then breaker:**
A student might reason that Ruth's reckless act "almost" caused a death and that involuntary manslaughter covers reckless killings, so the attempt version should apply. The breaker: attempt is a specific-intent crime — the defendant must have the specific intent to bring about the proscribed result. Involuntary manslaughter is, by definition, an *unintentional* killing. One cannot have the specific intent to commit an unintentional crime. The law does not recognize attempted involuntary manslaughter. This is a structural legal impossibility, not a factual one.

**A (Attempted voluntary manslaughter) — why attractive then breaker:**
The noise complaint and frustration look like provocation, and a student might map this onto "heat of passion" voluntary manslaughter. The breaker: attempted voluntary manslaughter requires the specific intent to kill. Ruth fired the gun to frighten, not to kill. There is no evidence she intended to cause death. Without intent to kill, there can be no attempted voluntary manslaughter. **Gold Key:** Voluntary manslaughter is an intentional killing in the heat of passion — attempt of it requires intent to kill, not merely intent to act recklessly.

**B (Attempted murder) — why attractive then breaker:**
Firing a gun toward an occupied space looks like the kind of extreme recklessness that could support a murder charge under the "depraved heart" theory. A student might therefore think attempted murder applies. The breaker: attempted murder requires the specific intent to kill. Ruth did not intend to kill anyone; she fired to frighten. Depraved-heart murder is a *substantive* murder theory that does not require intent to kill, but *attempted* murder does require that specific intent. Without it, attempted murder fails.

### 7. Black-Letter Legal Verification

**Battery (the credited answer):**
Criminal battery at common law and under the Model Penal Code is the unlawful application of force to another person resulting in harmful or offensive contact. Under the MPC, a person is guilty of battery (simple assault under MPC § 211.1) if she "attempts to cause or purposely, knowingly or recklessly causes bodily injury to another" or "negligently causes bodily injury to another with a deadly weapon." MPC § 211.1(1)(a)–(b). The MPC classifies battery/assault as a general-intent crime — the intent element is satisfied by the intent to perform the act (firing the gun) coupled with at least recklessness or negligence as to the risk of contact. *See* LaFave, *Substantive Criminal Law* § 16.2 (general intent for battery).

Ruth intentionally discharged a rifle toward a deck she knew was occupied. That is at minimum reckless (and likely criminally negligent) conduct with respect to the risk of harmful contact. The bullet struck and wounded a person. Battery is complete.

**Why attempted involuntary manslaughter is impossible:**
Attempt requires "the purpose to engage in conduct or cause a result that would constitute the crime." MPC § 5.01(1)(a). Involuntary manslaughter is an *unintentional* killing — death caused by criminal negligence or during an unlawful act not amounting to a felony. *See* MPC § 210.3(1)(b) (manslaughter includes "a homicide which would be murder but is committed under the influence of extreme mental or emotional disturbance" — voluntary — or "criminally negligent" homicide in some formulations). Because the crime by definition excludes intent to kill, one cannot form the specific intent to commit it. *See* *Cox v. State*, 311 Md. 326, 534 A.2d 1333 (1988) (holding attempted involuntary manslaughter is not a crime because attempt requires specific intent and involuntary manslaughter is unintentional).

**Why attempted voluntary manslaughter fails:**
Voluntary manslaughter is an intentional killing committed in the heat of passion after adequate provocation. MPC § 210.3(1)(b). Attempted voluntary manslaughter requires the specific intent to kill (the attempt layer) combined with heat-of-passion provocation (the substantive layer). *See* *Cox v. State*, 311 Md. 326, 534 A.2d 1333, 1336 (1988). Ruth did not intend to kill; she fired to frighten. No intent to kill = no attempted voluntary manslaughter.

**Why attempted murder fails:**
Attempted murder requires the specific intent to kill. *See* LaFave, *Substantive Criminal Law* § 14.2. Ruth's stated purpose was to frighten, not to kill. While her conduct might support a depraved-heart murder charge *if death had occurred* (extreme recklessness manifesting disregard for human life), depraved-heart murder does not require intent to kill — but *attempted* murder does. The attempt form requires the specific intent element that depraved-heart murder lacks. Without intent to kill, attempted murder is not made out.

**Conclusion:** The credited answer (Battery) is correct. The legal issue, tested rule, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original.
