At a private Christian academy's winter music retreat, Ruth, a senior, thought it would be a hilarious way to humble the handbell ensemble if the ringers became nauseated right before the evening hymn concert. ## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** Ruth intended a cruel prank. She intended the handbell ringers to drink the cider and become nauseated. But the charge is first-degree murder, and the stem does not show that Ruth intended to kill anyone. The death happened because Daniel had a severe almond allergy, not because Ruth set out to cause death or because the powder, as used for its expected stomach-upset effect, obviously carried a very high risk of death.

**Gold Key:** For first-degree murder, lock the grade charged. A defendant's intentional wrongful act is not the same thing as an intent to kill, and a reckless death does not become first-degree murder unless the facts supply a qualifying felony-murder route or premeditated killing.

**Silver Key:** When the call asks for a specific homicide grade, do not stop at "the defendant did something bad and someone died." Ask which grade the facts actually support. Here, the first-degree label fails because the stem gives no intent to kill and no qualifying first-degree felony-murder path.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**The breaker:** Abandoned-and-malignant-heart murder requires conduct that creates a very high risk of death or serious bodily injury. That may support a lesser homicide theory depending on the jurisdiction and risk facts, but it does not supply the extreme death-risk level needed to call the conduct abandoned-and-malignant-heart murder. **Gold Key:** the no-intent answer wins only if it stays tied to the charged grade; upgrading to depraved-heart murder needs a much stronger death-risk fact.

**The breaker:** Intent to make people drink a substance is not intent to kill. The choice proves an intentional prank and intentional administration, but it does not prove a premeditated killing.

## 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Tested rule:** First-degree murder requires the mental state and grading facts for first-degree murder, commonly a willful, deliberate, premeditated killing or a killing during a qualifying felony-murder category. Conduct causing death without intent to kill may support lesser homicide only if the risk and culpability level fit that lesser grade; it does not automatically become first-degree murder.

**Authority floor:** 18 U.S.C. § 1111(a) gives a reliable statutory grading model: murder is separated into first-degree categories such as premeditated killing and listed felony-murder categories, while other murder is second-degree. 18 U.S.C. § 1112(a) separately identifies manslaughter as unlawful killing without malice and includes involuntary manslaughter for an unlawful act not amounting to a felony or a lawful act done without due caution. Source: Office of the Law Revision Counsel, U.S. Code, 18 U.S.C. Chapter 51, §§ 1111-1112, https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&path=%2Fprelim%40title18%2Fpart1%2Fchapter51.

**Right-answer legal reasoning:** Ruth did not intend to kill. The facts show an intent to cause nausea and embarrassment, not a premeditated design to cause death. The allergy fact makes causation harsher, but it does not retroactively create intent to kill. The felony-murder route also fails because the facts do not identify a qualifying first-degree felony-murder predicate. The abandoned-and-malignant-heart theory is not the best reason for rejecting first-degree murder because the expected risk was illness, not a very high risk of death or serious bodily injury. The credited outcome remains unchanged: Ruth may not be convicted of first-degree murder on these facts.
