## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**D is correct.** The witness is not the defendant. A recent felony conviction can be used to attack that witness's character for truthfulness, subject to the ordinary Rule 403 screen. The aggravated-assault judgment qualifies because it is a felony and nothing in the stem gives a Rule 403 reason to keep it out. The vendor-license conviction also qualifies because the misdemeanor required knowingly making false statements. **Gold Key:** FRE 609 has two separate doors here: a felony door for a nondefendant witness and a dishonest-act-or-false-statement door for any conviction regardless of punishment. **Silver Key:** when the answer set offers "felony only," "false statement only," "both," and "neither," test each conviction through its own door instead of choosing the door that sounds most credibility-focused.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A - Knowingly making false statements in an application for a city vendor license only.** This is the best-looking trap because false statements are the cleanest credibility impeachment fact. The breaker is that FRE 609 does not stop with crimes of dishonesty. **Gold Key:** a nondefendant witness's recent felony conviction has its own admissibility path, so aggravated assault is not excluded merely because it is not a lie crime.

**B - Neither crime.** This answer sells a clean exclusion theory: prior convictions feel prejudicial and remote from whether Paul committed arson. The breaker is the purpose of the offer. The convictions are offered to impeach Lydia's character for truthfulness, and both fit a Rule 609 path.

**C - Aggravated assault only.** This answer spots the felony route but misses the separate false-statement route. The misdemeanor label does not defeat admissibility when the elements required a dishonest act or false statement. **Gold Key:** false-statement convictions come in regardless of punishment when the court can readily determine that the crime required the false statement.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule:** Federal Rule of Evidence 609 governs impeachment by evidence of a criminal conviction. Under FRE 609(a)(1)(A), for a crime punishable by death or imprisonment for more than one year, the conviction of a witness who is not the criminal defendant must be admitted, subject to Rule 403. Under FRE 609(a)(2), for any crime regardless of punishment, the conviction must be admitted if the court can readily determine that establishing the crime's elements required proving, or the witness admitting, a dishonest act or false statement.

**Authority:** FRE 609(a)(1)(A) and FRE 609(a)(2). The current rule text is available at https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_609. The Advisory Committee notes explain that convictions involving crimes in the nature of crimen falsi, including false-statement offenses, are admitted under subdivision (a)(2) because the offense elements involve deceit, untruthfulness, or falsification bearing on truthfulness.

**Confirmation:** The credited outcome is unchanged from the source: both convictions are admissible for impeachment. The legal issue is unchanged: whether a nondefendant prosecution witness may be impeached by a recent felony conviction and a recent misdemeanor false-statement conviction. The dominant trap is unchanged: treating the false-statement misdemeanor as the only admissible conviction and missing the felony route. Not on the doctrinal-currency hotlist. Authority floor satisfied by FRE 609(a)(1)(A) and FRE 609(a)(2).
