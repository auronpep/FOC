## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** A state-issued professional license is a property right protected by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. When the sole evidence against a licensee consists of affidavits from unnamed, absent informants whom the licensee cannot confront or cross-examine, the "hearing" is no hearing at all — the licensee is deprived of the meaningful opportunity to be heard that procedural due process requires. *See* Goldberg v. Kelly, 397 U.S. 254 (1970); Bell v. Burson, 402 U.S. 535 (1971).

C is correct. Timothy's license is a property right created by state law. The Fourteenth Amendment prohibits a state from depriving a person of property without due process of law. Procedural due process requires, at minimum, notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard. When the only evidence consists of affidavits from unnamed informants who are not present and not available for cross-examination, Timothy has no way to test the truth of the accusations. The right to confront and cross-examine adverse witnesses is a core component of a fair hearing in this context. The revocation based solely on this untested evidence violates Timothy's procedural due process rights.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original B) — attractive because:** Students may reason that the federal prosecutor's decision to drop the charges is a formal determination of innocence that the state must respect. The Full Faith and Credit Clause sounds like the right vehicle for requiring one sovereign to honor another's legal determinations.

**The breaker:** The Full Faith and Credit Clause (Art. IV, § 1) requires states to give effect to the "public acts, records, and judicial proceedings" of other states. A United States Attorney's discretionary decision not to prosecute is neither a "law" nor a "judgment" — it is an exercise of prosecutorial discretion that carries no preclusive effect. The state administrative proceeding addresses a different question (whether the licensee used his premises for an illegal purpose) under a different standard, and the federal declination does not adjudicate that question. Full faith and credit simply does not apply here.

**B (original C) — attractive because:** Article II vests the judicial power in courts, and students may intuit that a serious penalty like license revocation must come from a court, not an agency. The argument has a surface appeal rooted in separation-of-powers principles.

**The breaker:** Administrative agencies routinely exercise quasi-judicial powers, including the authority to revoke licenses. This is a well-established feature of the administrative state. Article II does not require that every penalty be imposed by a court; agencies may adjudicate and impose sanctions within their statutory authority, subject only to due process constraints. The issue here is not *who* imposed the penalty but *what process* was followed.

**D (original D) — dominant trap — attractive because:** The existence of comprehensive federal drug laws makes it seem obvious that federal law "occupies the field" and that any state action touching drug-related conduct is preempted. Students who have internalized the general concept of federal supremacy may leap to preemption without analyzing the specific preemption test.

**The breaker:** Federal law does not automatically preempt all state regulation merely because the same conduct is also federally prohibited. Preemption requires either (1) express preemption by statute, (2) field preemption (Congress intended to occupy the entire field), or (3) conflict preemption (state law actually conflicts with federal law). Federal drug statutes do not contain an express preemption clause for state professional licensing, Congress has not shown an intent to occupy the entire field of state professional regulation, and the state's revocation of a license for illegal activity on business premises does not conflict with federal drug enforcement. The state regulation stands. **Gold Key:** The preemption analysis requires an actual conflict or clear congressional intent to displace state law — the mere overlap of federal and state law on the same conduct is not enough.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment (U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1) prohibits a state from depriving any person of "life, liberty, or property, without due process of law." A state-issued professional license constitutes a property right protected by this clause. *Goldberg v. Kelly*, 397 U.S. 254 (1970); *Bell v. Burson*, 402 U.S. 535 (1971); *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319, 335 (1976).

**Three-Factor Balancing Test (Mathews v. Eldridge):** Courts evaluate procedural due process claims by balancing: (1) the private interest affected by the official action; (2) the risk of erroneous deprivation under current procedures and the value of additional safeguards; and (3) the government's interest, including the fiscal and administrative burden of additional procedures. 424 U.S. at 335.

**Application:** Timothy's interest in his tutor license is substantial — it is essential to his livelihood. The risk of erroneous deprivation is high when the sole evidence consists of affidavits from unnamed, absent informants whom the licensee cannot cross-examine. Cross-examination is the principal means of testing credibility and reliability. The government's interest in efficient licensing enforcement does not outweigh the licensee's interest when the procedure used provides no mechanism to challenge the only evidence offered.

**Authority:** *Goldberg v. Kelly*, 397 U.S. 254 (1970) (pre-termination hearing required for welfare benefits; right to confront and cross-examine witnesses is essential); *Bell v. Burson*, 402 U.S. 535 (1971) (due process requires meaningful hearing before license revocation); *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319 (1976) (three-factor balancing test for procedural due process).
