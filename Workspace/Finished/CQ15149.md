### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**A is correct.** The constable was summoned for the express purpose of confronting the brawl — the very peril Timothy's alleged negligence created. **Gold Key:** a public safety officer (police officer, firefighter, constable) cannot recover in negligence against the person whose conduct created the very emergency the officer was hired to confront; as to that peril the defendant owes the officer no duty. Because there is no duty, Timothy's negligent seating of the feuding households — however careless — gives the constable no claim for the broken arm sustained while doing the job he was called to do. This is the strongest ground for dismissal because it defeats the claim at the threshold (duty) rather than fighting over weaker, contestable elements. **Silver Key:** every choice here points toward dismissal, so the array is graded on *legal soundness, not direction* — lock the choice that states the correct and strongest no-duty ground (the professional-rescuer bar), not the choice that merely reaches "no duty" by a doctrine that would actually impose one.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B (dominant trap).** This choice is magnetic because it reaches the very conclusion the credited answer reaches — "no duty" — and it dresses it in a doctrine students know cold: a possessor owes a licensee only a limited duty (warn of known concealed traps), not a general duty of reasonable care. The breaker: licensee status would *impose* a duty (to warn), not erase one, so it is the wrong doorway to "no duty," and the injury came from the brawl, not from a hidden condition of the hall. **Gold Key:** the firefighter's rule, not the visitor's land-status classification, is what negates the duty owed to a responding officer. **Silver Key:** when two choices both say "no duty," pick the theory that actually *negates* duty, not the one that would create one.

**C.** The pull here is the noble instinct that a person who rushes in to avert a danger should bear the loss himself — it sounds like rugged fairness. The breaker: the rule runs the other way. The rescue doctrine lets an injured rescuer recover *from* the party whose negligence created the danger; this choice reverses the doctrine. **Gold Key:** an *ordinary* rescuer can recover from the negligent party — it is only the *professional* rescuer (the firefighter's rule) who is barred, which is why the constable's *status*, not the act of rescuing, decides the case.

**D.** This choice is attractive because "not the proximate cause as a matter of law" sounds like a clean, dispositive, motion-stage win, and causation is a familiar negligence element to attack. The breaker: it argues the wrong element. With brawls a foreseeable result of seating feuding households at a wine feast, causation is at best a contested jury question — not a matter of law — and once the firefighter's rule shows there is no duty at all, the causation element is never reached. It is a real argument, just a weaker and non-dispositive one, so it is not the *best* argument the call asks for.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are UNCHANGED from the original.** Both items test the **firefighter's rule** (also "fireman's rule" / professional-rescuer doctrine) and ask for the *best* argument to dismiss a public safety officer's negligence claim against the person whose negligence created the emergency. Credited ground in both: the professional-rescuer bar (no duty). Original key B → new key A.

- **Governing rule.** A public safety officer injured by the negligently created peril he was employed to confront may not recover in tort against the party who created that peril; the defendant owes no duty as to that risk. The doctrine operates as a recognized **exception to the rescue doctrine** (the rescue doctrine, by contrast, permits an *ordinary* rescuer to recover from the negligent party who created the danger).
- **Authority (real, lawyer-confirmation only).** *Krauth v. Geller*, 31 N.J. 270, 157 A.2d 129 (1960) (foundational firefighter's-rule statement — officer assumes the risks inherent in the duty confronted); *Walters v. Sloan*, 20 Cal. 3d 199, 571 P.2d 609 (1977) (extending the bar to police officers; grounding it in public policy / cost-spreading). The doctrine is widely treated as an exception to the rescue doctrine recognized in Restatement-aligned negligence authority.
- **Application.** The constable was dispatched specifically to quell the brawl — the precise peril Timothy's seating decision allegedly produced. The injury arose in the course of confronting that peril. The professional-rescuer bar therefore defeats the duty element and is the strongest dismissal argument, exactly as in the source item.
- **Why the distractors fail under black letter:** B misapplies premises liability (licensee status yields a limited *duty* to warn, not a no-duty bar, and historic firefighter's-rule cases that used a licensee theory still produced a bar, not a duty of reasonable care); C inverts the rescue doctrine; D contests proximate cause, a non-threshold element that a foreseeable-brawl record keeps alive and that is never reached once duty fails.
- **Doctrinal-currency check:** the firefighter's rule is not on the SKILL currency hotlist; framing and outcome are current. No key disagreement. `bank_validation_verdict: PASS`.
