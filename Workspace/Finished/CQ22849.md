# CQ22849 — Paul and the Boatbuilder's Blueprints

## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question (revised stem + call)

Paul sued a boatbuilding company in federal court, claiming that a fishing
vessel the company had built for him was defectively constructed. During the
final pretrial conference, the parties and the judge agreed on the exhibits each
side could offer at trial, and the judge listed those exhibits in the pretrial
order. Five days before trial was set to begin, Paul asked the court to modify
the pretrial order to add one more exhibit — the vessel's original construction
blueprints, which he had not previously listed. What must Paul show for the court
to allow the additional exhibit?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

A pretrial order issued after the *final* pretrial conference is the locked-in
blueprint for the trial, and the rules guard it with a deliberately high bar.
**Gold Key:** Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 16(e) lets a court modify the order
issued after a final pretrial conference *only to prevent manifest injustice* —
stricter than the "good cause" that governs scheduling-order changes and stricter
than the prejudice-weighing that governs leave to amend pleadings. So the showing
Paul must make is manifest injustice; nothing less unlocks the order. **Silver
Key:** in a "what must the party show" array, the choice that names a recognized
legal standard verbatim — "manifest injustice" — beats the choices that
paraphrase ordinary harm or prejudice, which are the diluted decoys. Whether the
opponent is prejudiced and whether Paul is harmed are *factors* a court weighs
inside that analysis, but the governing showing is manifest injustice itself.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B — That Paul's claim will be prejudiced by the exclusion of the additional
exhibit. (DOMINANT TRAP)**
This is the most seductive choice because harm to the moving party really is the
heart of the inquiry — "injustice" sounds like "prejudice to the side seeking
relief," so an ordinary showing of prejudice feels like enough. The breaker is
magnitude: **Gold Key:** Rule 16(e) demands *manifest injustice*, a heightened
threshold, not the everyday prejudice that would justify amending a pleading.
The choice states a true factor but drops the necessary element — the high bar —
so it understates what Paul must actually show.

**A — That the boatbuilding company will not be prejudiced by the additional
exhibit.**
This pulls because lack of prejudice to the opponent is exactly what wins a
motion to amend pleadings under Rule 15, so it feels like the natural test. The
breaker is that it points at the wrong actor: the opponent's position is one
factor a court may weigh, but it is not the showing the *movant* must make. The
governing standard runs to manifest injustice, not to the company's convenience —
a non-dispositive factor stated as if it were the test.

**D — That the additional exhibit is necessary for Paul's prima facie case.**
This is attractive because it sounds rigorous and importance-based — surely a
party must prove the evidence really matters. The breaker is that "necessary for
the prima facie case" is an invented threshold; Rule 16(e) imposes no such
requirement. It is a fabricated standard dressed up to look more demanding than
the real one.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested rule (unchanged from original):** the standard for modifying a pretrial
order entered after a final pretrial conference.

**Governing authority (lawyer_confirmation):** Fed. R. Civ. P. 16(e) — "The court
may modify the order issued after a final pretrial conference only to prevent
manifest injustice." The Advisory Committee chose this heightened standard
deliberately to distinguish the final pretrial order from earlier, more freely
revisable orders. Contrast Fed. R. Civ. P. 16(b)(4) (a scheduling order "may be
modified only for good cause") and Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)(2) (leave to amend
"freely give[n] when justice so requires," weighing prejudice to the opposing
party). Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_16

**Affirmative reasoning for C:** Because the blueprints were omitted from the
final pretrial order, that order controls the trial and supersedes anything not
preserved in it. To get the exhibit back in, Paul must satisfy the only key the
rule provides — a showing that refusing the modification would work manifest
injustice. The other choices each name something a court might consider
(opponent prejudice, movant harm) or invent a requirement (prima facie
necessity), but none states the governing standard. C does.

**Issue / outcome confirmation:** the tested rule (FRCP 16(e) modification
standard), the legal issue (what showing modifies a final pretrial order), and
the credited outcome (manifest injustice) are all UNCHANGED from the original.
Web research confirms the rule text and the heightened-standard rationale and
does not contradict the official key → verdict PASS, not NEEDS_HUMAN.
