## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Esther, a retired Christian schoolteacher in New England who had been quietly selling down her late husband's collection of antique books, orally told Daniel, a seminary student and serious Bible collector, that a 1699 Geneva Bible she was offering had "all of its original pages." Before paying, Daniel received in the mail an unsigned single-page provenance letter from a well-known third-party biblical-authentication firm; the letter was printed on firm letterhead and plainly stated on its first page: "PAGES 412-448 WERE REPLACED IN 1948; REMAINDER OF VOLUME CONSISTENT WITH 1699 ISSUE." Daniel did not read the letter. He paid Esther $4,800 for the Bible, later noticed the replaced pages, and now sues Esther for fraud.

What is Esther's strongest defense?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Esther's strongest defense is **the buyer may lack justifiable reliance because the provenance letter plainly contradicted the oral statement.** The tort of fraud (also called intentional misrepresentation) requires the plaintiff to prove, among other elements, justifiable reliance on the defendant's misrepresentation. Justifiable reliance is measured against the information reasonably available to the plaintiff at the time of the transaction; a plaintiff who ignores an obvious, plainly-worded contradiction in materials received before the sale may have crossed the line from reliance into "willful blindness" — a fact-finder can treat the contradiction as a warning the plaintiff was required to heed. The provenance letter Daniel received is the textbook example of a plainly-worded, on-its-face contradiction: the letter was delivered before payment, was printed on letterhead from a recognized authentication firm, and on its first page said that specific pages had been replaced — exactly what Esther's oral "all original pages" assurance contradicted. A reasonable buyer in Daniel's position would have read the letter (or at least the first page) before paying $4,800. **Gold Key:** justifiable reliance is defeated when the plaintiff ignores an obvious, plainly-worded contradiction in materials provided before the transaction. **Silver Key:** lock the call to the seller's strongest defense — that is the lack-of-reliance defense, not the opinion defense or the no-reliance defense.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. The buyer automatically wins because oral assurances always override written provenance in tort.**

This trap banks on a popular student myth: "oral beats written." The breaker is that the parol evidence rule and the "oral overrides written" trope are private-law contract doctrines; they do not control the reliance analysis in a tort fraud case. The relevant question in a fraud case is what a reasonable buyer would have noticed, not which version of a statement "wins" as a matter of form. The provenance letter was delivered to Daniel before the sale and plainly contradicted Esther's oral assurance; the question is whether Daniel's reliance on the oral assurance was justified in light of that obvious written contradiction. The "oral overrides written" framing is the wrong tool. **Silver Key:** when the call asks for the seller's strongest defense, look for the reliance-defeating mechanic (the written contradiction) — not for a "winning party" rule.

**C. The seller made no factual statement because the originality of Bible pages is always opinion.**

This is the dominant trap. It feels right because the fact/opinion distinction is the classic first-year fraud lever, and "is this the original?" sounds like a connoisseur's question rather than a fact. The breaker is that page originality in a specific copy of a specific Bible is a factual question susceptible of proof or disproof: the page exists or does not, it was replaced or it was not, the replacement can be dated or it cannot. Statements about specific physical facts about a specific copy of a book are factual; they are not the kind of "in my opinion, this is a beautiful book" statement that escapes fraud. **Gold Key:** the fact/opinion distinction in fraud turns on whether the statement is about a verifiable specific fact (factual) or about a value judgment or prediction (opinion); page originality in a specific book is verifiable fact, not opinion.

**D. Fraud does not require reliance if the seller's statement was intentional.**

This trap is the worst-of-both trap. It re-asserts an element of fraud (reliance) as if it were optional once the defendant's intent is established. The breaker is that reliance is an independent element of fraud; the plaintiff must prove it. It is true that the defendant's intent (scienter) is also a required element, and that intent and reliance are separately proved, but proving intent does not waive the reliance requirement. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 525 (fraud requires, among other elements, justifiable reliance). This choice fails the True & Responsive test on the elements of fraud.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18914, a Torts item on the reliance element of fraud. The seller orally told the buyer that a used truck had never been in a crash; before the sale, the buyer received title documents plainly stating "salvage after collision"; the buyer did not read the documents and later sued for fraud. The official key is B: the buyer may lack justifiable reliance because the documents plainly contradicted the oral statement.

The black-letter rule for the tort of fraud (intentional misrepresentation) is set out in Restatement (Second) of Torts § 525: a misrepresentation is fraudulent when the maker intends to induce reliance, the recipient is justified in relying, and the recipient does rely to his detriment. The reliance element has two prongs: (1) actual reliance (the misrepresentation actually caused the recipient's conduct) and (2) justifiable reliance (a reasonable person in the recipient's position would have relied). The justifiable-reliance prong is the issue here.

A leading and widely-cited articulation of the unjustified-reliance cutoff appears in *Wiley v. Home Depot, U.S.A., Inc.*, 411 F. Supp. 2d 829 (N.D. Ill. 2006) and in Prosser & Keeton, *The Law of Torts* § 108 (justifiable reliance is a flexible reasonableness standard; reliance on an obvious written contradiction of an oral statement can be unjustified as a matter of law). The Restatement (Second) of Torts § 545A and the comments recognize the same principle: if the plaintiff had the means of ascertaining the truth with reasonable certainty and the truth was readily ascertainable, the plaintiff's reliance may be unreasonable. The classic application is the buyer who ignores plainly-worded title documents, inspection reports, or pre-sale disclosures that contradict the seller's oral assurance.

The fact/opinion distinction (distractor C) is governed by Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 538–539. A statement is one of opinion when it expresses the maker's judgment about a matter on which the maker has, or purports to have, special knowledge but where the underlying fact is not verifiable by the recipient. A statement is one of fact when it describes an objectively verifiable event, condition, or property. The originality of a specific page in a specific book — and the historical fact that pages 412-448 were replaced in 1948 — is verifiable; it is a fact, not an opinion.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: Esther's strongest defense is the lack of justifiable reliance; the oral-vs.-written framing in distractor B is a private-law contract trope that does not control; the opinion framing in distractor C collapses a verifiable historical fact into a judgment; and the no-reliance framing in distractor D misstates an element of fraud.

### A. The buyer may lack justifiable reliance because the provenance letter plainly contradicted the oral statement.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** True and responsive. The provenance letter is a plainly-worded, pre-sale written contradiction of the oral assurance. Ignoring an obvious contradiction can defeat justifiable reliance as a matter of law.

### B. The buyer automatically wins because oral assurances always override written provenance in tort.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Wrong frame. The suit is for fraud (tort), not for breach of contract. The "oral overrides written" trope is a private-law contract doctrine (parol evidence rule) that does not control the reliance analysis in tort. **Silver Key:** lock the call to the seller's strongest defense — that is the lack-of-reliance defense, not a "winning party" rule.
2. **Student label:** Wrong frame — contract trope on a tort claim.
3. **True/responsive version:** "The relevant question in a tort fraud case is what a reasonable buyer would have noticed, not which version of a statement 'wins' as a matter of form."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The parol evidence rule and the "oral overrides written" framing are private-law contract doctrines; they do not control the reliance analysis in a tort fraud case.

### C. The seller made no factual statement because the originality of Bible pages is always opinion.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Fact/opinion confusion. Page originality in a specific copy of a specific book is a verifiable historical fact, not a value judgment or connoisseur's opinion. The "always opinion" framing is the tell. **Gold Key:** justifiable reliance is defeated when the plaintiff ignores an obvious, plainly-worded contradiction in materials provided before the transaction.
2. **Student label:** Fact/opinion confusion — verifiable historical fact, not opinion.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Page originality in a specific book is verifiable; it is fact, not opinion."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 538-539. A statement is one of opinion when it expresses a judgment about a matter on which the maker has special knowledge but where the underlying fact is not verifiable. Page originality in a specific book is verifiable.

### D. Fraud does not require reliance if the seller's statement was intentional.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Flat misstatement of the elements. Reliance is an independent element of fraud. Proving the defendant's scienter does not waive the reliance requirement.
2. **Student label:** Flat misstatement of the elements of fraud.

4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 525. Reliance is an element of fraud independent of scienter.
