## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

A private Bible-map publisher sued a devotional-calendar artist in federal court for copyright infringement. The case went to a jury trial. After deliberating, the jury returned a verdict for the artist. The publisher made a timely motion for a new trial, and the federal court granted the motion. The artist then timely filed an appeal from the order granting a new trial. The publisher moved to dismiss the appeal.

Should the appellate court grant the publisher's motion to dismiss the appeal?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

After a federal jury verdict, the trial court grants a timely new-trial motion and the verdict winner immediately appeals that order. Should the appeal be dismissed?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. No, because both the publisher and the artist have an interest in an appellate determination of whether the new-trial order was an abuse of discretion.

B. Yes, because the new-trial order does not aggrieve the artist since she has not lost on any claim or defense.

C. No, because final judgment would have been entered for the artist but for the new-trial order, and appellate review would be more efficient than holding another trial.

D. Yes, because no final judgment exists and there is no other basis for appellate jurisdiction.

### 5. Correct answer

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is correct. The appeal should be dismissed because the order granting a new trial leaves the case still pending in the trial court. There is no final judgment, and the facts do not give another route to immediate appellate jurisdiction. Gold Key: In federal court, a new-trial order is not the final decision; it reopens the case for another trial. Silver Key: Before asking whether review would be useful, ask whether the trial court is done with the whole case.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because it sounds like the court of appeals should step in when both sides would benefit from knowing whether the trial judge abused discretion. The breaker is that party interest is not an exception to the final-judgment rule. The case is still alive in the trial court.

B is attractive because it reaches the right yes/no result and then tries to explain dismissal through a party-injury frame. The breaker is the reason. The artist is aggrieved by having to face a second trial, but that does not create appellate jurisdiction.

C is attractive because it points to the verdict winner and says the case would already be over if the judge had not granted a new trial. The breaker is the Gold Key: but-for finality and efficiency do not turn a new-trial order into a final decision.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. The governing rule is the federal final-judgment rule. Under 28 U.S.C. section 1291, courts of appeals have jurisdiction over appeals from final decisions of federal district courts. A grant of a new trial is interlocutory because it does not end the litigation; it sends the case back for more trial-court proceedings. The Supreme Court stated the same rule in Allied Chemical Corp. v. Daiflon, Inc., 449 U.S. 33 (1980): an order granting a new trial is interlocutory and not immediately appealable.

Authority layer: 28 U.S.C. section 1291, https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/1291; Allied Chemical Corp. v. Daiflon, Inc., 449 U.S. 33 (1980), https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/449/33/.

Because the federal court granted a new-trial motion, no final decision exists. The order is not appealable merely because appellate review might save time or because the parties care about the abuse-of-discretion issue. The appellate court should grant the motion to dismiss.
