### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. The city did not cancel all programs, impose a neutral noise rule, or close the hall. It canceled only Esther's lecture because her message was expected to upset listeners. Gold Key: hostile audience reaction is not a content-neutral reason to suppress protected speech. Silver Key: when the reason sentence points to the speaker's message plus expected listener anger, treat the safety label as the trap, not the rule.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because crowd control sounds like ordinary city management. The breaker is the city's stated reason: Esther's message would upset listeners. Gold Key: a hostile-audience prediction does not become content neutral just because the city calls it crowd control.

B is attractive because the city owns the recital hall, and ownership can make public-forum limits feel optional. The breaker is the word "never." Gold Key: when a city opens a facility for private talks, forum limits can attach; government ownership alone does not let the city exclude a speaker because of the speaker's message.

D is attractive because it treats audience anger as a practical emergency. The breaker is the First Amendment rule against the heckler's veto. Listener anger may require lawful crowd management, but it does not give the city a license to cancel the protected speaker.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Constitutional Law, Individual Rights, Freedom of speech and expression, and the verified outline code is `44040502`. The First Amendment bars government from suppressing protected speech because of message, subject matter, content, or anticipated hostile audience reaction. The city is a government actor, and the recital hall has been opened for private lectures, so the forum analysis matters.

The governing authority confirms the credited outcome. The First Amendment protects speech against state and local suppression through the Fourteenth Amendment. U.S. Const. amend. I. In Police Department of Chicago v. Mosley, 408 U.S. 92 (1972), the Supreme Court treated message and subject-matter distinctions as forbidden content control. Source: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep408/usrep408092/usrep408092.pdf

Forsyth County v. Nationalist Movement, 505 U.S. 123 (1992), is the clean heckler-veto authority: the Court held that anticipated listener reaction is not a content-neutral basis for regulating speech and that government may not punish or ban speech because it may offend a hostile crowd. Source: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep505/usrep505123/usrep505123.pdf

The forum point also matches the source row. Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators' Association, 460 U.S. 37 (1983), recognizes that when the government opens property for public expressive activity, it must follow public-forum standards while that forum remains open; content-based exclusions must satisfy a demanding standard. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/460/37/ Southeastern Promotions, Ltd. v. Conrad, 420 U.S. 546 (1975), confirms that denial of access to a municipal performance facility can violate the First Amendment when officials suppress controversial expression. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/420/546/

Those authorities do not contradict the source key. The cancellation targets one lecture because of the speaker's message and likely audience reaction. That is content based, not a neutral safety regulation. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged.
