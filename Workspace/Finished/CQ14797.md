# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question

Peter is on trial for aggravated assault. The physical evidence showed the victim was struck in the back of the head and on the forearms with a metal rod and left in a parking lot. The medical examiner testified that the injuries to the victim's forearms were defensive wounds. The victim testified that he cannot remember who attacked him. He further testified that the only thing he recalls is that a stranger found him in the parking lot, and that he told the stranger that Peter had hit him with the metal rod; he then lost consciousness. Peter's attorney objects to this proposed testimony, arguing that it is hearsay and that the victim lacked personal knowledge of the attacker's identity.

Is the victim's testimony concerning his previous statement to the stranger admissible?

A crime victim who cannot remember the attack but recalls telling a bystander the defendant's name — and who suffered defensive wounds showing he faced his attacker — testifies at trial and is subject to cross-examination. ## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** The victim's prior statement identifying Peter is admissible under the hearsay exemption in FRE 801(d)(1)(C), which provides that a prior statement "of identification of a person made after perceiving the person" is not hearsay if the declarant "testifies at trial or hearing and is subject to cross-examination concerning the statement." The victim is testifying at trial and is subject to cross-examination. Under *United States v. Owens*, 484 U.S. 554 (1988), the fact that the victim has no memory of making the prior identification does not defeat the exemption — a witness is "subject to cross-examination" when placed on the stand, under oath, and responding willingly to questions. As for personal knowledge, FRE 602 requires only evidence "sufficient to support a finding" that the witness perceived the matter. The defensive wounds on the victim's forearms support the inference that he faced his attacker and could perceive the attacker's identity. This is enough to satisfy the low threshold of FRE 602.

> **Gold Key (GK-EVID-OWENS-01):** Under *United States v. Owens*, a witness who made a prior identification but now has memory loss of that identification is still "subject to cross-examination" for purposes of FRE 801(d)(1)(C) — the exemption is satisfied as long as the witness is on the stand, under oath, and answering questions. Memory loss does not defeat the prior-identification exemption.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A** is incorrect. This is the dominant trap. The choice sells the misconception that memory loss prevents "effective" cross-examination and therefore defeats the prior-identification exemption. The attraction is intuitive: if the witness can't remember the event, how can the defense cross-examine? But *United States v. Owens*, 484 U.S. 554 (1988), squarely rejected this reasoning. The Court held that a witness is "subject to cross-examination" for purposes of FRE 801(d)(1)(C) when the witness is placed on the stand, under oath, and responds willingly to questions — full memory of the prior statement is not required. The defense can still cross-examine the witness about the memory loss itself, the circumstances of the identification, and the witness's credibility. The Gold Key above — the *Owens* rule — is what kills this trap.

**B** is incorrect. This choice misstates the legal standard for personal knowledge under FRE 602. The rule does not require the proponent to show that it is "more likely than not" that the witness had personal knowledge. That would be a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard, which is higher than what the rule demands. FRE 602 requires only evidence "sufficient to support a finding" of personal knowledge — a low, conditional-relevance threshold. The defensive wounds on the victim's forearms easily meet this standard because they support the inference that the victim was facing his attacker during the assault.

**C** is incorrect. This choice reaches the correct outcome (admissible) but for the wrong reason. It is not true that a witness's own out-of-court statement is automatically admissible simply because the witness is now testifying. Hearsay rules apply to any out-of-court statement offered for its truth, including the witness's own prior statements. There is no general hearsay exception for "your own prior statement." The testimony is admissible here because the specific exemption for prior identifications in FRE 801(d)(1)(C) is satisfied — not because the statement is the victim's own.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**FRE 801(d)(1)(C)** provides that a statement is not hearsay if it is "[a] statement of identification of a person made after perceiving the person," provided the declarant "testifies at trial or hearing and is subject to cross-examination concerning the statement."

**FRE 602** provides that a witness may testify to a matter only if "evidence is introduced sufficient to support a finding that the witness has personal knowledge of the matter." This is a low threshold — the judge does not decide whether the witness actually perceived the matter, only whether a reasonable jury could so find. *See* FRE 602; FRE 104(b) (conditional relevance).

**United States v. Owens**, 484 U.S. 554 (1988): The Supreme Court held that a witness who made a prior identification but suffered memory loss of the identification was still "subject to cross-examination" for purposes of FRE 801(d)(1)(C). The Court reasoned that a witness is ordinarily regarded as subject to cross-examination when placed on the stand, under oath, and responding willingly to questions. Limitations on the scope of cross-examination (including memory loss) go to the weight of the evidence, not its admissibility. *Id.* at 558–61.

## Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

### Choice A
> No, because the victim has no memory of the attack itself and therefore cannot be effectively cross-examined.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice feels right because it seems logical — if the witness can't remember, how can the defense cross-examine? But the signal to watch for is: "effective cross-examination" is not the legal standard. The rule asks whether the declarant "is subject to cross-examination," not whether the cross-examination will be effective.
2. **Student label:** Memory-loss trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "No, because the victim is not available for cross-examination" — but that's not the case here; the victim IS on the stand.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 801(d)(1)(C) requires only that the declarant "testify at trial or hearing and is subject to cross-examination concerning the statement." *United States v. Owens*, 484 U.S. 554 (1988), held that memory loss does not defeat this requirement. The witness is "subject to cross-examination" when placed on the stand, under oath, and responding willingly to questions.

### Choice B
> No, because the prosecutor has failed to show that it is more likely than not that the victim had personal knowledge of the attacker's identity.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice invents a "more likely than not" (preponderance) standard. FRE 602 uses the phrase "sufficient to support a finding" — a visibly lower threshold. This is a fabricated heightened requirement.
2. **Student label:** Wrong-standard trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "No, because there is no evidence from which a jury could find that the victim perceived the attacker" — but the defensive wounds contradict this.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 602 requires only evidence "sufficient to support a finding" of personal knowledge. This is a conditional-relevance standard under FRE 104(b), not a preponderance standard. The defensive wounds easily meet this threshold.

### Choice C
> Yes, because it is the victim's own out-of-court statement.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The reason is structurally false. There is no hearsay exemption for "your own prior statement." If there were, every witness could narrate their own out-of-court statements without limitation. The choice states a fabricated rule.
2. **Student label:** Wrong-reason trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Yes, because the statement qualifies under a specific hearsay exemption" — but "it's my own statement" is not one.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 801(c) defines hearsay to include any out-of-court statement offered for its truth, including the witness's own prior statements. No exemption exists for "own statement." The correct exemption here is 801(d)(1)(C).

### Choice D
> Yes, because the victim is subject to cross-examination, and there is sufficient showing of personal knowledge.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** No visible defect. Correctly identifies both requirements: (1) the victim is subject to cross-examination (satisfying 801(d)(1)(C)), and (2) there is sufficient evidence of personal knowledge (satisfying FRE 602).
2. **Student label:** Correct answer.

4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 801(d)(1)(C) is satisfied because the victim testifies and is subject to cross-examination. FRE 602 is satisfied by the defensive wounds, which support an inference that the victim faced his attacker. *United States v. Owens*, 484 U.S. 554 (1988).
