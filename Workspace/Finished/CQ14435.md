### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** Under the common law, a modification to an existing contract requires new consideration to be enforceable. Here, Paul was already obligated to pay Lydia $8,000 on September 1. When Lydia agreed to accept installment payments instead, Paul gave nothing new in return — he merely promised to do what he was already legally obligated to do (pay the $8,000). A promise to perform a pre-existing legal duty is not valid consideration. Because the modification lacked consideration, it is unenforceable, and Lydia may demand the original $8,000 immediately.

**Gold Key:** Under the common law pre-existing duty rule, a promise to do what one is already legally obligated to do is not valid consideration for a contract modification. UCC § 2-209 abolishes this rule for sales of goods, but the common law rule governs service contracts and non-UCC transactions.

---

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect** because this choice invokes the wrong doctrine. The dealer's arrangement with Martha was not a delegation of duties — Paul remained liable on the debt and merely directed Martha to send payments to Lydia. A delegation would require the creditor to accept the third party's performance as discharging the original obligor's duty. Here, Paul never transferred his obligation; he only asked Martha to remit payment on his behalf. The real issue is not delegation but whether the modification itself was supported by consideration. **Gold Key:** The pre-existing duty rule, not delegation doctrine, governs whether this modification is enforceable.

**B is incorrect** because the Statute of Frauds is not the barrier here. While certain contracts must be in writing to be enforceable (e.g., contracts that cannot be performed within one year, contracts for the sale of land), an oral agreement to modify the payment terms of a debt is not within the Statute of Frauds. The modification fails not because it was oral but because it lacked consideration. Students who pick this choice are reaching for a familiar contract defense without identifying the precise doctrinal defect. **Gold Key:** The Statute of Frauds is a red herring on this fact pattern — the modification's fatal flaw is lack of consideration, not lack of a writing.

**C is incorrect** because the facts do not establish a novation. A novation requires the substitution of a new party or new obligation with the express or implied discharge of the original obligor. Here, Paul was not seeking to substitute Martha as the obligor on the debt to Lydia — he remained the debtor throughout. The arrangement was merely a request that Martha send her payments to Lydia, not a release of Paul's obligation. Without a release of the original obligor, there is no novation. **Gold Key:** A novation requires discharge of the original obligor; merely directing a third party to pay the creditor is not a novation.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule:** Under the common law, a modification of an existing contract requires new consideration to be enforceable. The pre-existing duty rule holds that a promise to perform an obligation one is already legally bound to perform does not constitute valid consideration for a new agreement. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 73 (1981) (performance of a legal duty owed to a promisor which is neither doubtful nor the subject of honest dispute is not consideration); *see also* *Margeson v. Artis*, 776 N.W.2d 652 (Iowa 2009) (modification lacked consideration where promisee gave nothing beyond pre-existing obligation).

**Application:** Paul owed Lydia $8,000 due September 1. Lydia's oral agreement to accept $800/month for 10 months was a modification of the original debt. Paul gave no new consideration — he was already obligated to pay $8,000. The installment promise was merely a different timing mechanism for the same pre-existing duty. Under the common law pre-existing duty rule, the modification is unenforceable.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 73; *Margeson v. Artis*, 776 N.W.2d 652 (Iowa 2009). UCC § 2-209(1) abolishes the pre-existing duty rule for modifications of contracts for the sale of goods, but this fact pattern involves a service/loan transaction governed by common law, not the UCC.

**Contrast with distractors:** (A) Statute of Frauds — oral modifications of debt payment terms are not within the SoF. (B) Delegation — no delegation occurred; Paul remained the obligor. (D) Novation — no novation because Paul was not released from the obligation.

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

#### Program Frame

This item tests the **pre-existing duty rule** in contract modifications under common law. The core issue is whether an oral agreement to accept installment payments in lieu of a lump-sum debt is enforceable when the debtor provides no new consideration. This is a classic MBE Contracts trap that tests whether students can distinguish the real defect (no consideration) from plausible-sounding but inapplicable doctrines (Statute of Frauds, delegation, novation).

#### Student Access Test

A smart 10-year-old who completed the C3 lessons should be able to:
1. Identify that Paul already owed $8,000 and was just promising to pay the same amount differently.
2. Recognize that "doing what you already have to do" isn't a new bargain.
3. See that the Statute of Frauds choice invents a writing requirement that doesn't apply.
4. See that no one was "replaced" in the obligation (killing novation).

The pre-existing duty rule itself is a Gold Key — it's a narrow doctrinal rule a student is unlikely to derive but that makes the question easy once known.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Paul operates a tent-making workshop and owes Lydia $8,000, due September 1. Paul contracts to build a custom awning for Martha at $8,000, payable $800/month for 10 months beginning September 1. Paul asks Lydia to accept Martha's payments. Lydia agrees orally, then refuses Martha's first payment and demands the full $8,000 from Paul immediately. Paul refuses. Lydia sues.

**Call:** Which argument best supports Lydia's claim for immediate payment?

**Choices:**
- **A:** Paul could not delegate his duty to pay to Martha.
- **B:** The agreement to extend the time for payment was not in writing.
- **C:** Paul's conduct was an attempted novation that Lydia could reject.
- **D:** Paul gave no consideration for the agreement to extend the time of payment.

**Correct Answer:** D

#### Distilled Core Question

A debtor and creditor orally agree to modify a pre-existing debt from lump-sum to installment payments, with no new consideration. What is the best argument that the modification is unenforceable?

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks for the "best argument" supporting the creditor's claim. This means we need to identify the legal defect that makes the modification unenforceable. Before looking at choices, a student should predict: "The modification probably fails because the debtor didn't give anything new — just a promise to do what they already owed."

#### Trigger Facts

1. Paul owes Lydia $8,000 due September 1 (pre-existing debt).
2. Paul contracts with Martha for $8,000 awning, $800/month (separate transaction).
3. Paul asks Lydia to accept Martha's payments; Lydia agrees orally (modification attempt).
4. Lydia refuses Martha's payment, demands full $8,000 from Paul (repudiation of modification).
5. Paul gave no new consideration for Lydia's promise to accept installments (key inferred fact).

#### Governing C3 Lane

**CUT** choices that misstate the law or invoke doctrines not in play. **CLASH** between the delegation trap and the correct answer. **CALL** the pre-existing duty rule as the deciding axis.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "Paul could not delegate his duty to pay to Martha."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** A third party (Martha) is involved, so delegation sounds relevant. But read carefully: did Paul actually delegate his duty, or did he just ask Martha to send money? Lydia insisted on payment "only from Paul" — that means Paul is still on the hook. No delegation happened.
2. **Student label:** Delegation trap — sounds right because a third party is involved.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Paul attempted to delegate his duty to Martha without Lydia's consent, and Lydia rejected the delegation." But that's not what happened here — Paul never transferred his obligation.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Delegation requires the obligee to accept the delegatee's performance in lieu of the obligor's. Lydia never accepted Martha as the obligor. The delegation doctrine is not in play.

**Choice B: "The agreement to extend the time for payment was not in writing."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice invents a writing requirement. The MBE loves testing whether students know when the Statute of Frauds applies. Modifying payment terms on a debt doesn't require a writing. This is a fabricated procedural barrier.
2. **Student label:** Statute of Frauds trap — students default to writing requirements.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "The agreement to extend the time for payment was not in writing, and the Statute of Frauds requires modifications of this type to be in writing." But no such SoF category exists for debt payment modifications.
