### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The federal court should consult **State A's preclusion law**. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1738 (the federal full-faith-and-credit statute), federal courts must give a state-court judgment the same preclusive effect that the rendering state would give it. Semtek International Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp., 531 U.S. 497 (2001), confirms that for a state-court judgment the source of preclusion law is the **rendering state** — not the forum state and not a uniform federal common law of preclusion. The fact that the second court happens to be federal (sitting in diversity) does not change that. **Gold Key:** for any state-court judgment, look to the preclusion law of the state that rendered the judgment — full faith and credit (§ 1738) makes the rendering state's rule binding, even on a federal court.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. State B preclusion law, because the second case is pending there.**

This is the "forum-state" trap. The student anchors on the second court's location and assumes the second forum's preclusion law applies. The breaker is § 1738 / Semtek: the source of preclusion law is the **rendering** state, not the **forum** state. State B is the forum, not the source court. The "always" + "because the second case is pending there" combination is a `tiered_absolute` overclaim — preclusion law does NOT follow the forum.

**B. No preclusion law applies unless both cases are in the same court.**

This is the "same-court" trap. The student assumes preclusion is a court-internal doctrine and cannot cross court systems. The breaker is the federal full-faith-and-credit statute (28 U.S.C. § 1738), which expressly operates across courts and gives a state-court judgment preclusive effect in federal court. The "unless both cases are in the same court" condition is a `fabricated_rule` — preclusion routinely operates across court systems.

**D. Federal preclusion law always, because the second court is federal.**

This is the dominant trap. The student anchors on "federal court" and infers that federal preclusion law must govern. The breaker is the rule for state-court judgments: § 1738 / Semtek directs the federal court to use the **rendering state's** preclusion law, not federal common law. The "always" is a `tiered_absolute` overclaim — federal preclusion law is NOT always the source. (Note: federal common law of preclusion does apply when the prior judgment was itself a federal-court judgment, but that is a different case not presented here.) **Gold Key:** § 1738 controls a state-court judgment; federal common law controls a federal-court judgment.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17704, a Civil Procedure item on the source of preclusion law for a state-court judgment invoked in federal court. The official key is A (State A preclusion law).

The controlling rule sits at the intersection of the Full Faith and Credit Clause (U.S. Const. art. IV, § 1) and its federal implementing statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1738. Section 1738 provides that the "records and judicial proceedings of any court of any such State . . . shall have the same full faith and credit in every court within the United States . . . as they have by law or usage in the courts of such State . . . from which they are taken." The Supreme Court confirmed in Semtek International Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp., 531 U.S. 497, 508 (2001), that for a state-court judgment, federal courts must apply the preclusion law that the rendering state would apply; federal common law of preclusion is the residual rule only when the prior judgment was a federal-court judgment.

### 2. The 17-section student case study

## program_frame

This is a Civil Procedure question on the source of preclusion law. The legal issue is narrow but doctrinal: when a state-court judgment is later raised for preclusion in a federal court, which preclusion law governs the prior judgment's effect? The trap structure is three wrong-direction sources of preclusion law (federal common law, forum-state law, and a fabricated same-court rule) versus the correct one (rendering-state law under § 1738). The discrimination between the correct answer and the dominant trap requires the § 1738 / Semtek anchor.

## student_access_test

A C3 student can cut **A** on the `tiered_absolute` mold — the "always" + "because the second case is pending there" combination is visibly too strong once the student knows preclusion law does not follow the forum. A C3 student can cut **B** on the `fabricated_rule` mold — the "unless both cases are in the same court" condition is not the law. **C** and **D** are both coherent-sounding answers; the Gold Key (§ 1738 / Semtek) discriminates them by reference to the type of the prior judgment (state-court vs federal-court). A student without the Gold Key sits in `anchor_assisted` territory on the C/D axis.

## distilled_core_question

A state court in State A enters a negligence judgment; the defendant is sued again in federal court in State B (diversity) and argues the State A judgment is preclusive. Which law governs the State A judgment's preclusive effect?

## call_and_prediction

Call: open "Which law should the federal court generally consult?" — each answer frames a different source of preclusion law. Prediction: the rendering state's preclusion law (§ 1738 / Semtek) controls when the prior judgment is state-court. The dominant trap is the "second court is federal → federal law" misstep.

## governing_c3_lane

Civil Procedure lane. First ask: what type of prior judgment? On the present facts, the prior judgment is a state-court negligence judgment. Then ask: which preclusion law governs a state-court judgment raised in federal court? Answer: the rendering state's preclusion law, under 28 U.S.C. § 1738 and Semtek. The second court's federal character is irrelevant; only the prior judgment's character matters.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. State B preclusion law, because the second case is pending there.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The "always" + "because the second case is pending there" is a `tiered_absolute` overclaim. Preclusion law does not follow the forum; it follows the rendering court.
2. **Student label:** Forum-state trap.
3. **True/responsive version:** "State B's preclusion law would apply only if State B had rendered the prior judgment, which it did not; the federal court in State B is the forum, not the source court."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** 28 U.S.C. § 1738 / Semtek: for a state-court judgment, the rendering state's preclusion law applies, not the forum's.

### B. No preclusion law applies unless both cases are in the same court.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The "unless both cases are in the same court" condition is a `fabricated_rule`. § 1738 expressly operates across court systems and gives state-court judgments preclusive effect in federal court.
2. **Student label:** Same-court trap.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Preclusion routinely operates across court systems; the federal full-faith-and-credit statute makes the state-court judgment binding in federal court."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** 28 U.S.C. § 1738: the full-faith-and-credit statute is, by its terms, addressed to "every court within the United States" and is not limited to the rendering court.

### C. State A preclusion law, because State A rendered the judgment.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the residual answer. The Gold Key tells the student that the source of preclusion law for a state-court judgment is the rendering state under § 1738 / Semtek.
2. **Student label:** Residual answer (rendering-state rule).
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** 28 U.S.C. § 1738; Semtek International Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp., 531 U.S. 497, 508 (2001).

### D. Federal preclusion law always, because the second court is federal.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the dominant trap. The "always" is a `tiered_absolute` overclaim. The second court's federal character is irrelevant when the prior judgment is state-court; § 1738 / Semtek directs the federal court to use the rendering state's preclusion law. Federal common law of preclusion is the residual rule only for federal-court judgments, which is not the present case.
2. **Student label:** Federal-preclusion-always trap (dominant).
3. **True/responsive version:** "Federal common law of preclusion applies when the prior judgment was a federal-court judgment, which is not this case. Here the prior judgment is state-court, so the rendering state's preclusion law applies."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** 28 U.S.C. § 1738; Semtek International Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp., 531 U.S. 497, 508 (2001). The student's "second court is federal" intuition is the trap; the discriminator is the type of the prior judgment.

## residual_answer

The residual is **C**. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1738 and Semtek, a federal court must give a state-court judgment the same preclusive effect the rendering state would give. The second court's federal character is irrelevant when the prior judgment is state-court.
