## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**B is correct.** Timothy's duty to perform was subject to a condition precedent: Peter had to identify the correct job site so Timothy could build there. When Peter accidentally wrote the wrong address in the work order, he failed to satisfy that condition. Because the contract called for completion before Peter returned from his trip, and Peter was away the entire time, it was too late for Peter to correct the address and fulfill the condition once the crew had already built on the wrong lot. Under basic contract principles, when a condition precedent is not fulfilled and the time for its occurrence has passed, the obligor's duty is discharged. Timothy is therefore not in breach, and the judgment should be for Timothy.

**Gold Key:** A condition precedent is an event (often an act by one party) that must occur before the other party's duty to perform becomes due. If the party whose cooperation is needed to fulfill that condition fails to act — and the time for the condition passes — the other party's obligation is discharged. The cooperating party cannot then sue for nonperformance.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### Choice A (original B): The contract would have resulted in a loss for Timothy.

**Why attractive:** Students feel the intuitive pull that a losing contract should be escapable — it seems unfair to force a contractor to work at a loss. This taps the misconception that contract law contains a "loss defense" or that financial hardship excuses performance.

**The breaker:** Contract law does not guarantee a profit. Absent a recognized excuse (impossibility, impracticability, frustration of purpose, or failure of a condition), the fact that a contract will lose money does not relieve a party of the duty to perform. Here, the below-market price was a bargained-for term Timothy agreed to. **Gold Key:** A condition precedent is an event that must occur before the obligor's duty becomes due — the builder's potential loss is simply not a recognized excuse.

### Choice B is the correct answer (see above).

### Choice C (original D): Timothy was unsuccessful in collecting payment from the neighboring property owner.

**Why attractive:** This feels like it should matter — if Timothy built something valuable on the neighbor's land and can't get paid for it, it seems unjust that Peter gets to demand free rework. Students conflate the two separate transactions (Timothy–Peter and Timothy–neighbor) and think the neighbor's nonpayment should somehow reduce Peter's rights.

**The breaker:** The neighbor transaction is entirely separate from the Peter–Timothy contract. Whether Timothy can or cannot collect from the neighbor has no bearing on Peter's contractual rights or Timothy's obligations under the Peter–Timothy agreement. This choice is legally irrelevant to the dispute being litigated. **Gold Key:** A condition precedent analysis focuses on whether the cooperating party fulfilled the condition — the builder's unrelated dealings with third parties don't change that analysis.

### Choice D (original A): There is another contractor who will install the patio and outdoor kitchen for Peter at the same contract price.

**Why attractive:** If Peter can get the same work done elsewhere for the same price, it seems like Peter suffers no real harm — so why should Timothy be liable? This appeals to the intuition that damages, not performance, are what matter.

**Why this is wrong:** The availability of a substitute contractor relates only to the measure of damages, not to whether Timothy breached. The standard remedy for breach of a services contract is the difference between the contract price and the market price. Here, the contract was at 12% below market, so Peter could recover that difference regardless of whether another contractor would do it at the same price. The question asks what leads to a judgment *for Timothy* — meaning Timothy is not liable at all. The existence of a substitute contractor doesn't eliminate Timothy's breach; it only affects damages. Timothy would still be the breaching party.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Tested doctrine:** Condition precedent — failure of a condition precedent by the cooperating party discharges the obligor's duty when the time for the condition has passed.

**Governing authority:**

- **Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 224**: A condition is an event, not certain to occur, which must occur, unless its nonoccurrence is excused, before performance under a contract becomes due.
- **Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 225(1)**: Performance of a duty subject to a condition cannot become due unless the condition occurs or its nonoccurrence is excused.
- **Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 226**: If the occurrence of a condition is prevented by the party whose performance is subject to the condition, the condition may be excused — but this cuts the *other* way: it protects the obligor from the obligee's sabotage. Here, the obligee (Peter) failed to satisfy the condition through his own error, so the obligor (Timothy) is discharged.
- **D & S Realty, Inc. v. Markel Insurance Co.**, 816 N.W.2d 1 (Neb. 2012): "Performance of a duty subject to a condition cannot become due unless the condition occurs or its nonoccurrence is excused." (Headnote 2.)

**Application to this question:** The contract required Timothy to build a patio and outdoor kitchen on Peter's property. An implied (or constructive) condition precedent to Timothy's duty was that Peter identify the correct location. Peter's work order specified the address — and Peter wrote the wrong address. Because Peter was out of the country for three weeks and the work was to be completed before his return, there was no opportunity for Peter to correct the mistake before the time for performance passed. The condition was not fulfilled, the time expired, and Timothy's obligation was discharged.

**Distractor verification:**
- **A (loss)**: No rule excuses performance due to financial loss. Correctly eliminated.
- **C (neighbor payment)**: Irrelevant to the Peter–Timothy contract. Correctly eliminated.
- **D (substitute contractor)**: Goes to damages, not liability. Correctly eliminated.

**Verdict:** The credited answer (C in original, B in variant) is legally sound. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are UNCHANGED from the original. `bank_validation_verdict: PASS`.

## Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

### Choice A: "The contract would have resulted in a loss for Timothy."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Does contract law excuse a party from performing a contract just because the deal will lose money? Name the rule. If you can't name it, it probably doesn't exist.
2. **Student label:** The losing-contract trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would be: "Performance has become impossible due to destruction of the subject matter" or "Supervening illegality prevents performance." Those are recognized excuses. "It's unprofitable" is not.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Contract law contains no general defense of financial hardship or loss. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 225. A party is bound by the price it agreed to.

### Choice B: "Peter had accidentally written the wrong address in the work order."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Peter had to tell Timothy where to build. If Peter gave the wrong address, Peter failed to do something Timothy needed before Timothy could perform. When the cooperating party fails to act and time runs out, the other party is off the hook.
2. **Student label:** The condition-precedent defense.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true-and-responsive answer. Peter's own failure to satisfy a condition precedent (providing the correct address) discharged Timothy's duty.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 224, 225. A condition precedent that is not fulfilled, where the time for its occurrence has passed, discharges the obligor's duty to perform.

### Choice C: "Timothy was unsuccessful in collecting payment from the neighboring property owner."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The lawsuit is Peter vs. Timothy. The neighbor is not a party to this contract. Ask: does what happened between Timothy and the neighbor change whether Timothy breached the Peter–Timothy contract? No.
2. **Student label:** The irrelevant-third-party trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would be: "The neighbor was a third-party beneficiary of the Peter–Timothy contract" or "Peter had assigned the contract to the neighbor." Those would make the neighbor relevant. Simple nonpayment by the neighbor is not.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The Timothy–neighbor transaction is a separate contractual relationship. It has no bearing on Timothy's obligations or defenses under the Peter–Timothy contract.

### Choice D: "There is another contractor who will install the patio and outdoor kitchen for Peter at the same contract price."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The question asks what leads to a judgment *for Timothy* — meaning Timothy is not liable at all. Does another contractor's availability mean Timothy didn't breach? Or does it just affect how much Peter can recover?
2. **Student label:** The substitute-contractor trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would be: "Peter waived the contract" or "The contract was rescinded by mutual agreement." Those would eliminate liability. A substitute contractor only affects damages.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The measure of damages for breach of a services contract is the difference between contract price and market price. The availability of a substitute contractor does not eliminate breach — it only affects the damage calculation.

## Wrong-Answer Recovery Paths

**If a student picked A (losing contract):** Go back to the basics — contract law does not guarantee a profit. Review the excuse defenses.

**If a student picked C (neighbor nonpayment):** Identify the parties to the lawsuit. The suit is Peter vs. Timothy. The neighbor is not a party. The neighbor's nonpayment is a separate contractual dispute. Ask: does this fact change whether Timothy breached the Peter–Timothy contract? No. Practice identifying which facts are legally responsive to the call.

**If a student picked D (substitute contractor):** Distinguish between liability and damages. The question asks what leads to a judgment *for Timothy* — meaning no liability at all. A substitute contractor affects how much Peter can recover (damages), not whether Timothy is liable (breach). Practice the liability-vs-damages distinction.
