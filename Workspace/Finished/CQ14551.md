## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** There was no effective assignment of Paul's right to receive payment from Timothy. Under the Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 317(1), an assignment is "a manifestation of the assignor's intention to transfer it by virtue of which the assignor's right to performance by the obligor is extinguished in whole or in part and the assignee acquires a right to such performance." The writing Paul gave Lydia stated that money he *would receive* from Timothy would be paid to Lydia — but it did not extinguish Paul's own right to receive that payment, nor did it clearly transfer that right to Lydia. The language is a direction or authorization to pay, not a present transfer of the right itself. Without an effective assignment, Lydia never acquired a right to demand payment directly from Timothy, and Timothy may properly pay Paul's estate.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original C):** This choice sells the idea that Paul's subjective intent to have Timothy pay Lydia directly was enough to create a valid assignment. It was not. A mere expression of intent to pay — even in writing — does not satisfy the requirement that the assignor manifest an intention to *extinguish the assignor's own right* and transfer it. The writing must do more than express a wish; it must effectuate a present transfer. This trap preys on students who conflate intent to benefit someone with the legal mechanics of assignment.

**B (original D):** This is the dominant trap. It reframes the claim as a third-party beneficiary theory. But Lydia is at most an *incidental* beneficiary of the Paul–Timothy contract. Timothy's contract with Paul was for the awning job; there is no evidence that Timothy intended to benefit Lydia directly. An intended beneficiary must be one whom the promisee (Paul) intended to benefit *and* whom the promisor (Timothy) understood would be the direct beneficiary of the duty owed. Here, Timothy had no such understanding. This trap works because students see a third party benefiting from a contract and jump to "intended beneficiary" without checking the dual-intent requirement.

**D (original A):** This choice asserts that Paul's death terminated Lydia's rights — but that presupposes an assignment existed in the first place. Since there was no effective assignment, there were no rights to terminate. Death only matters *after* a valid assignment has vested rights in the assignee. This trap baits students who know that death can affect contractual rights but forget to ask whether any rights were ever transferred.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 317(1): "An assignment of a right is a manifestation of the assignor's intention to transfer it by virtue of which the assignor's right to performance by the obligor is extinguished in whole or in part and the assignee acquires a right to such performance."

**Application:** The writing must manifest a present intent to transfer — not merely a future intention or a direction to pay. The language "any money I receive … will be paid to" describes what will happen to funds *if and when* the assignor receives them; it does not divest the assignor of the right to receive them. Courts distinguish between a mere payment direction (which preserves the assignor's right) and a true assignment (which extinguishes it). See Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 317, 322; *Trak Microwave Corp. v. Medaris Mgmt.* (a promise to assign in the future is ineffective as an actual transfer).

**Third-party beneficiary distinction:** An intended beneficiary under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 302 must be a party whom the promisee intended to benefit and whom the promisee sought to make enforceable. A creditor who merely receives a payment direction from the promisee is an incidental beneficiary at best — the promisor need not even know of the arrangement.

### Choice A: "No, because Paul's death terminated Lydia's right to receive payment directly from Timothy."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice *assumes* Lydia had rights that were terminated by Paul's death. 2. **Student label:** Death-termination trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would first establish that an assignment occurred, then explain that death doesn't revoke it. But that's not what this choice does.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Death only affects rights that have already vested. No assignment = no vested rights = death is irrelevant. The choice reverses the logical dependency.

**Verdict: CUT — NOT_TRUE (backwards logic).**

### Choice B: "Yes, because Lydia is an intended beneficiary of the Paul–Timothy contract."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This reframes the problem as a third-party beneficiary question. But look at the frame: Timothy's contract with Paul was for a custom awning. Where in the stem does it say Timothy intended to benefit Lydia? The writing was between Paul and Lydia — Timothy may not even know about it. The "intended beneficiary" frame doesn't fit the facts.
2. **Student label:** Intended-beneficiary bait.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would require evidence that Timothy understood and intended that Lydia would directly benefit from his contractual duty. No such evidence exists.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** An intended beneficiary must be one whom both the promisee intended to benefit and whom the promisor understood would directly benefit. Lydia is at best an incidental beneficiary — Timothy's duty was to pay Paul for an awning, not to benefit Lydia.

**Verdict: CUT — NOT_RESPONSIVE (doctrine misfit).**

### Choice C: "No, because the writing Paul gave to Lydia did not transfer to Lydia the right to receive payment from Timothy."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Look at the actual language: "Any money I *receive* from Timothy … will be paid to Lydia." Paul says "I receive" — meaning Paul still has the right to receive. 2. **Student label:** Correct — no effective assignment.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true and responsive version. The writing fails to transfer the right because it preserves Paul's right to receive.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 317(1) requires that the assignor's right be "extinguished in whole or in part." The writing "any money I receive" presupposes Paul's right to receive continues — no extinguishment, no assignment.

**Verdict: CREDITED ANSWER — both true and responsive.**

### Choice D: "Yes, because Paul had manifested an intent that Timothy pay the $10,000 directly to Lydia."

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Paul did intend for Lydia to get money — that's not the issue. The issue is whether his *intent to have her paid* is the same as *transferring the right to receive payment*.
