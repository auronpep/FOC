### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Ruth owns Harborlight. The conveyance gave Peter a defeasible fee and then gave the future interest to Ruth, a named third party. Once Peter stopped using the land as the private Bible-map print studio during Peter's life, the estate shifted automatically to Ruth. Martha did not need to reenter, and Martha was not the future-interest holder. **Gold Key:** when the condition is followed by "then to" a named third party, the future interest is not a grantor right of entry; it is an executory limitation that shifts title automatically to the third party. **Silver Key:** after the condition, track the named destination. Here the destination is Ruth, not Martha.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Martha owns Harborlight because the print-studio condition was breached.**

This choice sells the breach-alone move: once the condition happened, the answer must go back to the grantor. The breaker is the destination language. The deed did not say the land returned to Martha; it said "then to Ruth." The breached condition matters, but it sends title to the named third party, not to the grantor.

**C. Peter owns Harborlight in fee simple absolute because Martha did not reenter.**

This choice turns Martha's inaction into a complete cure for Peter. The breaker is that Peter's estate was never absolute after the condition occurred. The deed gave Ruth the shifting future interest, so no reentry by Martha was required. **Gold Key:** the right-of-entry idea belongs to the grantor-retained future-interest frame; a third-party gift-over cuts off the first grantee automatically.

**D. Peter owns Harborlight until Martha exercises a right of entry.**

This is the dominant trap. It borrows the real rule for a fee simple subject to condition subsequent: the grantor must act to retake the property. The breaker is that the future interest was not kept by Martha. The deed gave it to Ruth. **Silver Key:** do not let the word "if" pull you into a right-of-entry frame before you check who is named after the condition.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The source row tests the distinction between a grantor-retained power of termination/right of entry and a third-party executory limitation. The credited outcome is unchanged: the named third party owns after the condition occurs.

Black-letter verification: a fee simple subject to a condition subsequent leaves a right of entry, also called a power of termination, in the grantor; the grantor must take action to reclaim possession after breach. By contrast, a fee simple subject to an executory limitation gives the future interest to someone other than the grantor and shifts automatically to that third party when the stated event occurs. The deed here says that if Peter stops the stated use during his life, the land goes "then to Ruth." Ruth, not Martha, holds the future interest. When Peter stopped the stated use during his life, Ruth's executory interest became possessory automatically.

Authority floor: Restatement of Property section 46, comment j describes language creating a fee simple subject to executory limitation as language showing that, on the stated event, a fee simple estate terminates in favor of an estate created in a person other than the conveyor. Kennewick Public Hospital District v. Diocese of Olympia, 196 P.3d 1006 (Wash. Ct. App. 2008), applies that Restatement framing and distinguishes executory-limitation language from grantor reverter language. Cornell Wex's entries on executory interest, fee simple subject to condition subsequent, and right of entry also confirm the exam distinction: a third-party executory interest operates automatically, while a right of entry requires action by the grantor.
