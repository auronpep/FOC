### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**A is correct.** The bakery's promise was a gratuitous promise — Boaz's retirement was "at his option," so the retirement was not a condition of the promise and no bargained-for exchange existed. Consideration is therefore absent. But the promise is still enforceable under the doctrine of promissory estoppel: (1) there was a clear and definite promise in the signed resolution; (2) Silas had reason to expect the promise would induce Boaz to act, because the conversation about Boaz's bookshop dream had been held a month before the resolution was signed; (3) Boaz actually relied — he retired, signed a five-year lease, and committed $28,000 of his savings; and (4) injustice can be avoided only by enforcement, because Boaz's chronic back condition prevents him from returning to bookkeeping work and he has no other realistic income source.

**Gold Key:** Promissory estoppel can enforce a gift promise when the promisor reasonably expected substantial reliance, the promisee actually relied, and injustice can be avoided only by enforcement.

**Silver Key:** When the promise says "at his option," the retirement is the promisee's unilateral choice, not a condition of the promise — no consideration flowed, so the bargain theory is dead on arrival.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B is incorrect.** The "win because retirement was bargained-for consideration" answer sounds like a classic consideration play, and the trap it sells is that any retirement-in-response-to-a-promise is consideration. The breaker is the **option clause**: the resolution conditioned the pension on Boaz's *option* to retire, not on his retirement itself. Because Boaz could have stayed and still received the pension, his retirement was not the price of the promise, and no bargained-for exchange existed. The student-friendly version of the move is the **Silver Key** above: "at his option" is the structural tell that consideration has failed.

**C is incorrect (dominant trap).** The "lose because the pension was an unenforceable gift promise" answer taps the well-known black-letter rule that gift promises are unenforceable for lack of consideration. The student version of the move is to stop at that rule. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: gift promises *can* be enforced when the promisor reasonably expects reliance, the promisee actually relies, and injustice can be avoided only by enforcement. Here all three elements are present — the conversation about the bookshop set up foreseeability, the retirement and $28,000 lease/inventory commitment are substantial reliance, and Boaz's unemployability is the injustice element.

**D is incorrect.** The "lose because he was an employee-at-will" answer applies the wrong doctrinal frame to the question. At-will status governs whether the employer can terminate the employment relationship; it says nothing about whether a separate, fully formed promise (the lifetime pension) is enforceable. Boaz's at-will status is a fact about the past employment relationship, not a defense to a separate contract claim on a different promise. The breaker is a `misfit` filter: the at-will doctrine is simply not in play on the question the call actually asks.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule remains the doctrine of promissory estoppel: a promise that the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance on the part of the promisee, and which does induce such action or forbearance, is binding if injustice can be avoided only by enforcement of the promise. The remedy may be limited as justice requires. *Restatement (Second) of Contracts* § 90 (1981).

The classic application of this rule to a gratuitous pension promise is *Feinberg v. Pfeiffer Co.*, 322 S.W.2d 163 (Mo. Ct. App. 1959), which held that a board-resolution pension promise could be enforced against an employer on a reliance theory when the employee retired in reliance on the promise and the employer later stopped paying. The Missouri Court of Appeals treated the resolution as a definite promise, the prior conversation about the employee's retirement plans as the basis for foreseeability of reliance, the retirement itself as the substantial change in position, and the employee's inability to return to work as the injustice element. The same four-element structure is satisfied on the new facts (signed resolution; conversation about the bookshop; retirement plus a five-year lease and $28,000 inventory commitment; chronic back condition preventing return to bookkeeping).

The original answer's reasoning is preserved verbatim in legal substance: (1) the gratuitous promise is not enforceable as a bargain because the retirement was "at his option"; (2) the gratuitous promise is enforceable under detrimental reliance; (3) reliance was foreseeable because of the prior conversation; (4) reliance was actual and substantial; (5) justice requires enforcement because the promisee is no longer employable. The new surface (bookshop, bakery, $1,400/month, $28,000 lease and inventory, seven months of payment, declining retail sales, chronic back condition) does not move any of those legal points.

The black-letter verification therefore meets the authority floor: Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90 supplies the rule, and *Feinberg v. Pfeiffer Co.*, 322 S.W.2d 163 (Mo. Ct. App. 1959), supplies the leading on-point case for gratuitous pension promises enforced on a reliance theory. There is no doctrinal-currency hotlist issue (this is a contracts doctrine, not a constitutional or evidence doctrine). ## distilled_core_question

After a long-tenured at-will employee retires and commits substantial savings to a retirement venture in reliance on a written, signed promise of a lifetime pension that the employer later revokes, will the employee win on a contract theory — and on which theory?

## call_and_prediction

Call: In a suit against the bakery for breach of contract, Boaz will probably

Prediction: A "win" choice built on detrimental reliance should win. A "win" choice built on consideration will fail because the option clause prevents a bargained-for exchange. Any "lose" choice will fail on the facts unless it is a true doctrinal defense, and neither the gift-promise rule nor the at-will status supplies such a defense here.

## trigger_facts

- Boaz, 62, worked 32 years for a small family bakery as a senior bookkeeper-at-will.
- Boaz told co-owner Silas about his post-retirement dream of opening a used-bookshop.
- A month later, Silas handed Boaz a written, signed resolution of the managing members promising a $1,400-per-month lifetime pension.
- The promise was conditioned on Boaz's option to leave, not on his leaving itself.
- The bakery had no regularized retirement plan for at-will employees.
- Boaz retired, signed a five-year lease on a small storefront, and committed $28,000 of his savings to stock the bookshop.
- Boaz received seven months of the $1,400 monthly pension and then a letter stopping the pension.
- Boaz's chronic back condition prevents him from returning to bookkeeping work.

## governing_c3_lane

Lane: Contracts → Formation → Promissory Estoppel.

Real tension: Will the student stop at the gift-promise rule and pick the wrong "lose" choice, or will the student see the four-element reliance theory and lock in the correct "win" choice?

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### Choice A

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal.** This is the only choice that names reliance as the doctrinal hook, and the four Restatement § 90 elements are all visible in the stem: definite promise (the signed resolution), foreseeable reliance (the bookshop conversation a month before the resolution), actual reliance (retirement plus a five-year lease and $28,000 inventory commitment), and injustice (chronic back condition prevents return to bookkeeping work).
2. **Student label.** Detrimental reliance.
3. **What a true / responsive version would look like.** Exactly this. A correct answer must point to substantial reliance on a promise that the promisor should reasonably have expected to induce reliance, combined with an injustice that can be avoided only by enforcement.
4. **Lawyer confirmation.** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90; *Feinberg v. Pfeiffer Co.*, 322 S.W.2d 163 (Mo. Ct. App. 1959).

### Choice B

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal.** The choice invokes the consideration doctrine, but the resolution conditioned the pension on Boaz's *option* to retire, not on his retirement itself. Boaz could have stayed and still received the pension, so his retirement was not the price of the promise.
2. **Student label.** Option clause kills consideration.
3. **What a true / responsive version would look like.** A consideration-based answer would have to identify retirement as a condition of the promise (a bargained-for exchange). The at-his-option language forecloses that reading.
4. **Lawyer confirmation.** Consideration requires a bargained-for exchange; an "at his option" unilateral benefit does not exchange retirement for the promise.

### Choice C

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal.** The choice states a true black-letter rule — gift promises are generally unenforceable for lack of consideration — but omits the promissory estoppel exception. The Gold Key defeats it.
2. **Student label.** Gift-promise half-truth.
3. **What a true / responsive version would look like.** A correct "lose" choice would have to identify a true doctrinal defense, not a half-truth that omits the controlling exception. The gift-promise rule is correct as far as it goes, but the four-element reliance theory applies.
4. **Lawyer confirmation.** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90; *Feinberg v. Pfeiffer Co.*, 322 S.W.2d 163 (Mo. Ct. App. 1959).

### Choice D

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal.** The at-will doctrine is about whether an employer can terminate the employment relationship; it is not a defense to a separate contract claim on a separate, fully formed promise (the lifetime pension). The choice answers a different question than the one the call actually asks.
2. **Student label.** Wrong doctrinal frame.
3. **What a true / responsive version would look like.** A correct "lose" choice would have to identify a defense specific to the lifetime-pension promise. At-will status is not that defense.
4. **Lawyer confirmation.** At-will employment doctrine concerns termination of the employment relationship, not the enforceability of a separate lifetime-pension promise.

## final_student_script

This is a gratuitous lifetime pension question, not a consideration question, so first throw out the "win on consideration" answer: the resolution says "at his option," so retirement was not a condition of the promise. Then throw out the at-will answer: at-will status is about terminating the employment relationship, not about whether a separate pension promise is enforceable. The "lose because it's a gift promise" answer looks like the right rule, but the Gold Key says promissory estoppel can enforce a gift promise when all four reliance elements are met — and they are met here. The correct "win" answer is the one that names reliance: pick `A`.
