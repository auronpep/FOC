### 7. Full right-answer explanation

A is correct. Daniel did not simply find money loose in the world. The money landed in the bookstore's account, and Daniel used the accounting access his employer gave him for institutional payment work to move those funds to himself. Gold Key: when the defendant's lawful access or possession comes first and the fraudulent conversion comes later, embezzlement is the better label than larceny. Silver Key: once two answers say "embezzlement," do not stop at the shared label; choose the one whose reason names the access-to-conversion bridge.

The mistaken refund still belonged to someone with superior rights, and Daniel knew it was not his. His authorized system access explains why this is not the clean common-law larceny pattern of an outsider's trespassory taking. The best answer is embezzlement.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

D is attractive because Daniel is an employee and the money was sitting in his employer's account, so the result feels like obvious employee theft. The breaker is that the employee label alone does not do the legal work. Gold Key: embezzlement needs lawful access or possession followed by fraudulent conversion; the stronger answer identifies Daniel's entrusted accounting access, not merely his payroll status.

B is attractive because Daniel moved money that was not his, and students often translate any wrongful taking into larceny. The breaker is built into the choice's own wording: larceny is the trespassory-taking crime, so "without a trespassory taking" points away from larceny rather than toward it.

C is attractive because the refund was misdirected before Daniel touched it, so the property chain feels messy. The breaker is that a clerical mistake does not erase ownership. The printer or intended recipient retained superior rights, and Daniel's knowledge of the mistake makes his later diversion a fraudulent conversion rather than a free-for-all.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested issue is the distinction between larceny and embezzlement in crimes against property. The source row listed `73030100` for larceny, but the researched credited outcome turns on embezzlement, and `OUTLINE_CODES_COMPLETE.md` contains the deeper fitting code `73030500  Crimes against Property > Embezzlement`. That code is used consistently here.

At common law, larceny requires a trespassory taking and carrying away of another's personal property with intent to permanently deprive. Embezzlement covers fraudulent appropriation or conversion by a person to whom property was entrusted or into whose hands it lawfully came. The Supreme Court stated that embezzlement differs from larceny because the original taking is lawful or with the owner's consent, while larceny requires felonious intent at the taking. See *Moore v. United States*, 160 U.S. 268 (1895), https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/160/268/. Federal bank-theft statutes also reflect the modern bank-employee pattern by separately covering embezzlement, stealing, purloining, knowing conversion, and willful misapplication of bank funds. See 18 U.S.C. ch. 31, including 18 U.S.C. § 656, https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&path=%2Fprelim%40title18%2Fpart1%2Fchapter31.

Applied here, Daniel had authorized access to the bookstore's accounting system for institutional payment processing. He then knowingly diverted money with superior ownership rights to himself. That makes embezzlement the best-fitting answer. The original source row contains tension because one supplied wrong-answer explanation says the particular funds were not specifically entrusted to the employee, while the keyed answer treats authorized institutional-funds access as sufficient. The transform preserves the keyed legal outcome and flags the tension, but the researched embezzlement rule supports the answer when lawful access through employment is the access that enables the later conversion.

No doctrinal-currency hotlist issue is triggered.
