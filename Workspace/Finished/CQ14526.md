## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Mary owned a private Christian retreat center that used a large canvas tabernacle tent for outdoor Bible conferences. She contracted with Stephen, an experienced event-rigging contractor, to take down and crate the tent for $1,000 "once the last conference service ended." The final service ended in late September, and Mary promptly told Stephen that the tent was ready to be taken down. Stephen delayed because he had other customers to serve. Mary was concerned that the delay might cause the tent to be lost, for strong windstorms were common in that area in early October. Before Stephen took down the tent, a windstorm destroyed it.

Is Stephen liable for the loss of the tent?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A contractor delays a time-triggered performance, and the delayed property is destroyed by a common seasonal risk that an experienced contractor had reason to anticipate. Is the contractor liable for that loss?

### 3. Final answer choices

D. No, because the contract did not establish a time for Stephen's performance.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Stephen is liable if, when the contract was made, he had reason to foresee the tent's destruction as a probable result of delay. The facts give that reason: Stephen was an experienced event-rigging contractor, the tent was to be taken down after the final late-September service, and early-October windstorms were common in that area. Gold Key: consequential damages turn on reason to foresee the kind of loss at contract formation, not on whether the injured party gave a later, specific warning. Silver Key: do not stop at the yes/no result; test whether the reason given is the contract damages limiter.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because it makes Mary's silence about the exact windstorm sound decisive. The breaker is the Gold Key: foreseeability can come from the circumstances known or reasonably knowable when the contract is made; a specific warning is not the only path.

C is attractive because it reaches the same yes result as the credited answer. The breaker is the reason. Contract damages do not sweep in every consequence of breach; they are limited by foreseeability and other damages limits.

D is attractive because the contract did not give a calendar date. The breaker is the visible time trigger. "Once the last conference service ended" established when Stephen's performance was due, so the absence of a calendar date does not eliminate the duty to perform.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. This item tests consequential damages and foreseeability in contract remedies.

Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 351, damages are not recoverable for loss that the breaching party did not have reason to foresee as a probable result of breach when the contract was made. Loss may be foreseeable because it follows in the ordinary course of events or because of special circumstances that the breaching party had reason to know. The leading Hadley v. Baxendale framework likewise limits consequential damages to losses naturally arising from the breach or losses within the parties' contemplation at contract formation.

Authority layer: Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 351, https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/4201-contracts-2022/resources/1.2.3-restatement-2d-351-unforeseeable-damages/; Hadley v. Baxendale, 156 Eng. Rep. 145, 9 Exch. 341 (1854), https://lawlibrarycollections.umn.edu/classic-cases-contract-hadley-v-baxendale.

Stephen is an experienced event-rigging contractor. The contract tied takedown to the end of the late-September conference. Strong early-October windstorms were common in that area. Those facts support that, at contract formation, Stephen had reason to foresee loss of the tent as a probable result of delaying performance. Mary did not have to give a separate, specific windstorm warning after the contract was formed. Stephen is liable for the loss because that loss was foreseeable under the governing rule.
