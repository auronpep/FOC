## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** This is evidence of bias. It shows that the declarant (Timothy) had a motive to implicate Barnabas falsely, because by doing so he would remove Barnabas from the leadership position that Timothy wanted for himself. Evidence of bias is considered important and, generally speaking, it is liberally admitted. Note that Timothy can be impeached even though he is not at trial to testify. Federal Rule of Evidence (FRE) 806 allows parties to impeach a hearsay declarant in the same ways that would be permitted if the declarant were to testify. This is because a hearsay declarant is essentially a witness in the case. The bias evidence directly attacks the declarant's credibility by showing a powerful personal motive to lie.

**Gold Key:** When a hearsay declarant is unavailable, FRE 806 treats the declarant as if he had testified, so any impeachment that would be allowed against a live witness — including extrinsic evidence of bias — is allowed against the declarant. Bias is one of the most powerful and liberally admitted forms of impeachment.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect.** Under FRE 609(a)(2), misdemeanor convictions are not admissible to impeach a witness (or hearsay declarant under FRE 806) unless they involved dishonesty or a false statement. Disorderly conduct convictions at youth events do not involve dishonesty or false statement. If the misdemeanor convictions had been for, say, lying to a government official or perjury-related conduct, then they would have been admissible to impeach the declarant. The trap sells the misconception that "any prior conviction" or "any bad behavior" can be used to attack credibility.

**B is incorrect (dominant trap).** Expert testimony on credibility is usually found inadmissible because credibility issues are for the jury, not for an expert. There have been a few cases in which expert testimony on credibility has been permitted, but the question asks for the most likely evidence to be admitted, and this is not the best answer. The trap is attractive because it sounds like sophisticated "forensic psychology" that explains why the declarant lied, but the law reserves credibility determinations for the jury and generally excludes such expert opinions.

**D is incorrect.** This is "bad act" evidence and to be admissible to impeach a witness (or hearsay declarant) under FRE 608, it must tend to prove that the witness is an untruthful person. If its probative value is substantially outweighed by the risk of prejudice, confusion, and delay, then it must be excluded under FRE 403. Courts have ruled that evidence of illicit commercial activity (drug dealing or piracy of protected works) is only minimally probative of truthfulness, and therefore is usually inadmissible to impeach the witness. While it is probably within the trial court's discretion to admit this evidence in some cases, the question asks for the most likely evidence to be admitted, and this is not the best answer. Additionally, impeachment by prior bad acts may not be proved up by extrinsic evidence.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification AND Legal Reasoning For The Right Answer

The tested issue remains Evidence, Presentation of Evidence, impeachment by bias of a hearsay declarant under FRE 806. The legal issue and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row: bias evidence showing a motive to falsely implicate the defendant to remove him from a leadership position the declarant wanted is the evidence most likely to be admitted.

The authority floor is satisfied by Federal Rule of Evidence 806 and supporting case law on bias impeachment. FRE 806 provides: "When a hearsay statement — or a statement described in Rule 801(d)(2)(C), (D), or (E) — has been admitted in evidence, the declarant's credibility may be attacked, and if attacked may be supported, by any evidence that would be admissible for those purposes if the declarant had testified as a witness." The Advisory Committee Note explains that the declarant is "in effect a witness" whose credibility should be subject to impeachment as though he had testified. 

Bias impeachment is a core, liberally admitted method. See United States v. Abel, 469 U.S. 45 (1984) (common membership in an organization probative of bias; extrinsic evidence admissible to prove bias; Rule 608(b) does not bar extrinsic bias proof). FRE 609(a)(2) limits misdemeanor convictions to those involving dishonesty or false statement. FRE 608(b) permits inquiry into specific instances of conduct probative of untruthfulness on cross-examination but bars extrinsic proof of such acts in most circumstances, and courts routinely find drug-related or commercial illicit activity minimally probative of truthfulness. Expert testimony on a witness's general veracity or tendency to fabricate is disfavored because it invades the jury's role on credibility. See, e.g., United States v. Barnard, 490 F.2d 907 (9th Cir. 1973) (expert testimony on credibility generally excluded).
