### 1. Final question

Lydia won an $84,000 tort judgment for injuries she suffered when two privately hired workers negligently dropped a heavy soundboard during a Lenten concert rehearsal in a rented community hall. ### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Lydia has an $84,000 judgment against two jointly and severally liable tortfeasors. That means she may collect the judgment from either tortfeasor, or from both in any combination, until she has received one full recovery. She has already collected $9,000, so the unpaid balance is $75,000. Gold Key: contribution allocates loss between tortfeasors; it does not cap the plaintiff's collection right. Silver Key: first separate the plaintiff's collection question from the defendants' later contribution question, then subtract only the amount already collected.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because it treats Paul's 65 percent fault allocation as Lydia's collection ceiling. That percentage matters to contribution between Peter and Paul, not to Lydia's right to collect the unpaid balance from a jointly and severally liable tortfeasor.

C is attractive because it does two seemingly careful things: it starts with Paul's 65 percent share and then credits the amount already collected. Gold Key: the percentage-share formula belongs to contribution between tortfeasors, so using it as Lydia's cap answers the wrong payment relationship.

D is attractive because joint and several liability sounds like Lydia can demand the whole judgment from Paul. The breaker is the one-satisfaction limit: Lydia has already received $9,000, so another $84,000 would overpay the judgment.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: a plaintiff with a judgment against jointly and severally liable tortfeasors may collect the entire judgment from any one liable tortfeasor, but prior collections reduce the remaining amount collectible so the plaintiff receives only one satisfaction. Restatement (Third) of Torts: Apportionment of Liability section 10 states the joint-and-several-liability principle that each jointly and severally liable defendant is liable for the full extent of the plaintiff's recoverable damages. Cornell Wex likewise describes joint and several liability as making each liable party independently liable for the full extent of the injury and allowing the plaintiff to collect the full judgment from any one of them. Sources checked: https://www.ali.org/publications/restatement-law-third/torts-apportionment-liability and https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/joint_and_several_liability.

Applying that rule, Lydia's judgment is $84,000. Peter's $9,000 payment partly satisfies the judgment. The remaining unpaid balance is $75,000. Paul's 65 percent fault allocation does not limit Lydia's collection right; it may matter later if Paul seeks contribution from Peter.
