## 1. Final Question

Daniel runs a produce-hauling service for a hillside orchard, using heavy flatbed wagons to move crates of fruit to market. One afternoon, one of Daniel's loaded wagons was left parked illegally on the road shoulder, just past a blind curve on a narrow county road. Ruth, driving her pickup at an excessive speed, lost traction and skidded into the parked wagon as she came around the curve. The wagon was not damaged, but Ruth was injured. Ruth has brought an action against Daniel to recover damages for her loss resulting from the accident. The jury determined that both parties were negligent, but that Daniel was less negligent than Ruth. The jurisdiction follows a pure comparative negligence rule.

In this action, Ruth should recover

**A.** nothing, because Daniel was less negligent than Ruth.

**B.** her entire loss, reduced by a percentage that reflects the negligence attributed to Ruth.

**C.** her entire loss, because Daniel's wagon suffered no damage.

**D.** nothing, because Daniel was not an active or efficient cause of Ruth's loss.

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** In a pure comparative negligence jurisdiction, a negligent plaintiff is never barred from recovery by her own fault, no matter how large her share. Her damages are simply reduced by the percentage of fault the factfinder attributes to her. Here the jury found both parties negligent, with Ruth more at fault than Daniel. Under the pure rule, Ruth still recovers — she collects her entire loss minus the portion attributable to her own excessive speed. The fact that she was *more* negligent than Daniel lowers the award but does not eliminate it. That is the defining feature of the **pure** rule, and it is what separates B from the "recovers nothing" answers (A and D) and from the un-reduced "entire loss" answer (C).

**Gold Key:** Under a **pure** comparative-negligence rule, a negligent plaintiff recovers full damages reduced by her own percentage of fault — *even when she is more at fault than the defendant*. Only a **modified** comparative rule (the 50%/51% bar) cuts off a plaintiff who is equally or more at fault; the old **contributory**-negligence rule bars any negligent plaintiff entirely. The label in the stem is the answer key.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### Choice A (Original B) — ⚠️ DOMINANT TRAP

**Why it's attractive:** The stem tells the student in plain words that "Daniel was less negligent than Ruth." Many students carry a fuzzy, blended memory of comparative negligence in which a plaintiff who is *more* at fault than the defendant takes nothing — which is exactly the result under a **modified** comparative regime (the 50%/51% bar) and under the older **contributory**-negligence rule. The choice rewards the student who pattern-matches "plaintiff more at fault → barred" and stops, without checking which variant the jurisdiction actually follows. The cognitive move: see "less negligent," reach for the bar, and lock the answer.

**The breaker:** The stem expressly says the jurisdiction follows a *pure* comparative negligence rule. Under the pure rule there is no fault ceiling on the plaintiff — recovery is reduced in proportion to her fault but never cut off. The "more-negligent-plaintiff-is-barred" rule is a real rule, but it belongs to the neighboring *modified* variant, not the one the stem named. The choice answers the question the stem did not ask. **Gold Key:** Pure comparative reduces; only modified comparative (50%/51%) or contributory negligence bars a more-at-fault plaintiff. **Silver Key:** When a stem labels the jurisdiction ("pure comparative"), the label is the answer key — any choice whose reasoning only works under a different regime is a wrong-jurisdiction bait.

### Choice C (Original D)

**Why it's attractive:** The choice lands on the right side of the recover/don't-recover line ("her entire loss") and offers a tidy-sounding reason — Daniel's wagon came through undamaged, so surely Ruth, the one who got hurt, should be made whole. It sells the intuition that the injured party recovers everything when the other party's property is fine. The cognitive move: pick "recovers," grab the sympathetic full-recovery figure, and accept the property-damage fact as the reason.

**The breaker:** Two problems. First, whether Daniel's wagon was damaged is irrelevant to what *Ruth* recovers for *her* injuries — it is a non-sequitur reason. Second, and decisively, "her entire loss" omits the reduction the pure rule requires. Ruth was negligent too, and more so than Daniel; her award must be cut by her own fault percentage. "Entire loss" with no reduction is the contributory-era all-or-nothing thinking running the other direction. **Gold Key:** Pure comparative gives the plaintiff her loss *reduced* by her own share — not the whole loss, and not nothing.

### Choice D (Original A)

**Why it's attractive:** "Active or efficient cause" has a lawyerly ring, and there is a grain of intuition that a vehicle sitting parked is a passive thing — a mere condition the speeding driver ran into — rather than an active cause of the crash. The choice invites the student to treat Daniel's parked wagon as too inert to count as a legal cause and to deny recovery on that ground. The cognitive move: recharacterize the parked wagon as a passive condition, deny causation, and bar recovery.

**The breaker:** Negligence liability requires actual cause and proximate cause — not an "active or efficient" cause; that phrase invents a requirement the law does not impose. An illegally parked vehicle left just past a blind curve can be both a cause-in-fact and a proximate cause of a foreseeable collision. More directly, the jury already found Daniel negligent and apportioned fault to him, which necessarily presupposes that his conduct was a contributing cause. A choice that says he "was not an active or efficient cause" contradicts the verdict the stem hands you. **Gold Key:** Pure comparative still requires recovery here; once the defendant is found negligent and assigned a fault share, causation is established, and the plaintiff's award is reduced — not zeroed.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule (comparative negligence — pure):** Under a *pure* comparative-negligence system, a plaintiff's contributory fault does not bar recovery; it diminishes the recoverable damages in proportion to the plaintiff's share of the total fault, with no cutoff even if the plaintiff's share exceeds the defendant's. This is the rule adopted in jurisdictions following *Li v. Yellow Cab Co.*, 13 Cal. 3d 804, 532 P.2d 1226 (Cal. 1975) (judicially adopting pure comparative negligence and abandoning the all-or-nothing contributory-negligence bar). The Restatement (Third) of Torts: Apportionment of Liability § 7 (2000) states the general principle that a plaintiff's negligence reduces recovery in proportion to the plaintiff's comparative responsibility.

**Pure vs. modified (the tested distinction):** A *modified* comparative system bars a plaintiff whose fault reaches the statutory ceiling — the "50% rule" (barred at 50% or more) or the "51% rule" (barred when more at fault than the defendant). A plaintiff who is "more negligent than the defendant" recovers nothing under a modified rule but still recovers a reduced amount under the pure rule. The stem's choice of the *pure* rule is therefore outcome-determinative on the dominant trap (Choice A).

**Governing rule (causation):** Negligence liability requires that the defendant's breach be both a factual cause (Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical and Emotional Harm § 26) and within the scope of liability/proximate cause (§ 29). There is no separate "active or efficient cause" element; a stationary but negligently placed object can be a legal cause of a foreseeable collision. The jury's negligence finding and fault apportionment presuppose that Daniel's conduct was a contributing cause, defeating Choice D.

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

### Program Frame

This item tests **Negligence → Defenses → Comparative Negligence** (outline code 61020603). The core conflict: a jury finds both parties negligent, with the plaintiff more at fault than the defendant, in a jurisdiction that follows the **pure** comparative-negligence rule. The question is what the plaintiff recovers. This is a Core-difficulty item requiring the student to (1) read the jurisdiction label as outcome-determinative, (2) hold the pure-vs-modified-vs-contributory distinction, and (3) resist the reflex that a more-at-fault plaintiff is barred.

### Student Access Test

A student who has learned the pure-vs-modified comparative distinction (the Gold Key) can solve this item. The CUT phase removes the two visibly defective choices (the un-reduced "entire loss" and the causation-denial that contradicts the verdict). The CLASH between the modified-bar answer and the correct reduced-recovery answer is resolved by the stem's "pure comparative" label. The Gold Key confirms that pure comparative reduces but never bars.

### Question Data

**Stem:** Daniel runs a produce-hauling service for a hillside orchard, using heavy flatbed wagons. One of his loaded wagons was left parked illegally on the road shoulder, just past a blind curve on a narrow county road. Ruth, driving her pickup at an excessive speed, skidded into the parked wagon as she came around the curve. The wagon was not damaged, but Ruth was injured. Ruth sues Daniel. The jury found both parties negligent, but Daniel less negligent than Ruth. The jurisdiction follows a pure comparative negligence rule.

**Call:** In this action, Ruth should recover

**Choices:**
- A: nothing, because Daniel was less negligent than Ruth.
- B: her entire loss, reduced by a percentage that reflects the negligence attributed to Ruth.
- C: her entire loss, because Daniel's wagon suffered no damage.
- D: nothing, because Daniel was not an active or efficient cause of Ruth's loss.

### Distilled Core Question

A jury finds both a plaintiff and a defendant negligent in a collision, but the plaintiff more at fault than the defendant, in a pure comparative negligence jurisdiction. What does the plaintiff recover?

### Call and Prediction

The call asks what the plaintiff recovers. The stem hands the student the dispositive fact: the jurisdiction follows the **pure** comparative-negligence rule. Under the pure rule, a negligent plaintiff's recovery is reduced by her own fault percentage with no cutoff — even when she is more at fault than the defendant. Prediction: she recovers a reduced amount. This points to B. The student must then reject the two "recovers nothing" answers (which apply the wrong regime or deny causation) and the un-reduced "entire loss" answer.

### Trigger Facts

1. **Daniel's wagon was parked illegally just past a blind curve** — static negligence (improper placement creating a road hazard); supports Daniel's fault share.
2. **Ruth was driving at an excessive speed and skidded into the wagon** — the plaintiff's active negligence; supports her larger fault share.
3. **The wagon was undamaged, but Ruth was injured** — sets up the irrelevant "no damage to defendant's property" reasoning in choice C.
4. **The jury found both negligent, Daniel less negligent than Ruth** — the comparative-fault apportionment; baits the modified-comparative reflex (choice A) and presupposes Daniel's causation (defeating choice D).
5. **The jurisdiction follows a pure comparative negligence rule** — the dispositive label; pure = reduce, never bar.

### Governing C3 Lane

**CUT → CLASH → CALL**

- **CUT:** Remove C (un-reduced "entire loss" ignores the plaintiff's own fault; wagon condition is irrelevant) and D (contradicts the jury's negligence/causation finding; invents an "active or efficient cause" element).
- **CLASH:** A ("nothing, less negligent defendant") vs. B ("reduced recovery"). Axis: does a more-at-fault plaintiff lose recovery? Splitting fact: the jurisdiction is **pure** comparative. Pure = no fault ceiling.
- **CALL:** B is the only true and responsive choice.

### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "Nothing, because Daniel was less negligent than Ruth." (DOMINANT TRAP)**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The stem says the jurisdiction is **pure** comparative. "A more-negligent plaintiff recovers nothing" is the **modified** / contributory rule — a true rule, but in the neighboring jurisdiction-variant the stem did not select. Under pure comparative there is no fault ceiling; the more-at-fault plaintiff still recovers a reduced amount.
2. **Student label:** The Modified-Comparative Reflex.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This answer would be correct in a **modified** comparative jurisdiction (51% rule) where the plaintiff is more at fault than the defendant — but the stem chose the pure rule.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Pure comparative reduces but never bars (Li v. Yellow Cab Co., 532 P.2d 1226; Restatement (Third) Apportionment § 7). `Gold Key:` Pure comparative reduces; only modified (50%/51%) or contributory bars a more-at-fault plaintiff. `Silver Key:` The jurisdiction label is the answer key — a choice whose reasoning only works under a different regime is a wrong-jurisdiction bait.

**Choice B: "Her entire loss, reduced by a percentage that reflects the negligence attributed to Ruth." (CORRECT)**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Pure comparative: the plaintiff recovers her loss reduced by her own fault percentage, even though she is more at fault. The stem's "pure comparative" label dictates exactly this.
2. **Student label:** Correct — Reduced, Not Barred.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true and responsive answer.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Third) Apportionment § 7; Li v. Yellow Cab Co. Plaintiff's fault diminishes recovery proportionally with no cutoff.

**Choice C: "Her entire loss, because Daniel's wagon suffered no damage."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice lands on "recovers," but "her entire loss" ignores the reduction the pure rule requires for Ruth's own fault, and whether Daniel's wagon was damaged has nothing to do with Ruth's personal-injury recovery. The reason is a visible non-sequitur.
2. **Student label:** The Entire-Loss Overclaim.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A correct full-recovery answer would exist only if Ruth were found not negligent at all — then there would be nothing to reduce. Here she was negligent and more at fault, so the award must be reduced.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Pure comparative reduces the award by the plaintiff's fault share; an un-reduced "entire loss" misstates the measure, and the defendant's property damage is not an element of the plaintiff's recovery.

**Choice D: "Nothing, because Daniel was not an active or efficient cause of Ruth's loss."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** "Active or efficient cause" is not a negligence element. More directly, the jury found Daniel negligent and assigned him a fault share — which presupposes his conduct was a contributing cause. The choice contradicts the verdict the stem hands you.
2. **Student label:** The Passive-Condition Causation Denial.

4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Negligence requires factual cause and proximate cause (Restatement (Third) Physical Harm §§ 26, 29); a negligently parked vehicle can be both. There is no "active/efficient cause" requirement. `Silver Key:` When the jury has already found the defendant negligent and apportioned fault, causation is settled — a "he wasn't really a cause" choice fights the given facts.
