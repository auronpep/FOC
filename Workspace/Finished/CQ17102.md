## 1. Final question

Paul is charged with vandalizing the painted sign outside Lydia's Christian bookstore by gouging it with a screwdriver. Five minutes after the damage was discovered, a store clerk found a screwdriver with fresh blue paint flakes in a planter directly beneath the sign. The prosecutor offers the screwdriver. Paul objects that the bookstore's repair closet contained many screwdrivers and that no one saw him use that particular screwdriver.

How should the court rule, assuming the screwdriver is authenticated as the one found in the planter?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

An authenticated tool found near damaged property has fresh residue that connects it to the damage, but no witness saw the defendant use that exact tool. Is the tool relevant enough to admit?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** The screwdriver should be admitted. **Gold Key:** Relevance is a low admissibility threshold. If authenticated physical evidence has any tendency to make a consequential fact more probable, gaps in the connection usually affect weight, not admissibility. **Silver Key:** When the wrong answers demand stronger proof before admission, separate admissibility from persuasiveness.

The screwdriver was found directly beneath the damaged sign five minutes after the damage was discovered, and it had fresh blue paint flakes on it. Those facts make it at least somewhat more likely that the screwdriver was connected to the gouging. The repair-closet fact and the lack of an eyewitness make the exhibit less persuasive, but they do not erase its relevance. A jury can hear the evidence and decide how much weight to give it.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Exclude because no witness saw Paul gouge the sign with it.** This is attractive because an eyewitness would make the evidence feel certain. The breaker is that certainty is not the relevance test. Physical evidence can be relevant through circumstantial links, such as where it was found and what condition it was in. **Gold Key:** FRE relevance does not require direct eyewitness proof. The missing eyewitness attacks the strength of the inference, not admissibility.

**B. Admit only if Paul admits that he touched it.** This choice sells a confession-style shortcut: if Paul admits touching the screwdriver, the link is easy. The breaker is the word "only." A defendant's admission is not a prerequisite to admitting relevant physical evidence. Location, timing, and residue can create enough relevance without an admission.

**D. Exclude unless the prosecutor proves by a preponderance that it was the exact screwdriver used on the sign.** This is the dominant trap because "preponderance" sounds like a proper evidence threshold. The breaker is that the prosecutor does not have to prove the ultimate merits link before the exhibit comes in. The authenticated screwdriver's place and fresh paint flakes give it some tendency to connect to the vandalism; competing screwdriver possibilities are for the jury's weight assessment. **Silver Key:** Do not let a proof-strength word turn a relevance question into a mini-trial on the whole fact.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**Black-letter rule:** Under Federal Rule of Evidence 401, evidence is relevant if it has any tendency to make a consequential fact more or less probable. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 402, relevant evidence is admissible unless another source of law excludes it. If relevance depends on a fact, Federal Rule of Evidence 104(b) requires enough proof to support a finding of that fact; it does not require the proponent to conclusively prove the merits fact before admission.

**Authority and research sources:** FRE 401, FRE 402, and FRE 104(b). Research checked the current rule text at Legal Information Institute pages for Rule 401 (https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_401), Rule 402 (https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_402), and Rule 104 (https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_104). FRE 401 states the low "any tendency" test; FRE 402 admits relevant evidence absent another exclusion; FRE 104(b) uses a sufficient-support standard for conditional relevance.

**Application:** The issue is not whether the prosecutor has already proved beyond dispute that Paul used the screwdriver. The screwdriver is authenticated as the one found in the planter, and its location, timing, and fresh blue paint flakes make a consequential fact - whether the sign was gouged with that tool and whether Paul was connected to the vandalism - somewhat more probable. The existence of many screwdrivers and the lack of an eyewitness weaken the inference, but under FRE 401 and FRE 402 they do not make the exhibit irrelevant. No researched authority contradicts the supplied official key.

**Doctrinal currency:** The item does not involve the amended FRE 702 expert-testimony rule, the amended FRE 106 rule of completeness, or Smith v. Arizona confrontation/expert-basis issues. No hotlist update changes the credited outcome.

The tested rule, legal issue, trap structure, and credited outcome are unchanged: the admissibility threshold for relevant physical evidence is low, and weak proof of connection goes to weight.

**This item teaches:** The relevance threshold is low for authenticated physical evidence with circumstantial scene links.

**Fills:** weight versus admissibility; circumstantial physical evidence; over-demanding proof at the admissibility gate.

**Adjacent to master:** Authentication and identification; FRE 104(b) conditional relevance; FRE 403 discretionary exclusion.

## crossover_intersection_map

The criminal charge supplies the courtroom posture, but the tested move is Evidence, not Criminal Law. The vandalism facts matter only because they make the screwdriver consequential and explain why paint flakes and location are probative.

## review_truth

Relevant physical evidence is admissible if scene facts give it any tendency to make a consequential fact more probable; missing eyewitness or conclusive-link proof affects weight, not admissibility.
