### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is correct. Depraved-heart murder rests on extreme recklessness or malice, not a further specific-intent objective. Gold Key: voluntary intoxication may matter for some specific-intent elements, but it generally does not erase recklessness or malice recklessness when the defendant voluntarily got drunk. Silver Key: first identify the mental state the charge actually needs; if the charge is reckless malice, do not let a "too drunk to appreciate risk" story become a specific-intent defense. Daniel voluntarily drank and then created an obvious grave risk in a crowded room.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. Voluntary intoxication is limited as a mens rea defense and generally does not defeat crimes requiring recklessness, negligence, general intent, or malice recklessness. Model Penal Code section 2.08 provides the core rule: when recklessness establishes an element, self-induced intoxication does not make the actor's unawareness of the risk exculpatory if he would have been aware while sober. States following MPC-style language state the same rule directly; New Jersey Statutes section 2C:2-8 provides that self-induced intoxication is immaterial when recklessness is the mental element. Courts applying depraved-heart murder principles likewise recognize that voluntary drunkenness does not blot out the reckless malice needed for depraved-heart murder. People v. Langworthy and Weaver v. State quote the same principle: drunkenness does not negative a depraved heart by blotting out consciousness of risk. Daniel's voluntary intoxication therefore does not defeat second-degree murder based on extreme recklessness.

Sources: Model Penal Code section 2.08; New Jersey Statutes section 2C:2-8, https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-2c/section-2c-2-8/; People v. Langworthy, https://law.justia.com/cases/michigan/supreme-court/1982/65320-4.html; Weaver v. State, https://law.justia.com/cases/alabama/court-of-appeals-criminal/1991/cr-89-772-0.html.
