## 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Silver Key: this is a two-ground plus both/neither answer array. Test the evidence ground and the burden ground separately before selecting a combination answer.

The evidence ground fails. Gold Key: when a defendant raises entrapment, predisposition becomes a live rebuttal issue, so similar prior misconduct may be used to show willingness to commit that kind of offense. Timothy’s earlier counterfeit-ticket convictions were offered to rebut his claim that Barnabas induced an otherwise unwilling act.

The burden ground also fails. Gold Key: due process requires the prosecution to prove the offense elements, but a state may place the burden of proving an affirmative defense such as entrapment on the defendant when that defense does not negate an element of the offense. Because neither asserted reason supports reversal, the conviction should be affirmed.

## 8. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The official source row states that the original conviction was affirmed because the prior narcotics convictions were properly admitted to show willingness to commit the charged crime and because the state could require the defendant to prove entrapment by a preponderance.  The transformed item preserves both legal propositions.

For the evidence point, Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b)(2) allows other-crimes evidence for non-propensity purposes such as intent, knowledge, or absence of mistake, and the entrapment context specifically opens inquiry into predisposition. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) The Department of Justice’s entrapment manual summarizes Sorrells as permitting “an appropriate and searching inquiry” into predisposition once entrapment is claimed, and notes that predisposition may be shown by other crimes that might otherwise be inadmissible. ([Department of Justice][2]) Sherman likewise recognizes that prior criminal activities and prior disposition have often been admitted to prove predisposition, while warning about prejudice. ([Legal Information Institute][3])

For the burden point, Patterson v. New York confirms that due process requires the prosecution to prove offense elements beyond a reasonable doubt, but permits a state to require a defendant to carry the burden of persuasion on an affirmative defense that does not negate those elements. ([Justia Law][4]) The legal research does not contradict the supplied official key.
