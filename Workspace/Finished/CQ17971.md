### 7. Full right-answer explanation
A is correct. Ruth's deed did not fail just because it used the famous tract name and a directional acreage rather than the jurisdiction's customary survey-marker language. A deed description is sufficient when it identifies the property conveyed with reasonable clarity. Here, the Good Shepherd Apiary was famous and easily identifiable by name, and Daniel's deed carved out the east 30 acres of that identifiable parcel. Because that earlier deed effectively conveyed the east 30 acres to Ruth, Daniel no longer held that portion when he later gave Lydia a quitclaim deed.

Gold Key: A deed description does not need metes-and-bounds or government survey-marker wording if the land conveyed can be identified with reasonable certainty. A quitclaim deed then transfers only whatever interest the grantor still has; it does not pull back land already effectively conveyed.

Silver Key: Read the deeds chronologically. First decide whether Ruth's earlier deed worked; only then ask what interest Daniel had left for Lydia's quitclaim deed.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Real Property, Titles, Types of deeds, with verified outline code `84040204`. The item tests the interaction between a prior general warranty deed, a later quitclaim deed, and the sufficiency of a deed's property description.

The black-letter rule is that a deed must identify the property conveyed with reasonable certainty, but it need not use metes-and-bounds or government survey-marker terminology if the description otherwise supplies a reliable way to identify the land. AIC Management v. Crews, 246 S.W.3d 640, 645 (Tex. 2008), states the common deed-description principle: a real-property conveyance is valid when the writing furnishes, within itself or by reference to another existing writing, the means or data by which the particular land may be identified with reasonable certainty. See https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914b346add7b04934765417.

The deed-type layer also remains unchanged. A warranty deed conveys and warrants the described real estate, while a quitclaim deed conveys only the grantor's then-existing rights in the described premises. Washington's deed statutes are consistent with that distinction: RCW 64.04.030 describes the effect of a warranty deed, and RCW 64.04.050 treats a quitclaim deed as a conveyance of the grantor's then-existing legal and equitable rights. See https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=64.04&full=true.

Applied here, Daniel's deed to Ruth identified the Good Shepherd Apiary by its famous name and described the portion conveyed as the east 30 acres. The local custom of using government survey markers does not create a mandatory validity rule. Ruth therefore received the east 30 acres before Daniel later quitclaimed his remaining interest to Lydia. Lydia may receive whatever interest Daniel still had, but not the portion already conveyed to Ruth. The credited answer remains correct after the transform. No doctrinal-currency hotlist issue is triggered.
