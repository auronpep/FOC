## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** A phone call is authenticated against the alleged speaker by evidence from which a reasonable jury could find that the speaker is in fact that person. One well-recognized authentication path is *distinctive characteristics* under Federal Rule of Evidence 901(b)(4): the speaker demonstrates knowledge of facts that only a particular person — or a small, defined group — would have. The witness's unlisted dock-office direct line had been disclosed only to the defendant and a small handful of marina staff and a dockmate, so the caller's ability to reach that number sharply narrows the field of possible speakers. That makes the unlisted-number fact the best ground for admitting the call against Peter.

**Gold Key (GK-EVIDENCE-PHONE-AUTH-01):** On an *outgoing* call, the caller's self-identification is *not* sufficient authentication. Authentication rests on knowledge of facts only a particular person (or a small group) would have — a private number, internal information, restricted access, or restricted context.

**Silver Key (SK-EVIDENCE-PHONE-AUTH-01):** "Which choice *narrows the pool of possible speakers*?" Private-fact knowledge is a pool-narrower; self-identification, motive, and capability belief are not.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A — prior damaging testimony (motive).** This is the most attractive "common-sense" answer: a defendant who has a reason to threaten a witness is more likely to be the caller. The breaker is that authentication asks whether the speaker is in fact the defendant, not whether the defendant had a motive. The deposition testimony supplies motive; it does not, by itself, identify the caller. **Silver Key:** motive is a relevance/motive input, not a pool-narrower. The choice is answer-to-different-question and is filtered out as `NOT_RESPONSIVE`.

**C — witness's capability belief.** This sounds intuitive — the witness "knows" the defendant is the threatening type. The breaker is that a subjective belief about a person's character does nothing to show that *this caller* was *this person*. Capability is a propensity-style character inference, not an authentication fact. The choice is filtered as `NOT_RESPONSIVE` (`misfit`).

**D — caller self-identified as the defendant (dominant trap).** This is the trap. Self-identification feels powerful because the witness heard "this is Peter." But the self-identification exception runs the other way: it works when the *recipient* of a call hears a familiar voice identify itself, or when someone calls a number and the person who *answers* identifies himself. When the *caller* self-identifies on an outgoing call, the rule treats that statement as the very thing being offered for its truth — and that is exactly what an impostor could do. The unlisted-number fact is what makes this call authenticate; self-ID alone does not. **Gold Key:** outgoing-call self-identification is the textbook NOT_TRUE trap on this rule.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested rule (preserved).** Authentication under Federal Rule of Evidence 901(a) requires "evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what its proponent claims it is." Rule 901(b) lists illustrative methods, including Rule 901(b)(1) (witness with knowledge), Rule 901(b)(4) (distinctive characteristics), and Rule 901(b)(5) (voice identification). The Advisory Committee's Note to Rule 901 explicitly recognizes that "a telephone conversation may be irrelevant because … the speaker is not identified," and treats identification as a Rule 104(b) conditional-relevance question.

**Self-identification on an outgoing call — insufficient.** The established doctrine is that a caller's bare self-identification on an outgoing call is not, by itself, sufficient authentication. The well-known counter-rule is that a phone call may be authenticated if a party called Mr. A's number and the person who *answered* identified himself — that voice identification is reliable because the witness is hearing a familiar voice self-identify. The reverse — the caller says "this is Peter" — is the very fact at issue and does not authenticate. This is the doctrinal anchor for the dominant trap (choice D).

**Knowledge of a private fact — the credited path.** Authentication is satisfied when the speaker demonstrates knowledge of facts known only to a particular person. The unlisted-number fact here is a textbook Rule 901(b)(4) distinctive-characteristic: only the defendant and a small group knew the number, and the caller's ability to reach it narrows the field of possible speakers. This is the doctrinal anchor for the credited answer (choice B) and the original key C.

**Motive and belief — not authentication.** A defendant's prior adverse relationship with a witness may supply motive, but motive is a separate question from identity. A subjective belief about the speaker's character is a propensity-style inference, not an authentication fact.

**Authority floor (lawyer-confirmation).**
- Fed. R. Evid. 901(a), 901(b)(1), 901(b)(4), 901(b)(5); Advisory Committee Note to Rule 901.
- *United States v. Barbiere*, 666 F.2d 49, 56–57 (2d Cir. 1981) (authentication of telephone calls; voice identification by recipient; self-identification issues).
- *United States v. McMillan*, 405 F.2d 431 (8th Cir. 1968) (authentication of sound recordings / voice identification on phone calls; foundation required).

**Distilled Core Question.**
A witness receives a phone call that purports to be from the defendant, threatening her about her testimony. The witness's unlisted number had been given only to the defendant and a few other people. Which fact best authenticates the call against the defendant?

**Call and prediction.**
Call: "The call is most likely to be admitted against Peter if." This is an authentication call. Three choices are about the witness's relationship to or belief about the defendant; one choice is about a fact known only to a small group. Predict the choice that supplies the *authentication ground*, not the choice that supplies motive or character.

**Trigger facts.**
- The call purports to be from Peter.
- The call was placed to Lydia's **unlisted** dock-office direct line.
- That unlisted number had been given to only **four people**: Peter, two other marina staff, and one boat owner.
- The caller said "this is Peter" at the start of the call.
- Lydia had previously given deposition testimony in the same suit (motive).

**Choice-by-choice walkthrough.**

**A — Prior damaging testimony (motive).**
1. *C3 signal:* Prior deposition testimony is motive evidence, not authentication evidence. The breaker is that authentication asks whether the speaker is the defendant, not whether the defendant had a reason to call.
2. *Student label:* "Motive is not identity."
3. *What a true/responsive version would look like:* A fact that narrows the field of possible speakers — e.g., the speaker's knowledge of an internal fact known only to the defendant (or a small group).
4. *Lawyer confirmation:* FRE 901(a) requires evidence sufficient to support a finding that the speaker is the defendant. Motive is a separate relevance question and does not narrow the field of possible speakers. Filter broken: NOT_RESPONSIVE (`wrong_element`).

**B — Unlisted number known to a small group. (Correct.)**
1. *C3 signal:* The unlisted number is known to a small, defined group. Reaching it narrows the field of possible speakers to that group. This is the *distinctive-characteristic* authentication ground.
2. *Student label:* "Pool-narrower."
3. *What a true/responsive version would look like:* This is the true/responsive version.
4. *Lawyer confirmation:* FRE 901(b)(4) — distinctive characteristics. The unlisted number is a paradigmatic distinctive-characteristic authentication ground. The caller's ability to reach a number disclosed only to a small group supports a finding that the caller is in that group. Filter broken: none.

**C — Witness's capability belief.**
1. *C3 signal:* A subjective belief about a person's character is propensity-style, not authentication. It does not narrow the field of possible speakers.
2. *Student label:* "Subjective belief is not authentication."
3. *What a true/responsive version would look like:* An external fact linking the speaker to a particular person — e.g., voice, handwriting, internal information, restricted access, distinctive content.
4. *Lawyer confirmation:* Authentication requires an external fact that links the speaker to a particular person. A subjective belief is internal to the witness and does not narrow the field of possible speakers. Filter broken: NOT_RESPONSIVE (`misfit`).

**D — Caller self-identified as the defendant. (Dominant trap.)**
1. *C3 signal:* The caller saying "this is Peter" is the very fact at issue; an impostor could say the same thing. Self-identification works on incoming calls (where the recipient hears a familiar voice), not on outgoing calls.
2. *Student label:* "Outgoing-call self-identification is not authentication."
3. *What a true/responsive version would look like:* A fact that supplies knowledge of something only the defendant would know — e.g., an unlisted number, a private internal fact, a restricted-access detail.
4. *Lawyer confirmation:* Advisory Committee Note to FRE 901; the self-identification authentication ground runs the other way — it works when the recipient of a call hears a familiar voice identify itself or when the witness recognizes a known voice. When the *caller* self-identifies on an *outgoing* call, the statement is the very thing being offered for its truth and does not, by itself, authenticate. Filter broken: NOT_TRUE (`flat_misstatement`).
