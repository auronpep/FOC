## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** Under FRE 803(4), statements made for purposes of medical diagnosis or treatment are admissible as an exception to the hearsay rule. This exception covers not just statements about present symptoms, but also statements describing the **cause or source** of the injury, so long as they are **pertinent to diagnosis**. Here, the plaintiff's statement — "When the ladder gave way, I landed on my right wrist and heard a loud crack" — describes the mechanism of injury and is pertinent to determining the nature and extent of his wrist fracture. The fact that the examination was conducted in preparation for testimony does not destroy the diagnosis purpose; the physician was still attempting to form a medical diagnosis. The statement is admissible.

**Gold Key:** FRE 803(4) covers statements about the **cause** of an injury (not just present symptoms) if made for diagnosis and pertinent to diagnosis. The "diagnosis" purpose survives even when litigation is also a motive.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (Yes, since the statement is hearsay).** This choice is attractive because the statement *is* hearsay — it is an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of what it asserts (that the plaintiff fell and heard a crack). A student who spots the hearsay character but doesn't know the exceptions will stop here and pick this answer. **The breaker:** the statement falls within the FRE 803(4) medical-diagnosis exception, which specifically carves out statements made for diagnosis from the hearsay bar. The Gold Key — that FRE 803(4) covers cause-of-injury statements pertinent to diagnosis — kills this trap.

**B (Yes, since the examination was not performed for the purpose of diagnosis or treatment).** This choice is attractive because the facts *do* say the exam was "made specifically in preparation for her testimony." A student may seize on this language and conclude the exam had no diagnostic purpose. **The breaker:** the examination can serve *both* purposes. The physician was still attempting to form a diagnosis of the plaintiff's injury, and FRE 803(4) does not require that diagnosis be the *sole* purpose — only that the statement was made for purposes of diagnosis and is pertinent to it.

**C (No, since the statement described a former sense impression).** This choice is attractive because it reaches the right outcome (No, the objection should not be sustained) and sounds like a plausible hearsay exception. **The breaker:** there is no hearsay exception for a "former sense impression." The correct exception is FRE 803(4) (medical diagnosis), not some non-existent "former sense impression" doctrine. This choice rewards students who guess the right conclusion using the wrong reasoning.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Rule:** Federal Rule of Evidence 803(4) provides that the following is not excluded by the rule against hearsay: "Statements made for purposes of medical diagnosis or treatment and describing medical history, or past or present symptoms, pain, or sensations, or the inception or general character of the cause or external source thereof, insofar as reasonably pertinent to diagnosis or treatment."

**Application:** The plaintiff's statement — describing how the ladder gave way, how he landed, and the sound his wrist made — falls squarely within the "inception or general character of the cause or external source thereof" language of FRE 803(4). It was made during a medical examination for the purpose of diagnosis (determining the nature of the wrist injury) and is pertinent to that diagnosis. The fact that the examination was also conducted in anticipation of testimony does not negate the diagnostic purpose. *See* FRE 803(4); *see also* Fed. R. Evid. 803(4) advisory committee's note (statements as to cause of injury admissible when pertinent to diagnosis).
