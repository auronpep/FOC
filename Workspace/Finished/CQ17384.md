## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question
The Federal Trade Commission filed a civil action in federal district court against Candlelight Retreat Ministries, alleging that its public fundraising brochure falsely described the ministry's safety inspections and scholarship spending. After trial, the federal district court ruled in favor of the FTC and found the brochure false and misleading. Ruth, a donor who had relied on the brochure, later filed a fraud action against the ministry in federal district court. Ruth moved for partial summary judgment, arguing that the ministry should be barred from relitigating whether the brochure was false and misleading. How should the court rule on Ruth's motion?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
After a defendant loses a government enforcement case on whether a public statement was false, may a later private plaintiff use that ruling offensively when the plaintiff likely could not have joined the first case?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. The ministry already had the falsity issue decided against it in the FTC action, and Ruth is trying to use that prior ruling offensively in her later fraud case. Gold Key: Nonparty offensive collateral estoppel is allowed when the party seeking estoppel could not have intervened in the first action (Parklane Hosiery v. Shore, 1979). Silver Key: separate issue preclusion from claim preclusion before choosing; the later plaintiff's nonparty status is not the automatic breaker for issue preclusion. Because Ruth likely could not have intervened in the FTC enforcement action, the court may grant partial summary judgment on that issue.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because a later damages suit sounds like it should preserve a fresh jury decision on every disputed fact. The breaker is that preclusion can remove an already-litigated issue from the later case; Parklane also rejects the idea that this use of offensive issue preclusion violates the Seventh Amendment.

B is attractive because students remember that preclusion often cares about parties and privity, then import claim-preclusion logic into an issue-preclusion question. Gold Key: Nonparty offensive collateral estoppel is allowed when the party seeking estoppel could not have intervened in the first action (Parklane Hosiery v. Shore, 1979), so Ruth's nonparty status alone does not defeat the motion.

D is attractive because it grabs the strongest true fact in the stem: the falsity issue was already decided. The breaker is that a prior decision is not enough by itself for offensive nonmutual issue preclusion; the court must also consider fairness, including whether the later plaintiff could have joined or intervened in the first action.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Civil Procedure, Verdicts and Judgments, Issue and Claim Preclusion, and the verified outline code is `97100300`. Issue preclusion can bar relitigation of an issue that was actually litigated, decided, and necessary to a prior judgment when asserted against a party that had a full and fair opportunity to litigate. In *Parklane Hosiery Co. v. Shore*, 439 U.S. 322 (1979), the Supreme Court allowed offensive nonmutual collateral estoppel in federal court when use would be fair, including when the later plaintiff could not easily have joined the first action; the Court also held that this use did not violate the Seventh Amendment. Here, the FTC action decided the same falsity issue against the ministry, and Ruth likely could not have intervened in that government enforcement action. The credited outcome is therefore unchanged from the source: the court should grant the motion.

Authority checked: *Parklane Hosiery Co. v. Shore*, 439 U.S. 322 (1979), available at https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/439/322/ and https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/439/322.
