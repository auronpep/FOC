### 7. Right-Answer Explanation

**Choice C is correct.**

A search incident to a lawful arrest (SITA) automatically justifies a full search of the arrestee's person. The cocaine found in Esther's pocket is therefore admissible — if her arrest was lawful. For the arrest to be lawful, the officers needed probable cause to believe Esther was guilty of possessing the counterfeit currency.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIM-SITA-PC-01):** Probable cause means facts sufficient for a reasonable person to believe the defendant committed the offense. Esther was seated at a workstation directly above the locked drawer containing counterfeit bills. That proximity, in the context of a premises specifically tied to a counterfeiting operation, could lead a reasonable officer to believe Esther had knowledge of or access to the contraband. If PC for the arrest is established, SITA validates the search that uncovered the cocaine, and the cocaine is admissible.

The answer acknowledges the prosecution's argument "might" succeed — the PC call is not certain — but it is the only argument among the four that has any legal foothold.

**Silver Key (SK-CRIM-MOST-EFFECTIVE-01):** On a "most effective prosecution argument" question after a SITA find, trace the chain backward: admissible item ← valid SITA ← lawful arrest ← probable cause. The winning argument is always the one that establishes PC for the arrest.

### 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A** draws on the real doctrine that officers executing a search warrant may frisk persons present for their protection. The instinct is sound — officer safety during a premises search is a recognized Fourth Amendment concern. But a Terry frisk is limited to a pat-down of the outer clothing for weapons. It does not authorize reaching into pockets unless the pat-down has revealed something that feels like a weapon. The stem gives no indication that the bag of cocaine felt like a weapon through Esther's jacket. A frisk argument therefore runs out before it reaches the cocaine, making it an ineffective prosecution argument.

**Silver Key (trap-spotting, tested_choice A):** When a frisk argument appears in a suppression question, ask: did the pat-down reveal something that felt like a weapon? If the stem is silent, the answer is no — and the frisk stops at the outer clothing.

**Choice B** invites the court to treat the search warrant as authorization to arrest everyone present. This is a flat misstatement. A warrant to search premises is not an arrest warrant. Officers may not arrest a person present during the execution of a search warrant unless they independently have probable cause to believe that person has committed or is committing an offense. Because B states an incorrect rule, it is not an effective prosecution argument.

**Choice D** inverts the exclusionary rule. "Fruit of the poisonous tree" is the doctrine under which evidence derived from unconstitutional government conduct is excluded. It is an exclusionary rule — a reason to keep evidence OUT. Invoking it as a reason to admit evidence is backwards. The prosecution cannot benefit from a doctrine that operates entirely against it.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIM-FPT-DIRECTION-01, tested_choice D):** FPT means EXCLUDE. Any choice that uses the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine to justify admission of evidence is backwards on the rule. Cut immediately.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule 1 — Search Incident to Arrest:** When officers lawfully arrest a person, they may conduct a full warrantless search of that person's body and the area within the arrestee's immediate control. Items found during this search are admissible. The legality of the SITA depends entirely on the legality of the arrest.

**Rule 2 — Probable Cause for Arrest:** Officers may arrest without an arrest warrant when they have probable cause — facts sufficient to lead a reasonable person to believe the suspect has committed or is committing a crime. Proximity to contraband discovered during a valid premises search is one factor that can contribute to PC, though it is not per se sufficient.

**Rule 3 — Terry Frisk Limits:** During the execution of a search warrant, officers may conduct a protective pat-down of persons present for their safety. This pat-down is limited to the outer clothing for the purpose of detecting weapons. Reaching into pockets is justified only if the pat-down reveals an object that reasonably feels like a weapon. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968).

**Rule 4 — Search Warrant vs. Arrest Warrant:** A search warrant authorizes a search of the described premises and seizure of specified items. It does not authorize the arrest of persons found on the premises absent independent probable cause. *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85 (1979) (mere presence on premises subject to a search warrant does not establish probable cause to search or arrest).

**Rule 5 — Fruit of the Poisonous Tree:** Evidence derived from a Fourth Amendment violation is excluded under the FPT doctrine. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963). FPT is an exclusionary rule; it never supports admission of evidence.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer):**
- *Chimel v. California*, 395 U.S. 752 (1969) — SITA doctrine; search of arrestee's person and area within immediate control.
- *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968) — protective pat-down limited to outer clothing for weapons.
- *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85 (1979) — mere presence during a search does not supply PC to search or arrest.
- *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963) — FPT is exclusionary, not admissibility-creating.

#### wrong_answer_recovery_paths

**Choice A — Frisk half-truth (dominant trap):**
Why picked: Officers executing a search warrant CAN frisk persons present — that's real doctrine, and the instinct toward officer safety feels sound. Students who know the frisk doctrine may not know its outer-clothing limit.
Skipped move: The Terry frisk stops at the outer clothing. Reaching into pockets requires that the pat-down revealed something that felt like a weapon. The stem is silent on weapon-feel.
Recovery: Every time you see a frisk argument, apply the weapon-feel checkpoint before accepting it as the basis for a pocket search. Frisk + no weapon-feel = no pocket search.

**Choice B — Search warrant equals arrest warrant:**
Why picked: Students who think search warrants are a general authorization to control everyone on the premises may extend that logic to arrests.
Skipped move: A search warrant authorizes a search of the described place and seizure of described items. It does not independently authorize arresting people found there.
Recovery: Distinguish the warrant types. Search warrant → place. Arrest warrant → person. Arresting a person during a premises search requires independent PC, not just the search warrant.

**Choice D — FPT backwards:**
Why picked: Students who have heard of "fruit of the poisonous tree" but haven't worked with it may think it's a general evidence doctrine that could cut either way, or may confuse the name with a positive admissibility rule.
Skipped move: FPT is the doctrine under which unlawfully obtained evidence is excluded. It is always the defendant's argument, never the prosecution's.
Recovery: FPT = out. If a choice uses FPT to let evidence IN, cut it immediately. The doctrine operates in only one direction.
