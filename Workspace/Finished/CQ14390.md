# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question (stem + call)

Stephen, who knew nothing about musical instruments, inherited an old violin from his grandfather. It had sat untouched in a closet for years and looked scratched and battered. Stephen sold the violin for $1,500 to Lydia, an experienced instrument dealer who knew of Stephen's ignorance about violins. At the time of the sale, Stephen said to Lydia, "I'll be honest with you — it's worn and scratched and nothing special." Assume that soon after the sale, the violin was authenticated as a rare master's instrument and Lydia resold it for $400,000.

Which of the following additional facts, if established by Stephen, would best support his chance of obtaining rescission of the sale to Lydia?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

An ignorant seller sells a unique item cheaply to an expert buyer who knew of the seller's ignorance; the item turns out to be worth vastly more. Which added fact best supports the seller's rescission claim — the one showing the buyer secretly knew the item's true value and hid it, or facts about the seller's own ignorance, mood, or the buyer's identity?

## 3. Final answer choices (A–D, post-shuffle)

- **C.** At a private appraisal session of which Lydia knew Stephen was not aware, Lydia had a master luthier examine and play the violin, confirming that its tone and craftsmanship far surpassed any ordinary instrument.
- **D.** Stephen did not learn until after the sale that the buyer was an experienced instrument dealer.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**C is correct.** A party's nondisclosure of a fact can be equivalent to an assertion that the fact does not exist — enough to make a contract voidable — where that party knows disclosure would correct the other side's mistake about a **basic assumption** of the deal and where nondisclosure amounts to a failure to act in **good faith**. The violin's resale for $400,000 (over 250 times the price) shows the parties were mistaken about a basic assumption: the instrument's true worth. Choice C is the only fact that puts a **specific material fact inside the buyer's knowledge** — Lydia, through a secret appraisal Stephen didn't know about, learned the violin's tone and craftsmanship far surpassed an ordinary instrument — while she knew Stephen was ignorant of it. That asymmetry of known, concealed, material fact is exactly what converts silence into actionable misrepresentation and supports rescission. **Gold Key:** silence becomes actionable only when the buyer secretly *knows* a material fact, *knows* the seller is mistaken about the thing's basic worth, and good faith demanded disclosure. **Silver Key:** on a "which fact best supports rescission" call, pick the single fact showing the buyer secretly knew and hid a material fact — concealment, not the seller's ignorance, mood, or the buyer's job title, creates the duty.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

- **A.** The story invites sympathy: a grieving seller, rattled and emotional, agreeing to a deal he might not have made with a clear head. But emotional distress is not duress — there was no wrongful threat that overbore his will, and an upset state of mind does not undo an otherwise valid contract. Stephen's mood is simply not what the rescission rule turns on. *Silver Key:* this is the self-pity fact the call dangles to pull you off the only fact (C) that shows the buyer's concealed knowledge.
- **B.** This feels like the heart of the case — the seller really was ignorant, and the price really was a fraction of true value, so it seems unfair to hold him to it. The catch is that a seller's own unilateral mistake about value, standing alone, does not justify rescission; you still need the *other* element — the buyer's knowing nondisclosure of a material fact. B supplies the seller's half of the picture and omits the dispositive half. *Gold Key:* unilateral mistake + good-faith bargaining counterparty = no rescission; you need the buyer's concealed knowledge.
- **D.** It is tempting to think the deal was lopsided because an expert bought from a novice who didn't even know he was dealing with a pro. But the buyer's status is a non-dispositive element — expertise alone imposes no duty to volunteer one's superior judgment of value. What matters is not *who* Lydia was but *what specific material fact she secretly knew and concealed*; D never establishes that. *Gold Key:* the nondisclosure duty attaches to concealed knowledge of a material fact, not to the buyer's occupation. *(Dominant trap.)*

## 9. Black-letter-law verification & legal reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Rule:** Non-disclosure of a fact is equivalent to an assertion that the fact does not exist where the party knows that disclosure would correct a mistake of the other party as to a **basic assumption** on which that party is making the contract and **non-disclosure amounts to a failure to act in good faith** and in accordance with reasonable standards of fair dealing. **Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts **§ 161(b)** (when non-disclosure is equivalent to an assertion); **§ 164(1)** (when a misrepresentation makes a contract voidable); **§ 159 & cmt.** (assertion not in accord with the facts). A material disparity between price and true value is probative that the mistake went to a basic assumption (value). The credited outcome (C/old B supports rescission) is **unchanged** from the original: the tested rule (nondisclosure-as-misrepresentation tied to a basic assumption + good faith), the legal issue (rescission for nondisclosure), and the credited fact (buyer's secret knowledge of the item's true value) all map one-for-one onto the original horse item. No doctrinal-currency hotlist area is implicated. **Authority-class:** Restatement sections (primary-secondary) satisfy the rule-number floor; no case cite asserted. **Verdict: PASS.**

## 13. Review Truth
