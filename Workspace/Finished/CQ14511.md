## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Peter contracted in a signed writing to sell to Paul a converted barn that Peter had been using as a sound-recording studio, together with the surrounding three-acre lot, for $150,000 in cash. The contract provided for exchange of the deed and purchase price on April 15. Possession was to be given to Paul on the same date.

On April 15, Peter notified Paul that because the tenant who leased the studio space wrongfully refused to quit the premises until April 30, Peter would be unable to deliver possession of the barn until then, but he assured Paul that he would tender the deed and possession on that date. Throughout April, the market value of the property was $155,000, and its fair monthly rental value was $2,000.

Peter had no reason to know that Paul intended to use the barn as a worship-recording studio or that he had rented the mobile trailer.

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A seller delayed delivery of possession of real property for 15 days. The buyer accepted late possession, reserved his rights, and now seeks damages, including the cost of a mobile trailer he rented because he could not use the property. Is the buyer limited to the fair rental value for the delay, or can he also recover the trailer cost?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

Paul's best recovery is **B**. Peter's 15-day delay in delivering possession was a partial breach. Paul remained obligated to close, but he could recover damages for the loss caused by the delay. The ordinary measure of that loss is the fair rental value of the property for the 15-day period — here, $1,000. **Gold Key:** consequential damages are limited to losses the breaching party had reason to foresee as a probable result of the breach at the time of contracting; Peter had no reason to know about the worship-album tracking block or the trailer rental, so the $1,000 trailer cost is not recoverable.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Nominal damages only, because the market value of the property exceeded the contract price.**
This choice is attractive because the property did appreciate above the contract price, so a student may think "no economic loss." The breaker is that the damages question is not about the bargain as a whole; it is about the loss of use during the 15-day delay. Fair rental value is the proper measure for that loss.

**C. $1,000 (the fair rental value of the barn for 15 days), plus $1,000 (the cost of the mobile recording trailer for 15 days).**
This is the dominant trap because it packages the correct base amount with a second, sympathetic cost. The breaker is the Gold Key: the trailer rental was a special cost flowing from Paul's undisclosed worship-album plans. Because Peter had no reason to know of those plans at the time of contracting, the trailer cost is not recoverable as consequential damages.

**D. Nothing, because by paying the purchase price on April 30, Paul waived whatever cause of action he may have had.**
This is attractive because Paul did close and pay the full price. The breaker is the explicit stem fact that Paul notified Peter he was reserving his rights to damages when he accepted the deed and paid.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The original row tests a Contracts Remedies item: damages for a seller's delay in delivering possession of real property. The non-breaching buyer performs the contract (accepts deed and pays) but may recover damages for the partial breach. The measure of damages for loss of use during the delay is the fair rental value of the property for the delayed period. Consequential or special damages are recoverable only if they were foreseeable to the breaching party at the time of contracting as a probable result of the breach.

For lawyer confirmation, the foreseeability limitation is classically stated in *Hadley v. Baxendale* (1854) and codified in Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 351: damages are not recoverable for loss that the party in breach did not have reason to foresee as a probable result of the breach at the time the contract was made. The official BarBri/Brainscape explanation for a structurally identical item confirms that in real-estate contracts requiring delivery of possession, late delivery entitles the buyer to expectation damages measured by the fair rental value for the time the buyer was denied possession, while storage or substitute costs are not recoverable if they were neither foreseeable nor commercially reasonable incident to the delay.

The transformed outcome is identical: the fair rental value for the 15-day delay ($1,000) is recoverable; the mobile-trailer cost ($1,000) is not recoverable because Peter had no reason to know of Paul's worship-recording plans.
