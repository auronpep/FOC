### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The court is unlikely to permit relation back. Rule 15(c)(1)(C) permits a party-change amendment to relate back only when three conditions are met: (i) the claim arose out of the conduct, transaction, or occurrence set out in the original pleading; (ii) the new party received such notice of the action that it will not be prejudiced in maintaining a defense; and (iii) the new party knew or should have known that, but for a mistake concerning the identity of the proper party, it would have been named as a defendant. The facts knock out conditions (ii) and (iii): the real driver had no actual notice within the Rule 4(m) period, and the plaintiff has not shown the omission was a Rule 15(c) "mistake concerning identity" — the plaintiff did not know who the driver was, and a mere lack of knowledge is not automatically the required identity mistake. **Gold Key:** John Doe pleading plus post-limitations identification is not, by itself, a Rule 15(c) mistake; the plaintiff must show that the new party knew or should have known it would have been named but for an identity mistake, and timely notice to the new party is also required.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**D. Yes, because a John Doe pleading always satisfies the Rule 15(c) mistake requirement.**

The dominant trap. The overclaim is attractive because John Doe pleadings exist precisely to bridge the gap between an unknown defendant and a named one. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: a John Doe pleading is not, by itself, a Rule 15(c) "mistake concerning identity"; the plaintiff must show the new party knew or should have known it would have been named but for a mistake, and a simple lack of knowledge is not automatically that mistake.

**B. Yes, because notice to the new driver after limitations is enough.**

The post-limitations-notice trap. This is attractive because the driver did ultimately receive notice of the suit. The breaker is the timing rule: Rule 15(c)(1)(C)(ii) requires notice within the Rule 4(m) period (or shortly thereafter when the plaintiff could not have identified the new party); notice after limitations is the failure the rule is designed to police.

**C. No, because claims against drivers can never be amended.**

The overclaim of immutability. This sounds like a black-letter rule, but Rule 15 freely grants leave to amend, and a proper party-change amendment does relate back. The breaker is the call function: the question is whether this specific amendment satisfies Rule 15(c), not whether all such amendments are foreclosed.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18488, a Civil Procedure item on Rule 15(c) party-change relation back. The official key is C: relation back is unlikely because the new driver lacked timely notice and the omission was not shown to be a Rule 15(c) mistake. The supplied lb_rule_reference confirms the rule: "Party-change relation back requires that the new party knew or should have known it would have been named but for a mistake concerning identity."

Rule 15(c)(1)(C) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure codifies the party-change relation-back doctrine. The text requires (i) that the claim arose out of the conduct, transaction, or occurrence set out in the original pleading; (ii) that the new party received such notice of the action within 90 days after the pleading was served (the Rule 4(m) period) such that it will not be prejudiced in maintaining a defense; and (iii) that the new party knew or should have known that, but for a mistake concerning the identity of the proper party, it would have been named as a defendant. The Advisory Committee Notes to the 1991 amendment make clear that the "mistake" requirement is not satisfied by mere lack of knowledge; the new party must have had a sufficient identity link to the original proceedings to be charged with constructive notice of the suit. The 1991 amendment (subdivision (C)) was designed to overrule decisions like *Barrow v. Fulbright* that had required the new party to have had actual notice within the limitations period. The transformed outcome is identical: Daniel filed a John Doe pleading, did not serve the real driver within the 90-day Rule 4(m) period, did not show that the omission was a Rule 15(c) identity mistake, and accordingly cannot satisfy Rule 15(c)(1)(C)(ii) and (iii). Relation back is unlikely.

## distilled_core_question

A plaintiff sues a John Doe driver before limitations, never serves the real driver within Rule 4(m), and the real driver has no actual notice within that period. After limitations expires, the plaintiff learns the real driver's identity and seeks to amend and relate back under Rule 15(c). Is relation back likely?

## call_and_prediction

Call: is relation back likely?
Prediction: Stephen had no notice within the 90-day period, and the plaintiff has not shown the omission was a Rule 15(c) identity mistake. The likely answer is that relation back fails.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. No, because the new driver lacked timely notice of the action and the omission was not shown to be a Rule 15(c) mistake concerning identity.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice names both required failures under Rule 15(c)(1)(C): the timely-notice failure and the identity-mistake failure.
2. **Student label:** Residual answer.
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Rule 15(c)(1)(C) requires that within the 90-day Rule 4(m) period the new party received notice of the action and that the new party knew or should have known it would have been named but for a mistake. Neither is satisfied on these facts.

### B. Yes, because notice to the new driver after limitations is enough.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The choice ignores the timing rule. Notice after limitations is the failure the rule is designed to police.
2. **Student label:** Timing bait.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Notice after limitations would be enough only if the plaintiff had served within the 90-day Rule 4(m) period; here the plaintiff did not."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Rule 15(c)(1)(C)(ii) requires notice within the 90-day Rule 4(m) period (or shortly thereafter when the plaintiff could not have identified the new party). Post-limitations notice does not satisfy the rule.

### C. No, because claims against drivers can never be amended.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is an immutability overclaim. Rule 15 freely grants leave to amend, and a proper party-change amendment does relate back.
2. **Student label:** Immutability overclaim.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Claims against drivers are amendable; the question is whether the amendment satisfies Rule 15(c)."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Rule 15(a) freely grants leave to amend, and Rule 15(c) permits relation back for qualifying party changes.

### D. Yes, because a John Doe pleading always satisfies the Rule 15(c) mistake requirement.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is a placeholder overclaim. A John Doe pleading is not, by itself, a Rule 15(c) identity mistake.
2. **Student label:** Placeholder overclaim.
3. **True/responsive version:** "A John Doe pleading might be evidence of a Rule 15(c) identity mistake, but the plaintiff must still show the new party knew or should have known it would have been named but for a mistake; a mere lack of knowledge is not automatically that mistake."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Rule 15(c)(1)(C)(iii) requires that the new party knew or should have known it would have been named but for an identity mistake. The Advisory Committee Notes to the 1991 amendment make clear that the rule is not satisfied by mere lack of knowledge; the new party must have had a sufficient identity link to the original proceedings.

## residual_answer

The residual is **A**. Stephen had no timely notice within the 90-day Rule 4(m) period, and Daniel has not shown the omission was a Rule 15(c) identity mistake.
