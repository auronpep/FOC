# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question (revised stem + call)

Stephen owned a lakeside lodge where he had lived for many years. Deciding to move to a coastal town to be near family, he offered to sell the lodge to Lydia, a traveler who had admired it during a long stay nearby, for $190,000. After inspecting the lodge, Lydia accepted. They signed a written contract of sale setting closing of title for two months after signing. Because Stephen had already arranged to leave and Lydia had nowhere else to stay, the contract included a clause permitting Lydia to move into the lodge immediately. Lydia moved in a few days after signing. Stephen kept the fire insurance policy on the lodge in force, intending to cancel it once he conveyed title; Lydia, for her part, bought her own fire insurance policy on the lodge soon after signing. Two weeks after Lydia moved in, a fire — sparked by a lightning strike during a night storm and attributable to neither party — partially destroyed the roof, the entire kitchen, and part of the exterior porch. Lydia immediately told Stephen that she was unwilling to complete the purchase at the agreed price, but that she would renegotiate a lower price reflecting the lodge's diminished value.

**If Stephen sues for damages based on Lydia's anticipatory repudiation of the contract of sale, what is Stephen's most effective argument?**

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A buyer takes possession of real property before closing under the sale contract; before closing, a casualty the seller did not cause partially destroys it; the buyer refuses to pay the agreed price. In the seller's suit, what is the seller's best argument that the buyer must still perform?

## 3. Final answer choices (A–D, post-shuffle)

- **A.** Stephen had a policy of insurance insuring him against fire damage to the lodge.
- **B.** The risk of loss passed to Lydia when she took possession of the lodge pursuant to the contract.
- **C.** A fire is presumed to be the fault of the person in possession of the property when it occurs.
- **D.** Lydia purchased a policy of fire insurance covering the lodge after agreeing to buy it.

## 4. Correct answer

## 6. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

The call asks for the seller's *most effective argument*, i.e., the only choice that supplies a legal reason the buyer must pay the full price despite the fire. **B** is that choice. **Gold Key:** on a land-sale contract, a casualty loss the seller did not cause falls on the buyer once the buyer takes possession (or title) before closing — under both the majority equitable-conversion rule and the Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act, possession shifts the risk, and the buyer still owes the full price. Lydia moved in under an express possession clause two weeks before the lightning fire, which was attributable to neither party. Risk of loss had passed to her; her diminished-value renegotiation demand is a repudiation, and Stephen's strongest argument is exactly that the risk was hers. **Silver Key:** "most effective argument" wants the one choice with a real legal mechanism for shifting the loss, not the most sympathetic-sounding fact.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**D — Lydia purchased her own fire policy (dominant trap).** The stem plants that Lydia bought her own coverage right after signing, so it feels like she insured herself into responsibility for the loss. But who bought insurance is a collateral fact about reimbursement; it answers a different question than the call. **Gold Key:** passage of risk on a land-sale contract never depends on either party's insurance. **Silver Key:** two choices here rest on insurance (A and D), one each party's — when a matched pair turns on the same collateral fact pointing opposite ways, the rule can't hinge on it, so cut both.

**C — fire presumed the fault of the person in possession.** It hands the seller a clean, legal-sounding hook: the occupant was in the building when it burned, so presume her at fault and make her pay. The presumption is invented — an overclaim built on the spot. **Gold Key:** no jurisdiction presumes an occupant caused an accidental fire; risk turns on possession/title allocation, not on a fault presumption.

**A — Stephen's own fire policy.** Insurance is salient because the stem says Stephen kept his policy in force, and a student grabs the first insurance fact as "the argument." But the seller pointing to *his own* coverage cuts the wrong way — it shows he can be made whole, which undercuts rather than supports his demand that the buyer pay. **Gold Key:** the vendor's insurance does not allocate risk of loss between the parties.

## 8. Black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

**Tested rule (unchanged):** risk of casualty loss during the executory period of a land-sale contract, where the loss is not the vendor's fault. Under the majority **doctrine of equitable conversion**, the buyer is treated as equitable owner from the moment the contract becomes specifically enforceable and bears the risk of loss; the buyer must close at the full price even if the premises are damaged. The **Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act (UVPRA) §1** modifies this for adopting states: risk stays on the vendor *unless legal title or possession has passed to the purchaser*. Because choice **B** keys on **possession**, it states the seller's winning argument under *either* framework — equitable-conversion states already place the risk on the buyer from contract, and UVPRA states place it on the buyer once she takes possession. A small minority (e.g., the Massachusetts rule) keeps the risk on the seller until closing, but **B** is the only choice that *could* support the seller, exactly as the source item holds. Credited outcome, legal issue, and tested rule are **unchanged** from the original.

*Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):* Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act §1; doctrine of equitable conversion; codified e.g. at N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law §5-1311. Rule-class authority; no case citation asserted. Risk-of-loss framing is current and not on the doctrinal-currency hotlist; no superseded-test issue. ([UVPRA / equitable conversion overview](https://legalclarity.org/uniform-vendor-and-purchaser-risk-act-risk-of-loss/), [Wikipedia: Equitable conversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equitable_conversion))
