6. **Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)**

**Gold Key:** A landowner may mortgage land to secure another person's debt; the landowner does not have to sign the note or become personally liable before the mortgage can be foreclosed against the land.

**Silver Key:** Separate personal liability from collateral liability. The bank is not trying to collect a money judgment from Martha; it is enforcing the mortgage Martha gave on her own land.

Galilee Bank should succeed. Timothy signed the note, Martha owned the warehouse parcel, and Martha gave the bank a mortgage on that parcel to secure Timothy's repayment obligation. Martha's missing signature on the note means she is not personally liable on the note. It does not make the mortgage invalid. Once Timothy defaulted and the debt was accelerated, Galilee Bank could foreclose the valid mortgage against the parcel.

7. **Full wrong-answer explanations**

**A.** The attractive move is to treat the note signer and the land signer as the same required person. That feels tidy, but it adds a rule the mortgage law does not require: Martha may pledge her land as collateral for Timothy's debt without owing the note herself. **Gold Key:** nondebtor collateral can still be valid collateral.

**C.** The attractive move is the correct yes-result attached to a familiar credit word. The role is backward. Galilee Bank is the lender and mortgagee, not a surety for Martha; the bank succeeds because Martha granted a valid mortgage on her land. **Silver Key:** when the answer says "because," test whether the stated role matches the foreclosure posture.

**D.** The attractive move is to grab the outline label "equitable mortgage" when the signer of the note and the signer of the mortgage are different people. But an equitable-mortgage label is not needed here. Martha executed an actual mortgage on land she owned to secure Timothy's note, so the bank has a legal mortgage it can foreclose.

8. **Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer**

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: a mortgage is a security interest in real property securing performance of an obligation, and the obligation may be owed by someone other than the mortgagor. The mortgagor's lack of personal liability on the note does not invalidate the mortgage or prevent foreclosure against the mortgaged land after default.

Authority floor: Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 1.1 states the mortgage concept as real property used as security for performance of an obligation, and § 1.3 addresses mortgages securing obligations of nonmortgagors. Courts applying that rule have recognized that a mortgage securing another person's obligation may be enforceable even when the owner-mortgagor did not receive the loan proceeds or sign the note. See Nebari Natural Resources Credit Fund I, LP v. Speyside Holdings LLC, 2022 NY Slip Op 50164(U), https://www.nycourts.gov/reporter/3dseries/2022/2022_50164.htm; Overman v. Minnwest Bank South, No. A07-1147, 2008 WL 2492351, https://law.justia.com/cases/minnesota/court-of-appeals/2008/opa071147-0701.html. Restatement publication page consulted: https://www.ali.org/publications/restatement-law-third/property-mortgages.

Applied here, Timothy's obligation is measurable in money and documented by a note. Martha owned the parcel and executed the mortgage as security for that obligation. Galilee Bank is enforcing the security against the parcel, not imposing personal note liability on Martha. No researched authority contradicts the supplied key. No doctrinal-currency hotlist issue is implicated.
