### 1. Final question

On March 3, Ruth agreed in a signed writing to sell Stephen a one-of-a-kind hand-carved Nativity music box for $12,000. The music box belonged to Ruth personally and, when they signed, was on loan for display at a private Scripture-arts exhibit in another city. Ruth was to deliver the music box to Stephen on May 1. What is the likely outcome of this suit?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** The music box was a specific existing good identified to the contract when Ruth and Stephen signed the writing. It was then totally destroyed by an accidental fire before the risk of loss had passed to Stephen and without fault of either party. **Gold Key:** Under UCC section 2-613, when goods identified at contract formation suffer a total casualty without either party's fault before the risk of loss passes to the buyer, the contract is avoided. Because the contract is avoided, Ruth cannot collect the $12,000 contract price, and Stephen cannot recover the $18,000 appreciated value. **Silver Key:** Read the answer array as a claim matrix. Once the casualty rule makes the contract avoided, the seller's price claim is no and the buyer's market-value claim is also no.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Only Ruth's claim for the contract price will succeed.**

This choice is attractive because Ruth had a signed writing, a fixed $12,000 price, and a buyer who had not yet paid. The breaker is the timing of risk. The fire happened before risk of loss passed to Stephen, and the identified music box was totally destroyed without fault. **Gold Key:** UCC section 2-613 avoids the contract in that exact setting, so Ruth has no price claim.

**B. Only Stephen's counterclaim for the music box's market value will succeed.**

This is the dominant trap. It feels right because the music box appreciated from $12,000 to $18,000 before the fire, so Stephen appears to have lost the benefit of a bargain. The breaker is that nobody breached and the contract is avoided by casualty to identified goods before risk passed. Stephen does not get expectancy damages or the full market value of property he never received. **Silver Key:** A buyer's counterclaim needs a live contract breach or a risk-shift fact; appreciation alone does not beat the casualty rule.

**D. Both claims will succeed, and Stephen will recover $6,000.**

This choice sells an arithmetic compromise: Ruth gets the $12,000 price, Stephen gets the $18,000 value, and the difference looks like a neat damages calculation. The breaker is that the casualty rule does not keep both claims alive and net them. Total no-fault destruction of identified goods before risk passes avoids the contract. Once the contract is avoided, neither side recovers on the contract.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The governing rule is UCC section 2-613, "Casualty to Identified Goods." It provides that when the contract requires goods identified when the contract is made, and those goods suffer casualty without either party's fault before risk of loss passes to the buyer, a total loss avoids the contract. See [UCC section 2-613](https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-613).

The identification element is satisfied under UCC section 2-501. Existing goods are identified when the contract is made if the contract is for the sale of goods already existing and identified. The variant names a one-of-a-kind hand-carved Nativity music box, owned by Ruth personally and already on display when the parties signed, so the goods are identified at formation. See [UCC section 2-501](https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-501).

The risk-of-loss element is satisfied because the facts expressly state that the fire occurred before risk passed to Stephen. UCC section 2-509 supplies Article 2's default risk-of-loss framework and confirms that risk does not shift merely because a contract exists; the timing depends on shipment, bailee documents, receipt, tender, or contrary agreement. See [UCC section 2-509](https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-509).

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row: a specific good identified at contract formation is totally destroyed without fault before risk passes to the buyer. The contract is avoided. The seller cannot recover the price, and the buyer cannot recover market-value expectancy damages. Legal research does not contradict the supplied official key. No doctrinal-currency hotlist issue affects this classic UCC casualty rule.
