### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. Common-law burglary requires a breaking, but breaking does not require damage, a locked door, or dramatic force. The question is whether the defendant created or enlarged an opening or removed a barrier.

In Scenario 1, Peter opened a closed door. The door was unlocked, but it was still closed and still functioned as a barrier. Pushing it open creates the opening needed for common-law breaking.

In Scenario 2, Ruth walked through a doorway that Naomi had already left wide open. Ruth entered without permission, but she did not create an opening, enlarge an opening, or remove a barrier. That is an entry, not a breaking.

In Scenario 3, Stephen used an already-open window only to reach the locked inner kitchen door. He then slid back the bolt and opened that locked door. Removing that barrier satisfies breaking.

**Gold Key:** Common-law burglary breaking means creating or enlarging an opening or removing a barrier. Opening a closed but unlocked door counts; walking through an already wide-open doorway does not; unfastening a locked inner door after reaching through an existing opening counts.

**Silver Key:** Do not sort the scenarios by "permission," "locked," or "how much force." Sort each scenario by one question: did the defendant have to move a barrier before entering the protected space?

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is unchanged: common-law burglary requires a breaking, and a breaking occurs when the defendant creates or enlarges an opening or removes a barrier. Opening a closed but unlocked door is enough; merely walking through an already open doorway is not; opening a locked inner door after entering through an existing opening is a breaking as to that inner barrier.

Primary-law authority supports the bank's key. In **Passwater v. State**, 248 Ind. 454, 229 N.E.2d 718 (1967), the Indiana Supreme Court repeated the rule that walking through an open door is not breaking, but the slightest force in pushing aside a door is enough, and opening an unlocked door or raising an unlocked window is sufficient. Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/indiana/supreme-court/1967/30-828-2.html. In **State v. Carelock**, 293 N.C. 577, 238 S.E.2d 297 (1977), the North Carolina Supreme Court stated that opening a closed but unlocked door is a sufficient breaking. Source: https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914946badd7b049345bc2af. Oklahoma's court in **Busby v. State**, 2022 OK CR 4, also states the barrier principle: walking through an open door or climbing through an open window is not breaking because no obstacle is removed, while pushing an already open door wider can be enough. Source: https://www.okcca.net/cases/2022/OK-CR-4/. Cornell Wex gives the same exam-level summary: common-law burglary includes breaking and entering, and slight force such as pushing open a door is enough. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/breaking_and_entering.
