# CQ21331

## 1. Final Question

Naomi paid for a Bible-journaling workshop at a private craft studio. During the class, an instructor negligently brought down the arm of a large paper cutter before Naomi had moved her hand. The cutter struck Naomi's forearm, and Naomi suffered a sprain. Afterward, Naomi became anxious around paper cutters and cutting tables. The studio argues that the anxiety cannot be included in damages unless Naomi satisfies the jurisdiction's standalone negligent-infliction-of-emotional-distress test.

What is the best response?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

When a negligence plaintiff suffers a physical injury, can related anxiety be recovered as part of damages without proving a standalone NIED claim?

## 3. Final Answer Choices

A. The anxiety is recoverable only if Naomi witnessed a close family member being injured.

B. The anxiety is recoverable only if the instructor intended to cause emotional distress.

C. The anxiety may be considered as parasitic damages if caused by the arm injury.

D. The anxiety is automatically barred because emotional distress is never recoverable in negligence.

## 5. Correct Answer

C (original key B).

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Naomi is not trying to recover for free-standing emotional harm after only a scare. She has a physical injury: the sprained forearm caused by the negligent paper-cutter incident. Gold Key: physical injury changes the lane. Related anxiety can be considered as parasitic pain-and-suffering damages if the jury finds it was caused by the physical injury or the negligent conduct that caused that injury. Silver Key: ask whether emotional distress is the claim itself or a damages component attached to a physical tort injury. Here it is a damages component, so the standalone NIED gate is not the right screen.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the bystander-emotional-distress shortcut. The close-relative witness test belongs to a different lane: a plaintiff who suffers emotional harm from seeing another person injured. The breaker is Naomi's own sprained forearm. She is the directly injured plaintiff, so the emotional harm can be evaluated as parasitic damages instead of a standalone bystander NIED claim. Gold Key: physical injury changes the lane from standalone emotional-distress limits to ordinary tort damages.

B sells an intentional-tort gate. Intent would matter for intentional infliction of emotional distress, but the stem gives a negligent instructor and a physical injury. The breaker is claim identity. Naomi does not need to prove that the instructor meant to upset her when she is seeking damages connected to a negligence-caused bodily injury.

D sells the extreme "no emotional distress in negligence" overclaim. Negligence law does limit pure emotional-distress claims, but it does not erase mental anguish, fear, or anxiety that follows a compensable bodily injury. The breaker is the word "never." Physical-injury-linked emotional distress can be part of compensatory damages.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: when negligent conduct causes physical injury, related emotional distress may be recovered as part of compensatory damages without satisfying the jurisdiction's standalone NIED requirements. Naomi's anxiety is tied to a sprained forearm caused by the negligent paper-cutter incident, so the studio's standalone-NIED argument misframes the damages issue.

Restatement (Second) of Torts section 456(a) supports liability for emotional disturbance resulting from bodily harm or from the conduct that causes the bodily harm. Restatement (Second) of Torts section 905 also treats pain-and-suffering style noneconomic loss as part of bodily-injury damages. The Supreme Court's discussion in Norfolk & Western Railway Co. v. Ayers, 538 U.S. 135 (2003), likewise distinguishes stand-alone emotional-distress claims from emotional distress tied to physical injury and recognizes the latter as pain-and-suffering damages. Ayers is a FELA case, so it is used here only as common-law confirmation; the controlling MBE rule is the general tort damages rule.

Sources checked: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-963.ZS.html and https://www.ali.org/publications/restatement-law-third/torts-liability-physical-and-emotional-harm.
