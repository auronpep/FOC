### 7. Full right-answer explanation
A is correct. The call asks for Timothy's mental state as to the 247 non-Barnabas deaths, and the answer turns on the difference between wanting a result and knowing it is practically certain. Under MPC § 2.02(2)(a)(i), a person acts purposely with respect to a result when "it is his conscious object to cause such result." Timothy's conscious object was Barnabas's death; he did not want the other communicants to die. Under MPC § 2.02(2)(b)(ii), a person acts knowingly with respect to a result when "he is aware that it is practically certain that his conduct will cause such result." Timothy laced a shared cup that every communicant would drink from, with a toxin he knew would kill any adult who sipped it, knowing that the only way to reach Barnabas was through a cup every other communicant would also drink from. That is practical certainty, not a gamble, and not blind unawareness. The actor who does not want the result but knows it is practically certain kills with knowledge. **Gold Key:** Practical-certainty awareness is the MPC's knowledge tier — it is one rung down from purpose on the culpability ladder, not a synonym for purpose and not a synonym for recklessness. **Silver Key:** When the answer array pits knowledge against recklessness, name the axis: was the bystanders' death a risk the actor was taking, or a result he was sure of? If sure, it is knowledge; if he was rolling the dice, it is recklessness.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
C is attractive because Timothy plainly knew the other communicants would die, and a student reads "knew they would die" as identical to "wanted them to die." The breaker is the MPC's express split between purpose and knowledge. The cognitive move the choice sells is conflating awareness of a result with conscious object to cause it. Gold Key: purpose is the conscious-object tier; knowledge is the practical-certainty tier. They are different rungs on the culpability ladder, and the test expressly separates them. Timothy was indifferent to the 247 others, and indifference is exactly the mental posture the MPC places at knowledge, not at purpose.

D is attractive because poisoning a shared communion cup sounds like a gamble on a risk that might or might not materialize — and "consciously disregarded a risk" is the textbook recklessness formula. The breaker is that the cup was shared by every communicant and the toxin was a one-sip killer. The actor was not weighing odds; he was operating on practical certainty. The cognitive move the choice sells is pulling the actor's mental state down one tier on the culpability scale by relabeling certainty as risk. Silver Key: if you can name the certainty/risk axis and the actor is on the certainty side, recklessness cannot reach him.

B is attractive because "should have considered the impact" reads like a faithful restatement of the reasonable-person standard, and the conduct is so outrageous that calling it "merely negligent" feels obviously wrong. The breaker is that MPC negligence is reserved for actors who lacked subjective awareness of a substantial and unjustifiable risk — a reasonable-person failure to perceive. Timothy had actual, subjective awareness of the practical certainty of the other communicants' deaths. The cognitive move the choice sells is reversing the trigger: negligence requires lack of awareness, and the actor had awareness. You cannot label a subjectively-aware actor as merely negligent; that mental posture lives one or two tiers higher on the culpability scale.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law, General Principles, Model Penal Code, and the verified outline code is `72010103` (General Principles > Sources of Criminal Law > Model Penal Code). The governing authority is Model Penal Code § 2.02 (General Requirements of Culpability), as promulgated by the American Law Institute in 1962 and adopted (with variations) by a substantial majority of U.S. jurisdictions. Section 2.02(2) sets four culpability tiers in descending order: purposely, knowingly, recklessly, and negligently. As applied to a result, "purposely" requires a conscious object to cause the result (§ 2.02(2)(a)(i)), while "knowingly" requires awareness that the result is practically certain (§ 2.02(2)(b)(ii)). "Recklessly" is reserved for conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk that the result might occur (§ 2.02(2)(c)), and "negligently" applies when the actor should be aware of a substantial and unjustifiable risk (§ 2.02(2)(d)). The same hierarchy is reflected in the substitute-proof rule of § 2.02(5): a higher mental state always satisfies a lower one. Timothy's conscious object as to Barnabas supplies purpose, and his awareness of practical certainty as to the other communicants supplies knowledge. The credited answer is unchanged from the original.

## outline_mastery_map
- Placement: Criminal Law > General Principles > Sources of Criminal Law > Model Penal Code (72010103).
- This item teaches: The four MPC culpability tiers as applied to a result (purpose, knowledge, recklessness, negligence), the precise text of "practically certain" at the knowledge rung, and the indifference-plus-certainty posture.
- Fills: "practical certainty" is the MPC's knowledge rung (not purpose, not recklessness); criminal negligence is reserved for actors who fail to perceive a risk a reasonable person would have perceived.
- Adjacent to master: MPC § 2.02(5) substitute-proof rule (purpose satisfies knowledge satisfies recklessness satisfies negligence); the "depraved-indifference" line of cases that sometimes blurs recklessness and knowledge at the death-result tier.

## crossover_intersection_map
- Torts: the "subjective awareness" anchor in this item is the inverse of the civil "reasonable person" standard; the same fact pattern can produce criminal liability under MPC § 2.02(2)(b) and a wrongful-death civil claim without any internal contradiction.
- Criminal Procedure: the same indifference posture has been used (variously) by state courts as a bridge between MPC "knowledge" and common-law "depraved-heart" murder; if the call were recast in common-law terms, the dominant trap would shift.
- Constitutional Law: statutes that import "purpose or knowledge" murder liability may raise vagueness or overbreadth questions at the edges, but a practical-certainty finding under MPC § 2.02(2)(b)(ii) is generally a safe harbor for legislative drafting.
