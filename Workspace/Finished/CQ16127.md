### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The men can be convicted of **both arson and conspiracy**. The statute expressly extends the common-law definition of arson (intentional burning of a dwelling) to other buildings, and an industrial chemical-processing plant is a non-dwelling building. Because the men wanted to burn it down and succeeded, the arson elements are met. Conspiracy requires an agreement between two or more persons to commit a crime, with intent to achieve the criminal objective; the men agreed to commit arson and carried out the plan, so the conspiracy elements are met. **Gold Key:** conspiracy and the substantive offense are separate crimes; a defendant can be convicted of both for the same conduct. The only narrowing exception is Wharton's Rule, which applies only to crimes that by definition require two persons (dueling, adultery, bigamy, incest, the like). Arson is not a Wharton's Rule crime, so both charges stack.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Arson or conspiracy, but not both.**

This is the dominant trap. The merger myth is the classic MBE misconception here: a student who half-remembers "conspiracy merges" picks this and walks into it. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: conspiracy does not merge with the substantive offense in the general rule, and Wharton's Rule (the only true no-stack exception) does not apply to arson. A defendant can be convicted of conspiracy to commit arson AND arson itself for the same conduct.

**B. Arson only.**

This is the "I forgot conspiracy" trap. The student focuses on the completed burning and never registers the group-agreement element that triggers conspiracy. The breaker is that conspiracy is a separate inchoate crime, complete upon agreement (with an overt act in most jurisdictions, satisfied here by the bombing). The men were two persons who agreed to commit arson; conspiracy exists in addition to the arson.

**D. Conspiracy only.**

This is the mirror-image trap: the student registers the agreement but treats the substantive offense as if it absorbed the agreement. The breaker is the same as for B — the burning was completed, the statute covers the building, and arson is satisfied. The agreement is not a substitute for the substantive crime; the two stack.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 16127, a Criminal Law item involving two members of a group who agreed to burn down a building, succeeded, and the jurisdiction's arson statute extends the common-law definition of arson (the intentional burning of a dwelling) to non-dwellings. The official key is D: both arson and conspiracy.

Common-law arson is the malicious burning of the dwelling of another. The statute given extends that definition to other buildings, so the burning of the chemical-processing plant fits the statute's expanded definition. The men had the requisite mens rea (they wanted the plant burned and succeeded), satisfying the intentional-burning element.

Conspiracy at common law is an agreement between two or more persons to commit an unlawful act, with specific intent to achieve the criminal objective; in most jurisdictions, an overt act in furtherance of the agreement is also required. Model Penal Code § 5.03 codifies the modern formulation, defining conspiracy as an agreement with another person to engage in conduct that constitutes a crime, with the purpose of promoting or facilitating its commission. The men's agreement and the bombing satisfy both prongs.

The merger question is the central pivot. Conspiracy does not merge with the substantive offense as a general rule; the two are treated as separate wrongs because conspiracy threatens distinct harms (the danger of group criminality, the heightened risk that the plan will be carried out) that the substantive offense does not. Model Penal Code § 1.07(4) similarly recognizes that a conspiracy and the substantive offense are separate crimes. The principal narrowing exception is Wharton's Rule, which bars a conspiracy conviction for an agreement that is itself the substantive crime only when the substantive crime is one that by definition requires two persons (e.g., bigamy, adultery, incest, dueling). See Iannelli v. United States, 420 U.S. 770 (1975) (Wharton's Rule and its scope). Arson is not a Wharton's Rule crime; the arson statute can be committed by a single person. Both convictions stand.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: the men are properly convicted of both arson (under the extended statute) and conspiracy (the agreement to commit the arson).
