# BarMatrix C3 Divergent Transform — Q15030

## 1. Final question

Twenty-eight years ago, Barnabas, fee owner of Pinehaven Vineyard, delivered to his neighbor Lydia a writing titled “Right-of-Way Deed.” In it, Barnabas granted to Lydia and her heirs and assigns a right-of-way across Pinehaven Vineyard for ingress to and egress from Lydia’s Cedar Cottage. Assume the language created a valid interest and the deed otherwise satisfied all grant requirements. Lydia owned Cedar Cottage, which adjoined Pinehaven Vineyard.

Nine years ago, Barnabas’s nephew Timothy succeeded to Barnabas’s fee title in Pinehaven Vineyard. Four years ago, Lydia’s daughter Miriam succeeded to Lydia’s fee title in Cedar Cottage by a deed that did not mention the right-of-way. When Miriam took title, a visible gravel driveway crossed Pinehaven Vineyard along the route Barnabas and Lydia had staked and used to reach the county road from Cedar Cottage. Cedar Cottage also had frontage on a narrow rear lane, but Miriam seldom used it because it was steeper and less convenient. Miriam continued to use the gravel driveway. The prescriptive period in the jurisdiction is 10 years.

Three months ago, Timothy told Miriam he planned to develop Pinehaven Vineyard as a family retreat campground and asked her to stop using the gravel driveway. After negotiations, Timothy offered to build a new driveway connecting Cedar Cottage with the campground loop road. Miriam declined because travel to the county road would be longer and less convenient.

Miriam brought an appropriate declaratory action against Timothy. In the lawsuit, Timothy argued that, because the deed did not identify the exact route, the location of the easement was governed by reasonableness and his campground-loop route was reasonable. Should Timothy’s defense succeed?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A deed creates an access easement over one parcel for the benefit of an adjoining parcel, but the deed does not state the exact route. The parties later use a visible driveway for years. A later owner of the burdened parcel wants to move the driveway to a different route for development and says the new route is reasonable. Can the later burdened owner relocate the easement unilaterally?

## 3. Final answer choices (post-shuffle)

A. Prevail, because the owner of the burdened parcel may choose the location of an access easement when the grant does not identify the route.

B. Fail, because the route had already been fixed by the acts of Barnabas and Lydia.

C. Fail, because the route had become fixed by prescription.

D. Prevail, because Timothy’s proposed route was reasonable and Miriam failed to propose an equally convenient substitute.

## 5. Correct answer

B. The defense should fail because the easement route had already been fixed by the acts of the original parties, Barnabas and Lydia. The original key was A.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. The deed created an express easement appurtenant for access to Cedar Cottage. The grant did not describe the exact route, but the original owner and original grantee later used a visible gravel driveway as the access route. That conduct fixed the location of the easement. Once the route is fixed, the servient owner does not have an inherent power to move it just because a later development plan makes another route look reasonable.

Gold Key: when an express easement does not describe a route, the servient owner may select a reasonable location only until the parties’ conduct fixes one; after that, a later servient owner cannot relocate it unilaterally merely by offering another reasonable route.

Silver Key: do not stop at “the deed is silent.” First ask whether the parties’ conduct already fixed a route; silence matters only before a route is fixed.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells the rule for the wrong moment. It is attractive because a servient owner can select a reasonable route when an access easement is created without a described location. But that selection power belongs before the route is fixed. Here, Barnabas and Lydia had already used the gravel driveway as the route.

C sells the prescription story. It is attractive because the facts mention a long-used visible driveway and a 10-year prescriptive period. The Gold Key defeats that trap: Miriam’s use is being protected as an express easement whose route was fixed by the parties’ acts, not as a new prescriptive easement.

D sells a reasonableness contest. It is attractive because Timothy’s campground-loop route is framed as reasonable and Miriam does not propose an equal substitute. But once the easement location is fixed, the servient owner cannot force relocation merely by showing that the new route is reasonable or that the dominant owner offered no better alternative.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

Authority floor: Restatement (Third) of Property (Servitudes) § 4.8 recognizes the rule that, when a servitude’s location is not specified by the creating instrument, surrounding circumstances, or later agreement, the servient owner may determine a reasonable location. The traditional common-law rule, illustrated by *Stowell v. Andrews*, 2018 N.H. LEXIS 168 (N.H. Sept. 14, 2018), is that once an easement location is fixed, the servient owner may not unilaterally relocate it without the easement holder’s consent. Cornell Wex also defines an easement as a nonpossessory property interest allowing use of another’s land and distinguishes servient and dominant tenements.

The transformed item preserves the same legal issue: an express access easement appurtenant with an unspecified route, later fixed by the parties’ conduct, and later challenged by the servient owner who wants to move it. The credited answer remains unchanged in substance. The modern Restatement relocation discussion does not change the MBE-style traditional rule tested here: the servient owner’s reasonableness argument fails after the route has already been fixed.
