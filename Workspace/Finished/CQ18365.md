## 1. Final Question

Peter swung a heavy brass handbell at Esther during a chapel fundraiser, striking her shoulder and causing a serious but non-life-threatening fracture. Esther was taken to a clinic and admitted for observation. By the next evening, she was recovering and her doctors expected her to go home soon.

Timothy, who had an unrelated grudge against Esther from a separate business dispute, slipped into her clinic room and poured a fatal poison into her water cup. Esther drank it and died within minutes. Timothy was arrested and charged separately. Peter is charged with Esther's murder.

Is Peter the proximate cause of Esther's death?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

Is the first attacker the proximate cause of death when an unrelated person later intentionally poisons the recovering victim?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. Peter's assault explains why Esther was in the clinic, but murder causation needs more than a factual chain. Gold Key: criminal causation requires both actual cause and proximate cause, and an independent, unforeseeable, intentional third-party killing can supersede the first actor's conduct. Silver Key: separate "how the victim got there" from "what legally caused the death." Timothy acted from an unrelated grievance and made a new deliberate decision to poison Esther. That breaks the causal chain for the murder charge.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the vulnerability shortcut. The clinic room made Esther easier for Timothy to reach, but vulnerability is not the test unless the later harm is a foreseeable result of Peter's attack. Timothy's unrelated, intentional poisoning is a new superseding cause.

C sells the dominant but-for trap. Peter's assault was part of the factual chain that put Esther in the clinic, but actual cause alone is not enough for murder liability. The Gold Key cuts the trap: proximate cause fails when an unforeseeable, independent, intentional criminal act becomes the legal cause of death.

D lands on the right bottom-line answer but gives the wrong deciding reason. A nonfatal wound can still support homicide liability if a foreseeable complication or dependent response causes death. The Silver Key is to focus on the later act's foreseeability and independence, not on whether Esther was already improving.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: criminal causation requires actual cause and proximate or legal cause. A later event can be a superseding cause when it is independent, unforeseeable, and sufficient to make the death too remote from the defendant's conduct.

Model Penal Code section 2.03 supplies the authority floor: for result offenses, the actual result must not be too remote or accidental in its occurrence to have a just bearing on liability. The foreseeability framing is consistent with criminal-causation cases such as People v. Kibbe, 35 N.Y.2d 407 (1974), which treats liability as turning on whether the ultimate harm is a reasonably foreseeable consequence of the defendant's conduct. Sources checked: https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/6141-tanaka-criminal-law-casebook/resources/8.1.1-model-penal-code-mpc-203-causal-relationship-between-conduct-and-result-divergence-between-result-designed-or-contemplated-and-actual-result-or-between-probable-and-actual-result/ and https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/intervening_cause.

Peter's assault was an actual cause in the loose factual-chain sense because Esther would not have been in the clinic but for the injury. But Timothy's later act was deliberate, criminal, independent, and motivated by an unrelated grievance. That act was not a foreseeable consequence of Peter's handbell assault. Timothy's poisoning, not Peter's earlier fracture, is the proximate cause of Esther's death.
