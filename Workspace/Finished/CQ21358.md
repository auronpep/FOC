## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**B is correct.** Voluntary intoxication operates as a partial defense only for specific-intent crimes by potentially negating the required specific mental state. Theft (larceny) requires the specific intent to permanently deprive — a purposeful mental state. If Peter was so intoxicated that he genuinely believed the guitar was his own, he may have lacked that specific intent, though this is a fact question for the jury. DUI is a general-intent crime: the act of driving while intoxicated satisfies the mental-state requirement because recklessness or negligence is sufficient. Intoxication cannot negate general intent; it typically demonstrates it. Peter remains fully liable for DUI regardless of how intoxicated he was.

**Gold Key — INTOXICATION / INTENT DOCTRINE:** Voluntary intoxication is a defense only to specific-intent crimes — it can negate the required specific mental state (purpose, knowledge, premeditation). It is never a defense to general-intent crimes, where recklessness or negligence suffices for liability. Intoxication is the very condition that proves the recklessness or negligence element of DUI.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

### Governing Rule

Under the common law and the Model Penal Code (MPC § 2.08), voluntary intoxication is not a defense to criminal liability *unless* it negatives an element of the offense. In practice, this means:

1. **Specific-intent crimes:** Voluntary intoxication may be raised to show that the defendant could not form the required specific intent (e.g., purpose, knowledge, premeditation). If the intoxication was so severe that the defendant genuinely lacked the specific mental state, the prosecution cannot prove that element beyond a reasonable doubt. *See* Model Penal Code § 2.08(1); *United States v. Morgan*, 37 M.J. 407 (C.M.A. 1993).

2. **General-intent crimes:** Voluntary intoxication is never a defense. Where recklessness or negligence suffices as the mens rea, intoxication does not negate — it demonstrates — the required mental state. *See* *United States v. Brosius*, 37 M.J. 652 (A.C.M.R. 1993); *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37 (1996) (states may constitutionally eliminate the voluntary intoxication defense).

3. **Insanity vs. intoxication:** Voluntary intoxication, no matter how severe, does not constitute insanity. The insanity defense requires a "mental disease or defect" — a recognized psychiatric condition, not acute alcohol-induced impairment. *See* *Jones v. State*, 1982 OK CR 112, 648 P.2d 1251, 1255; *Myers v. State*, 1946 OK CR 109, 174 P.2d 395.

### Application to Peter

- **Count 1 (Theft/Larceny):** Theft is a specific-intent crime requiring the intent to permanently deprive. If Peter genuinely believed the guitar was his own due to his intoxication, he lacked the specific intent to permanently deprive. This is a valid defense, though the jury must assess whether his belief was genuine.

- **Count 2 (DUI):** DUI is a general-intent crime. The act of driving while intoxicated itself satisfies the mental-state requirement. Intoxication cannot negate general intent; it proves it. Peter is fully liable for DUI.
