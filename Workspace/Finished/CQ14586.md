## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.** The call asks how the grand jury may compel production over a Fifth Amendment assertion. The answer is not “never,” and it is not “only with total immunity from prosecution.” The needed protection is use and derivative use immunity for the act of production.

**Gold Key:** A subpoena for a preexisting private writing does not compel the writing’s contents, but producing it can be testimonial when production admits existence, possession, or authenticity. Use and derivative use immunity is enough to compel that act; transactional immunity is not required.

**Silver Key:** Read the choices as an immunity ladder: no immunity, use-and-derivative immunity, transactional immunity, or no compulsion ever. The middle protection is the exam answer when the privilege is real but can be displaced.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** The attraction is the personal nature of the ledger. It feels like a private devotional writing should be outside compulsory process altogether. The breaker is the word **“under any circumstances.”** The privilege can be overcome by protection that blocks use and derivative use of the compelled production act.

**B.** This is the dominant trap. It sells a true-sounding distinction: Stephen was not forced to create the ledger. That answers the content/authorship question, not the production question. **Gold Key:** the compelled act of producing a document can itself communicate existence, possession, and authenticity. **Silver Key:** the choice sits at the “no immunity” end of the ladder; do not pick an endpoint when a coextensive middle protection is available.

**D.** This choice sounds safest because transactional immunity is broader than use and derivative use immunity. The breaker is the word **“only.”** The Fifth Amendment requires protection coextensive with the privilege, not broader immunity from prosecution.

## 9. Black-letter verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row identifies the tested issue as a criminal-procedure Fifth Amendment grand-jury production question, gives the source pick rates, and marks the original credited answer as use and derivative use immunity.  

The black-letter rule is stable. The Fifth Amendment protects against compelled testimonial self-incrimination. In the document-subpoena setting, the contents of a preexisting document are not compelled by the subpoena, but the act of producing the document may have testimonial significance if production admits existence, possession, or authenticity. *Fisher* states the testimonial-communication limit and discusses the act-of-production theory; *Doe* frames the inquiry as whether production has communicative aspects. ([Justia Law][1])

Use and derivative use immunity is constitutionally sufficient because it is coextensive with the Fifth Amendment privilege. Transactional immunity is broader and is not constitutionally required. *Kastigar* states that use and derivative use immunity suffices to compel testimony over a privilege claim, and 18 U.S.C. § 6002 reflects the same use-and-fruits protection model in federal proceedings. ([Justia Law][2])

Because this is a state grand jury, the immunity must also protect against use of the compelled testimony and its fruits in another jurisdiction’s prosecution. *Murphy v. Waterfront Commission* supplies that cross-jurisdiction protection principle. The transformed item does not alter the tested rule, issue, or credited outcome. ([Justia Law][3])

**GK-CRIMINAL-ACT-PRODUCTION-01**
A subpoena for a preexisting private writing does not compel the writing’s contents, but producing it can be testimonial when production admits existence, possession, or authenticity. Use and derivative use immunity is enough to compel that act; transactional immunity is not required.

## Silver Key(s)

**SK-CRIMINAL-ADVENT-LEDGER-01**
Read the choices as an immunity ladder: none, use-and-derivative, transactional, or never. The middle protection usually wins when the call asks how compulsion can happen, not whether the suspect wrote the item voluntarily.
