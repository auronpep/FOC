### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Gold Key: an omission supports criminal liability only when the law first gives the defendant a legal duty to act. Lydia may have had a strong moral reason to warn the officers, but the facts do not give her a legal duty to protect the crowd, report Peter, or stop the fire. Silver Key: when three homicide choices blame a bystander for silence, pause before grading murder versus manslaughter and ask whether the stem gives that bystander a duty to act. It does not, so the clean answer is no crime.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A. Involuntary manslaughter sells the most attractive trap: Lydia saw danger, did nothing, and people died. The breaker is the missing duty. Gold Key: negligence-like silence is not enough for homicide unless the defendant first had a legal duty to act, such as a statute, status relationship, contract, voluntary assumption of care, or creation of the peril.

C. Murder overcharges the same silence as malice. The answer has no act by Lydia, no legal duty supporting omission liability, and no malice-causing homicide path tied to her conduct.

D. Voluntary manslaughter imports the wrong homicide label. Heat of passion, adequate provocation, and an intentional killing by Lydia are not the issue; the stem is about a bystander's failure to warn.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is unchanged: criminal liability for an omission requires a legal duty to act. In Jones v. United States, 308 F.2d 307 (D.C. Cir. 1962), the court treated legal duty as the critical element for omission-based manslaughter and identified duty sources such as statute, status relationship, contract, and voluntary assumption of care that prevents others from helping. See https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/308/307/132737/.

Lydia is a bystander. She did not create the peril, take custody of the victims, contract to protect them, stand in a protected status relationship, or violate a stated reporting statute. Her silence therefore cannot supply the actus reus for murder, voluntary manslaughter, or involuntary manslaughter. Misprision does not change the homicide answer: Cornell's Wex summary notes that modern federal misprision requires affirmative concealment and that mere failure to report is not sufficient. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/misprision_of_a_felony.
