Paul operated a private Christian youth-retreat company that needed laminated verse-card sets for its summer camps. ### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** Lydia Printworks did not promise to supply all of Paul's requirements, all of its own output, or any minimum quantity. It promised only to sell Paul the verse-card sets it chose to sell. That leaves Lydia free to decide to sell none to Paul.

**Gold Key:** A requirements term can save an open quantity only when the party whose quantity controls is actually bound by good faith requirements or output. A seller's promise to sell only what the seller chooses to sell is optional performance, so it is illusory.

**Silver Key:** When the answer set gives you both "requirements contract" and "illusory," track whose quantity is constrained. The buyer's all-it-needs promise does not force the seller to supply anything if the seller kept the choice whether to sell.

The court should not rule for Paul because the agreement is unenforceable against Lydia Printworks.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A.** This choice attracts the student who treats a signed writing as the formation finish line. The breaker is that signatures do not supply a real promise where one party's performance remains optional. **Gold Key:** an apparent promise is illusory when the promisor keeps the choice whether to perform at all.

**C.** This choice gets the outcome word right but targets the wrong flaw. The problem is not that the agreement lacks an exact number like "600"; true requirements and output contracts often use variable quantity terms. The breaker is that Lydia's quantity was not tied to output, requirements, or good faith business need. It was tied only to what Lydia chose to sell.

**D.** This is the dominant trap. It sees Paul's promise to buy all the verse-card sets his retreat company needed from Lydia and labels the deal a requirements contract. **Silver Key:** the requirements label must constrain the seller's duty too. Lydia promised only the sets she chose to sell, so the label does not create an enforceable supply duty.

### 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested issue is unchanged from the source item: whether a supposed goods-supply agreement is enforceable when the buyer promises to buy all it needs, but the seller promises only to sell what it wants or chooses to sell. The credited outcome is also unchanged: the buyer loses because the seller's apparent promise is illusory.

UCC section 2-306 validates real output and requirements contracts by reading the quantity as the seller's actual good-faith output or the buyer's actual good-faith requirements, subject to the unreasonably-disproportionate limit. The official comment to the enacted UCC text explains that such contracts are not too indefinite and do not lack mutuality because good faith constrains the party determining quantity. [1]

That rule does not save this agreement. Lydia Printworks did not agree to sell all of its output, and did not agree to satisfy all of Paul's requirements. It agreed only to sell what it chose to sell. Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 77 states the illusory-promise rule: an apparent promise is not consideration when the promisor reserves optional performance unless each alternative would itself be consideration or a real contingency may eliminate the non-consideration alternative. [2] The American Law Institute's Restatement page confirms that Restatement (Second) of Contracts Volume 1 covers formation and consideration. [3]

The source row's parent outline code `55020000` is refined to the deepest verified node that covers the tested rule: `55020202 Formation > Consideration > Illusory promises`. The requirements-contract distractor remains doctrinally relevant because UCC section 2-306 is the reason an actual requirements contract would not fail for open quantity. The dispositive flaw here is that the seller did not make an output, requirements, minimum-quantity, exclusive-dealing, or other real supply commitment.
