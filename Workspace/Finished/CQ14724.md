### 1. Final question

At a crowded Christian college choir festival, Daniel saw a small leather envelope slip out of Lydia's tote bag and land beside a row of folding chairs. Daniel picked up the envelope and saw that it contained $100 in cash. Thinking that he could use the money and seeing no one looking at him, Daniel put the envelope inside his jacket. Moments later, Lydia walked back to the row and asked Daniel whether he had seen her missing envelope. Daniel said no and left with the envelope.

Of what crime, if any, is Daniel guilty?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A person sees another person's cash envelope fall, picks it up knowing whose it is, decides to keep it, lies when asked, and leaves. What crime is it?

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Daniel saw the envelope fall from Lydia's tote, so he knew the property belonged to someone else before he pocketed it. When he put it inside his jacket intending to keep the cash, the taking was trespassory and carried the intent to permanently deprive. Gold Key: known-owner lost property plus intent to keep at the taking is larceny, even if the item first hit the floor. Silver Key: sort the theft labels by how possession began; no entrustment and no title transfer leave larceny as the label that fits.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

A. False pretenses is attractive because Daniel lied when Lydia asked about the envelope. The lie came after he already had possession, and Lydia never transferred title because of that lie, so the false-pretenses label answers the wrong theft variant.

C. No crime is attractive because the envelope was dropped before Daniel picked it up. Gold Key: a dropped item is not free to keep when the finder knows whose it is and forms the keep-it intent at the taking.

D. Embezzlement is attractive because Daniel later converted property that was not his. Silver Key: embezzlement needs lawful possession through entrustment first, and Lydia never entrusted Daniel with the envelope.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The credited answer is larceny. Common-law larceny requires a wrongful taking and carrying away of another's personal property with intent to deprive the owner. The U.S. Department of Justice criminal resource manual summarizes larceny as requiring wrongful taking and carrying away, lack of consent, and intent to deprive. Modern theft statutes also preserve the same owner-known lost-property idea: Pennsylvania section 3924 treats control over known lost or mislaid property, coupled with intent to deprive and failure to restore it, as theft; New York Penal Law section 155.05 includes common-law larceny and acquiring known lost property without reasonable return measures within larceny. Those authorities confirm the supplied answer. Daniel knew Lydia owned the envelope when he picked it up, and he intended to keep it then. That leaves larceny, not embezzlement, false pretenses, or no crime.
