A company sells a portable Bible-study lantern designed with a ceramic heat shield between the bulb housing and the handle. When the lantern is made to specifications, it passes temperature testing. ### 5. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

C is correct. The problem is not that all lanterns in the model are badly designed. The problem is that this one lantern left the factory without a heat shield that the intended design required. Gold Key: manufacturing defect is the "rogue unit" category. If the particular product departs from its intended design while other units are properly made, the best theory is manufacturing defect. Silver Key: lock the clash on unit versus line. One missing part on one unit points to manufacturing defect, not design defect.

### 6. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A (dominant trap) sells the model-wide-design instinct. The words "model," "designed," and "temperature testing" can make design defect sound plausible. The breaker is the single-unit fact: the model passes testing when made to specifications, and other lanterns do not lack the heat shield. Silver Key: the deciding axis is unit versus line. A design-defect answer attacks the whole product plan; this stem attacks one lantern's departure from that plan.

B sells a negligence-proof requirement. It sounds careful because an omitted part usually happened through someone's assembly error. The breaker is strict products liability. Gold Key: for a manufacturing defect, the plaintiff does not have to prove exactly which employee made the mistake; the defect is that the product departed from its intended design when it left the seller's control.

D sells a warning-frame shortcut. A warning theory belongs when the product needs adequate instructions or risk warnings. Here the harm came from a missing heat shield, not from the lack of a warning about the ordinary fact that lamps can become warm. The warning label would not turn the defective unit into a properly manufactured lantern.

### 7. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. The legal issue is whether a product-liability fact pattern involving one unit that departed from intended specifications is best classified as a manufacturing defect. The credited answer remains manufacturing defect.

Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability section 2(a) states the governing category: a product has a manufacturing defect when it departs from its intended design even though reasonable care may have been used in preparation and marketing. The American Law Institute identifies manufacturing defects, design defects, and inadequate warning/instruction defects as distinct product-defect categories in the Restatement (Third) products-liability framework. Cornell's Legal Information Institute likewise describes a manufacturing defect as a departure from intended design affecting some products because of a flaw in manufacture, in contrast to design defects that affect all products.
