**Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)**

B is correct. Fourth Amendment suppression is personal. A defendant must show that the challenged police conduct violated his OWN protected interest in the place searched. Lydia did not work at Stephen's church office, had no key, stored nothing there, and had never visited. She has no privacy or property interest in the office, so the unconstitutional search of Stephen's office violated Stephen's rights, not hers. The fact that the ledger hurts her defense does not give her standing to suppress it. The motion fails.

Silver Key: When evaluating a suppression motion, first check standing: does the defendant have a personal privacy or property interest in the place searched? If not, the motion fails regardless of how the evidence was obtained or how harmful it is to the defense.

**Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer**

Tested rule: Fourth Amendment suppression is personal. A defendant seeking to suppress evidence obtained through an unlawful search must show that the search violated his OWN Fourth Amendment rights — typically, that he had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the place searched or a property interest in the place searched. Standing to suppress is NOT established by: (1) the fact that the evidence is harmful to the defendant's defense; (2) the defendant's membership in a conspiracy with the person whose rights were violated; (3) the type of evidence involved (documentary or otherwise). The co-conspirator rule in conspiracy law (e.g., statements of one co-conspirator in furtherance of the conspiracy are admissible against all) is a rule of evidentiary admissibility under the hearsay rule, not a rule of Fourth Amendment standing.
