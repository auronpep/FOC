### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

C is correct. Under FRE 104(a), preliminary questions concerning the admissibility of evidence — including foundational requirements for hearsay exceptions like dying declarations — are decided by the court, not the jury. Both sides are entitled to present evidence and argument on the admissibility question. Under FRE 104(c), the court must conduct the hearing outside the jury's presence when justice so requires, which is routinely the case in criminal trials to prevent inadmissible evidence from reaching the jury by any means. Here, the admissibility of Daniel's statement turns on whether he believed his death was imminent — a preliminary question of fact for the judge under FRE 104(a). Both the prosecution and the defense may present evidence on that issue, and the hearing must be held outside the jury's presence.

**Gold Key (FRE 104 Gatekeeper Rule):** The judge is the gatekeeper on all preliminary questions of admissibility (FRE 104(a)). The jury never decides admissibility — that is the single most tested structural rule in Evidence. When the admissibility of evidence depends on a disputed fact (like the declarant's state of mind), the judge applies the sufficiency standard under FRE 104(b): whether the proponent has introduced enough evidence to support a finding that the fact exists. But the judge still decides — the jury does not.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect** because it places the jury at the preliminary hearing. This is the "jury-present" trap. FRE 104(c) requires that hearings on preliminary questions in criminal cases be conducted outside the jury's presence when justice so requires — and a dying-declaration foundation hearing in a murder trial plainly qualifies. The whole point of the preliminary hearing is to keep potentially inadmissible evidence away from the jury. Having the jury present defeats the purpose. The choice correctly identifies that both sides participate and that the judge decides, but the jury-presence element is a structural violation.

**B is incorrect** because it excludes the defense from the preliminary hearing and applies the wrong standard. This is the **dominant trap** — it sounds reasonable because it references the "reasonable finding" language of FRE 104(b), and it correctly keeps the jury out. But two things make it wrong. First, FRE 104(a) entitles *both* sides to present evidence and argument on preliminary questions. Allowing only the prosecutor to present evidence violates the opposing party's right to be heard on admissibility. Second, the judge does not merely ask whether a *jury could reasonably find* the foundational fact — the judge *decides* the preliminary question. The "reasonable finding" standard under FRE 104(b) applies to conditional relevance, not to the judge's own determination of admissibility. **Gold Key:** Both sides get to be heard on preliminary questions — the opposing party is never shut out of a 104 hearing.

**D is incorrect** because it hands the admissibility decision to the jury. This is the "jury-decides" trap. FRE 104(a) is unambiguous: the *court* decides preliminary questions of admissibility. The jury's role is to weigh admissible evidence, not to gatekeep what comes in. Allowing the jury to determine whether the dying declaration is admissible conflates the jury's fact-finding function with the judge's gatekeeping function. Additionally, the jury should not be present at the hearing for the same reasons stated in the explanation for A.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** FRE 104(a) and (c), Federal Rules of Evidence.

**FRE 104(a):** "The court must decide any preliminary question about whether a witness is qualified, a privilege exists, or evidence is admissible. In so deciding, the court is not bound by evidence rules, except those on privilege."

**FRE 104(c):** "The court must conduct any hearing on a preliminary question so that the jury cannot hear it if: (1) the hearing involves the admissibility of a confession; (2) a defendant in a criminal case is a witness and so requests; or (3) justice so requires."

**Authority:** The text of FRE 104(a) and (c) is unambiguous. The judge decides preliminary questions of admissibility — not the jury. Both sides participate. The hearing is conducted outside the jury's presence when justice requires (as in a criminal murder trial). The dying declaration exception (FRE 804(b)(2)) requires that the declarant believed death was imminent — a foundational fact that is a preliminary question for the court under FRE 104(a).

**Verification against original:** The tested rule (FRE 104(a) — judge decides, both sides participate, jury excluded), the legal issue (preliminary hearing on dying declaration foundation), and the credited outcome (judge decides with both sides participating, jury not present) are all unchanged from the original. The official key is confirmed correct.

**Source:** Fed. R. Evid. 104(a), (c). See also *Bourjaily v. United States*, 483 U.S. 171 (1987) (preliminary questions of admissibility are for the court under Rule 104(a)); *United States v. James*, 590 U.S. 302 (2020) (dying declaration foundation is a preliminary question for the court).

#### Wrong Answer Recovery Paths

**If you picked A (jury present):** You correctly identified that both sides participate and the judge decides, but you missed the jury-exclusion requirement. Remember: FRE 104(c) requires the hearing be outside the jury's presence when justice so requires. In a criminal murder trial, justice always requires it. The whole point of the preliminary hearing is to shield the jury from inadmissible evidence.

**If you picked B (dominant trap):** This is the most attractive distractor because it correctly keeps the jury out and name-drops the "reasonable finding" standard. But two things went wrong: (1) both sides — not just the prosecutor — are entitled to present evidence on preliminary questions, and (2) the judge decides the preliminary question, rather than asking whether a jury could reasonably find the fact. The "reasonable finding" standard of FRE 104(b) applies to conditional relevance, not to the judge's own admissibility determination.

**If you picked D (jury decides):** You confused the judge's gatekeeping role with the jury's fact-finding role. The jury weighs admissible evidence; the judge decides what comes in. FRE 104(a) is unambiguous: "The court must decide any preliminary question about whether evidence is admissible."
