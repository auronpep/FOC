## 6. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**B is correct.** Under Fed. R. Civ. P. 13(g), a crossclaim is a claim by one party against a coparty that arises out of the transaction or occurrence that is the subject matter of the original action. The rule uses the word "may," making the crossclaim permissive rather than compulsory. The camp (co-defendant) may assert its claim against the carrier (the other co-defendant) in this federal action, but it is not required to do so. It can wait and file a separate action in state court after this case concludes. 

**Silver Key (call focus):** The call asks whether the camp "may" file separately later. The permissive language of Rule 13(g) directly answers that the claim is optional, so the camp is free to choose the later state-court filing.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A is attractive because** students often confuse crossclaims with compulsory counterclaims under Rule 13(a), which do require a party to bring related claims against an opposing party in the same action or lose them. The breaker is that Rule 13(g) expressly uses "may state as a crossclaim" for claims against a coparty; nothing in the rule or the doctrine of claim preclusion forces the camp to bring the claim here. The same-T/O language sets the outer boundary of what *can* be brought as a crossclaim, but does not create a "must bring now" obligation.

**C is attractive because** students assume that once liability facts are aired in one lawsuit, a second suit on the same facts will be barred by res judicata or collateral estoppel. The breaker is that claim preclusion requires that the claimant have had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the claim in the first action. Because crossclaims are optional, the camp will not have had that opportunity if it chooses not to assert the claim here; the federal judgment will not bar the later state-court action against the carrier.

**D is attractive because** the camp and the carrier share State B citizenship, which would destroy complete diversity if they were on opposite sides of a new federal action. The breaker is that the camp is not trying to start a new federal action; it plans a state-court suit. State courts are courts of general jurisdiction and do not require diversity. The shared citizenship is irrelevant to the question whether the camp can wait and sue in state court.

## 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited answer is correct and unchanged from the source row. Fed. R. Civ. P. 13(g) states: "A pleading may state as a crossclaim any claim by one party against a coparty if the claim arises out of the transaction or occurrence that is the subject matter of the original action or of a counterclaim therein, or if the claim relates to any property that is the subject matter of the original action." (Official text from U.S. Courts, Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, Dec. 1, 2024 compilation.)

The key word is "may." This is the textual foundation for the long-settled rule that crossclaims are permissive. Wright & Miller and Moore's Federal Practice both treat Rule 13(g) crossclaims as optional; there is no compulsory-crossclaim doctrine parallel to Rule 13(a). The same-T/O requirement is a limitation on what *may* be asserted as a crossclaim in the pending action; it does not create an obligation to assert it. Because the rule is permissive, a party that elects not to bring a qualifying crossclaim retains the right to assert that claim in a later, separate action (subject to ordinary statutes of limitations and any preclusion doctrines that might apply if the party actually litigated the issue). The citizenship facts are irrelevant to the crossclaim-permissiveness question; they matter only for federal subject-matter jurisdiction over the original action or any new federal action the camp might contemplate (which it is not).

No doctrinal-currency issue applies (this is a stable 1937/2007/2009 rule text with no recent amendment altering the permissive character). The official key (C in source, B after shuffle) is confirmed by the plain text of Rule 13(g).
