### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is the only answer that locks onto the moment named in the call. The officer did more than ask questions: he kept Hannah's license and stood in the gate to the exit ramp. Gold Key: a consensual police encounter becomes a seizure when, under all the circumstances, a reasonable person would not feel free to leave or otherwise end the encounter. Silver Key: do not freeze the analysis at the first polite question; mark the moment the officer adds a restraint.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because the encounter began consensually. The breaker is that consent at the beginning does not control the later moment when the officer kept the license and blocked the path.

B is attractive because the requested satchel search is the obvious police action. The breaker is timing: the call asks what happened when the license was kept and the path was blocked, before any physical search.

D is attractive because handcuffs are an unmistakable restraint. Gold Key: handcuffs are not required; a show of authority can create a seizure when a reasonable person would not feel free to leave.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The credited answer remains unchanged in substance. A seizure of a person occurs when, considering all the circumstances, police use physical force or a show of authority that restrains liberty so that a reasonable person would not feel free to leave or otherwise terminate the encounter. Asking questions or requesting identification can remain consensual, but retaining a traveler's identification while adding a coercive restriction can turn the encounter into a Fourth Amendment seizure. With no reasonable suspicion, that detention is unlawful.

Authority floor: the Fourth Amendment governs seizures of persons; United States v. Mendenhall, 446 U.S. 544 (1980), states the reasonable-person-not-free-to-leave test; Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), treats retention of ticket/license along with coercive movement as an effective seizure; Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. 429 (1991), frames the inquiry as whether a reasonable person would feel free to decline requests or terminate the encounter. Sources checked: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/446/544/, https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/460/491/, and https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/89-1717.ZO.html.
