## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question
An insurance-fraud investigator, working undercover for the state, approached Peter at his small Christian-themed boat-repair shop and proposed that they jointly stage the sinking of a customer's boat so they could collect the insurance money and split the payout. Peter agreed enthusiastically, suggested a meeting location at a closed boatyard to plan the staging, and provided the investigator with a list of customers whose boats would be "good candidates." At no point did the investigator actually intend to sink any boat or split any payout. Peter was arrested and charged with conspiracy to commit insurance fraud under common law. Peter argues the conspiracy charge must fail. Under the common-law bilateral conspiracy rule, is Peter correct?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. Common-law conspiracy is inherently bilateral: the crime is the agreement of two or more criminal minds to pursue an unlawful objective. Because the investigator never actually intended to stage a boat sinking or split any payout, she never genuinely joined the criminal scheme. Without a second genuine criminal mind, there is no "meeting of the minds" — and so no common-law conspiracy. Gold Key: at common law, conspiracy requires that *each* party to the purported agreement actually intend to commit the target crime; an undercover agent's feigned consent is not a "meeting of the minds." Silver Key: when two distractors both say "yes, the charge fails," pick the one that names the bilateral-agreement reason, not the one that invents a special-agent-immunity rule. The C answer gives the doctrinal reason the common-law charge collapses; the A answer gives the wrong reason and therefore does not survive. Peter's argument therefore succeeds: the conspiracy charge must fail.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

The breaker is that the failure is not a special immunity rule for state agents; it is the bilateral-agreement requirement. Gold Key: the bilateral rule means the charge fails because the *agreement* element is missing, not because of who the second party is. B is attractive because it echoes the Model Penal Code's unilateral approach and the common student habit of treating overt acts as the conspiracy's "glue." It also reads like a perfectly natural summary of the facts: Peter clearly intended, Peter clearly acted. The breaker is that the common-law rule is bilateral — Peter's intent and overt acts cannot supply the second criminal mind. Gold Key: at common law, conspiracy is the agreement of two or more persons; one party's intent is not enough. B is the dominant trap because it is the version of the rule most students half-remember.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law, Inchoate Crimes, Conspiracy, and the verified outline code is `71040200` (Inchoate Offenses > Conspiracy). At common law, conspiracy is the agreement of two or more persons to commit an unlawful act, accomplished by a genuine "meeting of the minds." Because both parties must actually share the criminal purpose, a feigning undercover officer or informant who lacks genuine criminal intent cannot supply the second mind, and the bilateral agreement element fails. The Supreme Court has long described "the essence of a conspiracy" as "an agreement to commit an unlawful act." *Iannelli v. United States*, 420 U.S. 770, 777 (1975). The federal general conspiracy statute, 18 U.S.C. § 371, has been interpreted in many circuits to be unilateral (it is satisfied even if the only other party is an undercover agent who feigns consent), but that is a *federal statutory* reading, not a common-law one. See *United States v. Feola*, 420 U.S. 671 (1975) (federal statute does not require a co-conspirator to know the official status of the intended victim); *United States v. Vallee*, 807 F.3d 508, 522 (2d Cir. 2015) (noting that some circuits have "taken a bilateral approach to the crime of conspiracy: at least two people must agree"). The Model Penal Code § 5.03(1) is the leading codification of the unilateral approach, again in contrast to the common-law rule. The question here is explicitly the "common-law bilateral conspiracy rule," so the C answer is correct: at common law, the feigning investigator's lack of genuine criminal intent defeats the agreement element, and Peter's charge fails. The source explanation's MPC contrast is preserved verbatim in the variant. The official key (original B / new C) is consistent with the common-law rule, and the variant does not change the credited outcome.
