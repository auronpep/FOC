## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** Timothy validly waived his Miranda rights. His later statement — "If I'm in real trouble here, I'd like to talk to someone who knows the law" — was conditional ("if") and equivocal ("someone who knows the law" not "a lawyer"). Under *Davis v. United States*, 512 U.S. 452 (1994), a suspect must invoke the right to counsel *unambiguously*. An ambiguous or equivocal reference does not require officers to stop questioning. Timothy's statement was not a clear invocation, so officials could continue, and his admission is not suppressible on this ground.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. "No, because the right to counsel during questioning applies only after formal charges are filed."**
Right conclusion, wrong reason. Flat misstatement: Miranda applies during custodial interrogation *before* charges. The actual reason for no suppression is that Timothy's ambiguous statement didn't invoke his right — not that Miranda doesn't apply pre-charge.

**B. "Yes, because any mention of wanting legal help invokes the right to counsel."** ⚠️ DOMINANT TRAP
Overclaim trap. Takes a kernel of truth (invoking counsel stops questioning) and pushes it too far. Not *any* mention triggers the right — only *unambiguous* invocations do. "If," "maybe," and "someone who knows the law" are the kind of conditional, equivocal language *Davis* held insufficient.

**C. "Yes, because officials were required to clarify whether Timothy wanted a lawyer."**
Backwards trap. Officers *may* clarify ambiguous references but are not *constitutionally required* to. *Davis* held the obligation to stop arises only from an unambiguous invocation. This choice turns a permissible option into a mandatory one.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Governing rule:** A suspect who has waived Miranda rights must *unambiguously* invoke the right to counsel for questioning to cease. Ambiguous or equivocal references do not invoke the right. *Davis v. United States*, 512 U.S. 452 (1994).

**Authority:** *Davis v. United States*, 512 U.S. 452 (1994) — "Maybe I should talk to a lawyer" was not an unambiguous invocation; officers not required to stop. *Edwards v. Arizona*, 451 U.S. 477 (1981) — unambiguous invocation requires cessation. *Berghuis v. Thompkins*, 560 U.S. 370 (2010) — invocation must be unambiguous and unequivocal.

**Application:** Timothy's statement was conditional ("if I'm in real trouble") and equivocal ("someone who knows the law"). Under *Davis*, not an unambiguous invocation. Officials had no duty to stop or clarify. Admission stands.
