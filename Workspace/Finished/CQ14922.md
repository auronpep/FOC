## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Lydia owned a small lakeside parcel that several local families used for weekend Bible-study picnics. She signed a written contract to sell the parcel to Barnabas. The contract said nothing about the quality of title Lydia had to convey. The closing occurred, and Lydia delivered a warranty deed to Barnabas.

A few months later, a popular Christian retreat center opened nearby, and the parcel became much more valuable. Barnabas then signed a written contract to sell the parcel to Ruth. That contract expressly required Barnabas to convey marketable title. Ruth’s attorney discovered that title to the parcel was not marketable and had already been unmarketable when Lydia delivered the deed to Barnabas. Ruth refused to close.

Barnabas sued Lydia for breach of the land-sale contract, seeking damages for the lost resale to Ruth. On this count, who is likely to prevail?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

After a land sale closes by warranty deed, can the buyer sue the original seller on the prior land-sale contract for failure to convey marketable title?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. Lydia, because she did not expressly promise to convey marketable title.
B. Lydia, because her contract obligations as to title merged into the deed.
C. Barnabas, because Lydia is liable for all reasonably foreseeable damages caused by the failed resale.
D. Barnabas, because the law implies in a land-sale contract a covenant that title will be marketable.

### 5. Correct answer

**B**. Original key: **C**.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** The claim is framed as breach of the land-sale contract. That matters. Before closing, a land-sale contract ordinarily carries an implied obligation to deliver marketable title. But after closing and delivery of the deed, the title-related contract obligations generally merge into the deed. **Gold Key:** marketable-title contract duty is a pre-closing contract-stage duty; after deed delivery, the buyer’s title remedy generally must be based on the deed, not the prior contract. **Silver Key:** lock the procedural-looking phrase “on this count”; the count is contract breach, not a warranty-deed claim.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells the idea that silence in the first contract ends the case. That is attractive because Lydia’s original contract did not expressly discuss title. The breaker is that silence is not the right reason: land-sale contracts generally imply marketable title before closing, so the problem is not the absence of express words. The problem is that the contract-stage title duty merged into the deed.

**C.** This choice sells the lost resale as the main event. The resale loss sounds foreseeable because the later buyer refused to close for the same title defect. The breaker is source-of-duty: foreseeable damages matter only after a surviving breach claim exists. Here, the contract claim on title has been displaced by the deed.

**D.** This is the dominant trap. It states a real land-sale contract rule: marketable title is generally implied. The trap is stage. **Gold Key:** that implied contract duty is enforceable before closing; after the buyer accepts the deed, title remedies generally move to the deed. **Silver Key:** stage beats attractiveness — do not pick the true pre-closing rule when the call asks about a post-closing contract claim.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The source row states QID 14922, Real Property / Contracts, with original answer C credited and original A as the most selected distractor at 55%. It frames the issue as a post-closing breach-of-contract claim against the original seller after delivery of a warranty deed. 

The legal rule is unchanged. A seller of real property generally has an implied promise to deliver marketable title; Cornell Wex states that when a seller sells property to a buyer, an implied promise is that the seller will deliver marketable title. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) But under the merger doctrine, provisions in a real-property sale contract generally merge into the deed once the deed is executed, delivered, and accepted; one cited formulation states that the deed then regulates the parties’ rights and liabilities. ([Law Resource][2])

Applied here, Barnabas sued on the original land-sale contract for failure to convey marketable title. That contract-stage title obligation merged into Lydia’s warranty deed at closing. The correct result remains that Lydia prevails on the contract count. Any separate deed-covenant claim is outside the call.
