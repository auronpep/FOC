## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

In a federal negligence trial over a delivery-van collision outside a private Christian bookshop, Lydia calls Stephen, expecting him to testify that the delivery van rolled through a stop sign before striking a handbell cart. Stephen instead testifies that the van stopped before entering the intersection. While Stephen is still on the stand, Lydia shows him a signed but unsworn statement he gave to an investigator two days after the collision, in which he said the van did not stop. Stephen denies making the statement. Lydia offers the statement only to impeach Stephen. The defendant objects that Lydia called Stephen as her own witness.

How should the court rule?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

Can a party impeach its own witness with an unsworn prior inconsistent statement when the statement is offered only to attack credibility?

### 3. Final answer choices

C. Admit the statement for impeachment only, subject to a limiting instruction on request.

D. Exclude the statement because prior inconsistent statements are never usable unless given at trial.

### 5. Correct answer

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. Lydia called Stephen, but that does not bar Lydia from attacking Stephen's credibility after he gives testimony contrary to what she expected. Gold Key: FRE 607 abolished the old voucher rule; any party, including the party that called the witness, may attack the witness's credibility. Gold Key: an ordinary signed but unsworn prior inconsistent statement can impeach a witness when the Rule 613 foundation is satisfied, but it is not substantive prior-statement evidence under current Rule 801(d)(1)(A). Silver Key: lock the purpose before choosing the evidence label. Lydia offered the statement only to show that Stephen changed stories, so the statement is admitted for impeachment only, with a limiting instruction if requested.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

B sells the dominant old-voucher trap. The objection sounds historically plausible because Lydia called Stephen and now wants to attack him. The breaker is FRE 607: a party may impeach its own witness.

A sells a powerful truth-use upgrade. The statement directly contradicts Stephen's trial testimony and would help Lydia prove the van failed to stop. The breaker is that the statement was signed but unsworn and made to an investigator, so it is not current Rule 801(d)(1)(A) substantive prior-statement evidence. It comes in only for credibility.

D sells an absolute-formalism trap. The answer hears "prior inconsistent statement" and demands that the earlier statement itself have been given at trial. The breaker is that prior inconsistent statements may be used for impeachment; trial, hearing, proceeding, or deposition status matters to substantive use, not to the basic impeachment lane.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: a party may impeach its own witness with a prior inconsistent statement, but an ordinary unsworn inconsistent statement offered only for impeachment is not substantive proof of the asserted fact.

Federal Rule of Evidence 607 provides the authority floor: any party, including the party that called the witness, may attack the witness's credibility. Federal Rule of Evidence 613 governs witness-prior-statement impeachment and, after the December 1, 2024 amendment, generally requires the witness to receive an opportunity to explain or deny a prior inconsistent statement before extrinsic evidence of it is admitted, with exceptions the court may allow. Stephen was confronted while still on the stand and denied making the signed statement, so the impeachment foundation is satisfied on these facts. Federal Rule of Evidence 801(d)(1)(A), as currently in force on June 16, 2026, treats an inconsistent prior statement as substantive non-hearsay only when the declarant testifies, is subject to cross-examination, and the prior statement was given under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition. This investigator statement was signed but unsworn, so it is not substantive prior-statement evidence. Rule 105 supports limiting the jury to the impeachment use if a party timely requests a limiting instruction.
