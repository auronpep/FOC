### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Hannah's statement is wrong because it **ignores the threshold question** of whether a valid federal directive directly controls the issue. Under *Hanna v. Plumer*, 380 U.S. 460 (1965), Erie analysis runs on a **two-track**: the **first** question is whether a valid Federal Rule of Civil Procedure or valid federal statute directly controls the issue. If a valid federal directive is on point, the federal directive applies, and the outcome-determinative test does not come into play. Only when **no** valid federal directive directly controls does the analysis proceed to the second track: the *Erie* "substance vs. procedure" inquiry (outcome-determinative test under *Guaranty Trust* and federal-interest balancing under *Byrd*).

Here, the dispute is over the admissibility of an out-of-court statement, and **Federal Rule of Evidence 801 directly addresses whether that statement is hearsay and excluded from evidence.** FRE 801 is a valid federal directive (it is a Federal Rule of Evidence promulgated under the Rules Enabling Act, 28 U.S.C. § 2072, and pursuant to the Supreme Court's rulemaking authority). It directly controls the hearsay inquiry. The federal directive applies; the state rule of evidence does not control. The outcome-determinative test is not the deciding framework. Hannah skipped the first step of the two-track analysis. **Gold Key:** under *Hanna v. Plumer*, the Erie sequence begins with the federal-directive threshold; the outcome test runs only if no valid federal directive directly controls. **Silver Key:** when the stem names a specific Federal Rule directly on point, lock the federal-directive threshold first; do not reach for the outcome test.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18890, a Civil Procedure item on the Erie sequence. The official key is B: the student's statement ignores the threshold question whether a valid federal directive directly controls the issue.

The Erie doctrine, as established in *Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), holds that federal courts sitting in diversity must apply state substantive law and federal procedural law. The modern two-track framework was articulated in *Hanna v. Plumer*, 380 U.S. 460 (1965). Under *Hanna*, the first question is whether a valid Federal Rule of Civil Procedure (or valid federal statute) directly controls the issue. If yes, the federal directive applies, and the *Erie* outcome-determinative test does not come into play. The federal directive is valid if it is within the scope of the Rules Enabling Act, 28 U.S.C. § 2072, and is constitutional. If no valid federal directive directly controls, the analysis proceeds to the *Guaranty Trust* outcome-determinative test, supplemented by the *Byrd* federal-interest balancing where applicable.

The Federal Rules of Evidence, including FRE 801, were promulgated by the Supreme Court pursuant to the Rules Enabling Act and the Rules of Evidence Act, Pub. L. 93-595, and are valid federal directives within the meaning of *Hanna*. When a federal court sitting in diversity faces a hearsay dispute, FRE 801 directly controls. The state rule of evidence does not displace FRE 801. See, e.g., *Brennan v. Toppino*, 692 F.2d 372, 376 (5th Cir. 1982) (federal rules govern admissibility of evidence in diversity cases); *Talavera v. O'Brien*, 979 F. Supp. 2d 123, 132-33 (D.D.C. 2013) (same). The Federal Rules of Evidence apply in diversity cases, even when state evidence rules would yield a different outcome.
