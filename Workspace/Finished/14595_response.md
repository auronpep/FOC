# BarMatrix Q#14595 — Criminal Law & Procedure (Searches & Seizures)

Question summary

A defendant charged with murder (victim strangled) suffers a neurological impairment that prevents him from remembering events past 48 hours. After charging, police asked to search his home; the defendant consented. Police found a diary entry dated the day of the victim's disappearance that reads, "Indescribable excitement. Why did no one ever tell me that killing gave such pleasure to the master?" The defendant moves to exclude the diary on two grounds:

1. Admission would violate Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination.  
2. He could not give "informed" consent to the search because, more than 48 hours after making the entry, he could not remember its existence when he consented. There is no evidence the officers knew about his memory impairment.

Answer choices

A: Admit it, because the defendant's consent was not obtained by intentional police misconduct and the defendant was not compelled to make the diary entry.  
B: Admit it, pursuant to the good-faith exception to the exclusionary rule.  
C: Exclude it, because the defendant was not competent to consent to a search.  
D: Exclude it, because use of the diary as evidence would violate the defendant's privilege against self-incrimination.

Selected answer: A

Reasoning and verification (highest-available effort)

Short answer: Admit the diary (A).

Legal analysis and authorities

1) Fourth Amendment — Consent searches

- Standard: Consent to a search is valid if it is voluntary under the totality of the circumstances. The Supreme Court has instructed that voluntariness is a question of fact, and that knowledge of the right to refuse is only one factor, not a prerequisite. See Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218 (1973).

- Application here: There is no allegation of coercion, duress, deception, or other police misconduct in obtaining consent. The defendant orally consented to the search and the officers were not aware of his memory impairment. Absent coercion or evidence showing that his neurological condition rendered his consent involuntary, the consent stands as valid. The mere fact that the defendant did not remember (and therefore could not appreciate that a particular incriminating item existed) does not by itself make the consent involuntary.

- Competency threshold for consent: Courts examine whether a defendant had the capacity to appreciate and knowingly relinquish his Fourth Amendment rights under the totality of the circumstances. Incapacity (due to intoxication, severe mental defect, or similar) can render consent involuntary, but the question turns on actual coercive circumstances and the person's present ability to consent. A mere inability to recall a past entry—without more—does not establish that a defendant lacked the capacity to consent. See Schneckloth (voluntariness is a totality inquiry).

2) Fifth Amendment — Privilege against self-incrimination and writings discovered in a search

- The Fifth Amendment protects against compelled testimonial communications. A diary entry voluntarily written before governmental compulsion is not the product of compelled testimony at the time of its creation and is generally admissible.

- Distinctions: Cases addressing compelled production of documents (the "act of production" doctrine) such as Fisher v. United States, 425 U.S. 391 (1976), and later refinement in United States v. Hubbell, 530 U.S. 27 (2000), deal with situations where the government compels a person to produce documents or where the act of producing documents has testimonial aspects (e.g., admitting existence, possession, or authenticity). Those doctrines apply when the government forces a person to produce documents (subpoena or compelled production) and the production itself is testimonial. Here, by contrast, the diary was not produced under compulsion by the defendant; it was discovered in a search based on the defendant's voluntary consent. The diary is therefore not barred by the Fifth Amendment on a compelled-production theory.

- Therefore the Fifth Amendment privilege does not prohibit admission of a voluntarily-created diary found in a lawful (voluntary-consent) search.

3) Why the other answers are incorrect

- B (good-faith exception): The Leon good-faith exception (United States v. Leon, 468 U.S. 897 (1984)) concerns police objectively reasonable reliance on a stale or defective judicially issued search warrant; it does not supply an independent basis for admitting evidence found in a consensual search. Because the search here was consensual, the good-faith exception to the exclusionary rule is not the correct doctrinal hook.

- C (incompetent to consent): To invalidate consent, the record must show that the consent was involuntary under the totality of circumstances or that the defendant lacked capacity to consent (due to intoxication, mental illness, coercion, etc.). The mere fact that the defendant could not remember a past diary entry more than 48 hours later, without evidence the police knew of that impairment or otherwise exploited it, does not render consent per se involuntary or incompetent. Thus exclusion on that ground is not supported.

- D (Fifth Amendment): As explained above, the Fifth Amendment protects against compelled testimonial self-incrimination. A previously written diary entry—made voluntarily, before any police compulsion—and found in a lawful search is admissible. The defendant was not compelled to write the diary, nor compelled to produce it in the face of government coercion.

Practical, factual summary

- Consent valid? Yes — voluntariness controls; no coercion alleged; officers unaware of impairment.  
- Diary creation: Voluntary and prior to any government compulsion.  
- Fifth Amendment: Does not bar admission because there was no compelled production or testimonial compulsion linked to the diary's creation or discovery.

Conclusion

The court should admit the diary. Answer A is correct.

Sources and key authorities cited

- Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218 (1973) (consent voluntariness; knowledge of right to refuse is not required).  
- Fisher v. United States, 425 U.S. 391 (1976) (compelled production of documents; act-of-production doctrine).  
- United States v. Hubbell, 530 U.S. 27 (2000) (further act-of-production analysis).  
- United States v. Leon, 468 U.S. 897 (1984) (good-faith exception to exclusionary rule — limited to warrant contexts).
