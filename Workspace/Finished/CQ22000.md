## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Ruth owned a six-acre hillside vineyard parcel in fee simple near a town known for weekend hymn sings. After an unexpected medical bill, Ruth wrote out a deed purporting to convey the parcel in fee simple to "the holder of this instrument" and brought it to Lydia, a real estate investor. Lydia paid Ruth $750 in cash. Ruth signed the deed, handed it to Lydia, and said, "The vineyard field is yours now."

A state statute provides that no document purporting to convey any interest in real property may be recorded unless it is in writing; clearly identifies the grantor, the grantee, and the interest conveyed; and is signed and acknowledged by the grantor.

Lydia later brought an ejectment action against Ruth. Ruth defended on the ground that she still held fee simple title and was entitled to possess the parcel.

Which argument most strongly supports Ruth's defense?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

Does a deed that says the grantee is only "the holder of this instrument" validly convey land when the grantor signed and delivered it?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. The deed could not be recorded.

B. The $750 price was inadequate consideration.

C. Ruth signed only because her desperate finances left her no real choice.

D. The deed failed to identify a grantee.

### 5. Correct answer

D (original key B).

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is the strongest argument because a deed must identify an ascertainable grantee. "The holder of this instrument" tries to make a real-property deed work like bearer paper. That does not identify who takes title. Gold Key: land is not conveyed by a bearer-style deed; the instrument must identify a grantee with reasonable certainty. Silver Key: separate a defect that blocks recording from a defect that blocks the deed from passing title between the parties.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because the statute practically points at recordability. The trap is treating "cannot be recorded" as the same thing as "cannot affect the parties." Silver Key: when the stem gives a recording statute, ask whether the answer proves only a recording problem or a title-passage problem. A deed may be unrecordable yet still matter between grantor and grantee, so recordability alone is not Ruth's best argument.

B sells the fairness instinct: $750 for a vineyard parcel sounds painfully low. The legal breaker is that adequacy of consideration is not the validity test for a deed. A deed can convey title even for nominal or inadequate consideration if the deed requirements are otherwise met.

C turns Ruth's urgent need for money into a moral-pressure answer. Economic pressure alone is not enough. The answer gives no fact that Lydia wrongfully created the pressure or coerced Ruth, so it does not defeat an otherwise valid conveyance.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited outcome is unchanged: the landowner keeps title because the attempted deed does not identify a grantee. The tested rule is a deed-validity rule, not a recording-priority rule. A valid deed must identify the parties, including an ascertainable grantee, and describe the property and interest conveyed. A bearer-style label does not designate a person or entity capable of taking title.

Primary authority: at common law a valid deed must designate an ascertainable grantee — a person or entity in existence and capable of taking title. A deed that names no grantee, or only a "bearer" or "holder," is void and passes no title; this is settled black-letter deed-validity doctrine. No controlling case or Restatement section is confidently identified here, so this authority line is flagged for attorney review to attach a primary citation before the item is treated as fully verified. Practitioner support only (secondary; not a primary-law floor): the California Department of Real Estate Reference Book, chapter 7, and the Colorado Real Estate Manual, chapter 8, both state that the grantee must be designated with reasonable certainty. Sources: https://www.dre.ca.gov/files/pdf/refbook/ref07.pdf and https://spl.cde.state.co.us/artemis/regserials/reg52908r22internet/2009%20manual/reg52908r22200910internet.pdf.

The recording statute in the stem also lists grantee identification, but Ruth's best argument is not merely that the document could not be recorded. The best argument is that the instrument failed as a deed because no grantee was identified. The low price and Ruth's financial need do not defeat the deed-validity analysis.
