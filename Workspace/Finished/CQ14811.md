### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Ruth offers Daniel's apology against Grace & Galilee, the company Daniel worked for. The statement concerned the very cart-handling incident that happened while Daniel was restocking the company's booth, and Daniel still worked the booth when he made the statement. **Gold Key:** an agent or employee statement is a party-opponent statement when it is offered against the employer, concerns a matter within the scope of the relationship, and was made while that relationship existed. **Silver Key:** the call asks for a route that admits the statement to prove negligence, so the winning answer must be a substantive admissibility route, not merely an impeachment use.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because Daniel sounded sorry in the moment, and "state of mind" feels close to an apology. The breaker is the purpose of the offer. Ruth is not using the words to prove Daniel felt regret in the apartment lobby; she is using them to prove past negligent conduct at the booth. The state-of-mind exception does not turn a backward-looking apology into proof of the past act.

**C.** This is the dominant trap. "I'm sorry for what I did" sounds damaging to Daniel, so students may reach for the statement-against-interest label. The breaker is the missing unavailability requirement. **Gold Key:** a statement against interest under FRE 804(b)(3) is an unavailability exception; Daniel is available and has testified at trial. The stronger route is the employer-agent admission route, not the declarant-unavailable route.

**D.** This choice sells a real tension: Daniel's lobby apology pulls against his trial testimony that he handled the cart carefully. The breaker is the use in the call. **Silver Key:** "admissible to prove negligence" means substantive proof, not just impeachment. A prior inconsistent statement is substantive non-hearsay under FRE 801(d)(1)(A) only when it was made under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition. Daniel's casual lobby statement was not.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row. The original item tested why an employee's apologetic statement after a work-related accident is admissible against the employer to prove negligence. The transformed item keeps that same rule: an employee made a statement about the work incident while the employment relationship still existed, and the injured plaintiff offers it against the employer.

Federal Rule of Evidence 801(d)(2)(D) supplies the governing answer. A statement is not hearsay when it is offered against an opposing party and was made by the party's agent or employee on a matter within the scope of that relationship while the relationship existed. Daniel's cart-handling statement concerned his work for Grace & Galilee and was made while he still worked for the company, so Ruth may use it substantively against Grace & Galilee.

The distractors remain wrong under current federal evidence rules. FRE 801(d)(1)(A) allows a prior inconsistent statement as substantive non-hearsay only when the declarant testifies and the earlier inconsistent statement was made under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition. FRE 804(b)(3) is an exception for statements against interest only when the declarant is unavailable. FRE 803(3) covers then-existing mental, emotional, or physical condition, but not memory or belief offered to prove the remembered or believed past fact. Daniel testified at trial, and the apology was offered to prove past negligence, not present regret.
