## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** Peter's strongest acquittal argument is causation. Manslaughter requires the prosecution to prove that the defendant's criminally negligent or unlawful conduct caused the death. If the crash would have happened even if Peter had been sober, then his intoxicated driving was not a but-for cause of Lydia's death.

**Gold Key:** Manslaughter still needs causation. When the fact pattern says the fatal result would have occurred even without the risky act, attack cause in fact before arguing excuses.

**Silver Key:** In a "best chance for acquittal" call, pick the answer that attacks an element the prosecution must prove. Here, causation is the clean element attack; victim negligence and intoxication-as-excuse are weaker frames.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A** sells the familiar idea that drunkenness wipes out mens rea. That is attractive because the stem makes Peter's intoxication vivid and the charge is manslaughter. The breaker is the Gold Key: voluntary intoxication does not defeat a criminal negligence or recklessness-based manslaughter charge. It is not Peter's best acquittal argument.

**B** tempts the student to import civil negligence language into a criminal homicide problem. Lydia's driving may matter if it breaks causation, but "contributory negligence" by itself is a civil defense label, not Peter's criminal-law acquittal theory. The better version of that idea is the causation answer: the crash would have happened even if Peter had been sober.

**C** sounds technical because it talks about mens rea and misdemeanor manslaughter. The problem is that it fights only one possible theory and leaves the core criminal-negligence manslaughter path alive. Peter's best argument is not that drunk driving can never support manslaughter; it is that his intoxicated driving did not cause this death.

## 9. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Authority

The tested rule is unchanged: criminal homicide liability requires actual causation. Model Penal Code § 2.03(1)(a) treats conduct as a cause of a result only when the result would not have occurred without that conduct. The Supreme Court's but-for causation discussion in *Burrage v. United States*, 571 U.S. 204 (2014), is consistent with that baseline: ordinary result-causation language requires proof that the harm would not have happened in the absence of the defendant's conduct when no independently sufficient cause rule applies. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/12-7515

The intoxication trap is also unchanged. Model Penal Code § 2.08 limits voluntary-intoxication defenses; self-induced intoxication does not ordinarily excuse recklessness-based liability, and official jury-instruction commentary likewise treats voluntary intoxication as unavailable for crimes based on general mens rea such as criminal negligence. Sources: https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/6141-tanaka-criminal-law-casebook/resources/12.3.1-model-penal-code-mpc-208-intoxication/ and https://okcca.net/ouji-cr/8-36/

The credited outcome remains the same. If Peter can show the collision and death would have occurred even had he not been intoxicated, the prosecution lacks cause in fact for manslaughter on the intoxicated-driving theory. The supplied official key is consistent with the research; no key disagreement or doctrinal-current issue was found.
