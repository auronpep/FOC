Acting independently and without any communication, Mary and Paul each released a heavy overhead counterweight during a private Bible-pageant rehearsal. One counterweight crushed Silas's chest, and the other crushed his skull, landing at virtually the same moment. Medical testimony established that either blow alone would have killed Silas instantly, and that the two blows landed within a fraction of a second of each other. There is no basis to determine which blow caused death first. Mary and Paul were prosecuted separately. Is Mary a cause of Silas's death?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
When two independent acts hit the victim at essentially the same time, and either act alone would have killed instantly, is one actor a cause of death?

D. No, because under the but-for test, Silas would have died from Paul's blow even without Mary's act, so Mary's act was not a cause of death.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. Mary does not escape causation just because Paul's act would also have killed Silas. Gold Key: when two independent acts are each sufficient to cause death and operate at the same time, the ordinary but-for test breaks down; each independently fatal act can be a substantial factor in bringing about death. Silver Key: do not ask which force "won" the race by a fraction of a second. Ask whether Mary's own force was independently lethal and operative in the death event. It was, so Mary is a cause of Silas's death.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because it treats forensic uncertainty as a tie that cancels causation for both actors. The breaker is that the prosecution does not need to identify the first lethal blow when each blow was independently sufficient and operating in the same death event.

C is attractive because temporal priority feels like a clean way to choose a cause. The breaker is that the substantial-factor rule does not require the jury to find that Mary's blow landed first; simultaneity plus independent sufficiency is enough.

D is the dominant trap because it applies the ordinary but-for test too mechanically. The breaker is the Gold Key: strict but-for analysis fails in simultaneous independently sufficient cause cases because it would wrongly erase both lethal actors from causation.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law and Procedure, Homicide, causation, and the verified outline code is `74020100`. The credited outcome is unchanged: Mary is a cause of death because her act was independently sufficient to kill and was a substantial factor in bringing about the death.

The governing black-letter rule is that criminal causation may recognize more than one cause of death. CALCRIM No. 620 states that an act causes death if it is a substantial factor in causing the death, and that it need not be the only factor. See CALCRIM No. 620, Causation: Special Issues, Judicial Council of California Criminal Jury Instructions (2026), https://www.justia.com/criminal/docs/calcrim/500/620/. In *People v. Pike*, 197 Cal. App. 3d 732, 746-747 (1988), the court treated concurrent causes as legally sufficient where multiple persons' conduct contributes concurrently as proximate causes of death, and discussed the substantial-factor requirement. See https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/197/732.html. The multiple-sufficient-cause principle is also reflected in Restatement (Second) of Torts section 432(2), which recognizes substantial-factor causation where two forces are actively operating and each is independently sufficient to bring about the harm; that Restatement formulation supplies the classic overdetermination rule but is used here only as lawyer-confirmation support. Cornell Wex likewise summarizes the criminal-law use of the substantial-factor test for multiple causes. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/substantial_factor_test.

Research does not contradict the supplied key. It supports the source explanation's result: a strict but-for answer would produce the incoherent result that neither independently lethal actor caused the death.
