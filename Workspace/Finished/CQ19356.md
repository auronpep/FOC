## 1. Final question — stem + call

A federal grand jury is investigating a counterfeit-currency operation. Under a federal immunity statute, the government grants Esther use and derivative use immunity and orders her to testify about the operation's finances. Esther refuses, arguing that use immunity is not enough because prosecutors could still charge her for the underlying counterfeiting using evidence they gather on their own, apart from anything she says. The government asks the court to compel her testimony. Must Esther testify?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A grand jury witness is granted use and derivative-use immunity and ordered to testify; she refuses because she could still be prosecuted for the underlying crime on independent evidence. Must she testify?

## 3. Final answer choices (A–D, post-shuffle)

**A.** Yes, because use and derivative use immunity is constitutionally coextensive with the Fifth Amendment privilege and suffices to compel testimony under *Kastigar v. United States*.

**B.** No, because the Fifth Amendment requires transactional immunity — immunity from prosecution for the underlying counterfeiting — before a witness may be compelled to testify.

**C.** Yes, because once a court grants immunity and orders her to testify, a witness loses all Fifth Amendment protection and may be prosecuted based on her own testimony.

**D.** No, because prosecutors could still use independently gathered evidence to charge Esther with the counterfeiting, so her privilege is not fully protected.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (A)

Yes — use and derivative-use immunity is constitutionally coextensive with the Fifth Amendment privilege and suffices to compel testimony. The government may not use Esther's compelled testimony or anything derived from it against her; that immunity displaces the privilege. **Gold Key:** transactional immunity is broader but is not constitutionally required — coextensiveness is the standard. If prosecutors later charge her, they bear the burden of proving every piece of evidence came from sources independent of her testimony.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B (dominant trap).** Transactional immunity is the broadest, most familiar form, so it is easy to assume the Constitution demands that maximum before anyone can be forced to talk. **Gold Key:** *Kastigar* holds use and derivative-use immunity is coextensive with the privilege and is enough; transactional immunity is broader but not constitutionally required.

**C.** Reaching the right bottom line — yes, she must testify — makes this feel safe. The reason self-destructs: immunity does not strip all Fifth Amendment protection; it bars the government from using her compelled testimony or its fruits, so "prosecuted based on her testimony" contradicts the very grant. **Silver Key:** a "yes" choice that says she loses *all* protection reaches the right outcome by destroying the doctrine.

**D.** Esther's worry is real — prosecutors might still build a case from independent evidence — and "not fully protected" sounds like the privilege fails. The privilege requires only coextensiveness, not immunity from all prosecution; residual prosecution on truly independent evidence is permitted, with the government bearing the burden of proving independence.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

**Tested rule (unchanged).** Use and derivative-use immunity is constitutionally **coextensive** with the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination; it is therefore sufficient to compel testimony over a Fifth Amendment objection. **Transactional immunity** (barring all prosecution for the underlying offense) is broader but **not** constitutionally required. The residual risk that the government may prosecute on independent evidence does not render the privilege inadequately protected; at any later prosecution the government bears the burden of proving its evidence derived from sources wholly independent of the compelled testimony.
