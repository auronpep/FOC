### 7. Full right-answer explanation
A is correct. Martha intentionally helped Lydia commit the target crime of vandalizing Naomi's booth, and the jurisdiction expressly uses the natural and probable consequences doctrine. A confrontation by the booth owner was a foreseeable result of that property-damage scheme, and a punch during that confrontation was a foreseeable escalation rather than a random side mission. Gold Key: under the natural-and-probable-consequences doctrine, the accomplice need not specifically intend the later offense if it was a reasonably foreseeable result of the target crime aided. Silver Key: once the stem tells you the doctrine applies, do not ask whether Martha pre-agreed to the punch; ask whether the punch grew naturally out of the aided vandalism.

B is attractive because students may connect "natural and probable consequences" with conspiracy-scope doctrines like Pinkerton and then over-restrict the label. The breaker is that the stem asks accomplice liability in a jurisdiction applying the doctrine, and the doctrine can operate as a complicity rule independent of a charged conspiracy.

C sells the dominant trap: Martha did not agree to assault Naomi and did not know Lydia would punch her. The breaker is the Gold Key: in a jurisdiction using the natural-and-probable-consequences doctrine, those missing mental-state facts do not end the analysis if the later assault was a reasonably foreseeable consequence of the target vandalism Martha aided.

D is attractive because it keeps the "yes" result and remembers derivative liability, but it turns foreseeability into an unlimited sweep. The breaker is that natural-and-probable-consequences liability reaches foreseeable crimes connected to the aided enterprise, not every unrelated offense the principal commits that day.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule is accomplice liability for a nontarget offense under the natural and probable consequences doctrine. The source row listed `71040100` for Solicitation, but the actual tested rule fits the verified outline code `72050400` (General Principles > Parties to Crime > Scope of liability). I treated the source code as an outline-source defect and used the deepest matching listed code.

Authority floor: California Penal Code section 31 treats aiders and abettors as principals. In *People v. Smith*, 60 Cal. 4th 603 (2014), the California Supreme Court explained that an aider and abettor of a target crime is guilty of another crime committed by a principal if it was a natural and probable, meaning reasonably foreseeable, consequence of the target crime. *People v. Chiu*, 59 Cal. 4th 155 (2014), quoting *People v. Prettyman*, 14 Cal. 4th 248 (1996), describes the doctrine as derivative liability for an offense that is a natural and probable consequence of the target crime the defendant encouraged or facilitated. The Third Circuit's 2024 model-instruction commentary notes that courts are split on the doctrine and that the Third Circuit has not adopted it, but the source stem expressly says the jurisdiction applies it.
