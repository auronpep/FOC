### 1. Final Question

A church building committee commissioned a local artist to design and install custom mosaic tile work for the church's new baptistry. The committee chair, Lydia, had particular involvement in the project because the baptistry was being built in memory of her mother. On March 1, the committee and the artist entered into a written contract under which the artist would complete and install the tile work by August 1 for $8,000. The contract contained the following clause: "It is expressly understood that the personal satisfaction of the committee chair is a condition precedent to the committee's obligation to make payment hereunder. Upon completion of the tile work, it shall be made available for inspection by said chair. If said chair is unsatisfied with the work, she shall notify the artist immediately, and the contract shall be canceled without liability of any party."

The artist completed the mosaic on July 20. The tile work depicted a lamb standing beside still water, rendered in blue and gold tones. Lydia inspected the completed work on July 22. She said, "The blue is too dark—it looks almost purple in this light. I just don't like it. The deal is off." Lydia genuinely believed the color was too dark and was personally dissatisfied with the mosaic. It was also true that the church had recently received a large anonymous donation specifically earmarked for a different building project, and some committee members had privately suggested redirecting funds away from the baptistry. The artist demanded payment, but the church committee refused.

**C.** No, because Lydia was genuinely dissatisfied with the color of the tile work.

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** When a contract expressly conditions payment on the personal satisfaction of a designated party, the standard for satisfaction is subjective — the party need only be honestly dissatisfied in good faith. Here, the contract made Lydia's personal satisfaction a condition precedent to the committee's payment obligation. Lydia inspected the mosaic, found the blue too dark, and was genuinely dissatisfied. Because her dissatisfaction was honest and in good faith, the condition precedent was not met, and the committee's payment obligation was discharged.

The fact that some committee members privately wanted to redirect funds (choice A) is irrelevant — the question is whether Lydia's stated reason was genuine, and it was. The key doctrinal unlock here is:

> **Gold Key:** An express "personal satisfaction" clause in a services contract creates a subjective standard — the approving party's honest good-faith dissatisfaction is sufficient, even if a reasonable person would have been satisfied. The clause is not wholly unlimited (bad faith would breach the implied covenant of good faith), but the burden is on the performing party to prove bad faith, and a genuinely held aesthetic preference meets the subjective standard.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. No, because the committee members privately wanted to redirect baptistry funds to another project.**

This choice tempts the student who spots the fund-redirection fact and reasons that the committee had an ulterior financial motive for not paying. That cognitive move — "they had a bad reason, so they must be in breach" — is attractive because it mirrors real-world suspicion of pretext. But the call asks whether the committee is *obligated* to pay, and the legal question turns on whether the condition precedent (Lydia's satisfaction) was met. Lydia's genuine dissatisfaction with the color is established. Even if the committee had mixed motives, the subjective standard means that an honest aesthetic objection is enough. The fund-redirection fact is a red herring — it doesn't negate Lydia's genuine dissatisfaction. This choice is **not responsive** to the legal issue: whether the satisfaction clause was triggered.

> **Silver Key:** When a fact pattern includes a potentially suspicious ulterior motive alongside a genuine stated reason, lock onto the stated reason first. If the stated reason satisfies the legal standard (here, subjective good-faith dissatisfaction), the ulterior motive is legally irrelevant.

**B. Yes, because a reasonable person would have found the tile work to be satisfactory.**

This is the **dominant trap**. Most contracts for services imply a promise to perform in a manner satisfactory to a reasonable person — the objective default. The student who applies that default here will pick B, because the mosaic sounds objectively fine (a lamb beside still water in blue and gold is not unreasonable). But the contract here did not use the default. It expressly conditioned payment on the *personal satisfaction* of a named individual. That express language shifts the standard from objective to subjective. The student must see that the clause overrides the default and that Lydia's honest dislike of the color — however idiosyncratic — satisfies the subjective standard. The trap is: applying the general rule (objective standard) when the facts trigger the exception (express personal satisfaction clause).

**D. Yes, because the contract gave Lydia the absolute right to reject the finished work.**

This choice tempts the student who reads the satisfaction clause and reasons that "personal satisfaction" means the approving party can reject for any reason at all — an unlimited veto. That reading overclaims. Even when a contract uses subjective-satisfaction language, the law reads in a good-faith limitation: the party whose satisfaction is required must act honestly, not capriciously or in bad faith. The clause does not create an "absolute right" to reject. The word "absolute" in this choice is the tell — it pushes the doctrine too far. The correct reading is: subjective satisfaction, exercised in good faith. Because Lydia's dissatisfaction was genuine, the outcome (no payment) is correct, but the *reason* stated in this choice (absolute right) misstates the law.

### 9. Black-Letter Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** Where a contract expressly conditions performance (here, payment) on the personal satisfaction of a designated party, the standard is subjective — the party need only be honestly dissatisfied in good faith. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 228 (comment: where satisfaction is conditioned on the will of a party, the standard is subjective good faith, not objective reasonableness); *Mattei v. Hopper*, 51 Cal. 2d 119 (1958) (leading case: express personal satisfaction clause in a land-sale contract enforced under subjective good-faith standard); *Int'l Fin. Corp. v. Carrera Holdings Inc.*, 2016 NY Slip Op. 31341(U) (distinguishing subjective standard for "fancy, taste, sensibility, or judgment" from objective standard for "operative fitness, utility or marketability").

**Application:** The contract here expressly made Lydia's personal satisfaction a condition precedent to payment. Lydia inspected the work, found the blue too dark, and was genuinely dissatisfied. Her dissatisfaction was honest — the stem states she "genuinely believed the color was too dark." Under the subjective standard, that is sufficient. The committee's payment obligation is discharged.

**Good-faith limitation:** The subjective standard is not unlimited. If Lydia had *liked* the tile work but rejected it solely because she wanted to redirect funds (bad faith), the committee would be in breach. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 205 (implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing). But the stem establishes genuine dissatisfaction, so good faith is not at issue here.

**Confirmed:** The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original. The original tested whether a mayor's genuine dissatisfaction with a statue discharged the town's payment obligation under a personal satisfaction clause. This variation tests the same rule with the same outcome: genuine subjective dissatisfaction in good faith discharges the obligation.
