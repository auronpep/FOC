### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** False pretenses requires that the defendant obtain title to the property by a knowingly false representation of a present material fact made with intent to defraud. Lydia represented that she was authorized to charge the church women's ministry credit card — a present fact about her authorization status — which was false. The booth's explicit policy stated that title passes upon completion of the payment transaction. Priscilla, intending to complete a sale under that policy, processed the charge as final and transferred both possession and title to the boxed study library. Because title passed as a direct result of Lydia's fraudulent misrepresentation of present fact, all elements of false pretenses are satisfied.

**Gold Key:** The title-versus-possession line is dispositive. When the victim's own policy and intent (here, the posted booth rule) is to transfer full ownership upon "completed sale," fraud that induces that transfer yields false pretenses, not larceny by trick. Larceny by trick applies only when the victim intends to transfer possession only (e.g., a loan or conditional delivery before payment clears).

Priscilla and the vendor may have civil remedies and the card issuer reversed the charge, but the criminal classification turns on what interest the victim intended to (and did) pass at the moment of the fraud-induced "completed sale."

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because the transaction occurred in a "ministry-related" setting and Lydia invoked a church card, which can feel like a position of trust. The breaker is the entrustment requirement. Embezzlement requires that the defendant initially receive the property through a lawful entrustment or fiduciary/employment relationship. Lydia obtained the library by fraud from the first moment; fraud vitiates any appearance of lawful entrustment. The vendor intended an outright sale and title transfer, not to place the goods in Lydia's custody for a limited purpose.

**B.** This is the dominant trap. Students see clear fraud used to obtain the physical set and immediately select "larceny by trick." The breaker is the title-pass fact the stem supplies. Larceny by trick requires that the victim, induced by fraud, transfers only possession (not title) to the defendant. Here the booth's standard policy and Priscilla's actions under it were to treat the transaction as a completed sale that transferred title. When the victim intends to and does transfer title (even if induced by fraud), the crime is false pretenses.

**D.** This choice sells the intuitive sense that Lydia "took something that wasn't hers." The breaker is the voluntary (fraud-induced) transfer of title by the owner. Generic larceny requires a trespassory taking — the property is taken from the victim's possession without consent. Here the victim willingly (though mistakenly due to the false representation) transferred title as part of what it believed was a completed sale. When property is obtained through fraudulent inducement of an ownership transfer, the specific crime is false pretenses (title passed) or larceny by trick (possession only passed). "Larceny" is imprecise and incorrect on these facts.

### 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Right-Answer Legal Reasoning

The source row identifies Q21572 (internal_id CR-070) as a Criminal Law item testing the distinction between false pretenses and larceny by trick in the "Other Crimes" subtopic. The credited answer is C (False pretenses) because the store's policy caused title to pass upon completion of the payment transaction, and the defendant's knowing false representation of present fact (authorization to use the credit card) induced that title transfer.

Research confirms the black-letter distinction is unchanged and the credited outcome is correct:

- Larceny by trick applies when fraud induces the victim to transfer **possession only** (the victim does not intend to pass title; classic examples include borrowing a car by false pretenses with intent to keep it, or goods delivered on credit before payment).
- False pretenses applies when fraud induces the victim to transfer **title** (the victim intends to pass ownership as part of the transaction). The key is the victim's intent at the moment of delivery, often shown by the parties' understanding or the seller's regular course of dealing / posted policy.

Multiple bar-prep and treatise sources use exactly this title-versus-possession test as the dispositive question on MBE-style facts.
