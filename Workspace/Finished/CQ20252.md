### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** A person who repeats or republishes a defamatory statement to a third party is liable as a publisher of that statement, regardless of whether the person originated it. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 578.

Timothy took a false, verifiable factual accusation — that Hannah embezzled choir funds — and communicated it to 12,000 podcast listeners. That is publication. His added endorsement ("sounds credible to me") does not convert the underlying factual accusation into protected opinion; it reinforces it. The fact that someone else wrote the original post is irrelevant. Each person who republishes defamatory matter to a third party is independently liable as a publisher. Timothy cannot avoid liability by pointing to the original poster.

---

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A (new D) — "Yes, because only the original speaker can publish a defamatory statement."**

*Why attractive:* Students often intuit that liability attaches to the "source" — the person who first said the defamatory thing. It feels unfair to hold the repeater responsible. This distractor sells that intuition as law.

*Breaker:* The law is the opposite. **Gold Key:** Each person who repeats or republishes defamatory matter to a third party is independently liable as a publisher. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 578. Timothy's republication to 12,000 listeners is a new, separate publication. The "only original speaker" rule does not exist.

**Choice C (new A) — "Yes, because adding 'sounds credible to me' made his statement an expression of opinion."**

*Why attractive:* Students know that pure opinion is protected under the First Amendment and not actionable as defamation. Timothy's hedging language ("sounds credible") sounds like he's sharing an impression, not asserting a fact.

*Breaker:* Timothy didn't just offer a vague impression. He read a specific, verifiable factual accusation — embezzlement of choir funds — aloud to his audience and then endorsed its credibility. A reasonable listener would understand the statement as an assertion of fact, not mere opinion. The hedging language does not convert a defamatory factual claim into protected speech.

**Choice D (new C) — "No, but only because the original poster later deleted the claim."**

*Why attractive:* The original poster's deletion feels like it should matter — perhaps it "undoes" the defamation or shifts all blame to the originator. Students look for the fact that makes the answer "different" and latch onto the deletion.

*Breaker:* Publication is complete the moment the defamatory statement is communicated to a third party. Timothy's podcast episode reached 12,000 listeners — publication occurred at that instant. What the original poster did afterward (deleting the post) has no legal effect on Timothy's independent act of republication. The "only because" framing makes this choice wrong: the deletion is irrelevant, and liability does not depend on it.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** Under the common law of defamation, any person who repeats or republishes a defamatory statement to a third party is liable as a publisher of that statement, even if the statement was originally made by someone else. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 578 ("Republication by Third Person") provides that each person who participates in the publication of defamatory matter is subject to liability to the same extent as the original publisher.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 578; see also *Durand v. Higgins*, 2024 ABKB 108 (Alberta Court of King's Bench holding individual liable for reposting second-hand allegations on social media); Minc Law, "Am I Liable for Republishing Defamatory Content?" ("The general rule is that a person who repeats or republishes a defamatory statement is subject to the same liability as if he/she had originally published the statement."); Grigoras Law, "Can I be liable for repeating or sharing someone else's defamatory statement?" ("Every person who participates in publishing or republishing a defamatory remark can be held liable if there is no valid defence.").

**Application:** Timothy communicated a false, defamatory factual accusation (embezzlement) to 12,000 podcast listeners. This satisfies the publication element. His added endorsement ("sounds credible to me") does not change the character of the underlying factual claim. The original poster's subsequent deletion does not undo Timothy's independent publication. Timothy is liable as a republisher.

**Invariant layer confirmed:** The tested rule (republication liability), the legal issue (whether a republisher can avoid liability by pointing to the original speaker), and the credited outcome (no, the republisher is liable) are all unchanged from the original.
