### 9. Full Black-Letter Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row. At common law, murder is an unlawful killing with malice aforethought, and implied malice includes depraved-heart killings caused by extreme, life-endangering recklessness. Cornell Wex's murder and malice-aforethought entries summarize that common-law murder includes depraved-heart murder, and 18 U.S.C. section 1111 likewise defines murder as the unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought. In Commonwealth v. Malone, 354 Pa. 180, 47 A.2d 445 (1946), the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held that a defendant may be guilty of murder without a specific intent to kill when he intentionally commits a grossly reckless gun act in callous disregard of its likely harmful effects and death results. That is the right analog here. Peter intentionally sent a live round just past Stephen's shoulder, despite having never fired the revolver before, and the shot killed Stephen after ricocheting. That supports murder on a depraved-heart theory.

Attempted murder is unavailable because attempt to cause a particular result requires purpose to cause that result. Model Penal Code section 5.01(1)(b) requires purpose of causing the prohibited result, and Cornell Wex likewise explains that common-law attempt generally requires specific intent to complete the underlying crime. Peter meant to miss Stephen, so the attempt charge fails. Voluntary manslaughter also fails because it requires an intentional killing in heat of passion or on adequate provocation; the stem supplies neither. And the ordinance offense fails on its own text because the quoted misdemeanor applies only to a person who discharges a firearm knowing he is within the municipal limits, while the stem says both men believed they were outside.
