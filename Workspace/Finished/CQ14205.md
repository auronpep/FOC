## 1. Final question — revised stem and call

Two years ago, Lydia, the treasurer of Grace Fellowship Church, filed a jurisdictionally valid diversity lawsuit in State A federal court against Barnabas, a contractor, to recover damages for breach of a construction contract. The contract called for Barnabas to build a new fellowship hall and commercial kitchen for the church's youth ministry programs, weekly Bible studies, and community outreach dinners. Barnabas's answer raised three affirmative defenses: (i) that the statute of limitations barred the suit in State A; (ii) that the contract was invalid because the church board had not followed its own bylaws when authorizing the project; and (iii) that the church would not have been entitled to damages under the contract in any event. That lawsuit went to a bench trial and resulted in judgment for Barnabas. Afterward, Lydia's attorney wanted to determine what, if any, preclusionary effect the judgment would have. After asking his associate to pull the judgment from the files, the attorney was surprised to find that the only words written on the judgment were "judgment for Barnabas." Does this judgment constitute a final judgment for Barnabas?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**D is the best answer.** The judgment is not final. **Gold Key:** After a bench trial, FRCP 52(a)(1) requires the court to "find the facts specially and state its conclusions of law separately." A bare "judgment for Barnabas" supplies neither. **Silver Key:** Lock the call on "final judgment" and trace the bench-trial signal; the findings requirement is what the rule makes mandatory for finality in non-jury cases.

The facts establish a bench trial (no jury). The document contains only the bare outcome phrase. That is exactly the defect Rule 52 targets. Without the special findings and separate conclusions, the judgment does not meet the formal requirements that make it final for preclusion or appeal purposes.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Yes, because this is a proper final judgment, regardless of whether the case was tried at a bench or jury trial.**  
This is the dominant trap. It sells the attractive "a judgment is a judgment" intuition and erases the bench/jury distinction. **Gold Key:** FRCP 52(a)(1) draws the distinction deliberately: only non-jury trials require special findings and separate conclusions of law. The rule would be pointless if every bare outcome phrase counted as final.

**B. No, because it does not state that an actual judgment was entered.**  
This sells a paperwork/notation story that feels concrete and procedural. The breaker is that the rule defect is the missing findings and conclusions, not the absence of an "entered" stamp. A notation of entry would not cure the absence of the required 52(a) support.

**C. Yes, because this is a proper final judgment issued by the court following a bench trial.**  
This is the half-truth trap that concedes the bench-trial setting but then overclaims that the bare phrase is sufficient. **Gold Key:** The bench-trial setting is precisely why the bare phrase fails. Rule 52(a)(1) exists to force the judge to create a reviewable record of facts found and law applied; omitting that record defeats finality.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested black-letter rule (unchanged):** In an action tried on the facts without a jury, the court must find the facts specially and state its conclusions of law separately. A document that merely states "judgment for the defendant" after a bench trial does not constitute a final judgment because it lacks the findings and conclusions required by the rule.

**Governing authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):** Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 52(a)(1): "In an action tried on the facts without a jury or with an advisory jury, the court must find the facts specially and state its conclusions of law separately. The findings and conclusions may be stated on the record after the close of the evidence or may appear in an opinion or a memorandum of decision filed by the court. Judgment must be entered under Rule 58."

The source row's official explanation and additional_info match this rule exactly: the judge cannot simply state "judgment for the electrician"; specific findings of fact and conclusions of law are mandatory for a final judgment following a non-jury trial. FRCP 52(c) judgment on partial findings also requires the same support. The credited outcome is unchanged: the bare judgment is not final. Research confirms no doctrinal shift; the 2007 restyling and later technical amendments preserved the core "must find... specially and state... separately" command.

No key disagreement. The official key (A, now post-shuffle D) is correct under current FRCP 52(a)(1).
