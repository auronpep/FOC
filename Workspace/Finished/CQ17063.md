### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**D is correct.** An anonymous tip, standing alone, is generally insufficient to provide reasonable suspicion for a *Terry* stop because there is no way to assess the tipster's reliability. *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266 (2000). But when police corroborate **predictive details** — future movements or behavior the tipster could only know through **inside knowledge** of criminal activity — the tip gains the reliability needed to support a brief investigative stop. *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. 325 (1990). Here, the unnamed source predicted that Lydia would (1) leave her shop at noon, (2) walk to the river path, and (3) deliver a bag of silver coins to a man under the cypress tree. Each of these future movements was verified by Barnabas before the magistrates intercepted Lydia. That pattern of corroborated predictive detail shows the tipster had inside knowledge of Lydia's planned conduct, which is sufficient for reasonable suspicion.

**Silver Key:** When the call asks whether an anonymous tip supports a Terry stop, don't ask "was the tip anonymous?" — ask "did the tip predict future behavior that police verified?" If yes → reasonable suspicion. If the tip only described what someone looks like or where they are right now → not enough.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original B — DOMINANT TRAP):** This choice states a broad, memorable-sounding rule: anonymous tips can *never* justify a stop. The attraction is that it echoes a real case — *Florida v. J.L.* held that an anonymous tip describing only a person's appearance and location was insufficient. But *J.L.*'s rule is narrower than this choice claims. The Supreme Court has explicitly held that an anonymous tip **can** support a stop when it contains predictive details that police corroborate, showing the tipster had inside knowledge. *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. 325 (1990). The word "never" is the tell — this choice overstates the rule by ignoring the predictive-details exception.

**B (original D):** This choice correctly identifies that authorities verified something about Lydia — her identity and her route. The attraction is that verification did occur, and the student may remember that "corroboration" matters. But this choice misidentifies *what* was corroborated and *why* it matters. The tip's reliability comes not from confirming who Lydia is or what path she took, but from the fact that the source **predicted future behavior** that the tipster could only have known about through inside knowledge of the planned criminal activity. Verifying a name or a route is not the same as verifying predictive detail.

**C (original C):** This choice says the authorities needed **probable cause** before stopping Lydia. The attraction is that many students conflate the Fourth Amendment standards for different police actions: arrests require probable cause, but brief investigative stops (*Terry* stops) require only **reasonable suspicion** — a lower standard. The call asks specifically about reasonable suspicion for a stop, not probable cause for an arrest. This choice raises the bar above what the law requires.

### 9. Black-Letter Verification

**Authority.** The Fourth Amendment permits brief investigative stops when an officer has reasonable suspicion of criminal activity. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). An anonymous tip, without more, generally lacks the indicia of reliability needed for reasonable suspicion because there is no way to assess the tipster's credibility. *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266 (2000). However, when an anonymous tip contains **predictive information** — future movements or behavior that the tipster could only know through inside knowledge — and police corroborate those predictions, the tip gains sufficient reliability to support a *Terry* stop. *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. 325 (1990). The corroboration of predictive detail is the key that unlocks the tip's reliability.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266 (2000), held that an anonymous tip describing only a person's appearance and present location is insufficient for a *Terry* stop. The word "never" in this choice is the tell — it overclaims.
