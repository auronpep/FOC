### 7. Full right-answer explanation
A is correct. The prosecution has Daniel's murderous intent, but conspiracy requires more than one person's private plan. Gold Key: under the common-law bilateral conspiracy rule, the agreement must be real on both sides; a supposed helper who only pretends and does not intend the target crime does not supply the second guilty mind. Timothy's belief matters because it shows he did not share Daniel's intent to murder Esther. Silver Key: when the call asks for the defendant's best conspiracy defense, look for the answer that breaks the agreement itself before chasing later harm, likelihood, or merger.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
C is attractive because Esther's survival feels like a clean reason to reject a murder-related charge. The breaker is that conspiracy is complete when the required criminal agreement is formed; success of the target crime is not required.

D is attractive because the buzzer's low danger makes Daniel's plan look practically impossible. The breaker is that low likelihood attacks the means, not the agreement; if a real murder agreement existed, factual impossibility or poor tools would not defeat conspiracy.

B is attractive because it borrows real inchoate-crime vocabulary and tries to turn Daniel's later act into a merger defense. The breaker is that conspiracy does not merge into the substantive offense, and Gold Key: the missing piece here is not proximity or merger but the absence of a second person who truly agreed to the murder.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law and Procedure, Inchoate Crimes, Conspiracy, and the verified outline code is `71040200`. Under the common-law bilateral conspiracy rule tested by this item, conspiracy requires an agreement between two or more persons plus the intent to accomplish the unlawful objective. Cornell's Wex summary states the baseline as an agreement between two or more people to commit an illegal act, with intent to achieve the agreement's goal: https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/conspiracy. The bilateral version also requires an actual meeting of guilty minds; *United States v. Valigura* explains that without an actual agreement or meeting of the minds, there is no conspiracy under the bilateral theory: https://www.armfor.uscourts.gov/opinions/2000Term/99-5005.htm. The Model Penal Code's unilateral approach differs, but the source item's credited answer and the BarMatrix outline frame this as the traditional conspiracy rule. The wrong merger answer is also false: *Iannelli v. United States*, 420 U.S. 770 (1975), treats conspiracy and the substantive offense as discrete crimes and discusses non-merger as the general rule: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep420/usrep420770/usrep420770.pdf. Those authorities confirm that the credited outcome is unchanged: Daniel's strongest defense is that Timothy did not actually intend Esther's death, so there was no true conspiracy agreement.
