## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Consequential damages for lost profits are recoverable only if the special circumstances giving rise to those profits were reasonably foreseeable to the defendant **at the time the contract was formed**. Under the rule of Hadley v. Baxendale, damages for breach of contract are limited to those that (1) arise naturally from the breach in the ordinary course of things, or (2) were within the contemplation of both parties at the time of contracting as a probable result of breach. Here, Peter did not tell Lydia about the conference until after she had already breached. Lydia therefore had no reason to know, at the time of contracting, that her failure to deliver display cases would cause Peter to lose $30,000 in conference profits. Those profits are too remote.

**Gold Key:** Consequential damages require the defendant to have reason to know of the special circumstances **at the time of contracting**—not at breach, not at trial. The timing gate is absolute.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Rule:** Consequential (special) damages for breach of contract are recoverable only if they were reasonably foreseeable to the defendant at the time the contract was formed. *Hadley v. Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341. The rule has two prongs: (1) damages arising naturally from the breach in the ordinary course of things (general damages), and (2) damages that were within the reasonable contemplation of both parties at the time of contracting as a probable result of breach (special/consequential damages). The second prong requires the plaintiff to have communicated the special circumstances to the defendant before or at the time of contracting.

**Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 351** codifies the foreseeability test: consequential damages are not recoverable unless the defendant had reason to foresee them as a probable result of breach at the time of contracting.

**Application:** Peter's $30,000 in expected conference profits are special (consequential) damages—they do not arise naturally from a breach of a display-case contract. They arise from Peter's separate conference arrangement, which Lydia had no knowledge of at the time of contracting. Therefore, the profits fail the foreseeability test and are not recoverable.

**Authority:** *Hadley v. Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341; Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 351.

**Verification:** The tested rule (foreseeability at time of contracting), the legal issue (consequential lost profits), and the credited outcome (no recovery) are all unchanged from the original question. The official key is confirmed correct.
