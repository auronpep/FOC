## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question

Peter was validly arrested for arson after a boathouse fire at a lake marina. At the county interview room, he was given Miranda warnings. When the detective asked, "Do you understand your Miranda rights, and are you willing to give up those rights and talk to us?" Peter replied, "Yes." When asked, "Did you start the fire?" he replied, "No." When asked, "Where were you when the fire began?" he replied, "Maybe I should talk to a lawyer." The detective asked, "Why would you want to talk with a lawyer?" and Peter replied, "Because I started the fire by accident, and I think I need a lawyer to help me." The detective stopped questioning.

Later, Peter was formally charged with arson. He moves to suppress his statement "I started the fire by accident" on the ground that it was elicited in violation of his Miranda rights. Should the motion be granted?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

After a valid Miranda waiver, does an ambiguous lawyer remark require suppression of the defendant's later confession?

## 3. Final Answer Choices

A. Yes, because Peter effectively asserted the right to counsel, and the question "Why would you want to talk with a lawyer?" constituted custodial interrogation.

B. Yes, because although Peter did not effectively assert the right to counsel, his conduct before making the statement did not constitute a valid waiver of his Miranda rights.

C. No, because although Peter effectively asserted the right to counsel, the question "Why would you want to talk with a lawyer?" did not constitute custodial interrogation.

D. No, because Peter did not effectively assert the right to counsel, and his conduct before making the statement constituted a valid waiver of his Miranda rights.

## 5. Correct Answer

**D**. Original key: **B**.

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

Peter's best answer is **D**. He said yes when the detective asked whether he understood and was willing to give up his Miranda rights. That is a valid waiver. His later remark, "Maybe I should talk to a lawyer," is ambiguous and does not clearly invoke counsel. Under Davis and Berghuis, officers do not have to stop questioning unless the suspect makes an unambiguous request for counsel.

**Gold Key:** an ambiguous reference to a lawyer does not invoke Miranda; the suspect must clearly ask for counsel, and an earlier valid waiver remains effective until clearly revoked.

**Silver Key:** do not let the later lawyer remark erase the earlier yes. If the waiver came first and the invocation was only maybe, the suppression motion usually fails.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. Yes, because Peter effectively asserted the right to counsel, and the question "Why would you want to talk with a lawyer?" constituted custodial interrogation.**
This is the dominant trap. It sounds right because the detective asked a follow-up question about the lawyer remark, and that feels like the moment the police should have stopped. The break is that Peter never effectively invoked counsel in the first place. A vague statement like "Maybe I should talk to a lawyer" is not enough. Without a clear invocation, the detective did not have to end the interview.

**B. Yes, because although Peter did not effectively assert the right to counsel, his conduct before making the statement did not constitute a valid waiver of his Miranda rights.**
This is attractive because it preserves the idea that the later confession should come out. The breaker is the front end of the stem: Peter was asked if he understood his rights and was willing to give them up, and he said yes. That is a valid waiver. The answer invents a waiver problem that the facts do not support.

**C. No, because although Peter effectively asserted the right to counsel, the question "Why would you want to talk with a lawyer?" did not constitute custodial interrogation.**
This choice uses a plausible clarification-theme move. The breaker is that the whole answer depends on an invocation Peter never made. The question may or may not be interrogation, but that point does not decide the motion once the waiver is valid and the invocation is ambiguous.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The credited answer is consistent with current controlling authority.

- [Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/384/436/) requires warnings before custodial interrogation and permits waiver if the suspect understands the rights being relinquished.
- [Davis v. United States, 512 U.S. 452 (1994)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/512/452/) holds that police need not stop questioning unless the suspect clearly requests counsel; an ambiguous reference to a lawyer is not enough.
- [Berghuis v. Thompkins, 560 U.S. 370 (2010)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/560/370/) confirms that invocation must be unambiguous and that a waiver may be shown by warnings plus understanding plus a course of conduct.
- [Edwards v. Arizona, 451 U.S. 477 (1981)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/451/477/) controls only after a clear invocation; it does not help a suspect who has only made an equivocal remark.

Here, Peter received warnings, said he understood and would talk, and only later made an equivocal lawyer reference. The first yes is a valid waiver. The later maybe is not a clear invocation. So the detective could continue until Peter confessed, and the statement is not suppressible on the theory stated in the motion.

## 10. Brief Note on Why the Variation Preserves the Original Rigor/Difficulty

The legal issue is unchanged: a post-warning, pre-confession ambiguous lawyer remark after an initial waiver. The surface facts are different enough to kill recognition, but the same trap survives: students who treat any mention of a lawyer as a stop signal will pick the wrong answer. The item still turns on the Davis/Berghuis line between ambiguity and clear invocation.
