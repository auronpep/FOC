## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question
Ruth, an amateur embroidery-pattern designer, agreed in writing to create four new Scripture-themed embroidery patterns each year for a six-year period for a private company that sold Christian craft kits. The writing provided, in a clause separately signed by Ruth, that "No changes to this agreement shall bind the company unless made in writing and signed by the company's authorized representative." For the first three years, Ruth delivered and the company accepted only three patterns each year. The patterns sold well, and the company made no objection.

Relying on that course of dealing, Ruth spent substantial sums on a new digitizing tablet and stitching software to speed future pattern work. In the first quarter of the fourth year, however, the company terminated the contract on the ground that Ruth had breached the annual-pattern term. In Ruth's suit against the company for damages, the jury found that the annual-pattern term had been modified by conduct and the trial court awarded Ruth substantial compensatory damages.

Is this result likely to be reversed on appeal?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
If a company repeatedly accepts fewer yearly deliverables than a written contract requires, says nothing, and the designer buys equipment in reliance on that course of performance, can the company later terminate for the shortfall without warning despite a signed no-written-change clause?

### 3. Final answer choices
A. No, because the contract's no-written-change clause was unconscionable as against an amateur pattern designer.

B. No, because the company by its conduct waived the annual-pattern term and Ruth materially changed her position in reasonable reliance on that waiver.

C. Yes, because the contract's no-written-change clause was not expressly waived by the company.

D. Yes, because the contract's no-written-change clause was a material part of the agreed exchange and could not be avoided without new consideration.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. The company accepted the short performance for three straight years, sold the patterns successfully, and never objected. That conduct waived the annual-pattern term as a ground for treating Ruth's performance as a total breach. Gold Key: a party can waive a condition or performance term by accepting performance despite the known shortfall, and that waiver cannot be retracted when the other side has materially changed position in reasonable reliance. Ruth then bought a new digitizing tablet and stitching software to speed future work. Because she materially relied on the company's course of acceptance, the company could not suddenly terminate in year four on the same annual-quantity ground without fair notice. Silver Key: when the stem pairs a formal no-written-change clause with repeated acceptance and reliance spending, the deciding axis is conduct-plus-reliance, not the clause by itself.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because the amateur-designer fact makes the company look more powerful and makes the clause feel harsh. The breaker is that unconscionability is not the reason the judgment stands. The facts do not show a one-sided bargain or lack of meaningful choice at contract formation; they show later acceptance of short performance and reliance on that acceptance.

C is attractive because the contract did have a signed no-written-change clause, and no company representative signed a later written waiver. The breaker is the Gold Key: a waiver can arise from conduct even when a formal modification clause would block an unwritten modification. The company's repeated acceptance without objection, followed by Ruth's reliance spending, defeats a sudden retraction.

D is the dominant trap. It is attractive because "material part of the bargain" and "new consideration" sound like formal contract defenses to an unwritten change. The breaker is that the case is not won by proving a new bargained-for modification. It is won by waiver of a performance condition plus material reliance, so fresh consideration is not the deciding requirement.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested black-letter rule is unchanged: a party that knowingly accepts performance despite the nonoccurrence of a performance condition can waive the condition, and a waiver affecting future performance cannot be retracted when the other party has materially changed position in reasonable reliance.

Common-law grounding: Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 84 treats a promise to perform despite nonoccurrence of a condition as binding in the waiver setting, especially where reliance makes enforcement necessary to avoid injustice. Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 246 links acceptance or retention of performance, with knowledge of the condition failure, to the Section 84 waiver framework. The ALI Adviser identifies Section 84 as the general contract waiver doctrine and explains that implied waiver can arise from conduct objectively showing an intent not to insist on the term (https://www.thealiadviser.org/liability-insurance/waiver-and-estoppel-part-1/). UCC Section 2-209(4)-(5), while directed to Article 2 transactions, confirms the same tested no-written-modification trap: an attempted modification that fails a signed-writing clause can still operate as a waiver, and waiver retraction fails when unjust because of material reliance (https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-209).
