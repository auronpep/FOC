## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Esther sees a teenager trying to steal a wheeled sound-console case from her driveway after a backyard hymn night. The teenager is unarmed and is moving away from Esther, pushing the case toward the sidewalk. Esther yells for him to stop. When he keeps moving, Esther fires a rifle at his back and seriously injures him. Esther is charged with aggravated assault.

Is Esther's use of force justified as defense of property?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

If an owner shoots an unarmed thief in the back solely to stop him from taking personal property, is the shooting justified as defense of property?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. Yes, because deadly force is allowed to stop any felony theft.

B. No, because deadly force is not justified solely to protect personal property.

C. No, because defense of property is never recognized in criminal law.

D. Yes, because the sound-console case was valuable and the thief ignored Esther's warning.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is the best answer. Esther was protecting personal property, not herself or another person. The teenager was unarmed and moving away. Firing a rifle at his back is deadly force. Gold Key: defense of property permits reasonable nondeadly force, but deadly force is not justified solely to protect property. Silver Key: when a defense-of-property answer says yes, look for a person-safety threat before accepting deadly force.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is the strongest trap because the theft sounds serious and the word felony can make deadly force feel available. The breaker is that a property felony alone does not create a deadly person-safety threat. Gold Key: deadly force needs more than a property-loss justification.

C sounds clean because Esther's rifle shot was excessive. The breaker is that defense of property does exist; the doctrine is limited to reasonable force and generally nondeadly force for property alone.

D uses two emotionally strong facts: the property was valuable, and Esther gave a warning. The breaker is that neither value nor warning turns a fleeing property theft into a deadly-force situation.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: reasonable nondeadly force may be used to protect property, but deadly force is not justified solely to prevent loss of personal property.

Model Penal Code section 3.06 is the rule-number authority floor. It permits force to protect possession of land or tangible movable property when immediately necessary, but separately limits deadly force to narrow circumstances involving dwelling dispossession or serious person-safety risks during specified crimes: https://www.criminallawweb.net/web1/mpc/PART1/snippets/3_06.htm. People v. Ceballos, 12 Cal. 3d 470 (1974), states the common-law principle in criminal-law terms: deadly force generally could not be used solely for protection of property, and the preservation of human life and limb is valued above property: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/3d/12/470.html.

Under that rule, Esther's defense fails. She shot an unarmed, fleeing thief in the back to protect a sound-console case. That is deadly force used solely for personal property, not a response to death or serious bodily harm.
