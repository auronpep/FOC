## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Ruth's original promise to pay $100 was voidable because she was a minor. But her later promise — made after she turned 18 — is enforceable. A person who enters a contract as a minor may ratify that obligation (or a modified version of it) after reaching the age of majority, and no new consideration is required for that ratification. Ruth affirmed the transaction by promising to pay $75 after her birthday. That promise is binding. Paul can recover the $75 she committed to, even though her original contract was voidable and even though no new consideration supported the $75 figure.

**Gold Key:** A voidable contract of a minor is ratified by an express or implied promise to perform after the minor reaches majority — new consideration is NOT required for the ratification to be enforceable. (Black-letter rule.)

**Note for distractor D→B:** The Gold Key above is the unlock. Without it, a student might think the original $100 obligation survives intact. But Ruth's post-majority statement ($75, not $100) operated as a modification, and only the amount she affirmed after majority is collectible.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** Contracts entered during minority are voidable at the minor's election (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 14). Upon reaching the age of majority, the former minor may ratify — i.e., affirm — the prior obligation, and such ratification does NOT require additional consideration to be enforceable (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 82, illus.; majority rule). Ratification may be express (oral or written promise to pay) or implied (continued use of the goods after majority). A post-majority promise to pay a reduced amount operates as both ratification of the obligation and modification of the price term.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 14, 82. Leading case law: *Joseph v. Sch. Dist. of Philadelphia* (Pa. 1960) (minor's post-majority promise to pay for services received during minority enforceable without new consideration); *Bucham v. Frimm* (N.Y.) (ratification after majority). The majority American rule holds that the pre-existing-duty rule does not bar affirmance of a voidable obligation after the disability ceases.

**Application:** Ruth was a minor when she agreed to buy the kayak for $100. Her infancy made the contract voidable. After turning 18, she orally promised Paul she would pay $75. This post-majority promise is a ratification — enforceable without new consideration. Paul can collect $75, the amount Ruth affirmed after reaching majority. He cannot collect $100 because Ruth's post-majority statement modified the price term, and only the ratified ($75) obligation survived.

**Conclusion:** Tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are UNCHANGED from the original.

#### Call and Prediction

**Call:** "Paul's probable recovery is:"

This is a **recovery amount** call. It's asking for a dollar figure, not a yes/no liability finding. That means the student must determine:
1. Is Paul entitled to *any* recovery? (Yes — ratification.)
2. If yes, *how much*? (The $75 Ruth promised after majority, not the original $100.)

**Prediction:** Students who know the ratification rule will cut straight to C ($75). Students who don't will likely land on B ($100 — the dominant trap) or A (consideration reflex).

#### Trigger Facts

1. Ruth was a minor (17) when she bought the kayak on credit for $100 — establishes the original contract is voidable.
2. Ruth used the kayak all summer — establishes receipt and continued use; implies ratification-by-conduct potential even before the oral promise.
3. Ruth turned 18 — the disability (infancy) is now gone.
4. Ruth voluntarily told Paul she'd pay $5 at a craft fair after turning 18 — express post-majority ratification of a modified ($75) obligation.
5. Ruth later refused to pay — triggers the breach-of-contract action.
6. Paul is suing for breach — establishes the litigation posture.
