## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Esther owned a traveling Christian handbell-restoration booth called **Hallelujah Handbells**. The booth had recently been robbed of several rare bronze handbells used by gospel choirs.

A police detective showed Esther a single photograph of Peter, who previously had committed other similar booth robberies. Esther studied the photograph and then asked the detective whether the police thought the man in the photograph was the robber. The detective replied, “We’re pretty sure.” Esther then stated that the man in the photograph was the one who had robbed her.

Peter was indicted for the robbery. His counsel moved to suppress any trial testimony by Esther identifying Peter as the robber.

Should the court grant the motion and suppress Esther’s trial testimony identifying Peter as the robber?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**A is correct.** The police-arranged identification procedure was suggestive: the detective showed Esther a single suspect photograph and then reinforced the police view by saying, “We’re pretty sure.” That makes the out-of-court identification improper.

But the remedy is not automatic exclusion of all later in-court identification testimony. **Gold Key:** Even after an unnecessarily suggestive police identification, the prosecution may still use the in-court identification if it shows the identification is reliable. **Silver Key:** Read the A/B pair as “reliability screen” versus “automatic taint.” The answer with the reliability condition beats the answer that says the taint necessarily carries over.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B.** This answer sells the strongest pro-defense instinct: once the police contaminate the photo identification, the later courtroom identification feels ruined forever. The breaker is the **Gold Key:** suggestiveness triggers a reliability screen; it does not create automatic suppression of every in-court identification.

**C.** This answer sells a remedy cutoff: the photo procedure may have been bad, but the courtroom identification supposedly cannot be suppressed. The breaker is the remedy link. A suggestive police-arranged identification can lead to suppression if the prosecution cannot show reliability.

**D.** This answer sells police confidence as legitimacy. The breaker is the setup itself: one suspect photograph plus the detective’s confidence signal is the very kind of suggestive police-arranged procedure that makes the identification improper.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The source row identifies QID 14590, gives the 42% correct rate and answer-choice pick rates, states the original key as D, and explains that a single-photo procedure plus the detective’s leading statement was improper but not automatically fatal if reliability can be shown. 

The governing doctrine is the Supreme Court’s due-process rule for police-arranged suggestive eyewitness identifications. **Neil v. Biggers** states that the central question is whether, under the totality of the circumstances, the identification was reliable even though the confrontation procedure was suggestive, and lists reliability factors such as opportunity to view, degree of attention, prior-description accuracy, certainty, and time between crime and confrontation. ([Justia Law][1]) **Manson v. Brathwaite** confirms that suggestive and unnecessary identification procedures do not violate due process if the identification has sufficient reliability. ([Justia Law][2]) **Perry v. New Hampshire** states the current framing: due-process screening applies when law enforcement arranged suggestive circumstances, suppression is not inevitable, and reliability remains the linchpin. ([Legal Information Institute][3])

The transform preserves the same legal issue and outcome: police-arranged suggestiveness creates a suppression question, but the prosecution can defeat suppression by showing reliability.
