### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation
The felony murder rule holds a defendant liable for a death that occurs during the commission of an inherently dangerous felony, including the period of immediate flight from the scene. However, felony murder liability terminates once the perpetrator reaches a "place of temporary safety." In this scenario, Silas and his accomplice had successfully escaped, changed their appearance, reached a secluded house, and remained undisturbed for four hours while sharing a meal. These facts establish that they reached a place of temporary safety. Therefore, the subsequent killing was a separate criminal episode rather than an event occurring "during" the robbery or its immediate flight.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations
- **A:** While robbery is indeed an inherently dangerous felony that can support a felony murder charge, this choice is a "half-truth." It identifies a necessary condition (a qualifying felony) but ignores the temporal requirement that the death must occur during the felony transaction.
- **B:** This is the dominant trap. Some jurisdictions have rules limiting felony murder when the victim is a co-felon (e.g., the Redline rule for killings by police or victims). However, that rule is a specific jurisdictional variant and usually applies to "justifiable" killings of co-felons by third parties. The more fundamental and universal reason felony murder fails here is the "place of safety" rule, which ends the felony transaction entirely.
- **D:** This choice relies on a "but-for" causation theory. While the argument over the gold wouldn't have happened without the robbery, felony murder requires a tighter temporal and causal "res gestae" connection. The death must occur in the "course of and in furtherance of" the felony. Once safety is reached, that connection is severed.

### 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification
**Authority:** *People v. Gladman*, 41 N.Y.2d 123 (1976); *State v. Hearron*, 228 Kan. 693 (1980).
The felony murder doctrine encompasses deaths occurring during the "res gestae" of the crime. The transaction continues through immediate flight until the felon has reached a place of temporary safety. Factors for safety include time elapsed, distance from the scene, and whether the felon is still being pursued. Hiding in a safe house for four hours comfortably satisfies the safety standard, ending felony murder eligibility for subsequent acts. The transform preserves the rule (Place of Safety), the issue (termination of felony transaction), and the credited outcome.
