### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**C is correct.** Esther fully performed a fixed-price service contract, and Timothy's only remaining duty was to pay the promised price. The recovery is therefore the unpaid contract price: **$8,400**.

**Gold Key:** When the service provider has completed a fixed-price contract and only payment remains, start with the unpaid price. The provider does not get to replace the agreed price with a higher reasonable-value measure.

**Silver Key:** Lock the completed-work price first. Then test each answer that adds a later consequence or swaps in market value for a recognized damages path.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because Timothy did receive services worth more than the contract price. The breaker is the completed fixed-price contract: once Esther has fully performed and only payment remains, the claim is for the agreed price, not a higher reasonable-value substitute. **Gold Key:** the price-cap rule defeats the restitution frame.

**B.** This choice is attractive because the lost cart discount looks like a precise economic consequence of Timothy's refusal to pay. The breaker is foreseeability at contract formation: the later cart bargain was a special circumstance Timothy had no reason to know when he made the restoration contract. **Gold Key:** special side-deal losses need contract-formation foreseeability.

**D.** This choice is attractive because the medical expenses feel directly tied to the refusal to pay. The breaker is the same damages limiter: personal medical expenses from Esther's stress reaction were not a foreseeable contract-loss measure when Timothy hired her to restore the hymnals.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. Under the expectation-damages measure, the injured party is placed in the position performance would have produced, subject to limitations on consequential loss. Here, performance by Timothy would have produced payment of the fixed price, $8,400.

Authority floor: Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 347 supplies the expectation-damages measure; § 351 limits damages to losses the breaching party had reason to foresee when the contract was made; and § 373(2) bars restitution when the injured party has fully performed and the only remaining performance due is payment of a definite sum. Hadley v. Baxendale is the classic foreseeability authority for excluding special losses not communicated at contract formation.
