## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures and states that warrants require probable cause and particularity. ([Congress.gov][1]) In **New Jersey v. T.L.O.**, the Supreme Court held that the Fourth Amendment applies to public-school officials, but ordinary school officials acting under school authority need not meet the probable-cause/warrant standard; the school search is judged under a reasonableness / reasonable-suspicion framework. ([Justia Law][2]) The same decision expressly limited its holding to searches by school authorities acting alone and on their own authority, and did not decide searches by school officials acting with or at the behest of law enforcement; the Court noted authority applying a probable-cause standard when police are involved. ([Justia Law][2])

The police-direction point is also supported by the ordinary agency principle from **Coolidge v. New Hampshire**: when a private or third-party actor’s conduct is attributable to police, the question is whether the person acted as an instrument or agent of the state. ([Justia Law][3]) Once the search is treated as an unconstitutional police-directed search, **Mapp v. Ohio** supplies the exclusionary-rule remedy: evidence obtained by searches and seizures in violation of the federal Constitution is inadmissible in a state criminal trial. ([Justia Law][4])
