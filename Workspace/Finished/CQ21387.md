### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**Answer C is correct.** Claim preclusion (res judicata) bars relitigation when there is (1) a final judgment on the merits, (2) involving the same parties or their privies, and (3) arising from the same transaction or occurrence as the prior action. A stipulated dismissal with prejudice, entered by the court, qualifies as a final judgment on the merits for claim-preclusion purposes—even though no trial occurred and no specific issues were actually litigated.

The key insight is that claim preclusion looks to whether the *claim* (the transaction underlying the lawsuit) was resolved, not whether specific issues were debated. The "with prejudice" language signals that the plaintiff放弃s the right to sue again on the same matter. When Mary dismissed her breach-of-contract claim with prejudice, she barred herself from suing Daniel again for the same roof leaks, regardless of whether she now labels her theory "negligent performance" instead of "breach of contract."

**Gold Key:** A dismissal with prejudice operates as a final judgment on the merits for claim-preclusion purposes, even when the dismissal results from a settlement stipulation rather than a trial verdict.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Answer A (original B) — 26% (predicted, dominant trap):** This choice is attractive because it uses the word "preclusion" correctly and recognizes that the dismissal with prejudice has legal consequences. However, it confuses claim preclusion with issue preclusion. Issue preclusion (collateral estoppel) requires that a specific issue was actually litigated and necessarily decided in the prior action. A stipulated dismissal means the parties settled; nothing was actually litigated. The roof leaks, the quality of Daniel's work, and whether he breached any duty were never argued to a judge or jury. Issue preclusion cannot apply when there was no actual litigation.

**Answer B (original D) — 7% (predicted):** This choice appeals to the intuition that different legal labels should mean different claims. Students may think "negligent performance" sounds distinct from "breach of contract." But claim preclusion does not turn on the plaintiff's chosen legal theory. It turns on whether the second suit arises from the same transaction—the same roof, the same repairs, the same parties, the same alleged defects. Mary cannot avoid claim preclusion by re-labeling her claim. The transaction is identical.

**Answer D (original C) — 9% (predicted):** This choice exploits a common misconception that a "judgment on the merits" requires a full trial with witness testimony. In reality, claim preclusion applies to many dispositions that never involve testimony: summary judgments, default judgments, and stipulated dismissals with prejudice all qualify as final judgments on the merits. The absence of testimony does not prevent claim preclusion from attaching to a dismissal with prejudice.

### 8. Black-Letter Verification with Authority

**Rule:** Claim preclusion (res judicata) bars relitigation when: (1) a court of competent jurisdiction rendered a final judgment on the merits; (2) the parties in the second action are identical to or in privity with the parties in the first action; and (3) the second action arises from the same transaction or series of transactions as the first action. *Montana v. United States*, 440 U.S. 147, 153 (1979); *Semtek Int'l Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp.*, 531 U.S. 497, 502–05 (2001).

**Authority for dismissal with prejudice as final judgment:** A voluntary dismissal with prejudice "has the same preclusive effect as a judgment on the merits." See *Busher v. Barry* (N.Y. App. Div. 2024) (holding that voluntary dismissal with prejudice in federal court barred subsequent state-court action under res judicata); Restatement (Second) of Judgments § 27 (a valid and final personal judgment on the merits extinguishes claim). Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 41(a)(1)(B) provides that a notice of dismissal operates as an adjudication on the merits when filed by the plaintiff, unless otherwise stated—but parties frequently stipulate to dismissal "with prejudice" to signal finality and trigger claim-preclusion effect.

**Verification:** The tested rule is that a dismissal with prejudice qualifies as a final judgment on the merits for claim-preclusion purposes. The credited answer (claim preclusion bars the second suit) is correct under settled federal and state law. No doctrinal-currency hotlist issue applies (this is standard civil procedure, not a superseded constitutional test). The transform preserves the original rule, issue, and credited outcome exactly.

**This Item Teaches:** Dismissal with prejudice qualifies as final judgment on merits for res judicata  
**Fills:** Gap between "judgment" and "settlement" — students often think only trial verdicts trigger preclusion  
**Adjacent to Master:** 97100100 (Types of Jury Verdicts), 97100500 (Dismissals)

## Crossover Intersection Map

**Crossovers:** None (pure Civil Procedure preclusion question)  
**Intersections:** Contracts (breach of contract claim), Torts (negligent performance claim) — but preclusion doctrine is procedural, not substantive

## Review Truth

A dismissal with prejudice operates as a final judgment on the merits and bars relitigation of all claims arising from the same transaction between the same parties.
