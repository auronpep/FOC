## 7. Right-Answer Explanation

Lydia's role as treasurer gave her lawful, authorized possession of the camp's funds — she was entrusted to collect fees and manage disbursements. That lawful initial possession is the defining feature that places this squarely in embezzlement territory. When she wrote herself a personal check, she converted property she held in a fiduciary capacity for a purpose entirely outside her authority. That is the textbook fraudulent conversion element of embezzlement.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIM-EMBEZ-01):** Lawful initial possession converts the offense from larceny to embezzlement. Because Lydia received the funds with full authorization — not by any trespassory taking — the subsequent personal conversion is embezzlement, never larceny.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIM-EMBEZ-03):** Authorization to handle funds for a specific organizational purpose does not extend to personal conversion. Exceeding the scope of authority — using camp funds for personal groceries — is itself the fraudulent conversion element.

**Silver Key (SK-CRIM-EMBEZ-01):** Trace the timeline: lawful possession first → personal conversion later = embezzlement. Any choice that ignores the "lawful initial possession" fact fails the sequence test.

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A — No theft offense, because Lydia was authorized to sign checks on the camp's account.**

The pull: "authorized to sign checks" sounds like a complete permission. If she was allowed to sign, how could signing be theft?

The breaker: Authorization is always limited in scope. Lydia's authority was to sign checks for *camp purposes* — not to convert camp funds to personal use. The moment she used that authority to pay her own groceries, she exceeded the scope and committed fraudulent conversion.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIM-EMBEZ-03):** Authorization to handle funds for a specific organizational purpose does not extend to personal conversion. Exceeding the scope of authority is itself the fraudulent conversion element of embezzlement. This is the dominant trap — it sounds most like a complete defense but it is not.

**C — False pretenses, because the family was deceived about how their payment would be used.**

The pull: The family presumably expected their $3,600 to fund camp activities. It feels like they were "deceived" about how the money would be used.

The breaker: False pretenses requires a knowing false statement of past or present fact made *before* the transfer that induces the victim to hand over title. The family mailed a check to pay a registration fee — they were not induced to do so by any false statement from Lydia. The wrong is Lydia's later conversion of lawfully received funds, not a misrepresentation that caused the payment.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIM-FALSEPRET-02):** False pretenses requires (1) a knowing false statement of past or present fact (2) made before the transfer (3) that induces the victim to transfer title. Misuse of lawfully received funds — with no prior false statement — cannot be charged as false pretenses.

**D — Larceny, because any taking of another's money constitutes larceny.**

The pull: Lydia did take camp money for herself. "Taking" + "someone else's money" sounds like larceny.

The breaker: "Any taking is larceny" is simply wrong. Larceny requires a *trespassory* taking — one without any lawful consent or authorization from the start. Lydia began with full authorization. When initial possession is lawful and authorized, subsequent conversion is embezzlement. The absolute claim in this choice is a flat misstatement of the law.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIM-EMBEZ-01) / Silver Key (SK-CRIM-EMBEZ-01):** Trace the timeline. Lawful possession first → conversion later = embezzlement. This choice fails the sequence test immediately.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Tested rule:** Embezzlement at common law requires (1) fraudulent conversion (2) of property (3) of another (4) by one who was in lawful possession of it (5) by virtue of a fiduciary or trust relationship. The critical element distinguishing embezzlement from larceny is that possession is *lawfully obtained* before the conversion occurs.

**Applied:** Lydia lawfully possessed the $3,600 after depositing it — her role as treasurer gave her authorized control. She then converted those funds for personal grocery expenses, exceeding the scope of her fiduciary authority. All five elements are satisfied.

**Why not larceny:** Larceny requires a trespassory taking — a taking without consent from the outset. Because Lydia had full authorization to receive and handle the funds, there was no trespassory taking. (MPC § 223.2 covers theft by unlawful taking; MPC § 223.8 covers misapplication of entrusted property — the embezzlement analog.)

**Why not false pretenses:** False pretenses (MPC § 223.3) requires a purposeful false impression about a past or present fact that causes the victim to transfer title. No false statement by Lydia induced the family to send the check; they paid a registration fee voluntarily. The wrong occurred after lawful receipt.

**Why not no offense:** Authority to sign organizational checks does not include authority to use organizational funds for personal benefit. Exceeding the scope of fiduciary authority is itself the "fraudulent" element of conversion. See Wayne R. LaFave, Substantive Criminal Law § 19.6 (embezzlement: scope of authorization does not license personal conversion of entrusted funds).

#### Section 12 — Remediation Card

**Card:** CRIM-EMBEZ-01

**Title:** Theft Offense Triage: Lawful vs. Trespassory Possession

**Signal:** An authorized agent or fiduciary converts funds she was entrusted to manage.

**Student move:** Ask one question first: Was the initial possession *lawful* (authorized) or *trespassory* (without consent)? Lawful → embezzlement. Trespassory from the start → larceny.

**Tiny rule:** Lawful possession first + conversion later = embezzlement. No prior false statement to the payer = not false pretenses. Authorized scope ≠ permission for personal use.

**Trap to watch:** "She was authorized to sign checks" sounds like a full defense. It is not — authorization is always scope-limited. The moment she signed a personal check, she exceeded her authority and committed fraudulent conversion.

## Gold Keys

**GK-CRIM-EMBEZ-01 — Lawful Possession → Embezzlement (Not Larceny)**
Lawful initial possession converts the offense from larceny to embezzlement. If the agent received the property with authorization — not by trespass — subsequent personal conversion is embezzlement, never larceny.
*Type: distinction | Unlocks: D | Trigger: authorized agent converts funds she legitimately received*
*Authority (lawyer confirmation only): MPC §§ 223.2, 223.8; LaFave, Substantive Criminal Law § 19.6*

**GK-CRIM-FALSEPRET-02 — False Pretenses Requires a Prior False Statement**
False pretenses requires (1) a knowing false statement of past or present fact (2) made before the transfer (3) that induces the victim to transfer title. Misuse of lawfully received funds — with no prior false statement — cannot be charged as false pretenses.
*Type: rule | Unlocks: C | Trigger: funds converted after lawful receipt; no misrepresentation to the payer*
*Authority (lawyer confirmation only): MPC § 223.3; LaFave, Substantive Criminal Law § 19.7*

**GK-CRIM-EMBEZ-03 — Scope of Authorization Does Not License Personal Conversion**
Authorization to handle funds for a specific organizational purpose does not extend to personal conversion. Exceeding the scope of the fiduciary authority — using organizational funds for personal benefit — is itself the fraudulent conversion element of embezzlement.
*Type: distinction | Unlocks: A (dominant trap) | Trigger: authorized agent writes personal check on organizational account*
*Authority (lawyer confirmation only): Common law; LaFave, Substantive Criminal Law § 19.6*
