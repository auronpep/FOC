## 1. Final Question

Peter operates a small goat dairy. He contracted to sell thirty goats to Lydia, a cheesemaker. The signed agreement required Peter to deliver the goats by the first Saturday of each month, contained a merger clause, and stated that any changes required a signed writing. For two months after signing, Peter delivered the goats on the third Saturday, and Lydia accepted them without objection after her operations manager told Peter that third-Saturday delivery was fine. Should the court admit the evidence for the limited purpose of showing waiver or modification of the delivery date?

**B.** No, because the merger clause bars all evidence outside the signed agreement.

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** Under UCC § 2-209(4), an attempted modification that fails to satisfy the signed-writing requirement of subsection (2) or the statute of frauds of subsection (3) can still operate as a waiver. A waiver is not a modification — it is a party's voluntary relinquishment of a right, and it does not require a signed writing even when the contract says modifications must be in writing.

The evidence Peter offers — the manager's oral statements and the two accepted late deliveries — is post-signing conduct. The parol evidence rule (UCC § 2-202) bars evidence of prior or contemporaneous agreements that contradict a fully integrated writing. But post-signing conduct is not "parol evidence" at all; it is outside the rule's scope entirely. The merger clause reinforces integration for pre-contractual evidence but does not bar evidence of what the parties did *after* signing.

Under UCC § 1-303(f), course of performance is relevant to show waiver or modification of any term inconsistent with that performance. The two accepted late deliveries, combined with the manager's oral approval, constitute a course of performance showing that Lydia waived strict compliance with the first-Saturday delivery date.

The evidence is offered for the limited purpose of showing waiver, not to contradict the original writing with a prior agreement. That timing and purpose distinction is the decisive lock.

**Silver Key:** When you see a merger clause in a contracts question, lock the call before you lock the clause. If the evidence is offered to show post-signing waiver — not to vary the original terms with prior negotiations — the merger clause is a red herring. Cut any choice that treats the merger clause as an absolute bar to all extrinsic evidence.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. No, because a written delivery date cannot be affected by later course of performance.**

*Attractive because:* Students know that written terms are binding and that course of performance is a weaker source under the UCC hierarchy (§ 1-303(e)). It feels right to say the written date controls.

*Breaker:* This choice ignores UCC § 1-303(f), which expressly provides that course of performance is relevant to show waiver or modification of an inconsistent term. A written term can be waived by later conduct. The "cannot be affected" framing is an overclaim — course of performance doesn't automatically override the writing, but it is admissible evidence of waiver. **Gold Key:** UCC § 2-209(4) allows a failed modification to operate as a waiver, and § 1-303(f) makes course of performance relevant to show that waiver.

**B. No, because the merger clause bars all evidence outside the signed agreement.**

*Attractive because:* A merger clause means the writing is fully integrated, and the parol evidence rule does bar evidence of prior or contemporaneous agreements that contradict the writing. Students overgeneralize this into an absolute bar.

*Breaker:* The merger clause bars evidence of *prior or contemporaneous* agreements — not post-signing conduct offered to show waiver. The evidence here arose after the contract was signed and is offered to show what the parties did afterward, not what they agreed to before. The "all evidence" framing is a tiered absolute (always/never) that ignores the timing distinction. **Silver Key:** Lock the call first — this evidence is offered for the limited purpose of showing waiver, not to contradict the writing. The merger clause doesn't reach post-signing waiver evidence.

**C. Yes, but only as trade usage evidence, because the parties' own performance under this contract is irrelevant.**

*Attractive because:* The evidence *is* admissible, so "Yes" feels right. And trade usage is a recognized source under the UCC. Students who know the evidence should be admitted but aren't sure why will grab the "trade usage" framing as a safe-sounding reason.

*Breaker:* This choice gets the conclusion right but the reasoning wrong — and the reasoning is what the call asks about. Under UCC § 1-303(e), course of performance (the parties' own conduct under *this* contract) is actually the *strongest* interpretive source, prevailing over both course of trade and trade usage. Saying the parties' own performance is "irrelevant" is flatly wrong. The evidence is admissible as course of performance showing waiver, not as trade usage.

## 9. Black-Letter Legal Verification

**Governing authorities:**

1. **UCC § 2-209(4):** "Although an attempt at modification or rescission does not satisfy the requirements of subsection (2) or (3) it can operate as a waiver."
   - Source: [Cornell LII, UCC § 2-209](https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-209)

2. **UCC § 1-303(f):** "Subject to Section 2-209, a course of performance is relevant to show a waiver or modification of any term inconsistent with the course of performance."
   - Source: [Cornell LII, UCC § 1-303](https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/1/1-303)

3. **UCC § 2-202 (parol evidence rule):** The rule bars evidence of prior or contemporaneous agreements contradicting a fully integrated writing but expressly permits supplementation by course of performance, course of dealing, or usage of trade.
   - Source: [Cornell LII, Wex: Parol Evidence Rule](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/parol_evidence_rule)

When the buyer later rejects a late delivery, is evidence of the manager's oral statements and the two accepted late deliveries admissible to show waiver or modification of the delivery date?

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks whether evidence is admissible "for the limited purpose of showing waiver or modification." The key phrase is "limited purpose" — this signals the evidence is not being offered to contradict the writing with a prior agreement, but to show post-signing waiver. Any choice that treats the merger clause as an absolute bar to all extrinsic evidence is likely wrong.

Prediction: The correct answer will say the evidence is admissible and will correctly identify the legal basis (waiver, not trade usage).
