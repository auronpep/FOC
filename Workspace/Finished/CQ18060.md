### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**Gold Key:** Rule 12(h)(1) waives any defense listed in Rule 12(b)(2)-(5) — including lack of personal jurisdiction — if the party omits it from a pre-answer Rule 12 motion. Once a defendant files any Rule 12 motion, all four of the (b)(2)-(5) defenses must be consolidated into that first motion or they are forever waived.

Barnabas filed a pre-answer Rule 12 motion raising improper venue and insufficient service of process. Personal jurisdiction is also a Rule 12(b)(2) defense. Because Barnabas omitted it from that first motion, the defense is waived. The court should deny the second motion.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (incorrect — half-truth / SMJ confusion):** This choice is attractive because students learn early that subject matter jurisdiction can never be waived and can be raised at any time — even on appeal. That rule is real, but it applies to subject matter jurisdiction under Rule 12(b)(1), not personal jurisdiction under Rule 12(b)(2). Personal jurisdiction is waivable. The Gold Key (Rule 12(h)(1)) kills this trap: the defense was available, the defendant made a Rule 12 motion, and the defense was omitted. Waived.

**C (incorrect — flat misstatement):** This choice sounds reasonable because it treats the answer as the triggering event. But the consolidation rule is triggered by the *first Rule 12 motion*, not by the answer. Filing any pre-answer Rule 12 motion forces the defendant to include all (b)(2)-(5) defenses in that motion. The defendant cannot wait until the answer to raise personal jurisdiction after having already filed a Rule 12 motion.

**D (incorrect — colloquialism / merits-as-procedure):** This choice tempts students who know that personal jurisdiction turns on minimum contacts. But minimum contacts is the *substantive merits* question the court would reach only if the defense were properly before it. The procedural waiver under Rule 12(h)(1) blocks the defense before the court ever gets to minimum contacts. The plaintiff's burden on the merits is irrelevant when the defense has been procedurally forfeited.

### 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule:** Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(h)(1) provides that a party waives any defense listed in Rule 12(b)(2)-(5) by omitting it from a motion in the circumstances described in Rule 12(g)(2). Rule 12(g)(2) states that a party making a Rule 12 motion must not make another Rule 12 motion raising a defense that was available but omitted from the earlier motion.

The four waivable defenses are: (1) lack of personal jurisdiction (12(b)(2)), (2) improper venue (12(b)(3)), (3) insufficient process (12(b)(4)), and (4) insufficient service of process (12(b)(5)). By contrast, lack of subject matter jurisdiction (12(b)(1)) can be raised at any time and is never waived. *See* Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(h)(1); *Henderson ex rel. Henderson v. Shinseki*, 562 U.S. 428, 434-35 (2011) (subject matter jurisdiction cannot be waived).

The original question's credited answer (A) is correct under this rule. The transform preserves the identical black-letter rule, the same trap structure (dominant trap: SMJ/PJ confusion), and the same credited outcome.

#### Wrong Answer Recovery Paths

**Choice A recovery path:**
- A student picks A because they remember "jurisdiction can never be raised late" from the SMJ rule.
- Recovery: Ask — which *type* of jurisdiction does that rule apply to? Subject matter, not personal. Personal jurisdiction is in the (b)(2)-(5) group and *can* be waived. Go back to the Gold Key.

**Choice C recovery path:**
- A student picks C because they think the answer is what triggers the consolidation rule.
- Recovery: Ask — what happens when a defendant files a Rule 12 motion before answering? That motion *is* the first response. The consolidation rule is already triggered. The answer is irrelevant.

**Choice D recovery path:**
- A student picks D because they know personal jurisdiction requires minimum contacts.
- Recovery: Ask — is the call asking about the *merits* of personal jurisdiction, or the *procedural status* of the defense? The defense was waived before the court reaches minimum contacts. The merits don't matter if the defense is procedurally gone.
