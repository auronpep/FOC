### 6. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

B is correct. Murder by malice aforethought does not require a jury finding that Daniel specifically meant to kill Peter. The jury found that Daniel intended either to kill Peter or to inflict serious bodily harm when he struck Peter on the head with the heavy handbell. Gold Key: common-law malice includes intent to cause serious bodily harm, so either branch of that jury finding supports murder. ### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. because the jury found that the killing occurred in the course of an aggravated assault.** This is attractive because the jurisdiction's murder definition includes killings committed during an independent felony, and aggravated assault is labeled a felony. The breaker is the word "independent." Gold Key: an assaultive felony that is the very force causing the homicide merges with the killing and cannot be used as the independent felony for felony murder. The handbell assault is the killing's assaultive act, so this finding does not supply the murder route.

**C. only because the jury found that Daniel intended to kill Peter.** This sells the familiar shortcut that murder means intent to kill. The breaker is that the jury did not need to isolate intent to kill. Gold Key: malice also includes intent to inflict serious bodily harm, and the jury found intent to kill or inflict serious bodily harm.

**D. only because the jury found that Daniel did not act in a rage provoked by Peter's accusation.** This choice notices a real mitigation issue: heat of passion can reduce murder to manslaughter when the provocation is adequate and the defendant acts before cooling. The breaker is that absence of provoked rage does not itself prove malice. It clears away a possible mitigation story, but the murder element comes from the intent to kill or seriously injure.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is unchanged from the source row: common-law murder requires malice aforethought, and malice can be satisfied by intent to kill or intent to inflict serious bodily injury. The federal murder statute uses the traditional formulation, "unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought," in 18 U.S.C. section 1111. Federal criminal cases and pattern instructions identify intent to kill and intent to do serious bodily injury as separate ways to satisfy malice. See 18 U.S.C. section 1111, https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1111; United States v. Chino, 238 F.3d 1242, 1248 (10th Cir. 2001), https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/238/1242/515399/; Tenth Circuit Criminal Pattern Jury Instruction 2.53, https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/documents/downloads/2025%20Criminal%20Pattern%20Jury%20Instructions.pdf.

The felony-murder distractor is also unchanged. The jurisdiction requires an independent felony. Assaultive conduct that is the homicidal act is not independent for felony-murder purposes; allowing it would turn most fatal assaults into murder without a separate malice inquiry. People v. Ireland, 70 Cal. 2d 522 (1969), and People v. Chun, 45 Cal. 4th 1172 (2009), are real merger-rule authorities illustrating that assaultive felonies merge with the homicide and cannot serve as the felony-murder predicate. See https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2d/70/522.html and https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/people-v-chun-33066.

The heat-of-passion finding does not contradict the key. It removes a manslaughter-mitigation possibility, but it is not the affirmative malice finding. The credited outcome remains B: Daniel may be found guilty of murder because the jury found intent to kill or intent to inflict serious bodily harm. No researched authority contradicts the supplied key. Bank validation verdict: PASS.
