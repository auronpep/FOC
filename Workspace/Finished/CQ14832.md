## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Timothy was charged with arson after a retreat center van burned behind a chapel. During a recess in Timothy's trial, while walking past the open courtroom door with his attorney Lydia, Timothy said in a normal voice, "So what if I lit the van? The state still cannot prove it." Judge Hannah, who was presiding over the trial, overheard the remark. After Judge Hannah reported the incident to counsel, the prosecutor called Judge Hannah as a witness in the trial.

Is Judge Hannah's testimony about Timothy's statement admissible?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

May the prosecutor prove a defendant's overheard statement to counsel by calling the judge presiding over the defendant's trial as a witness in that same trial?

### 3. Final Answer Choices

A. No, because the remark was protected by the attorney-client privilege.

B. No, because a judge presiding at a trial may not testify as a witness in that trial.

C. Yes, as Timothy's own opposing-party statement.

D. Yes, because Timothy made the remark without reasonable efforts to keep it confidential.

### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. The deciding problem is not whether Timothy's statement looks like an admission or whether he kept it confidential. The prosecutor chose Judge Hannah as the witness, and Judge Hannah is presiding over the same trial. **Gold Key:** Under Federal Rule of Evidence 605, the presiding judge may not testify as a witness at the trial. **Silver Key:** When the proposed witness is the presiding judge, check the witness-competency gate before spending time on hearsay or privilege.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the natural attorney-client instinct: Timothy was speaking to Lydia, his lawyer, about the charged conduct. The breaker is confidentiality. The statement was made in a normal voice while walking past an open courtroom door, so the privilege answer overclaims. Even more importantly, the selected witness is the presiding judge. **Gold Key:** FRE 605 keeps that judge off the witness stand in the same trial.

C sells a true hearsay move. Timothy's own statement, offered against Timothy, is generally an opposing-party statement under FRE 801(d)(2)(A). That does not answer the witness-competency problem. The prosecutor is trying to prove the statement through the presiding judge, and FRE 605 blocks that witness.

D is the dominant trap because it notices the confidentiality failure. Timothy and Lydia did not take reasonable steps to keep the remark private, so the privilege objection is weak. But the conclusion jumps too far. Lack of privilege does not make every witness competent. **Silver Key:** admissibility still has separate gates, and the presiding-judge gate controls here.

### 8. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. Federal Rule of Evidence 605 states that the presiding judge may not testify as a witness at the trial and that a party need not object to preserve the issue. See FRE 605, Legal Information Institute: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_605. Timothy's statement would otherwise fit the opposing-party statement rule because it is his own statement offered against him. See FRE 801(d)(2)(A), Legal Information Institute: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_801. The privilege distractors remain wrong because a communication exposed to overhearing in a public setting lacks reasonable confidentiality. The controlling bar is still FRE 605: the prosecutor cannot prove the statement by calling the presiding judge as a witness in that same trial.
