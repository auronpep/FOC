## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question
Peter, the sole artisan of a small Christian devotional-goods workshop, entered into a written contract to carve for Paul 1,000 custom olive-wood keepsake crosses of a nonstandard, limited-edition pattern for a price of $10 per cross. Peter correctly calculated that it would cost him $8 in materials and bench time to carve each cross. Delivery was scheduled for 60 days later, in time for Paul's private Christian conference swag bags. Fifty-five days later, after Peter had finished carving all 1,000 crosses, Paul cancelled the conference and repudiated the contract. After notifying Paul of his intention to resell, Peter sold the 1,000 crosses to a wood-blank salvage supplier for $2 per cross. Peter then sued Paul for damages. What damages should the court award to Peter?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
A seller of specially manufactured goods has already completed production when the buyer repudiates; the seller resells the goods for far less than the contract price. How should the court measure the seller's damages?

### 3. Final answer choices
A. $2 per cross, representing the difference between the cost of carving and the price the buyer agreed to pay.
B. $6 per cross, representing the difference between the cost of carving and the salvage price.
C. Nominal damages, because Peter failed to resell the crosses by public auction.
D. $8 per cross, representing the lost profits plus the unrecovered cost of carving.

### 5. Correct answer
D (original key: C)

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
D is correct. The Uniform Commercial Code governs a sale of goods. The seller is entitled to be put in the position he would have been in if the contract had been performed. UCC § 2-708(1) measures damages by the contract-price/market-price difference; where that measure is inadequate, UCC § 2-708(2) supplies the alternative. Gold Key: under § 2-708(2), the seller recovers (lost profit, including reasonable overhead) + (costs reasonably incurred) − (proceeds of resale). On these facts, that yields $2 lost profit + $8 cost reasonably incurred − $2 resale proceeds = $8 per cross. That fully places the seller in the as-if-performed position. Silver Key: a public auction is the formal resale procedure under UCC § 2-706, but § 2-708(2) only requires a "due credit for payments or proceeds of resale"; a private resale to a salvage buyer still gives the credit, and the auction-format requirement is not the test for the § 2-708(2) calculation.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because the $2 figure is the lost profit, and "lost profit" sounds like the whole loss. The breaker is that § 2-708(2) does not strip out the costs already incurred; the seller is entitled to recover those costs as part of being made whole, in addition to the lost profit. A's $2 number leaves the seller's unrecovered cost on the table, so it undercompensates.

B is attractive because it looks like the cost-minus-salvage subtraction a student does when they think of the seller as a salvager trying to recover production costs. Gold Key: § 2-708(2) is not a cost-minus-salvage formula; it is a (profit + costs) − resale formula. B drops the lost-profit add-back, which is exactly the move the lost-volume seller is allowed to keep, and that drop is what makes B the dominant trap on this fact pattern.

C is attractive because it imports the resale procedure of UCC § 2-706 (a public, commercially reasonable sale) and treats any private resale as legally defective. The breaker is that § 2-706's procedural requirements are about how the seller computes damages on a resale claim, not about the § 2-708(2) lost-profit formula; the seller can give the buyer "due credit" for the resale proceeds even when the resale was not by public auction, and § 2-708(2) is the governing measure here in any event.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Contracts, Remedies, Expectation damages, and the verified outline code is `51060101`. The contract is for the sale of goods, so Article 2 of the Uniform Commercial Code controls. UCC § 2-708(1) sets the default market-price measure of damages for a seller's claim of nonacceptance or repudiation. Where that measure "is inadequate to put the seller in as good a position as performance would have done," UCC § 2-708(2) substitutes the lost-profit formula: "the profit (including reasonable overhead) which the seller would have made from full performance by the buyer, together with any incidental damages provided in this Article (Section 2-710), due allowance for costs reasonably incurred and due credit for payments or proceeds of resale." UCC § 2-708(2) (Official Text). The leading case applying that provision to a seller who both resold and sought lost profit is Neri v. Retail Marine Corp., 30 N.Y.2d 393, 285 N.E.2d 311 (N.Y. 1972). Because the seller had already finished production (so the cost is "reasonably incurred" and the § 2-708(1) market-price subtraction would yield zero on the same-price facts typical of a special-order item), the § 2-708(2) formula controls and the measure is $2 + $8 − $2 = $8 per cross. The official key is confirmed; the credited outcome is unchanged from the original.
