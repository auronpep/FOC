# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

A state has enacted this rule for criminal trials:

> “Whenever the U.S. Constitution permits, the burden of proof for a defense asserted by an accused shall be placed on the accused, and the burden shall be the greatest burden the Constitution permits.”

The same state defines a crime called **forcible seizure**:

> “Forcible seizure is taking a movable item from an unconsenting person by means of force or violence. Consent of the person is a complete defense to a charge of forcible seizure.”

During Daniel’s trial for forcible seizure, he testified that, at a gospel-festival rehearsal, Naomi consented to his taking her hand-painted psalm drum for the processional.

How should the trial judge instruct the jury regarding the burden of proof on the issue of consent?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A criminal statute defines the offense using an unconsenting person, also calls consent a defense, and the defendant claims consent. Who bears the burden on consent?

## 3. Final answer choices

A. The defendant must prove by clear and convincing evidence that Naomi consented.
B. The defendant must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Naomi consented.
C. The prosecution must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Naomi did not consent.
D. The defendant must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that Naomi consented.

## 5. Correct answer

**C**. Original key letter: **D**.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C** is the correct instruction. The offense definition itself requires taking from an **unconsenting** person. Daniel’s consent evidence therefore contests a fact built into the crime, not a separate fact outside the crime.

**Gold Key:** Element beats label. If the fact negated by the defense is inside the offense definition, the prosecution must prove that fact beyond a reasonable doubt; the state cannot move that burden to the accused by calling the same fact a defense.

**Silver Key:** In a 3-vs-1 burden array, decide **who bears the burden** before comparing proof levels. The three defendant-burden answers all keep the burden on the wrong party.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** Clear and convincing evidence sounds like the “greatest burden” the state might constitutionally place on a defendant for a serious defense. The breaker is the burden holder: this answer keeps the consent issue on Daniel, even though lack of consent is part of the offense definition.

**B.** Beyond a reasonable doubt borrows the criminal-trial proof phrase and feels maximally protective. **Gold Key:** the phrase belongs to the prosecution’s burden on offense elements; this answer points the same proof level at the wrong party. **Silver Key:** burden holder comes before proof level.

**D.** Preponderance is familiar because defendants can sometimes bear that burden for true affirmative defenses. The breaker is that consent here negates the statutory no-consent element, so the familiar affirmative-defense default does not control.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The source row supplies BARMATRIX Q#14588, Criminal Law, the Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons topic, the four answer choices, measured pick rates, and an official key of D before the shuffle. 

The governing constitutional rule is stable: due process requires the prosecution to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every fact necessary to constitute the charged crime. *In re Winship* states that the Due Process Clause protects the accused from conviction except on proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged. ([Justia Law][1])

The element-versus-defense distinction confirms the result. *Mullaney v. Wilbur* held that a state could not shift to the defendant the burden of proving a fact whose absence was necessary to the charged crime. ([Legal Information Institute][2]) *Patterson v. New York* permits a defendant burden only for a separate affirmative defense that does not negate any fact the state must prove to convict; it also repeats that due process requires the prosecution to prove the elements included in the offense definition beyond a reasonable doubt. ([Justia Law][3])

The transformed statute keeps the same operative structure: the offense is defined by taking from an **unconsenting** person, while consent is also labeled a complete defense. The label does not move the burden. The credited outcome remains: the prosecution must prove lack of consent beyond a reasonable doubt.
