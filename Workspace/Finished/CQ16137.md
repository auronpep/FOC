## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

Barnabas is guilty. Accomplice liability reaches one who intentionally aids, abets, counsels, commands, or encourages another person's crime. Physical assistance is not required if the defendant's words are meant to spur the crime on and do so. Barnabas did exactly that: in the middle of the confrontation, he yelled for Daniel to kill the stranger, and Daniel immediately carried out the killing.

**Gold Key:** Words alone can satisfy accomplice liability when they are spoken with the purpose of encouraging the crime. The law does not require the accomplice to hold the weapon, touch the victim, or do any additional physical act.

**Silver Key:** When three answers begin "not guilty, because..." ask whether they are quietly adding extra elements. Here, the wrong answers add a physical-help requirement, a no-words-alone rule, or a denial of intent despite an express command to kill.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A.** The attraction is that criminal liability often feels too serious to rest on speech alone, so this answer sells the common myth that words can never be enough. The breaker is the accomplice rule itself: encouragement is one of the classic ways to aid or abet. A direct exhortation to kill during the encounter is not idle commentary; it is assistance by encouragement.

**B.** The attraction is the instinct that an accomplice must hand over a weapon, restrain the victim, or otherwise physically participate. The breaker is that accomplice liability does not impose a physical-help requirement. Verbal encouragement intended to bring about the crime is enough.

**C.** The attraction is that accomplice liability does require intent, so a student who spots the mens rea issue may worry Barnabas was only venting. The breaker is the fact pattern itself: Barnabas expressly told Daniel to kill the stranger in the heat of the encounter, and Daniel acted at once. That is affirmative evidence of intent, not an absence of it.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification AND Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule is accomplice liability for homicide. Under common-law accomplice principles and modern formulations, a defendant is liable when he intentionally encourages or assists another person's offense. The Model Penal Code states that a person is an accomplice if, "with the purpose of promoting or facilitating the commission of the offense," he "solicits" or "aids" the other person. MPC § 2.06(3)(a)(i)-(ii). The Supreme Court has likewise described aiding-and-abetting liability as requiring an affirmative act in furtherance of the offense together with the intent to facilitate it. *Rosemond v. United States*, 572 U.S. 65 (2014).

Barnabas's conduct fits that rule. He did not merely watch the confrontation. He urged Daniel to kill the stranger, and Daniel immediately did so. The verbal command supplied encouragement, and the command itself is strong evidence Barnabas meant for the killing to occur. Because Daniel committed murder and Barnabas intentionally encouraged that murder, Barnabas is liable as an accomplice to murder.

The distractors fail for the same reasons they fail in the original:

- No physical-help requirement exists for accomplice liability.
- Words alone can suffice when they are meant to encourage the crime.
- Intent is not missing here; it is supported by Barnabas's own command.

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome remain unchanged from the original row.
