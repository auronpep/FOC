## 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct. Gold Key:** A Miranda warning is not a force field around a later confession. If officers use coercive threats and conditions that overbear the suspect’s will, the confession is involuntary and cannot be used in the prosecution’s case-in-chief.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**D.** This choice sells the strongest familiar fact in the stem: Martha got warnings and understood them. **Silver Key:** warnings answer the Miranda layer, but this stem is built around the pressure layer.

## 8. Full black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

The legal rule and outcome are unchanged from the source row: a confession is involuntary when coercive police conduct overbears the suspect’s will under the totality of circumstances, and an involuntary confession is not admissible in the prosecution’s case-in-chief. The Supreme Court treated threats that a suspect would lose her children if she did not cooperate as coercive in *Lynumn v. Illinois*; the chosen variation preserves that same legal character by using threats against family custody and immediate family arrest. ([Justia Law][1])

The case also preserves the state-action/coercion requirement from *Colorado v. Connelly*: the coercion comes from investigators, not from a private pressure source. ([Justia Law][2]) In *Mincey v. Arizona*, the Court stated that criminal-trial use of an involuntary statement violates due process, which supports inadmissibility in the prosecution’s case-in-chief here. ([Justia Law][3]) The written-waiver distractor remains legally defective because *North Carolina v. Butler* holds that an explicit written or oral waiver is not invariably necessary. ([Justia Law][4])
