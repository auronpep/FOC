## 6. Full right-answer explanation
Yes, the court may do so. In a federal court sitting in diversity, the measurement of damages and the standard for determining whether a jury's compensatory award is excessive are governed by state substantive law under the Erie doctrine. When the court concludes the award exceeds the state standard, remittitur is the proper device: the court offers the plaintiff the choice between accepting a reduced amount or submitting to a new trial on damages. This procedure does not violate the Seventh Amendment. The reexamination clause prohibits a court from unilaterally substituting its own damages figure for the jury's; the plaintiff's consent (via acceptance of the remittitur) or a new trial satisfies the constitutional requirement.

**Gold Key:** Remittitur (plaintiff offered reduced award OR new trial) is constitutional; additur (defendant offered higher award OR new trial) is the version that collides with the Seventh Amendment reexamination clause when it increases a jury award.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations
A: Students are drawn to any choice that invokes the Seventh Amendment and jury-trial sanctity. The breaker is that remittitur with the new-trial option is the long-settled constitutional workaround precisely because it avoids unilateral judicial reexamination of the jury's factual damages determination. (Hetzel v. Prince William County, 523 U.S. 208 (1998).) The choice fails because it treats the safeguard as the violation.

C: The “shock the conscience” formulation is a common federal standard many students memorize as universal. The breaker is the explicit diversity trigger in the stem: when the federal court borrows state law for this substantive damages question, the state standard (whatever its verbal formulation) controls. The choice is not responsive to the Erie borrowing the stem sets up.

D: The unilateral-reduction option sounds like an efficient, judge-empowering move. The breaker is the reexamination clause: a court may not simply enter judgment for a lower sum the jury never awarded. The plaintiff must be given the new-trial exit ramp. This is the classic distinction the correct answer exploits.

## 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The credited outcome is correct and unchanged from the source row. In diversity cases, federal courts apply state law to the question of excessiveness of compensatory damages because the issue is outcome-determinative and substantive under Erie and its progeny (see Gasperini v. Center for Humanities, Inc., 518 U.S. 415 (1996), confirming that state standards for new-trial motions based on excessive verdicts control in diversity). Remittitur is the traditional common-law and Rule 59 device that permits a trial court to condition denial of a new-trial motion on the plaintiff's acceptance of a reduced award. The Supreme Court has made clear that a court may not enter a reduced judgment without affording the plaintiff the new-trial alternative; doing so violates the Seventh Amendment's reexamination clause. Hetzel v. Prince William County, 523 U.S. 208 (1998) (per curiam). Remittitur itself does not violate the Seventh Amendment; additur does when it increases an inadequate jury award. The source row's official key (A) and all legal substance are preserved.

Authority floor: Hetzel v. Prince William County, 523 U.S. 208 (1998); Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938) and Gasperini (state standard for verdict excessiveness in diversity).
