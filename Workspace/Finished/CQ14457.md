## 1. Final question

Bethany Craft Kits, a private company owned by Lydia, assembles Scripture-memory Advent craft kits. In a signed writing, Bethany agreed to buy from Martha’s Paper House all of the cardstock, ribbon, and sticker sheets that Bethany would require for the next twelve months. Martha’s Paper House agreed to fill those requirements and to sell the items at 12% off the prices posted in its online catalog, but it reserved the right to change the catalog prices during the year.

After the parties had ordered and delivered under the agreement for four months, Martha’s Paper House notified Bethany that future orders would be charged at full catalog prices with no 12% discount.

Does Bethany have a viable claim against Martha’s Paper House for breach of contract?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A buyer agrees in writing to buy all of its requirements for goods from a seller for a year; the seller promises a fixed discount from catalog prices but later withdraws the discount. Does the buyer have a breach claim?

## 3. Final answer choices

A. No, because Martha’s Paper House could, and did, revoke its offer as to later deliveries.

B. No, because no stated minimum quantity made Bethany’s promise illusory.

C. Yes, because Bethany’s agreement to buy all of its kit-supply requirements from Martha’s Paper House made the agreement enforceable.

D. Yes, because four months of performance by both sides made the agreement enforceable for the entire year.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.** Bethany gave a real promise: it agreed to buy all of its kit-supply requirements from Martha’s Paper House for the stated period. That makes the agreement enforceable as a requirements contract. Martha’s Paper House reserved the right to change its online catalog prices, but it did not reserve the right to erase the promised 12% discount from those prices.

**Gold Key:** An agreement to buy all of the buyer’s requirements for goods is a valid quantity term. Good faith supplies the quantity guardrail, so a separate minimum quantity is not required.

**Silver Key:** Read the array by time slice. Once the stem gives a signed agreement and months of performance, offer-revocation answers are aimed at the wrong stage; the live question is breach of a formed requirements contract.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** The trap turns future deliveries into future offers, so “revocation” sounds available. **Silver Key:** once the stem gives a signed writing and four months of performance, the question is not whether an open offer can be revoked; it is whether a formed requirements contract was breached.

**B.** The trap sells the common quantity-term myth: no number means no contract. **Gold Key:** requirements language supplies the quantity term; good faith polices the quantity, so a stated minimum is not required.

**D.** The trap reaches for a familiar rescue doctrine because performance by both sides feels like the reason the year should bind. The breaker is that part performance answers a different enforceability problem; here, the requirements promise already makes the agreement enforceable.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The source row identifies this as BarMatrix Q14457, a Contracts question on Performance, Breach, and Discharge, with the official key as B and the highest wrong-answer pick rate on the revocation distractor.  

The tested legal issue is unchanged: a written requirements contract for goods is enforceable, and the seller breaches by refusing to honor a promised discount term after formation. UCC § 2-306(1) treats a quantity measured by the buyer’s requirements as the buyer’s actual good-faith requirements, subject to the unreasonably-disproportionate guardrail; UCC § 2-306(2) also imposes best-efforts obligations in lawful exclusive-dealing arrangements. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) UCC § 1-304 imposes good faith in UCC performance and enforcement, and UCC § 2-305(2) requires a price fixed by one party to be fixed in good faith. ([Legal Information Institute][2]) UCC § 2-301 states the seller’s obligation to transfer and deliver, and the buyer’s obligation to accept and pay, in accordance with the contract. ([Legal Information Institute][3])

The supplier’s reserved right to change catalog prices does not include a reserved right to remove the separate discount from those prices. The credited outcome remains: the buyer has a viable breach claim.
