### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**B is correct.** To establish a prima facie case for intentional infliction of emotional distress (IIED), a plaintiff must prove: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct; (2) intent or recklessness as to the effect; (3) causation; and (4) severe emotional distress. Physical harm is not an element of the tort. Hannah, a dog breeder acting as a debt collector, went to Peter's home and loudly threatened him with criminal theft charges over a puppy sale, even after being told he was hospitalized and seriously ill. This aggressive, confrontational conduct directed at a recovering patient would likely be found extreme and outrageous. Peter suffered severe emotional distress as a direct result. The fact that the underlying debt was valid does not immunize a collector who uses extreme methods. Because all four elements are satisfied, Peter would prevail.

**Silver Key:** When you see a valid debt + aggressive collection + a plaintiff who suffered emotional distress, lock onto the question: "Was the method extreme or outrageous?" — the debt's validity is a red herring, not a bar.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (No, because the debt was valid and past due)** is attractive because students intuitively think that if someone legitimately owes money, the collector cannot be liable for trying to collect. The breaker is that IIED targets the *method* of collection, not the existence of the debt. Courts have specifically identified extreme business conduct by debt collectors as a classic illustration of extreme and outrageous behavior. The validity of the underlying obligation is irrelevant to whether the collector's methods cross the line.

**C (No, because Hannah's conduct created almost no risk of physical harm)** is attractive because it sounds like a plausible limiting principle — students may think that without risk of physical harm, the conduct cannot be serious enough for IIED. The breaker is that risk of physical harm is simply not an element of the tort. The elements are extreme and outrageous conduct, intent/recklessness, causation, and severe emotional distress. A plaintiff need not show any risk of physical harm.

**D (No, because Peter did not suffer physical harm)** is attractive because many students conflate severe emotional distress with physical injury — they assume that without physical harm, the distress cannot be "severe" enough. The breaker is that while physical manifestations can serve as *evidence* of severe emotional distress, physical harm is not a required element. Severe emotional distress alone, caused by extreme and outrageous conduct, is sufficient.

### 8. Black-Letter Legal Verification

The four elements of IIED are well-established in the Restatement (Second) of Torts § 46: (1) the defendant acts; (2) the conduct is extreme and outrageous; (3) the defendant acts intentionally or recklessly; (4) the plaintiff suffers severe emotional distress. *See* Restatement (Second) of Torts § 46(1) (Am. L. Inst. 1965). The tort does not require physical harm. *See, e.g., Agis v. Howard Johnson Co.*, 371 Mass. 140 (1976) (recognizing IIED claim where plaintiff suffered no physical injury); *Hicks v. Casablanca Records*, 464 F. Supp. 426 (S.D.N.Y. 1978) (same). The Supreme Court has acknowledged the tort's validity while noting First Amendment limits in *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2010). Debt-collector harassment is a well-recognized category of IIED. *See, e.g., Lashley v. Bowman*, 561 F.3d 456 (4th Cir. 2009).

#### Wrong Answer Recovery Paths

**If a student picked A (valid debt):**

- **Recovery step:** Re-read the call. The call asks about IIED liability, not debt validity. Ask: "Even if the debt is valid, can the collector's *methods* be tortious?"

**If a student picked C (no risk of physical harm):**
- **Skipped move:** The student invented a "risk of physical harm" element that does not exist in IIED.
- **Recovery step:** Run the IIED checklist from memory. The elements are: extreme/outrageous conduct, intent/recklessness, causation, severe emotional distress. "Risk of physical harm" is not on the list.

**If a student picked D (no physical harm):**
- **Skipped move:** The student conflated physical harm with severe emotional distress.
- **Recovery step:** Ask: "Is physical harm on the IIED checklist?" No — it is not. Physical harm can be *evidence* of severe emotional distress, but it is not a required element. The question stem says Peter suffered severe emotional distress — that is enough.

#### Review Truth
