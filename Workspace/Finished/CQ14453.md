## 1. Final Question

Naomi operates a small farm supply store in a rural town. Her adult son, Timothy, needed a used tractor for his landscaping business but had only $2,500 available. Naomi wrote to Timothy: "Bring me your $2,500 and the old flatbed trailer behind the barn, and I'll sell you the red tractor I use to move hay around the property." Timothy was thrilled — he hauled the trailer to his mother's store that same afternoon and handed over the $2,500 in cash. As both Naomi and Timothy knew, the red tractor had a fair market value of roughly $9,000, and the trailer was worth about $1,200. Before Naomi could arrange delivery of the tractor, her store manager — unaware of the deal with Timothy — sold and delivered the red tractor to a customer for $9,500. Neither the manager nor the customer knew about Timothy's payment. Naomi later tendered a return of the $2,500 plus the trailer, but Timothy rejected the tender. Does Timothy have an action against Naomi for breach of contract?

**A.** No, because Naomi's store manager delivered the tractor to a customer, making it impossible for Naomi to perform her promise.

**B.** No, because the $2,500 payment, combined with a used trailer, was grossly inadequate consideration for a tractor worth $9,000.

**C.** Yes, because Timothy's payment of $2,500 and delivery of the trailer was bargained-for consideration for Naomi's promise.

**D.** Yes, because a parent's moral obligation to help an adult child in need is sufficient consideration to support a promise.

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The only requirement for valid consideration is that it be bargained for — that is, the performance or return promise must be sought by the promisor in exchange for the promise and given by the promisee in exchange for it. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 71. Timothy paid $2,500 and delivered a trailer, both at Naomi's request, in exchange for her promise to sell the tractor. That is textbook bargained-for exchange. Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 79, a court will not inquire into the adequacy of consideration once the bargained-for requirement is met. The fact that the tractor was worth roughly $9,000 while the total consideration was around $3,700 is legally irrelevant — the law does not require equivalence in value. *See also Hamer v. Sidway*, 124 N.Y. 538 (1891) (forbearance from legal activity valid consideration regardless of size). Because Timothy suffered a legal detriment he was not otherwise obligated to suffer, and Naomi received a benefit she bargained for, the contract is enforceable. The subsequent sale to a third party may affect Timothy's damages (e.g., limiting specific performance), but it does not undo the formation of the contract.

**Gold Key:** The adequacy-of-consideration shield — Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 79 provides that if the requirement of consideration is met, there is no additional requirement of equivalence in the values exchanged. A peppercorn can support a promise to convey a mansion.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original D) is incorrect** because it mistakes a performance obstacle for a formation defense. The store manager's sale to a customer may make it impossible for Naomi to deliver the tractor, but impossibility of performance is a defense to *breach*, not a negation of *contract formation*. The contract was formed the moment Timothy tendered the $2,500 and trailer in exchange for Naomi's promise. The proper remedy question — whether Timothy can get damages, specific performance, or restitution — is a separate inquiry. The call asks whether Timothy has an action for breach, not whether he can get the tractor itself. A contract that becomes impossible to perform is still a contract; the non-performing party is liable for breach unless a true impossibility defense applies (which requires, among other things, that the impossibility not be self-induced — and a manager's unauthorized sale is attributable to the promisor's own business).

**Silver Key:** Separate formation from performance. A contract forms when offer, acceptance, and consideration align. Whether the promisor can later deliver the goods is a performance question, not a formation question. If the call asks "action for breach," the student must first confirm the contract exists before analyzing the breach.

**B (original C) is incorrect** because it invokes the very rule the law rejects: that consideration must be adequate in value. This is the classic peppercorn trap. Courts do not weigh the economic fairness of a bargain. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 79 explicitly states there is no requirement of "equivalence in the values exchanged." Timothy gave up $2,500 and a trailer — both things he was not legally obligated to give — at Naomi's request. That is sufficient consideration. The gross disparity in value does not void the contract; it is simply the product of a family deal.

**Gold Key:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 79 — adequacy is not required. The student who reaches for "inadequate consideration" as a formation defense has misremembered the rule. The law protects even lopsided bargains.

**D (original B) is incorrect** because moral obligation, standing alone, is not consideration. A parent's love for a child, or a sense of duty to help family, does not constitute the bargained-for exchange that contract law requires. While some jurisdictions recognize moral obligation as a basis for enforcing a written promise (e.g., under some state statutes or the Restatement's promissory-estoppel framework), the MBE rule is clear: moral obligation is not consideration. The contract here is enforceable not because of any moral bond but because Timothy gave legal detriment — cash and a trailer — in exchange for Naomi's promise.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Tested rule:** Bargained-for consideration and the non-requirement of adequacy.

**Governing authority:**
- Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 71 (1981): To constitute consideration, a performance or return promise must be bargained for — sought by the promisor in exchange for the promise and given by the promisee in exchange for it.
- Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 79 (1981): If the requirement of consideration is met, there is no additional requirement of (a) gain, advantage, or benefit to the promisor or loss, disadvantage, or detriment to the promisee; or (b) equivalence in the values exchanged; or (c) "mutuality of obligation."
- *Hamer v. Sidway*, 124 N.Y. 538, 38 N.E. 27 (1891): A promisee's forbearance from activities he has a legal right to engage in (drinking, smoking, gambling) constitutes valid consideration even though the forbearance benefits the promisee. The court does not inquire into the economic value of the forbearance.
- *Chappell & Co Ltd v Nestle Co Ltd* [1960] AC 87 (HL): A peppercorn (or chocolate wrappers, in this case) can constitute good consideration even if the promisee does not value it. The House of Lords affirmed that the law does not examine the adequacy of consideration.

**Verification of unchanged outcome:** The original question tests whether a promise supported by uneven-but-bargained-for consideration is enforceable. The credited answer (A in the original, C in the variant) correctly states that bargained-for consideration exists and adequacy is irrelevant. The variant preserves this exact legal issue. The dominant trap (impossibility of performance as a formation defense) and the secondary trap (inadequacy of consideration) are both preserved. The credited outcome is unchanged.

**Attorney review:** Not required. The black-letter rule is well-settled and the cited authority is primary law.

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

#### Program Frame

This item tests the bargained-for consideration rule — the foundational requirement that a contract requires an exchange, not a gift. The key insight is that courts do not examine the fairness of a bargain. A promise supported by even a tiny legal detriment is enforceable. This case study will walk you through the C3 method to solve this question: CUT the choices that misstate the law, CLASH any survivors, and CALL the answer.

#### Student Access Test

Before we begin, confirm you can answer this without outside knowledge: If someone promises to sell you a bicycle in exchange for your old skateboard, and the bicycle is worth far more than the skateboard, is there a contract? Yes — because you gave something you didn't have to give, at their request, in exchange for their promise. The size of the exchange doesn't matter. That's the rule this question tests.

#### Question Data

Naomi operates a farm supply store. Her son Timothy needs a tractor. Naomi writes: "Bring me your $2,500 and the old flatbed trailer, and I'll sell you the red tractor." Timothy pays and delivers the trailer. The tractor is worth $9,000. Before delivery, Naomi's store manager sells the tractor to a customer. Timothy rejects Naomi's tender of return and sues for breach.

**Call:** Does Timothy have an action against Naomi for breach of contract?

#### Distilled Core Question

A mother promises to sell her son a tractor in exchange for $2,500 and a used trailer. The tractor is worth far more. Before delivery, the tractor is sold to a third party. Does the son have an action for breach of contract?

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks whether Timothy has an action for *breach of contract*. This means we must first determine whether a contract exists, and then whether it was breached. The key formation issue is whether the consideration was valid. The key performance issue is whether the third-party sale excuses non-delivery.

**Prediction:** The contract likely exists because Timothy gave something of value at Naomi's request. The third-party sale is a breach, not a formation defect.

#### Trigger Facts

1. **Timothy paid $2,500 and delivered a trailer at Naomi's request** → This is the bargained-for exchange. It establishes consideration.
2. **The tractor was worth $9,000; total consideration was ~$3,700** → This triggers the adequacy trap. Students may think the deal is too lopsided to be enforceable.
3. **Naomi's store manager sold the tractor to a customer** → This triggers the impossibility trap. Students may think the contract can't exist if performance is impossible.
4. **Neither the manager nor the customer knew about Timothy's deal** → This establishes the third party acted in good faith, but it's irrelevant to formation.
5. **Timothy rejected the tender of return** → This shows Timothy affirms the contract.

**Governing law type:** RULE (the bargained-for consideration rule is a fixed legal test, not a flexible standard)
**Deciding phase:** CUT — all three distractors fail on visible structural grounds
**Method class:** hard_structural — every choice can be eliminated using only the call, the stem facts, and the answer text itself

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "No, because Naomi's store manager delivered the tractor to a customer, making it impossible for Naomi to perform her promise."**

1. **C3 Signal:** The choice says the contract cannot exist because performance became impossible. But the call asks about *breach of contract* — breach requires a contract to exist. The choice conflates formation with performance.
2. **Student Label:** "Impossibility of performance negates contract formation."
3. **True/Responsive Version:** A correct version would say: "Timothy may have an action for breach, but his remedy may be limited to damages rather than specific performance, because the tractor was sold to a third party."
4. **Lawyer Confirmation:** Impossibility of performance is a defense to breach under Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 261-264, not a negation of formation. A contract that becomes impossible to perform is still a contract. Moreover, the impossibility here was caused by Naomi's own employee, which makes it a breach, not an excuse.

**Choice B: "No, because the $2,500 payment, combined with a used trailer, was grossly inadequate consideration for a tractor worth $9,000."**

1. **C3 Signal:** The choice claims the consideration was too small. But the law does not require consideration to be adequate in value. The choice states a rule that does not exist.
2. **Student Label:** "Inadequate consideration defeats the contract."
3. **True/Responsive Version:** A correct version would say: "The consideration was adequate as a matter of law, because the court does not inquire into the adequacy of the exchange."
4. **Lawyer Confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 79 explicitly states there is no requirement of "equivalence in the values exchanged." The peppercorn theory — affirmed in *Chappell & Co Ltd v Nestle Co Ltd* [1960] AC 87 — holds that even a token payment can support a valuable promise.

**Choice C: "Yes, because Timothy's payment of $2,500 and delivery of the trailer was bargained-for consideration for Naomi's promise."**

1. **C3 Signal:** Timothy paid $2,500 and delivered a trailer — both at Naomi's request — in exchange for her promise to sell the tractor. That is bargained-for exchange. The law does not require the values to be equal.
2. **Student Label:** "Bargained-for consideration exists."
3. **True/Responsive Version:** This choice is already true and responsive. It correctly identifies the legal basis for the contract.
4. **Lawyer Confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 71: Consideration requires a performance or return promise that is bargained for. Timothy's payment and trailer delivery satisfy this test. The contract is enforceable.

**Choice D: "Yes, because a parent's moral obligation to help an adult child in need is sufficient consideration to support a promise."**

1. **C3 Signal:** The choice claims a parent's moral duty is enough to enforce a promise. But moral obligation is not bargained-for exchange — it is sentiment, not contract.
2. **Student Label:** "Moral obligation is sufficient consideration."
3. **True/Responsive Version:** A correct version would say: "The contract is enforceable because of the bargained-for exchange, not because of any moral obligation."
4. **Lawyer Confirmation:** Moral obligation is not consideration under contract law. The MBE rule is clear: moral obligation alone does not constitute consideration. The contract here is enforceable because of the bargained-for exchange, not the family bond.

After cutting A, B, and D, the residual is **C** — the only choice that is both true and responsive. C correctly identifies that Timothy's payment and trailer delivery was bargained-for consideration, which is all the law requires.
