## 1. Final question (chosen variation)

A defendant is being tried for the murder of Brother Paul, a Christian bush pilot who for more than a decade flew medical supplies, food, and portions of Scripture into remote mountain villages for a small missions organization. Nine years ago, Brother Paul took off from a dirt airstrip on a routine supply flight, radioed his position as planned, and was never heard from again. Extensive air and ground searches found no wreckage and no trace of him. The prosecutor has presented strong circumstantial evidence that the defendant sabotaged the plane or attacked Brother Paul after a forced landing. To help establish the fact of Brother Paul's death, the prosecutor has requested that the judge give the following instruction, based on a recognized presumption in the jurisdiction: “A person missing and not heard from in the last seven years shall be presumed to be deceased.” Is the instruction proper?

**Answer A:** Yes, because the defendant has a chance to rebut the presumption by offering evidence that Brother Paul is alive or has been heard from in the last seven years.

**Answer B:** No, because the fact that someone has not been heard from in seven years does not necessarily lead to a conclusion that the person is dead.

**Answer C:** No, because mandatory presumptions are not allowed against a criminal defendant on an element of the charged crime.

**Answer D:** Yes, because it expresses a rational conclusion that the jury should be required to accept.

## 6. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** In a criminal case the prosecution must prove every element of the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt. A jury instruction that tells the jury a person missing for seven years "shall be presumed to be deceased" creates a mandatory presumption on the fact-of-death element. The word "shall" makes the presumption mandatory rather than permissive. A mandatory presumption on an element shifts the burden of proof to the defendant or relieves the prosecution of its burden, violating the defendant's due process rights. See Francis v. Franklin, 471 U.S. 307 (1985); Sandstrom v. Montana, 442 U.S. 510 (1979); In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970).

**Gold Key:** A jury instruction using "shall be presumed" (or equivalent mandatory language) on an element in a criminal case is unconstitutional even if the underlying inference is rational and even if the defendant is told he may rebut it. The defect is the mandatory force of the instruction itself.

**Silver Key (call focus):** When the call asks whether a presumption instruction is "proper," the decisive cut is whether the instruction is mandatory (forbidden on an element against a criminal defendant) or merely permissive (usually allowed if rational). The word "shall" is the tell that makes it mandatory.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A (dominant trap — rebuttable saves it).** This choice is attractive because it feels fair: the defendant still gets to put on evidence that the victim is alive, so the instruction seems harmless or even defendant-friendly. The breaker is that rebuttability does not cure the constitutional defect. Once the judge tells the jury the person "shall be presumed" dead, the prosecution has effectively been relieved of proving the element beyond a reasonable doubt; the burden has shifted. Rebuttable mandatory presumptions on elements are still unconstitutional. (Tested_choice: this is the trap the Gold Key directly defeats.)

**B.** This choice is attractive because it sounds like a safe, common-sense attack on the inference itself ("seven years missing doesn't always mean dead"). The breaker is that the constitutional problem is not the rationality of the inference. A presumption or inference does not have to be certain to be permissible; it only needs to be rational and more likely than not. The instruction fails for a different reason: it is mandatory and therefore impermissibly shifts the burden on an element in a criminal case.

**D.** This choice is attractive because it sounds lawyerly and balanced ("if the conclusion is rational, the jury should be required to accept it"). The breaker is that even a rational, well-supported conclusion may not be forced on the jury through a mandatory presumption instruction when the conclusion is an element the prosecution must prove beyond a reasonable doubt. Due process forbids the judge from removing that element from the prosecution's burden.

## 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested black-letter rule is unchanged from the source row: In criminal cases, jury instructions that create mandatory presumptions on an element of the charged crime violate the Due Process Clause because they relieve the prosecution of its burden to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt or shift that burden to the defendant. See Francis v. Franklin, 471 U.S. 307 (1985) (mandatory rebuttable presumption on intent element unconstitutional even when jury was told the presumption could be rebutted); Sandstrom v. Montana, 442 U.S. 510 (1979) (instruction that "the law presumes that a person intends the ordinary consequences of his voluntary acts" created unconstitutional mandatory presumption); In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970) (prosecution must prove every fact necessary to constitute the crime beyond a reasonable doubt).
