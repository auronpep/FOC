### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The retreat center owed Mary **reasonable care, including reasonable inspection for hazards it should discover**. Mary is an invitee: she paid admission to enter the lodge for a purpose that benefits the retreat center (a ticketed event the center runs for a fee). A landowner who invites the public onto the premises for a paid event owes the invitee the broader reasonable-inspection duty, not the narrower "warn only of known hazards" rule that applies to licensees. The loose floorboard had been rocking for several days, no inspection schedule existed, and reasonable care would have discovered and corrected the condition. **Gold Key:** invitees get the inspection duty; the "warn only of known" rule is the licensee standard. **Silver Key:** when the call asks "what duty did the possessor owe," first classify the entrant (invitee / licensee / trespasser), then match the duty to the entrant's status.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Strict liability for any injury on the premises.**

This is the over-broad-rule trap. The student is reaching for the strictest possible standard because a paying visitor was hurt. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: invitee status creates a duty of *reasonable care*, not automatic liability. Strict liability is a separate doctrine that requires an abnormally dangerous activity or a product in the stream of commerce; neither is in play on a routine premises-slip-and-fall.

**C. Only a duty to warn about hazards it actually knew about.**

This is the **dominant trap** — the classic invitee-vs-licensee conflation. The student half-remembers that "landowners only have to warn," and picks the narrower licensee rule. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: the warn-only-of-known rule is the **licensee** standard, not the **invitee** standard. Mary is a paying attendee, which makes her an invitee, and the duty that follows is the broader reasonable-inspection duty.

**D. No duty because the retreat center did not create the loose board.**

This is the "no creation, no duty" trap. The student assumes the duty runs only to hazards the possessor itself caused. The breaker is that the invitee duty is keyed to **discoverable** hazards, not to hazards the possessor created. A natural deterioration of a porch floorboard over several days, with no inspection schedule, is exactly the kind of hazard the invitee duty obliges the possessor to find and fix.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18135, a Torts item involving a museum that charged admission, a loose floor tile that had been rocking for several hours, no inspection schedule, and a visitor who tripped. The official key is A: reasonable care, including reasonable inspection for hazards the possessor should discover. The lb_rule_reference states: "A possessor owes invitees reasonable care, including reasonable inspection for hazards the possessor should discover."

For lawyer confirmation: a landowner / possessor of land owes different duties to entrants depending on their status. An **invitee** is a person who enters the land for a purpose that benefits the possessor, typically a member of the public invited to enter for a commercial or public-admission purpose. The Restatement (Second) of Torts § 332 defines an invitee. To invitees, the possessor owes the duty to exercise **reasonable care** to keep the premises reasonably safe, which includes the duty to **inspect** for hidden dangers and either remove them or warn of them. See Restatement (Second) of Torts § 343 (dangerous conditions known or discoverable). The narrower "warn only of known hazards" rule is the standard for **licensees** — entrants who enter for their own purpose with the possessor's permission. See Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 330, 341. The strict-liability answer is a separate doctrine (Restatement (Second) of Torts § 519, abnormally dangerous activities; or products liability) and is not the default rule for ordinary premises liability. The "no creation, no duty" answer misstates the rule because the invitee duty is keyed to **discoverable** hazards regardless of who caused them. The transformed outcome is identical to the original: the retreat center owed Mary the reasonable-care-including-inspection duty.

The transformed call ("What duty did the retreat center owe Mary?") preserves the original call's function (asking for the duty the possessor owes an entrant) and does not name the governing doctrine.

## program_frame

This is a Torts premises-liability question that turns on the duty a possessor owes an entrant. The possessor (the retreat center) invited the public onto the premises for a paid event, which makes the entrant (Mary) an invitee. ### A. Strict liability for any injury on the premises.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Overclaim cut. The choice asserts liability for any injury on the premises, which is broader than the invitee reasonable-care standard.

### D. No duty because the retreat center did not create the loose board.
