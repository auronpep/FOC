# BarMatrix Question 16040 - Analysis

**Subject:** Criminal Law and Procedure  
**Outline Code:** 72060201  
**Subtopic:** General Principles  
**Outline:** Self-defense  

## Question Summary

**Question 16040** presents a self-defense scenario where the defendant, after being confronted by an apparent armed robber, mistakenly shoots what turns out to be a police officer disguised as a civilian with no weapon. The key issue is whether the defendant's mistaken belief about the threat justifies self-defense.

## Legal Issue

Does a mistaken belief about the existence of a deadly threat constitute a valid self-defense claim when that mistake is reasonable under the circumstances?

## Relevant Law and Authority

### Courvoisier v. Raymond (1896)
Colorado Supreme Court established the crucial precedent: a defendant can be justified in using force if they had a reasonable but mistaken belief that they were facing an imminent deadly threat. This landmark case specifically held that:

> "The standard is not whether the danger was real, but whether a 'reasonable man' in the same position would have perceived a threat and acted similarly."

This case directly addresses the situation where the defendant mistakenly shoots someone who is not actually a threat but appears to be so under the circumstances. The Court ruled that a "reasonable mistake is a valid consideration in a self-defense claim."

**Legal Standard:** The test is objective - would a reasonable person in the defendant's situation, with the same knowledge, have believed force was necessary?

## Analysis of the Facts

**Supporting Factors for Reasonable Mistake:**

1. **Time and Location:** 3 A.M. in a high-crime area with recent thefts
2. **Presence of Weapon:** Defendant actually carried a large kitchen knife
3. **Perceived Threat:** Low voice growl, demand for wallet, gun pointed at head
4. **Defendant's State:** Coming from closing his business, experiencing genuine fear
7. **Objective Circumstances:** The perceived threat appeared real and imminent

**Key Legal Considerations:**

- **Subjective Belief:** The defendant genuinely believed he was facing a deadly threat
- **Reasonableness:** Under the circumstances, was this belief reasonable?
- **Immediacy:** The threat appeared immediate and unavoidable
- **Proportionality:** Deadly force was appropriate for perceived deadly threat

## Correct Answer Analysis

**Answer B: "not guilty, because the defendant's belief that the 'robber' would shoot him was reasonable."

**Why B is Correct:**

This answer correctly applies the Courvoisier v. Raymond principle. The defendant's belief, while factually mistaken, was reasonable given:

1. **Timing (3 A.M.)**: Late hour increases perceived danger
2. **Recent Crimes**: Pattern of thefts in the area creates reasonable fear
3. **Weapon Display**: Seeing what appears to be a gun triggers instinctive response
4. **Confrontational Context**: Direct demand with weapon pointed at head
5. **Defendant's Vulnerability**: Alone, after closing business

**Why Other Answers Are Wrong:**

**A:** Too narrow - ignores that mistaken self-defense is valid when reasonable
**C:** Incorrect - focuses on defendant's duty to verify rather than his reasonable belief
**D:** Incorrect - defines reasonableness too strictly; the belief was reasonable

## Application to Bar Exam Rationale

This question tests the distinction between perfect vs. imperfect self-defense and the importance of the objective reasonableness test in mistaken belief cases. The correct answer reflects current law that protects honest mistakes when those mistakes are reasonable under the circumstances.

**Key Takeaway:** Self-defense protects reasonable mistakes, not just correct perceptions. The law recognizes that in high-stress situations, people must make quick judgments based on what appears to be true, not what actually is true.

## Verdict

**B is the correct answer.** The defendant should be found not guilty because his mistaken belief about the 'robber' having a gun was reasonable under the circumstances. This follows the precedent established in Courvoisier v. Raymond and correctly applies the legal standard for self-defense with mistaken beliefs.
