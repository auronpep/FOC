## 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. False imprisonment is not just a locked door or latched gate. The plaintiff must be conscious of the confinement while it is happening, or suffer harm from the confinement. Daniel slept through the entire twelve-minute latch-up, the gate was unlatched before he woke, and he suffered no physical harm.

Gold Key: false imprisonment protects freedom from a confinement the plaintiff experiences, so a sleeping plaintiff generally needs either awareness during the confinement or harm from it.

Silver Key: when the choices split between "the boundary was locked" and "the plaintiff did not know and was not harmed," ask what the plaintiff experienced while the boundary was locked. Later discovery is not the same time stamp.

Because Daniel did not know about the confinement while it existed and was not harmed by it, he is not likely to prevail.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

D is the dominant trap because it grabs the most visible fact: the gate was latched with Daniel inside. That fact gives you confinement, but it does not finish false imprisonment. Daniel slept through the latch-up and suffered no harm, so the locked-boundary fact alone is not enough.

A sells the student who treats later knowledge as if it can substitute for awareness during the confinement. C sounds clean because arrests are a familiar false-imprisonment setting. The breaker is that private actors can commit false imprisonment too. The claim fails here because Daniel was unaware while confined and unharmed, not because the studio was private.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited outcome is unchanged from the source item: the plaintiff is not likely to prevail on false imprisonment when he slept through the brief confinement, was released before waking, and suffered no harm from the confinement.

The governing bar rule is reflected in Restatement (Second) of Torts section 35(1)(c): false-imprisonment liability requires that the person confined be conscious of the confinement or harmed by it. Cornell Wex's overview also frames false imprisonment as intentional restraint in a bounded area and lists plaintiff awareness as part of the prima facie case. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/false_imprisonment. Parvi v. City of Kingston, 41 N.Y.2d 553, 362 N.E.2d 960 (1977), is consistent with that timing distinction: later inability to remember does not defeat a claim if the plaintiff was aware at the time, but awareness must exist during the confinement. See https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/parvi-v-city-of-894622920. The Restatement excerpt is also available through H2O Open Casebook at https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/9130-torts/resources/10.3.1-restatement-second-of-torts-on-false-imprisonment/.

Applying that rule, Daniel had a complete physical boundary for twelve minutes, but he was asleep throughout the confinement, the gate was opened before he woke, and he suffered no physical harm. Later learning about the event from a camera clip does not supply awareness during the confinement. The public-official answer is also wrong because false imprisonment is not limited to arrests by government officers.
