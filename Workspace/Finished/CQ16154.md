### 7. Right-Answer Explanation

**Gold Key: Castle Doctrine Exception** — Even in jurisdictions that impose a general duty to retreat before using deadly force, *no court requires retreat when the defender is in his own home or curtilage*. The castle doctrine eliminates the retreat obligation entirely on one's own property.

(B) is correct because Peter satisfies every element of self-defense and faced no duty to retreat. (1) Daniel presented unlawful force — grabbing a hatchet and threatening to kill Peter. (2) Peter reasonably believed he faced imminent death or great bodily harm — a hatchet-wielding assailant advancing while making death threats creates an unmistakable lethal threat. (3) Peter used no more force than he reasonably believed necessary — he fired *toward* Daniel to frighten, not to strike. That the bullet unpredictably hit and killed Daniel does not retroactively make the force "unreasonable" — the standard is the defender's reasonable belief at the moment, not hindsight. Critically, Peter was in his own barn — his property — so even though he had an open door and a clear escape route, he had **no duty to retreat** under the castle doctrine. Self-defense is a complete defense to murder, so Peter must be acquitted.

### 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A (original B) — Dominant Trap**: *"Convicted, because he had a clear opportunity and duty to retreat."* This is powerful because the open barn door is right there in the facts — retreat was physically possible, so it *feels* like the law should require it. The trap conflates factual possibility with legal obligation. **Gold Key: Castle Doctrine Exception** — the castle doctrine means that even when retreat is factually possible, there is *no legal duty* to retreat when you are on your own property. The choice states the exact opposite of law that applies across all U.S. jurisdictions. Silver Key: the moment you see "his home/barn/property" paired with "deadly force," lock in "no duty to retreat" — the castle doctrine controls over the general retreat rule.

**Choice C (original A)**: *"Convicted, because the use of deadly force was unreasonable under the circumstances."* This tempts students who think "a gun against a hatchet is overkill" or who focus on the good aim. The breaker: Peter did not shoot *at* Daniel to kill or wound — he fired to frighten. The force he intended (a warning shot) was proportional. The reasonableness standard evaluates the defender's belief about the *force needed*, not the actual outcome. A shot fired in the direction of an attacker to ward off a lethal hatchet charge is within the range of reasonable responses. Outcome luck (the bullet finding its mark) does not criminalize an otherwise reasonable act.

**Choice D (original C)**: *"Acquitted, because he did not intend to kill Daniel."* This is tempting because the facts explicitly say Peter fired only to scare. The trap: it assumes intent-to-kill is the *only* mens rea for murder. It isn't. Firing a rifle in the direction of another person — even to frighten — can constitute **depraved-heart murder** (extreme indifference to the value of human life) if the act proves fatal. The correct answer cannot rely on lack of intent to kill, because murder does not require that specific intent. The real defense here is self-defense, not absence of intent. **This choice also cites the wrong doctrine** — it reaches the correct outcome (acquittal) but for a reason that would fail if self-defense were unavailable.

### 9. Black-Letter Verification & Legal Authority

**Rule**: Under the common law and the Model Penal Code (MPC § 3.04), the use of deadly force in self-defense is justified when: (1) the actor is confronted with unlawful force; (2) the actor reasonably believes he is threatened with imminent death or great bodily harm; and (3) the force used is no greater than the actor reasonably believes necessary to repel the threat. Self-defense is a complete defense to murder, meaning it results in acquittal, not merely mitigation.

**Castle doctrine**: Under both the common law and the MPC, a person in his own home has **no duty to retreat** before using deadly force, even if retreat is factually possible and safe. This is a universal rule — even jurisdictions that impose a general duty to retreat in public carve out the castle doctrine exception for the home and its curtilage. *Semayne's Case* (1604) 6 Co. Rep. 194 ("the house of every one is to him as his castle and fortress"); MPC § 3.04(b)(ii)(a); Cornell LII, *Wex*: Castle Doctrine; Wikipedia, *Castle doctrine* (accessed 2026).

**Application to this item**: Daniel unlawfully threatened Peter with lethal force (hatchet + death threats). Peter reasonably feared imminent death. Peter used the minimum force he believed necessary — a warning shot. Peter was in his own barn (curtilage), so no duty to retreat applied. All three elements satisfied + castle doctrine applies → self-defense complete → acquittal.

**Choice A: "Convicted, because he had a clear opportunity and duty to retreat."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal**: The open barn door is right there — retreat was physically possible. This makes the choice *feel* right. 3. **What a true/responsive version would look like**: This choice would be correct if Peter were in a public place in a duty-to-retreat jurisdiction AND retreat were safely possible. But he's in his own barn — his curtilage — so the castle doctrine eliminates the retreat duty entirely.

**Choice B: "Acquitted, because he was acting in self-defense and had no duty to retreat."**

**Choice C: "Convicted, because the use of deadly force was unreasonable under the circumstances."**

**Choice D: "Acquitted, because he did not intend to kill Daniel."**

4. **Lawyer confirmation**: Depraved-heart murder (extreme indifference to the value of human life) does not require intent to kill. Firing a rifle in the direction of another person can satisfy this mens rea.
