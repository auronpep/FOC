### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** In *New York v. Quarles*, 467 U.S. 649 (1984), the Supreme Court recognized a public safety exception to the Miranda warning requirement. When officers face an objectively reasonable need to protect the public from immediate danger, they may ask narrowly tailored questions to address that danger without first administering Miranda warnings. Here, Deputy Paul apprehended a robbery suspect in a warehouse full of workers while her empty holster showed a loaded firearm was unsecured nearby. That is the paradigmatic *Quarles* scenario — an immediate, objectively reasonable threat to public safety. Paul's single question was narrowly directed at locating the weapon. Because the public safety exception applies, there was no Miranda violation, and both the statement and the pistol are admissible.

**Gold Key:** The *Quarles* public safety exception is triggered by an objectively reasonable need to protect the public (or officers) from immediate danger. When it applies, no Miranda violation occurs at all — the statement and any physical fruits are fully admissible. The exception is narrow: the question must be reasonably prompted by a concern for public safety, not a pretext to elicit incriminating statements.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (dominant trap):** This choice is attractive because it states the general Miranda rule correctly — a suspect in custody was questioned without warnings, and the general rule does require suppression of unwarned custodial statements. The trap is that it stops at the general rule and never asks whether an exception applies. The *Quarles* public safety exception means there was no Miranda violation in the first place, so the suppression remedy never activates. **Gold Key:** Always ask — before applying the Miranda suppression default, check whether an exception (public safety, routine booking, etc.) defeats the premise that a violation occurred.

**D:** This choice is attractive because *United States v. Patane*, 542 U.S. 630 (2004), is indeed good law: physical evidence derived from a Miranda violation need not be suppressed. But reaching *Patane* here skips the threshold question. *Patane* only matters *after* you've established that a Miranda violation occurred. Under *Quarles*, there was no violation — the public safety exception applied — so *Patane* is irrelevant. Choosing this answer means you identified a real doctrine but applied it at the wrong analytical step.

**B:** This choice is attractive because voluntariness *is* a real constitutional requirement under the Due Process Clause, and students know that involuntary confessions are inadmissible. But voluntariness and Miranda compliance are separate inquiries. A statement can be fully voluntary and still be excluded for a Miranda violation. The correct basis for admissibility here is the *Quarles* public safety exception, not voluntariness. Relying on voluntariness alone is an incomplete and misleading analysis that conflates two distinct constitutional tests.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** *New York v. Quarles*, 467 U.S. 649 (1984), established that the Fifth Amendment does not require suppression of statements obtained without Miranda warnings when the questioning was prompted by an objectively reasonable concern for public safety. The Court held that the Miranda warnings are a prophylactic rule, not a constitutional right in themselves, and that the cost of losing probative evidence outweighs the marginal benefit of the warnings when public safety is at immediate risk. The exception applies when: (1) the officer reasonably believes there is an immediate threat to public safety; (2) the questioning is narrowly tailored to address that threat; and (3) the questioning is not primarily aimed at eliciting incriminating evidence.

**Application:** Deputy Paul had objective grounds to believe a loaded firearm was unsecured in a warehouse with workers present. The empty holster on the handcuffed suspect confirmed the threat. Paul asked a single, narrowly tailored question: "Where's the gun?" This is the core *Quarles* fact pattern. The public safety exception applies; no Miranda violation occurred; both the statement and the pistol are admissible.

**Authority:** *New York v. Quarles*, 467 U.S. 649 (1984) (leading case); *United States v. Patane*, 542 U.S. 630 (2004) (physical evidence from unwarned but voluntary statements admissible, but irrelevant here because no violation occurred); *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436 (1966) (general rule).

**Verification:** The tested rule (Quarles public safety exception), legal issue (admissibility of unwarned custodial statement and derivative physical evidence), and credited outcome (both admissible) are unchanged from the original. Research confirms the official key is correct.
