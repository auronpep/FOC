### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. The state prosecution should be denied only if the later prosecution is for the same offense in the constitutional sense. It is not enough that the same flash drive will be used in both cases. Gold Key: double jeopardy asks whether the offenses are the same, and separate sovereigns plus distinct statutory offenses defeat the claim. Silver Key: cut any answer that talks only about shared proof before asking whether the two charged crimes have the same legal identity.

Here, the federal conviction was for possessing stolen access-device data with intent to distribute it through interstate channels. The state charge is conspiracy to sell stolen access-device data under a state statute. The statutes and offense structures differ, and the prosecutions are brought by different sovereigns. The Double Jeopardy Clause does not require dismissal.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because conspiracy can feel like a smaller version of the completed distribution crime. The breaker is that conspiracy is the agreement offense; it does not merge into the substantive crime and is not automatically a lesser-included offense.

C is attractive because it expresses a simple one-prosecution instinct. The breaker is that the federal government and a state are separate sovereigns for double-jeopardy purposes.

D is the dominant trap. It focuses on the flash drive, the most concrete fact in the stem. Gold Key: the same evidence does not make two prosecutions the same offense. Silver Key: when an answer says "same evidence," translate the issue back into "same offense" before choosing.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. The Fifth Amendment Double Jeopardy Clause bars a second prosecution for the same offense, but "same offense" is not the same as "same evidence." Under Blockburger v. United States, 284 U.S. 299 (1932), two statutory offenses are not the same if each requires proof of a fact the other does not. United States v. Felix, 503 U.S. 378 (1992), confirms that mere overlap in proof between prosecutions does not establish a double-jeopardy violation and that conspiracy is distinct from the substantive offense. Gamble v. United States, 587 U.S. ___ (2019), reaffirmed that state and federal prosecutions are by separate sovereigns for double-jeopardy purposes.

The official key remains sound. Lydia's motion rests only on federal double jeopardy. The state is prosecuting a conspiracy offense under a state statute after a federal substantive conviction under a different federal statute. The same flash drive may be used as evidence, but shared proof does not equal the same offense.
