## 7. Right-Answer Explanation (D)

The prosecutor's requested instruction is a classic permissive inference: the jury **may** draw a conclusion (knowledge) from a proved foundational fact (unexplained recent possession), but it **need not**. The instruction neither requires the jury to accept the inference nor removes its authority to reject it. The government's burden of proof on each element of the offense — including knowledge — remains squarely on the government. Because the instruction is permissive and burden-preserving, it does not usurp the jury's role.

**Gold Key:** A "may, but need not, infer" instruction is a *permissive* inference. The judge is not determining any element. The jury retains full authority to accept or reject the inference, and the government still bears the burden of proving knowledge beyond a reasonable doubt.

**Silver Key:** When two "overrule" answers survive CUT, lock onto the *reason* for overruling. The stem's exact language — "may, but need not" — directly refutes any claim that the judge has determined anything. The choice that gives the permissive-and-burden-preserving reason wins.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Tested rule:** A permissive criminal inference instruction — one that allows but does not require the jury to infer an element from a proved foundational fact — is constitutionally valid and does not violate the defendant's due process rights or strip the jury of its fact-finding function, so long as a rational connection exists between the foundational fact and the inferred element.

**Governing authority:**
- *Barnes v. United States*, 412 U.S. 837 (1973): The Supreme Court upheld a jury instruction permitting (not requiring) the inference that a person in unexplained recent possession of recently stolen property knew it was stolen. The permissive nature of the instruction and the government's retained burden were dispositive.
- *County Court of Ulster County v. Allen*, 442 U.S. 140 (1979): Distinguished mandatory from permissive presumptions. Permissive inferences — where the jury "may" draw the conclusion — are evaluated under a more permissive rational-basis standard, because the jury remains free to reject the inference.
- *Francis v. Franklin*, 471 U.S. 307 (1985): Confirmed that mandatory presumptions in criminal cases shift the burden and raise serious due process concerns, while permissive inferences do not.

**Rule confirmed:** The credited answer D is correct. The instruction is explicitly permissive ("may, but need not"), preserves the government's burden, and leaves the jury free. The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged from the original.
