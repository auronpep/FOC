### 1. Final question
Lydia owns a small Christian bookstore and bought a private accident policy from Barnabas Courier Mutual to cover medical expenses if she were injured while making local deliveries on her e-bike. Lydia paid every premium, and the policy covered delivery-related medical expenses. After Lydia was hurt on a delivery route, Barnabas refused to pay, citing a clause that excluded injuries caused by a rider's "reckless or unsafe conduct."

In a federal diversity action, Lydia proved that she had paid the premiums and that her policy covered delivery-related medical expenses. After Lydia rested, Barnabas introduced the exclusion clause and called a witness who testified that Lydia's crash happened on a sidewalk segment where a posted sign said, "No e-bikes beyond this point." Lydia did not cross-examine the witness. After Barnabas rested, it moved for judgment as a matter of law.

Should the court grant the motion?

A. Yes, because the sign evidence was uncontradicted, so no reasonable jury could find that the policy covers Lydia's injury.

B. No, because a motion for judgment as a matter of law must first be made at the close of the plaintiff's case-in-chief.

C. No, because whether Lydia's conduct was unsafe enough to trigger the exclusion is a question for the jury, and the sign alone leaves room for a reasonable finding that Barnabas did not prove the exclusion.

D. Yes, because Lydia waived the issue by not anticipating and rebutting the sign evidence in her case-in-chief.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. Barnabas moved after both sides had rested, so the timing was proper. The real Rule 50(a) question is whether the evidence, viewed with reasonable inferences for Lydia, left any legally sufficient basis for a jury to find that Barnabas did not prove the exclusion. The sign said only "No e-bikes beyond this point." That could mean the area was unsafe for riders, but it could also mean the sign was posted for pedestrian space, private-property control, traffic flow, or another reason. Because the record did not establish why the sign was posted or how Lydia actually rode, a reasonable jury could find that Barnabas failed to prove "reckless or unsafe conduct." The court should deny judgment as a matter of law and let the jury decide.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because uncontradicted evidence can feel decisive, especially when the plaintiff did not cross-examine the witness. The breaker is that uncontradicted does not automatically mean legally sufficient. A reasonable jury could treat the sign as ambiguous and find that Barnabas did not prove the exclusion.

B is attractive because Rule 50(a) timing is technical and students may remember an older "close of plaintiff's case" phrase. The breaker is that Rule 50(a) allows a motion at any time before the case is submitted to the jury; after Barnabas rested was timely.

D is attractive because it sounds like a procedural-fairness rule: if you do not answer a defense early, maybe you waive it. The breaker is that Lydia had no duty to anticipate Barnabas's exclusion defense in her case-in-chief. Barnabas had the burden to prove the exclusion, and Lydia could cross-examine, remain silent, or let the jury decide.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Civil Procedure, Motions, Trial and Post-Trial Motions, Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law, and the verified outline code is `92080201`. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 50(a)(1) provides that, after a party has been fully heard on an issue during a jury trial, the court may resolve the issue against that party and grant judgment as a matter of law only if a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis to find for the party on that issue. Rule 50(a)(2) also states that a Rule 50(a) motion may be made at any time before the case is submitted to the jury. See Fed. R. Civ. P. 50(a)(1)-(2), https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_50. The Supreme Court's Rule 50 discussion in *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133 (2000), confirms that the motion turns on whether there is a legally sufficient evidentiary basis for a reasonable jury, with reasonable inferences considered for the nonmovant. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/99-536.ZO.html.

Applied here, Barnabas's motion was timely, but the evidence was not strong enough to remove the issue from the jury. The sign was uncontradicted, but the reason for the sign and the meaning of "reckless or unsafe conduct" remained open. A reasonable jury could find that Barnabas failed to prove the exclusion. The credited answer therefore remains correct after the transform: original B becomes post-shuffle C. No doctrinal-currency hotlist issue is triggered.
