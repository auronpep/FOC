## 1. Final question

Esther bought a new laser engraver from Timothy's Artisan Supply for her private Scripture-bookmark business. The written contract stated that the price of the engraver was $28,600 and that Esther would receive an "exchange credit of $9,300 for Esther's old engraver." The old engraver had recently been damaged when a storage-room sprinkler failed.

The contract contained a merger clause stating: "This writing is the complete and exclusive statement of the parties' agreement, and no other agreements or understandings are part of this sale."

When Esther picked up the new engraver, she delivered the old engraver to Timothy's Artisan Supply. At that time, Timothy's claimed that the exchange credit included an assignment of Esther's claim against her insurance company for the damage to the old engraver. Esther refused to assign the claim. Timothy's sued Esther to recover the insurance payment. Timothy's has offered evidence that the parties agreed during negotiations that Timothy's would be entitled to that insurance payment.

Should the court admit this evidence?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A written goods sale gives credit for a damaged trade-in and has a merger clause; may prior negotiations be admitted to explain whether the credit included the related insurance claim?

## 3. Final answer choices

B. Yes, because a merger clause does not bar evidence of fraud.

C. Yes, because the merger clause does not bar evidence offered to explain what the parties meant by "exchange credit."

D. No, because Timothy's acceptance of the old engraver bars any further claim to the insurance proceeds.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.** The offered negotiation evidence is not being used to contradict the written price term or to add a freestanding side promise. It is being offered to explain the quoted term "exchange credit" and whether that credit included the related insurance claim. **Gold Key:** a merger clause is powerful evidence of integration, but it does not automatically block evidence offered to explain what a written term means. **Silver Key:** sort parol-evidence choices by purpose of the evidence. Evidence offered to explain an existing term is different from evidence offered to contradict or add to the writing.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** The merger-clause choice sells the dominant shortcut: once the writing says "complete and exclusive," all earlier negotiation evidence must disappear. The breaker is the purpose of the offer. Timothy's is using the negotiation evidence to explain the written phrase "exchange credit," not to replace the written price or add an unrelated promise. **Gold Key:** a merger clause does not automatically bar explanatory evidence.

**B.** The fraud choice sells an exception-hunting move. Fraud can be a valid reason to admit extrinsic evidence, but the stem contains no fraud allegation, no misrepresentation, and no avoidance theory. The evidence is admissible for interpretation, not because of fraud.

**D.** The acceptance choice sells a common-sense handoff rule: Timothy's took the old engraver, so Timothy's must be stuck with only the old engraver. The breaker is that acceptance of the trade-in item does not answer what the parties meant by "exchange credit" and does not create a rule barring the claimed insurance proceeds.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are preserved. Lawyer-confirmation authority: UCC 2-202 provides that a final written expression may not be contradicted by prior or contemporaneous agreements, but may be explained or supplemented in the ways the section recognizes. Massachusetts General Laws chapter 106, section 2-202, a state enactment of UCC 2-202, uses the same final-expression/parol-evidence structure and allows explanation or supplementation by course of performance, course of dealing, usage of trade, and consistent additional terms subject to complete-and-exclusive integration. Restatement (Second) of Contracts 214(c) states the parallel interpretation point directly: prior or contemporaneous agreements and negotiations may be admitted to establish the meaning of the writing, whether or not integrated. The Kansas UCC 2-202 comment also confirms the MBE-relevant merger-clause point: a merger clause is strong evidence of complete integration but is not conclusive, and extrinsic evidence may aid interpretation even of a fully integrated writing.
