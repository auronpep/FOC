# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT — Q14517

## 1. Final Question

On April 10, Peter hired Timothy to repair Peter's commercial oven by April 18. On April 12, Peter contracted to supply 500 decorated cakes to a wedding venue for a May 1 event. Peter knew he would need the repaired oven to fulfill the wedding contract. Because the venue needed a guaranteed delivery, the parties included a liquidated damages clause requiring Peter to pay the venue $3,000 for each day's delay past May 1. Timothy was inexcusably six days late completing the repair, and Peter was consequently six days late delivering the cakes. Peter paid the venue $18,000 in liquidated damages and then sued Timothy for $18,000. Both Peter and Timothy knew when making their contract on April 10 that, under ordinary circumstances, Peter would sustain few or no damages of any kind from a six-day delay in the oven repair.

Assuming the $3,000-per-day liquidated damages clause in the contract between Peter and the venue is valid, which of the following arguments will serve as Timothy's best defense to Peter's action?

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Consequential damages — losses that do not flow directly from the breach itself but from the injured party's special circumstances — are recoverable only if they were reasonably foreseeable to the breaching party at the time the contract was formed. *Hadley v. Baxendale* (1854); Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 351. Here, both Peter and Timothy knew at formation that "under ordinary circumstances" Peter would sustain "few or no damages" from a six-day delay. The $18,000 in liquidated damages Peter paid the venue was an extraordinary consequence arising from Peter's separate contract — a special circumstance Timothy had no reason to foresee. Timothy is therefore not liable for that amount.

**Gold Key:** Consequential damages require foreseeability at contract formation, not just at the time of breach. The breaching party is liable only for losses a reasonable person in the breaching party's position would have anticipated as a probable result of the breach, or for special losses the breaching party actually knew about.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original C)** is attractive because it sounds like common-sense risk allocation: Peter took a gamble by signing the venue contract while his oven was in the shop, so he should bear the loss. The breaker: nothing in the facts supports an assumption-of-risk defense. Peter contracted with Timothy for a timely repair that would have allowed Peter to perform. The "assumed the risk" language is a fabricated doctrine in this context — contract law does not treat a party who relies on timely performance as having assumed the risk of the other party's breach. **Gold Key:** Consequential damages require foreseeability at contract formation, not just at the time of breach — the foreseeability test, not assumption of risk, governs.

**B (original D)** is attractive because it states a truth that law students know: liquidated damages and actual damages are different things, and courts scrutinize LD clauses for penalty. The breaker: the question explicitly tells you to assume the $3,000/day LD clause is valid. Once validity is assumed, the fact that LD and actual damages may differ is irrelevant. ## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Governing rule:** Consequential (special) damages for breach of contract are recoverable only if the loss was reasonably foreseeable to the breaching party at the time the contract was formed.

**Authority:**
- *Hadley v. Baxendale*, 9 Exch. 341 (1854): The foundational case. Baron Alderson articulated the rule that damages for breach should be those arising naturally from the breach or those reasonably supposed to have been in the contemplation of both parties at contracting.
- Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 351(1): "Damages are not recoverable for loss that the party in breach did not have reason to foresee as a probable result of the breach when the contract was made."
- Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 351(2): Loss is foreseeable if it follows (a) in the ordinary course of events, or (b) as a result of special circumstances the breaching party had reason to know.

**Application to this item:** The facts state both parties knew at formation that "under ordinary circumstances" Peter would sustain "few or no damages" from a six-day delay. The $18,000 LD payment arose from Peter's separate contract with the venue — a special circumstance Timothy had no reason to know about. Under § 351, Timothy is not liable for unforeseeable consequential losses.

**Verification of credited outcome:** The tested rule (foreseeability limitation on consequential damages), the legal issue (can the baker recover extraordinary LD payments from the repairer?), and the credited answer (no, because the loss was unforeseeable) are all unchanged from the original. **PASS.**
