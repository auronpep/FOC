## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam register)

C is correct. Ruth's statement to Hannah — that she was going to spend the next four days in Las Vegas helping her grandmother — is a statement of intent and plan. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 803(3), a statement of the declarant's then-existing state of mind (which expressly includes "motive, intent, or plan") is admissible as an exception to the hearsay rule, and FRE 803(3) does not require the declarant to be unavailable. The Hillmon doctrine (Mutual Life Ins. Co. v. Hillmon, 145 U.S. 285 (1892)) confirms that a declarant's out-of-court statement of future intent is admissible to prove that the declarant then held that intent, and to support an inference that the declarant later acted in accordance with the stated plan. The statement is offered to show that Ruth held the intent to be in Las Vegas at the relevant time — exactly the use 803(3) authorizes. **Gold Key:** Future plans are admissible under FRE 803(3) — the rule covers the declarant's then-existing intent even though the conduct described is future. **Silver Key:** When a statement is about a future plan, check 803(3) before 803(1) — present sense impression requires current perception, not future intent.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification AND Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

**Governing rule.** Federal Rule of Evidence 803(3) — "Then-Existing Mental, Emotional, or Physical Condition" — provides that the following is not excluded by the hearsay rule, regardless of the declarant's availability:

> A statement of the declarant's then-existing state of mind (such as motive, intent, or plan) or emotional, sensory, or physical condition (such as mental feeling, pain, or bodily health), but not including a statement of memory or belief to prove the fact remembered or believed unless it relates to the validity or terms of the declarant's will.

**Application.** Ruth's statement to Hannah is a present-tense declaration of intent to spend four days in Las Vegas. It is offered to prove that Ruth then held that intent — and by extension that she was in Las Vegas at the time of the alleged embezzlement. The statement is therefore within the text of Rule 803(3): it is a statement of the declarant's "then-existing state of mind … (such as … intent, or plan)." It is not offered to prove a fact remembered or believed (the rule's carve-out for backward-looking memory or belief), so the exception's exclusion does not apply. The exception does not require the declarant's unavailability (Rule 803's header: "Hearsay exceptions; enumerated; availability of declarant immaterial").

**Leading authority.** *Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Hillmon*, 145 U.S. 285 (1892), is the foundational Supreme Court decision recognizing the state-of-mind exception for statements of intent. Justice Gray's opinion held that an out-of-court statement of a person's future plans is admissible to prove the declarant later acted in accordance with the plan. The holding is codified in Federal Rule of Evidence 803(3) and is the standard MBE treatment of "future plan" statements. The doctrine is widely adopted across federal and state jurisdictions, and is "firmly rooted" for Confrontation Clause purposes. (See also FRE 803 advisory committee's note to the 1972 enactment.)

#### choice_by_choice_walkthrough

**A. inadmissible, because it is offered to establish an alibi by the defendant's own statement.**
1. **C3 signal (student-accessible):** Hearsay exceptions are proponent-neutral. The "party-opponent admission" exemption (FRE 801(d)(2)) is the *inverse* situation — it lets the *opponent* offer a *party's* statement against the party. It does not bar a *party* from offering her *own* statement. A C3 student who has learned that the hearsay rules are about *what the statement proves* (not *who is offering it*) can spot that this is a non-rule.
2. **Student label:** C — Defendant's Own Statement (flat denial).
3. **True/responsive version would look like:** an answer that names a real, applicable bar — for example, "inadmissible, because the statement is hearsay and no exception applies" (which is what D does — and D is also wrong, but for a different reason).
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 803(3) does not depend on who offers the statement. The defendant's own statement of plan is admissible when offered to prove her then-existing intent.

**B. admissible, because the statement falls within the present sense impression exception to the hearsay rule.**
1. **C3 signal (student-accessible):** Present sense impression (FRE 803(1)) requires the statement to "describ[e] or explain[] an event or condition, made while or immediately after the declarant perceived" it. A forward-looking plan for the next four days is not a description of a current perception — the temporal language in the choice itself ("next four days," "going to") flags the misfit. A C3 student who has been taught the PSI element (current perception) can spot this without legal research.
2. **Student label:** B — Present Sense Impression (misfit exception).
3. **True/responsive version would look like:** an answer that names an applicable no-unavailability exception. The forward-looking plan fits FRE 803(3) — see choice C.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 803(1) is facially inapplicable to a forward-looking statement of intent. The misfit is visible from the choice's own temporal language without legal research.

**C. admissible, because a statement of plans falls within the hearsay exception for then-existing state of mind.** *(CREDITED)*
1. **C3 signal (student-accessible):** Statement of intent and plan is the textbook use of FRE 803(3) — then-existing state of mind. No unavailability required. This is the most-tested no-unavailability exception on the MBE.
2. **Student label:** C — Then-Existing State of Mind (correct).
3. **True/responsive version would look like:** this one. (The choice is true and responsive as written.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 803(3) covers "motive, intent, or plan." *Mutual Life Ins. Co. v. Hillmon*, 145 U.S. 285 (1892), confirms a forward-looking plan statement is admissible to prove the declarant then held that intent. The rule does not require the declarant to be unavailable (Rule 803's header: "availability of declarant immaterial").

**D. inadmissible, because it is hearsay not within any exception.**
1. **C3 signal (student-accessible):** Default exclusion is wrong when a taught no-unavailability exception fits. FRE 803(3) fits a fact pattern that is the textbook use of the exception — a forward-looking plan. A C3 student who has been taught the 803(3) card by name can spot that the "no exception" claim is facially wrong on this fact pattern. (This is the dominant trap — the "I don't see an exception, so it's out" trap.)
2. **Student label:** D — No Exception (give-up / default exclusion).
3. **True/responsive version would look like:** an inadmissible answer that names a real, applicable bar — for example, "inadmissible, because the statement is offered for the truth of the matter asserted and is hearsay within no exception," which would only be correct if 803(3) did not exist. The choice is facially wrong because 803(3) does exist and does apply.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 803(3) applies directly. Saying "no exception exists" is a flat denial of the rule.
