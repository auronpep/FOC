## 1. Final Question (Chosen Variation)

Barnabas was charged with the second-degree murder of Silas under a statute defining that crime as "the unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought, but without premeditation." Barnabas's attorney asserted a defense of insanity and called Barnabas as a witness on his own behalf. After Barnabas testified on direct examination and cross-examination, his attorney called a doctor to the witness stand. The doctor stated that he was a psychiatrist, had practiced for 30 years, had treated thousands of patients with illnesses like Barnabas's, and had testified as an expert in hundreds of criminal homicide trials. He testified, "After listening to Barnabas's testimony, I am of the opinion that Barnabas did not have malice aforethought as our law defines it on the day of Silas's death." Which of the following would be the prosecuting attorney's most effective argument in support of a motion to exclude the doctor's statement?

**B:** The doctor's testimony embraces the ultimate issue.
**C:** Whether Barnabas had "malice aforethought" is a question to be decided by the jury.
**D:** The doctor had insufficient opportunity to examine Barnabas.

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**C is correct.** It is the jury's job to determine whether the evidence proves facts sufficient to satisfy the elements of the charged offense. Under FRE 702, expert opinion is admissible to assist the trier of fact in understanding the evidence or determining a fact in issue. But an expert may not displace the jury's function by offering an opinion on the very legal question the jury must decide. Here, the doctor did not merely describe Barnabas's mental condition — he opined that Barnabas "did not have malice aforethought as our law defines it." That is the precise legal element the jury must resolve. The prosecution's most effective argument is that this testimony invades the province of the jury.

**Gold Key (GK-EVI-ULTISS-1):** FRE 704 abolished the old common-law bar on "ultimate issue" testimony — experts *can* opine on the ultimate factual issue. But they may not opine on the *legal* conclusion that the jury must reach (e.g., "did not have malice aforethought as the law defines it"). The line is: mental condition = OK for expert; legal element = jury's domain.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (new letter A — original B):** *Attractive because* students may think that an expert who has never examined the defendant and relies solely on courtroom testimony is somehow disqualified. *But* FRE 703 expressly permits an expert to base opinions on facts observed in the courtroom, including testimony of other witnesses. The fact that the doctor's opinion was based entirely on courtroom observations goes to the weight of the testimony, not its admissibility.

**B (new letter B — original A) ⚠️ Dominant Trap:** *Attractive because* the common-law rule once prohibited expert testimony that "embraced the ultimate issue," and many students learn this rule without learning that FRE 704 abolished it. The phrasing sounds like a valid legal objection. *But* FRE 704(a) provides that "testimony in the form of an opinion or inference otherwise admissible is not objectionable because it embraces an ultimate issue." The doctor's testimony would only be excludable if it went beyond the ultimate *factual* issue and stated the *legal* conclusion — which is what answer C correctly identifies. **Gold Key GK-EVI-ULTISS-1** reminds us: FRE 704 killed the "ultimate issue" bar, but the jury-province limit still stands.

**D (new letter D — original C):** *Attractive because* a psychiatrist who has never examined the defendant seems unreliable, and students conflate reliability with admissibility. *But* the fact that the doctor never spoke to or examined the defendant goes to the weight (persuasive value) of the testimony, not its admissibility. FRE 702 and 703 do not require personal examination as a prerequisite to expert testimony.

He testified, "After listening to Barnabas's testimony, I am of the opinion that Barnabas did not have malice aforethought as our law defines it on the day of Silas's death." On cross-examination, the doctor admitted that he had never spoken to or seen Barnabas before, and that his opinion was based entirely on his observations of Barnabas's testimony.

**Call:** Which of the following would be the prosecuting attorney's most effective argument in support of a motion to exclude the doctor's statement?

**A:** The doctor's opinions were based entirely upon courtroom observations.
**B:** The doctor's testimony embraces the ultimate issue.
**C:** Whether Barnabas had "malice aforethought" is a question to be decided by the jury.
**D:** The doctor had insufficient opportunity to examine Barnabas.

### Distilled Core Question

A psychiatrist who never examined the defendant offers an opinion that the defendant lacked the mental state required by the charged offense. What is the prosecution's best argument to exclude this testimony?

### Call and Prediction

The call asks for the strongest legal ground to exclude expert testimony. The correct answer should identify that the expert stated the legal conclusion (not just the mental condition), invading the jury's province. Prediction: the correct choice will say something about the jury deciding the legal element.

### Trigger Facts

2. **Doctor never spoke to or saw Barnabas; opinion based entirely on courtroom observations** — this triggers traps B and D. Students may think this disqualifies the expert, but FRE 702/703 permit it.
3. **Doctor is highly qualified (30 years, thousands of patients, hundreds of trials)** — this eliminates any "not qualified" objection and forces the student to find a different ground.

### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**A: "The doctor's opinions were based entirely upon courtroom observations."**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** FRE 703 expressly permits an expert to base opinions on facts observed in the courtroom, including testimony of other witnesses. This choice says the opposite — that courtroom observations are a ground for exclusion. Direct structural contradiction.
2. **Student label:** "FRE 703 allows courtroom observations. This is a flat misstatement."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would need to identify a ground that actually makes testimony inadmissible — not a basis that the rules expressly permit.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 703 permits experts to base opinions on facts observed in the courtroom. LII, Cornell Law Institute, Rule 703.

**B: "The doctor's testimony embraces the ultimate issue." ⚠️ Dominant Trap**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Students who learned the old common-law rule will think this is valid. But FRE 704(a) abolished the ultimate-issue bar. The Gold Key distinction: FRE 704 permits ultimate-factual-issue opinions, but the expert still cannot state the legal conclusion. The doctor said "did not have malice aforethought as our law defines it" — that's the legal conclusion, not just a mental-condition description. But the objection "embraces the ultimate issue" is no longer valid under FRE 704. The correct objection is that it invades the jury's province (answer C).
2. **Student label:** "Ultimate-issue bar is dead — FRE 704 killed it. This is the trap."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would say the expert's testimony invades the province of the jury by stating the legal conclusion — not that it "embraces the ultimate issue."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 704(a): "Testimony in the form of an opinion or inference otherwise admissible is not objectionable because it embraces an ultimate issue." LII, Cornell Law Institute, Rule 704.
   **Gold Key GK-EVI-ULTISS-1:** FRE 704 abolished the old common-law bar on "ultimate issue" testimony — experts CAN opine on the ultimate factual issue. But they may not state the LEGAL conclusion the jury must reach.

**C: "Whether Barnabas had 'malice aforethought' is a question to be decided by the jury."**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The doctor said "did not have malice aforethought as our law defines it." That's the precise legal element the jury must decide. Even under FRE 704, the expert cannot displace the jury's function by stating the legal conclusion. This is the only answer that correctly identifies the surviving limit on expert testimony.
2. **Student label:** "Expert stated the legal conclusion — that's the jury's job. This is the correct objection."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This *is* the true and responsive statement of the correct objection.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** While FRE 704 permits opinions on ultimate factual issues, the expert may not invade the province of the jury by stating the legal conclusion. LII, Cornell Law Institute, Rule 704.

**D: "The doctor had insufficient opportunity to examine Barnabas."**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** FRE 702 does not require personal examination as a prerequisite to expert testimony. The fact that the doctor never examined Barnabas affects the weight (persuasiveness) of the testimony, not its admissibility.
2. **Student label:** "Weight, not admissibility. FRE 702 doesn't require personal examination."
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would need to identify an actual admissibility bar, not a weight objection.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 702 and 703 do not require personal examination. LII, Cornell Law Institute, Rules 702, 703.

After CUT eliminates A (courtroom observations permitted) and D (weight not admissibility), and CLASH resolves B (ultimate-issue bar abolished) vs. C (jury province survives), the residual is **C** — whether Barnabas had malice aforethought is a question for the jury.
