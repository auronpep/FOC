## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** Aggravated kidnapping at common law requires confinement plus one or more enumerated aggravating factors. Here, two independent aggravating factors are present: (1) the victim is a minor child, which is a per se aggravating circumstance at common law and in most statutory frameworks; and (2) Ruth drafted a written demand for $40,000, which establishes the intent to hold Timothy for ransom. The ransom aggravating factor does **not** require that the demand be transmitted, received, or acted upon — the intent to extort, evidenced by the drafted note, is sufficient. The confinement element is satisfied by transporting Timothy to the remote cabin. Because at least one aggravating factor is established, the charge elevates to aggravated kidnapping.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (Original B — Dominant Trap):** This choice is attractive because it seizes on a fact that *feels* decisive — the child was not physically hurt — and reasons that without injury, the kidnapping must be "simple." The cognitive move is: "aggravation = harm, no harm, no aggravation." The breaker is that physical harm is **not** a required element of aggravated kidnapping. The aggravating factors are enumerated — holding for ransom, using the victim as a shield, taking the victim across state lines, or taking a child — and none of them require injury. The ransom intent alone is sufficient, and the minor-victim factor independently aggravates. This choice collapses the aggravation inquiry into a single factor (injury) that the law does not require.

**C (Original D):** This choice correctly identifies that the minor-victim factor supports aggravated kidnapping, but it incorrectly dismisses the ransom note as legally irrelevant. The cognitive move is: "the note was never sent, so it can't count." The breaker is that the ransom aggravating factor turns on the **intent** to hold for ransom, not on successful communication of the demand. A drafted but undelivered ransom note is direct evidence of that intent. By discarding a valid aggravating factor, this choice reflects an incomplete legal analysis — it gets the right outcome for partially wrong reasons.

**D (Original A):** This choice zeroes in on the fact that the note was never sent and concludes that the ransom aggravation therefore fails. The cognitive move is: "no delivery, no ransom." The breaker is that the common-law aggravating factor for ransom does not require the demand to be received or even transmitted. The intent to hold for ransom — evidenced by the written demand — is the operative trigger. The aggravated kidnapping is complete upon confinement with the extortionate intent, not upon successful communication.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule:** Aggravated kidnapping at common law is established when the defendant confines the victim (the underlying kidnapping) and one or more enumerated aggravating factors are present. The paradigmatic aggravating factors include: (1) holding the victim for ransom or reward; (2) using the victim as a shield or hostage; (3) transporting the victim across state lines (under federal law, 18 U.S.C. § 1201); and (4) abducting a child below a specified age.

**Ransom aggravation — intent suffices:** The "holding for ransom" aggravator requires that the defendant confine the victim with the intent to extort money or other benefit as a condition of release. The demand need not be communicated, received, or acted upon. The defendant's intent, as evidenced by a written demand, a recorded statement, or other conduct, is sufficient. *See* the common-law framework as reflected in modern statutory codifications (e.g., Model Penal Code § 212.1; state aggravated kidnapping statutes).

**Minor-victim aggravation:** The abduction of a minor child is independently treated as an aggravating factor at common law and in virtually all modern statutes, often as a per se aggravation regardless of other circumstances.

**Authority:** Common-law aggravated kidnapping doctrine as reflected in the Model Penal Code § 212.1 (kidnapping; graded as a first-degree felony when the victim is a non-family minor or when the defendant holds the victim for ransom or as a hostage); 18 U.S.C. § 1201 (federal kidnapping statute, presuming aggravation when the victim is an unemancipated minor). The rule reference supplied with the source row confirms: "Aggravated kidnapping requires confinement plus one of several enumerated aggravating factors; holding for ransom — a demand for money or benefit as a condition for release — is the paradigmatic trigger; the demand need not be received or paid."
