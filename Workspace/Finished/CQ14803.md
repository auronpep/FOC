### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. The recording fits the recorded-recollection exception because the witness once knew the plate number, cannot now remember it well enough to testify fully and accurately, and adopted the recording when the matter was fresh by verifying that Ruth repeated his number correctly. Gold Key: a fresh record adopted by the witness can carry the witness's memory when the witness can no longer supply it from the stand. Silver Key: when two answers say the evidence is proper, do not stop at the result; test whether the because clause follows the foundation facts. The foundation facts point to recorded recollection, not to a public-record route.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because it correctly sees the hearsay problem: the recording is being offered to prove the plate number. The breaker is the omitted exception. Gold Key: recorded recollection admits a record the witness made or adopted while the matter was fresh if the witness once knew the fact and now cannot recall it well enough. Those facts are all in the stem, so the hearsay label does not end the analysis.

B is attractive because Ruth was the voice on the call and did not personally see the van's plate. The breaker is that the needed knowledge is the witness's knowledge. He saw the plate, dictated it, and then adopted the recording as accurate while the event was fresh; Ruth's role was the conduit for his statement, not the source of the identification.

D is attractive because a 911 recording sounds official and the choice reaches the same admissibility result as the credited answer. The breaker is the route. Silver Key: a right result with the wrong because clause does not survive when the stem gives a specific hearsay foundation. The recording is admissible as the witness's recorded recollection, not because the prosecution may use a law-enforcement public record against a criminal defendant.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Evidence, hearsay exceptions not requiring unavailability, and the verified outline code is `33040302`. The governing rule is Federal Rule of Evidence 803(5): a record is not excluded by the rule against hearsay when it concerns a matter the witness once knew, the witness cannot now recall well enough to testify fully and accurately, and the witness made or adopted the record while the matter was fresh and accurate. The U.S. Code text also provides that, if admitted, the record's contents may be presented to the jury, though the record itself is received as an exhibit only if offered by an adverse party. See Fed. R. Evid. 803(5), https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title28a-node232-article8-rule803. The public-record detour is wrong because Rule 803(8) excludes, in a criminal case, matters observed by law-enforcement personnel, and factual findings under that exception are usable in civil cases or against the government in criminal cases, not as the prosecution's shortcut against the accused. See Fed. R. Evid. 803(8), same source. The personal-knowledge distractor is also defeated by Rule 602 and the hearsay notes: the declarant whose perception matters is the witness who saw the plate, and the witness adopted the record. See Fed. R. Evid. 602, https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&path=%2Fprelim%40title28%2Ftitle28a%2Fnode232%2Farticle6. Current-rule check: the U.S. Courts page states the Evidence Rules were last amended in 2024, and the December 1, 2025 current-rules page lists new amendments to Appellate, Bankruptcy, and Civil Rules, not Evidence Rules. See https://www.uscourts.gov/forms-rules/current-rules-practice-procedure/federal-rules-evidence and https://www.uscourts.gov/forms-rules/current-rules-practice-procedure. No doctrinal-currency hotlist issue changes this answer. The credited outcome is unchanged from the source item.
