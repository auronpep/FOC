### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Daniel's failure-to-state-a-claim defense is **not waived**. **Gold Key:** Rule 12(h)(2) preserves the Rule 12(b)(6) defense for any later stage — including a Rule 12(c) motion for judgment on the pleadings after the pleadings close — even when the defense was never raised in the answer. The answer broke neither filter. **Silver Key:** when the call is "is the defense waived by omission," map the precise defense to the precise Rule 12(h) subsection before saying "waived." Rule 12(h)(1) waives 12(b)(2)–(5) and 12(b)(7) if not in the answer or in a pre-answer amendment; Rule 12(h)(2) preserves 12(b)(6) (and a few related defenses) for any later pleading, motion, or at trial. Read the subsection before answering.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. Yes, all Rule 12 defenses are waived if omitted from the first response.**

This is the broad-brush trap. The student treats Rule 12 as one big "include-it-or-lose-it" rule. The breaker is the **Silver Key**: Rule 12(h)(1) and Rule 12(h)(2) split the waiver rule. The 12(b)(2)–(5) and 12(b)(7) defenses are waived if not raised in the answer or an amendment; the 12(b)(6) defense and a small list of others are NOT.

**C. No, because subject-matter jurisdiction and failure to state a claim are the same.**

This is the conflation trap. The student sees that both defenses can be raised late and concludes they are the same. The breaker is that subject-matter jurisdiction (12(b)(1)) is a non-waivable separate beast (it can be raised at any time, even on appeal, and is never waived), while failure to state a claim (12(b)(6)) is waivable in a narrow sense — it can be raised later but is forfeited if not raised at the latest stage the defense becomes available. They are not the same.

**D. Yes, failure to state a claim must be raised before answering or never.**

This is the **dominant trap**. It is the most specific and most rule-sounding of the three wrong answers, which makes it the most attractive. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: Rule 12(h)(2) is the explicit textual rule that preserves the 12(b)(6) defense "at any stage" — including in a Rule 12(c) motion, in a later pleading, or at trial. A student who half-remembers "waive it or lose it" and reads the Rule 12(b)(6) defense as if it were 12(b)(2) (personal jurisdiction) will walk into this trap. Test the defense against Rule 12(h)(2) before saying "waived."

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17406, a Civil Procedure item involving a defendant who omits a Rule 12(b)(6) defense from the answer and later raises it in a Rule 12(c) motion for judgment on the pleadings. The official key is C: the defense can be raised by Rule 12(c) after the pleadings close.

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) authorizes a defense of "failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted." Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(h)(2) governs the preservation of certain Rule 12 defenses and provides: "Whenever it appears by suggestion of the parties or otherwise that the court lacks jurisdiction of the subject matter, the court shall dismiss the action." The companion subsection (h)(1) separately addresses the timing of the waivable defenses, and (h)(2) lists the defenses that are preserved for any later stage — including failure to state a claim, failure to join a required party under Rule 19, and a few others. The official notes to Rule 12 confirm that the failure-to-state-a-claim defense is preserved at any stage, in any pleading, motion, or at trial.

A Rule 12(c) motion for judgment on the pleadings is decided on the pleadings alone and applies the same standard as a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss. The two are functionally interchangeable for the 12(b)(6) inquiry, and Rule 12(h)(2) preserves the defense for the 12(c) motion in the same way it preserves it for a 12(b)(6) motion. The transformed outcome is identical to the original: the defense is preserved, the Rule 12(c) motion is procedurally proper, and the merits of whether the complaint actually fails to state a claim are an entirely separate question.
