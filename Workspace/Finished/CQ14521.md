### 1. Final question

Daniel contracted to create a privately owned walk-through Bible-story lantern garden for Peter’s family Easter festival. Daniel hired Lydia to build the “Sea of Galilee” stone walkway and hidden lighting bed for $260,000. Under the subcontract, Lydia completed work with a reasonable value of $190,000, and Daniel made required progress payments totaling $130,000 for that stage. Lydia then unjustifiably refused to perform further. Daniel reasonably paid Barnabas $155,000 to finish the same work. Lydia sues Daniel for the reasonable value of the benefits conferred, and Daniel counterclaims for breach of contract.

Which of the following should be the court’s decision?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A subcontractor is fully paid under the subcontract for the work stage completed, then unjustifiably walks off; the contractor pays more than the remaining subcontract price to finish. Who recovers?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. Lydia recovers $35,000, the benefit Lydia conferred on Daniel minus the $25,000 in damages incurred by Daniel.

C. Neither party recovers anything, because Lydia committed a material, unexcused breach and Daniel received a $60,000 benefit from Lydia for which Lydia has not been paid.

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B is the correct answer.** Daniel bargained to receive the completed walkway-and-lighting work for $260,000. Because Lydia unjustifiably stopped, Daniel had to pay a replacement $155,000 after already paying Lydia $130,000. That means Daniel’s total outlay for the promised completed performance is $285,000. The overrun is $25,000.

**Gold Key:** A breaching performer cannot use restitution to collect more than the contract allocation for the performance retained; the nonbreaching party is protected against paying more than the bargain price. **Silver Key:** In a dollar-outcome array, label **who gets paid** before trusting arithmetic. Here, the survivor pays Daniel, not Lydia.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This is the dominant trap. It sells clean arithmetic: $60,000 in unpaid reasonable value minus Daniel’s $25,000 overrun equals $35,000. **Gold Key:** the breaching performer does not get to convert a restitution claim into a better position than the subcontract allowed. **Silver Key:** before netting numbers, label the payee. This answer pays Lydia, the breaching performer, even though Daniel’s total outlay already exceeds the bargain price.

**C.** This answer sells a “wash.” Lydia breached, but Daniel also received unpaid value, so the answer feels balanced. The breaker is that a no-recovery compromise does not apply the remedy rule. Daniel’s reasonable completion overrun remains $25,000.

**D.** This answer sells the visible unpaid-benefit number: $190,000 value minus $130,000 progress payments equals $60,000. The missing move is the contract allocation. Lydia has already received the subcontract amount due for that stage, and her own breach forced Daniel to pay more than the contract price to obtain completion.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The source row’s official explanation credits the contractor’s $20,000 overrun in the original and rejects the breaching subcontractor’s attempt to recover reasonable value above the contract-price allocation.  The transformed numbers preserve the same structure: $130,000 already paid plus $155,000 completion cost equals $285,000 total outlay, which is $25,000 more than the $260,000 subcontract price.

The expectation-damages rule is grounded in Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 347: the injured party’s expectation interest is measured by loss in value plus other loss, less avoided cost or avoided loss. ([Open Casebook][1]) Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment § 36 also frames defaulting-party restitution by comparing the recipient’s position to the position it would have occupied had the contract been fully performed. ([Open Casebook][2]) The contract-price ceiling for a breaching contractor is reflected in *United States ex rel. Palmer Construction, Inc. v. Cal State Electric, Inc.*, where the Ninth Circuit stated that a breaching party may not recover an amount that, when added to amounts already paid and completion costs, would cause the contract price to be exceeded. ([Justia Law][3])

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. The surface story changed from an industrial power-plant foundation to a private Christian-themed lantern garden, but the remedy structure remains identical.
