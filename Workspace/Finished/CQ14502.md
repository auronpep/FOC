### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** A wrongfully discharged employee has a duty to mitigate damages by making reasonable efforts to seek comparable employment. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 350. If no comparable employment is reasonably available and the employee does not take another job, the employee may recover the full promised salary for the remainder of the contract term. The employer's breach is undisputed; the only question is what the employee did to limit her losses.

**C is correct.** The ministry's without-cause termination of Lydia's employment was a breach of the employment portion of the contract. Lydia is entitled to expectation damages — the unpaid salary for the remaining eight months. However, under the duty to mitigate, Lydia cannot simply sit idle and collect the salary. She must make reasonable efforts to find comparable work. If, despite those efforts, no comparable job is available and she remains unemployed, she recovers the full remaining salary. This is exactly what answer C states.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is attractive** because the ministry clearly breached the contract, and a breach can sometimes justify rescission. A student might think: "They broke the deal, so I want out of the whole thing." **The breaker:** The contract is divisible — the sale of the business and the $180,000 price are one pair; the employment promise and the salary are another. Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 240, where performances can be apportioned into corresponding pairs of agreed equivalents, the contract is divisible. Lydia can recover damages for the employment breach but cannot rescind the sale of her business. The employment breach, standing alone, is not a material breach of the entire contract, and even if it were, divisibility bars rescission of the sale portion.

**B is attractive** because Lydia was promised the job and the ministry took it away — why shouldn't a court force them to honor the promise? **The breaker:** Courts will not grant specific performance of a personal services contract against an employer. The general rule is that specific performance is unavailable for employment contracts — the remedy is monetary damages, not forced labor or forced employment. Lydia's recovery is limited to the unpaid salary (subject to mitigation), not a court order reinstating her.

**D is attractive** because Lydia was ready and willing to work, and the ministry prevented her from doing so. It feels unfair that she should have to go find a new job. **The breaker:** The duty to mitigate requires more than merely remaining ready to work. The wrongfully discharged employee must make reasonable efforts to seek comparable employment. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 350. Lydia's recovery will be reduced by compensation she could have earned had she made reasonable efforts to find a comparable position. "Ready to work" alone is not enough — she must actively look. This is the **dominant trap** because it sounds reasonable and tests whether the student knows the affirmative steps the duty requires.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Tested rule:** Duty to mitigate in breach of employment contract; measure of damages for wrongful termination; availability of rescission and specific performance as alternative remedies.

**Authority:**

1. **Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 350 (1981)** — Avoidability as Limitation on Damages: "Damages are not recoverable for loss that the injured party could have avoided without undue risk, burden or humiliation, but the injured party is not precluded from recovery by the rule stated in Subsection (1) to the extent that he has made reasonable but unsuccessful efforts to avoid loss." This is the core mitigation rule. A wrongfully discharged employee must make reasonable efforts to find comparable employment; failure to do so reduces recovery by the amount the employee could have earned.

2. **Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 240 (1981)** — Divisibility: Where "the performances to be exchanged under an exchange of promises can be apportioned into corresponding pairs of part performances so that the parts of each pair are properly regarded as agreed equivalents," the contract is divisible. Here, the sale of the business for $180,000 and the employment for $40,000/year are divisible pairs. Breach of the employment portion does not entitle the employee to rescind the sale portion.

3. **Specific performance of personal services:** The longstanding common-law rule, reflected in Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 367, is that specific performance of a contract for personal services will not be ordered. Courts will not compel an employer to continue employing someone against its will, just as they will not compel an employee to work. The remedy is damages.

**Credited outcome unchanged:** The original question tests whether the student knows that (1) the employee can recover unpaid salary subject to the duty to mitigate, (2) mitigation requires reasonable efforts to find comparable work, and (3) if no comparable work is available despite reasonable efforts, the employee recovers the full amount. **No doctrinal-currency concerns:** This is standard Contracts remedies doctrine. No recent Supreme Court or statutory changes affect these rules.
