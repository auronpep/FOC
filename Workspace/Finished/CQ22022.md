# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question (revised stem + call)

Naomi owned a small lakeside cottage that she leased to Timothy under a four-year lease providing, "Tenant agrees that he or she will not assign or sublet the premises without the written permission of the landlord." Nine months after taking possession under the lease, Timothy — who was about to spend four months helping rebuild a meeting hall in a distant town — asked Naomi's permission to sublet the cottage for that four-month period. Although Timothy offered to submit the names of prospective subtenants to Naomi for her approval, Naomi said that she would not approve a sublease to any person under any circumstances. Timothy thereafter sublet the premises to a sublessee.

**Which of the following statements is most correct regarding the rights of Naomi?**

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A tenant subleases in violation of a lease covenant barring sublease without the landlord's consent, and the lease reserves no right of re-entry. What are the landlord's rights?

## 6. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

The call asks which statement is *most correct* about Naomi's rights. **C** is correct. Timothy promised in writing not to sublet without Naomi's permission and then sublet without it, so Naomi has a damages claim against him for breach of covenant. **Gold Key:** breach of a no-assignment/sublease covenant gives the landlord a damages claim, but does *not* let her terminate the lease or evict the transferee unless the lease *expressly reserved a right of re-entry* — and it does not void the sublease, because courts favor alienability. Naomi's lease reserved no such right, so eviction is off the table; only the breach claim survives. **Silver Key:** with a "remedy 1 / remedy 2 / both / neither" array, test each remedy alone — the eviction remedy fails, so the "both" answer fails with it.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**D — both remedies (dominant trap).** It rewards the student who correctly spots the breach and then assumes a broken covenant lets the landlord throw the subtenant out too. The eviction half is false — termination requires an express reserved right of re-entry the lease never granted — so the compound statement is only half true and cannot be "most correct." **Gold Key:** no reserved right of re-entry, no termination or eviction. **Silver Key:** verify each remedy in a combination answer separately.

**B — terminate and evict only.** It treats eviction as the natural consequence of breach and drops the damages claim entirely. The landlord gets no termination right without an express re-entry clause, and the sublease is not void, so this overstates the remedy and omits the one Naomi actually has. **Gold Key:** forfeiture is not automatic — it must be reserved.

**A — neither remedy.** The stem makes Naomi look unreasonable — Timothy offered to vet subtenants and she refused flatly — nudging the student to conclude she forfeits everything. Under the majority rule a consent clause silent on reasonableness lets the landlord refuse for any reason, so the arbitrary refusal does not erase Timothy's breach. **Gold Key:** an arbitrary refusal under a silent-consent clause is permitted (majority), and the tenant who transfers anyway still breaches.

## 8. Black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

**Tested rule (unchanged):** a tenant's violation of a covenant against assignment or subletting is a breach for which the tenant is liable in damages, but (a) the transfer is *not void* — courts favor the alienability of estates — and (b) the landlord may *not* terminate the lease and evict the transferee unless the lease *expressly reserved a right of re-entry / forfeiture* for breach of the covenant. On these facts the lease reserved no such right, so Naomi's sole remedy is a damages claim against Timothy (**C**). The arbitrary-refusal facts are a red herring: under the **majority rule**, a consent clause silent as to reasonableness permits the landlord to withhold consent for any reason, so the refusal does not excuse the tenant's breach. Tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are **unchanged** from the original.

*Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):* Restatement (Second) of Property: Landlord and Tenant (transfers and remedies; §15.2 consent); common-law rule that forfeiture requires an express right of re-entry and is not implied. Minority/commercial reasonableness rule: Restatement (Second) §15.2(2); *Kendall v. Ernest Pestana, Inc.*, 40 Cal. 3d 488 (1985) (commercial leases) — not applied here (residential; majority rule controls; outcome unchanged either way because Timothy transferred *without* consent). Doctrine/Restatement and rule-class authority; the consent-reasonableness split is noted but is not on the doctrinal-currency hotlist and does not move the outcome. ([Forfeiture: the right of re-entry](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=25b166c5-239a-4e9b-acd7-523c8652ab79), [Assignment, subletting & mortgaging by tenant (PLI)](https://legacy.pli.edu/product_files/Titles/69/660_sample07_20150316094717.pdf))
