# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question

Ruth and Naomi owned a mountain cabin as joint tenants. Ruth wanted money to print a private Bible-story workbook for children, so she signed a mortgage with a credit union purporting to give the credit union a lien on the whole cabin. Naomi did not know about the mortgage. Ruth then died, and the credit union contacted Naomi, offering to let her make Ruth's mortgage payments in Ruth's name. Naomi refused. The credit union then sought to foreclose on the cabin.

May the credit union foreclose on the cabin?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

When one joint tenant gives a lien-theory mortgage on jointly held land and dies before foreclosure, can the mortgagee foreclose against the surviving joint tenant's land?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**D is correct.** Ruth and Naomi owned the cabin as joint tenants, so Ruth's interest carried a right of survivorship. Ruth could not give the credit union more than her own interest, and the stem frames the mortgage as a lien. When Ruth died before foreclosure, her joint-tenancy interest was extinguished and Naomi became sole owner by survivorship. The credit union had no surviving interest in the cabin to foreclose.

Gold Key: In a lien-theory mortgage frame, one joint tenant's mortgage does not sever the joint tenancy; if the mortgaging joint tenant dies before foreclosure, the lien attached to that tenant's interest disappears with the interest.

Silver Key: When the answers split between "no because joint tenants" and "no because no notice," pick the title-mechanics reason, not the sympathetic notice fact.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is attractive** because the credit union did give Naomi a practical chance to keep the payments going. The breaker is timing and source of rights: a post-death offer to assume payments does not create a foreclosure right against a survivor who never assumed the debt.

**B is attractive** because Naomi looks innocent, and lack of notice feels like the fairness reason to protect her. Silver Key: the call turns on title mechanics, not notice; Naomi wins because survivorship left the credit union with no enforceable lien after Ruth died.

**C is attractive** because the mortgage language sounds broad and because Ruth did sign a document purporting to lien the whole cabin. Gold Key: one joint tenant's lien-theory mortgage reaches only that joint tenant's interest, and that interest vanished when Ruth died before foreclosure.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested issue is unchanged: whether a mortgagee can foreclose after one joint tenant grants a lien-theory mortgage and then dies before foreclosure. The credited outcome is unchanged: no foreclosure, because the mortgaging joint tenant's interest ended at death and the surviving joint tenant took the whole property by survivorship.

For the lawyer-confirmation layer, **Harms v. Sprague, 473 N.E.2d 930 (Ill. 1984)**, held that a mortgage executed by one joint tenant did not sever the joint tenancy and did not survive the mortgagor joint tenant's death in a lien-theory setting: https://law.justia.com/cases/illinois/supreme-court/1984/59515-7.html. The holding fits the source facts because the original item expressly describes the bank as receiving a lien, not title, and because foreclosure occurred only after the mortgagor joint tenant died.

Applied here, Ruth could encumber only her own joint-tenancy interest. The credit union did not foreclose before Ruth died. Once Ruth died, Naomi took the whole cabin by right of survivorship, and Ruth's liened interest no longer existed. Naomi's lack of notice and the credit union's post-death payment offer are not the legal reason; the right of survivorship is.
