### 1. Final question — revised stem + call

Lydia owned an antique set of handbells that she used for neighborhood Easter concerts. She paid Barnabas Protection Plans for a written equipment-protection agreement covering accidental damage to the handbells. The agreement omitted coverage disclosures that a state service-plan statute requires for agreements of that kind. Is Lydia likely to recover for the loss?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is the best answer. The agreement violated a regulatory statute, but the party seeking enforcement is the party the statute was designed to protect. **Gold Key:** a statutory violation does not automatically let the violating drafter escape the deal; if nonenforcement would injure the protected class, enforcement may serve the statute’s purpose. **Silver Key:** do not stop at “yes.” In this array, one yes answer gives the right outcome for the wrong reason; the correct answer ties the yes result to enforceability, not interpretation.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This answer gives the attractive yes outcome and uses a familiar rule: ambiguous drafted language is often construed against the drafter. The breaker is the call. There is no fight over the meaning of contract language. The fight is whether a statutory defect makes the agreement unenforceable. **Silver Key:** a right outcome with an off-call reason still fails the responsive filter.

**C.** This answer sounds lawyerly because divisibility is a real contract concept. The breaker is that divisibility has no job here. No one is trying to split the agreement into performed and unperformed parts or recover a proportional contract price. The call is enforceability after a statutory defect.

**D.** This answer catches the real statutory violation and treats that fact as the whole case. The breaker is the missing exception. **Gold Key:** violation alone is not enough when refusing enforcement would harm the class the statute was meant to protect.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The supplied row identifies the original credited answer as the protected-class enforcement answer and explains that a contract violating a state statute may still be enforced when the statute was intended to benefit the contracting party seeking relief. 

The governing Contracts classification is `54031000`, because the codebook routes Contracts defenses involving illegality and public policy to “Defenses to Formation or Enforcement > Public Policy.” 

Authority floor: Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 178 states the public-policy enforceability framework: a promise or term is unenforceable if legislation says so or if enforcement is clearly outweighed by public policy. The American Law Institute’s publication page identifies Volume 2 of the Restatement (Second) of Contracts as covering “Unenforceability on Grounds of Public Policy,” including §§ 178–315, and describes the Restatement as a widely relied-on guide to common-law contract principles. ([Open Casebook][1])

The protected-class principle is also reflected in the Restatement’s treatment of parties not equally in the wrong. Geronta Funding v. Brighthouse Life Insurance Co. quotes Restatement § 198 and notes that a claimant may be treated as less culpable where the public policy was intended to protect persons in that claimant’s class. ([Justia][2])

The variant preserves the same legal issue and credited outcome: a drafter cannot use its own statutory noncompliance to defeat the protected party’s covered-loss claim when enforcement furthers the protective statute.
