Legal confirmation: robbery generally requires taking another’s property by force or threat of force, and the common-law timing analysis distinguishes force used before/during the taking from force used only after completion. Cornell’s Wex states the general robbery elements, and the Eleventh Circuit’s discussion in *United States v. 4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The common-law rule is narrower than the escape-force answer. Force used solely after a completed taking does not supply the robbery force element under the common-law timing rule discussed in *Grable*. ([Justia Law][2])

### C. “No, because the force occurred after Rex had completed the larceny”

4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The common-law robbery timing rule requires force or intimidation as part of the taking. The facts place the shove after completion, so C is the legally correct answer. ([Justia Law][2])

### D. “No, because phones cannot be taken by robbery”

4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Robbery can involve personal property; the defect here is not the phone. The defect is that force was not used in the common-law robbery timeframe. ([Legal Information Institute][1])
