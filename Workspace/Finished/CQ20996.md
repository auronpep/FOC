## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Tested rule:** Acute voluntary intoxication alone does not constitute legal insanity. Insanity requires a mental disease or defect. Voluntary intoxication is analyzed under intoxication doctrine, not under the insanity defense.

**Authority:**

- **Model Penal Code § 2.08(3):** "Intoxication does not, in itself, constitute mental disease within the meaning of the insanity rules." (American Law Institute, Model Penal Code § 2.08(3) (1985).)
- **M'Naghten's Case [1843] UKHL J16:** The insanity defense requires a "defect of reason, from disease of the mind." The House of Lords held that the defendant must not know the nature and quality of the act or that it was wrong — but the defect must arise from a *disease of the mind*, not from self-induced intoxication.
- **Insanity Defense Reform Act of 1984, 18 U.S.C. § 17(a):** "Mental disease or defect" does not include voluntary intoxication. The Senate Judiciary Committee stated that "the voluntary use of alcohol or drugs, even if they render the defendant unable to appreciate the nature and quality of his acts, does not constitute insanity."
- **State v. Barrett, 408 A.2d 1273, 1275 (Me. 1979):** Self-induced intoxication is explicitly unavailable as a defense where the culpable mental state is recklessness, and is analyzed under intoxication doctrine rather than insanity.

**Credited outcome confirmed:** C in the original maps to B in the variant. The legal substance is identical — voluntary intoxication alone is not insanity. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are UNCHANGED.

## Wrong Answer Recovery Paths

**If you chose B (the dominant trap):** You conflated a vivid mental impairment (hallucination) with legal insanity. Recovery: remember that M'Naghten requires the impairment to come from a *disease of the mind*, not from self-induced intoxication. The hallucination's vividness is irrelevant — the *source* is what matters.

**If you chose C (the sympathy trap):** You focused on the fact that Daniel genuinely didn't understand what he was doing. Recovery: the insanity defense doesn't ask whether the defendant was confused — it asks whether the confusion came from a *mental disease or defect*. Voluntary intoxication causing a temporary delusion is a different doctrine.

**If you chose D (the overclaim trap):** You overgeneralized a rule. Recovery: intoxication *can* be relevant in criminal law — it can negate specific-intent elements. The word "never" in a legal rule is almost always a red flag.
