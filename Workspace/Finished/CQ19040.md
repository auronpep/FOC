## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

Ruth and Naomi began as joint tenants with a right of survivorship. Under joint tenancy, when one joint tenant dies, the survivor automatically becomes the owner of the deceased's share by operation of law — the deceased's interest does not pass through her estate. However, a joint tenancy can be severed by the unilateral act of one joint tenant conveying her interest to a third party. When Ruth executed and delivered a quitclaim deed transferring her interest to Esther, the joint tenancy was severed. Ruth's conveyance destroyed the unity of title, converting the ownership into a tenancy in common between Esther (who held Ruth's former share) and Naomi. Tenants in common have no right of survivorship, so when Ruth died, her share was already Esther's by the prior conveyance. When Naomi died, her share passed under her will to Daniel. Esther and Daniel now hold the property as tenants in common.

**Gold Key:** A quitclaim deed from one joint tenant to a third party severs the joint tenancy — the right of survivorship is destroyed at the moment of conveyance, not at death.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (Esther is sole owner):** This choice appeals to the student who sees that Ruth conveyed to Esther and reasons that Esther got Ruth's half, and then — because Naomi died — assumes Naomi's half also went somewhere other than Daniel. But the question states Naomi devised her interest to Daniel. The only way Esther could be sole owner is if Naomi's will were invalid or if the devise failed, neither of which the facts support. The breaker: Naomi's valid will passed her tenancy-in-common share to Daniel, so Esther cannot be sole owner.

**B (Daniel is sole owner) — DOMINANT TRAP:** This is the most attractive wrong answer. The student reasons: Ruth and Naomi were joint tenants → Ruth died first → Naomi, as surviving joint tenant, became sole owner → Naomi's will gave everything to Daniel. The hidden flaw: this chain only works if the joint tenancy survived Ruth's lifetime. Ruth's quitclaim to Esther severed the joint tenancy before Ruth died, so Naomi was never a sole owner — she was a tenant in common with Esther. **Gold Key:** The quitclaim deed severed the joint tenancy at the moment of conveyance; survivorship never attached to Naomi's estate.

**C (Esther and Daniel as joint tenants):** This choice appeals to the student who correctly identifies that the joint tenancy was severed but then incorrectly assumes that Esther and Daniel somehow stepped into a new joint tenancy. A joint tenancy requires the four unities (time, title, interest, possession) and must be expressly created. Two people who inherit separate shares from different owners at different times do not become joint tenants — they are tenants in common. The breaker: nothing in the facts created a new joint tenancy between Esther and Daniel.

## 8. Black-Letter Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** A joint tenancy with right of survivorship may be severed by the unilateral conveyance of one joint tenant's interest to a third party. Upon severance, the ownership converts to a tenancy in common, and the right of survivorship is extinguished. *See* 20 Am. Jur. 2d Cotenancy and Joint Ownership § 19; *Weisblat v. Feldman*, 358 So. 3d 1238 (Fla. 2023); Restatement (Third) of Property: Wills and Other Donative Transfers § 17.2.

**Application:** Ruth's quitclaim deed to Esther conveyed Ruth's undivided interest. A quitclaim deed transfers whatever interest the grantor holds — here, Ruth's joint tenancy share. The conveyance to a third party (Esther) destroyed the unity of title, severing the joint tenancy. Esther became a tenant in common with Naomi. When Ruth died, her interest was already Esther's. When Naomi died, her tenancy-in-common share passed via her will to Daniel. Result: Esther and Daniel as tenants in common.

**Credited outcome unchanged:** The original question tests joint tenancy severance by quitclaim and its effect on survivorship. The credited answer (D — tenants in common) is correct under standard property law. The variation preserves the identical rule, issue, and outcome.

**Authority:** Restatement (Third) of Property: Wills and Other Donative Transfers § 17.2; 20 Am. Jur. 2d Cotenancy and Joint Ownership § 19; *Weisblat v. Feldman*, 358 So. 3d 1238 (Fla. 2023).
