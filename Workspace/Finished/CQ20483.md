Daniel asked Lydia to steal a box of rare hymnals from Peter's private Christian bookshop after closing, promising to pay her $2,400 if she delivered them by Friday. Lydia replied, "I'm in. ### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam-Register)

**B is correct.** Daniel's request was solicitation when he asked Lydia to steal the hymnals. Once Lydia agreed, that same criminal communication ripened into a conspiracy. **Gold Key:** solicitation is the ask; conspiracy is the agreement. When the ask succeeds and becomes the agreement, the solicitation merges upward into the conspiracy.

Daniel may be convicted of conspiracy to commit larceny. He may not also be convicted of solicitation to commit larceny for the same conversation. **Silver Key:** when a merger answer mentions both solicitation and conspiracy, first ask whether the other person agreed. If yes, keep the conspiracy and drop the solicitation.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A** sells a real-sounding non-merger rule. It is attractive because students correctly remember that conspiracy often does not merge into the completed target offense. The breaker is direction: this question is not asking whether conspiracy merges into larceny. It asks whether Daniel can stack solicitation on top of the conspiracy that formed when Lydia agreed. **Gold Key:** successful solicitation merges into conspiracy.

**C** sells a withdrawal/overt-act escape route. It is attractive because the stem mentions a later meeting and no completed theft, so students may look for a before-the-act defense. The breaker is that Daniel did not withdraw, and the charge-pair question turns on merger, not on whether a later overt act occurred.

**D** sells the separate-offenses shortcut. It is attractive because solicitation and conspiracy do have different labels and different elements. The breaker is the same-conversation merger rule. Once Lydia accepted the criminal request, the solicitation was absorbed into the conspiracy for conviction purposes.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule.** At common law for bar-exam purposes, solicitation is complete when the defendant intentionally asks, commands, encourages, or hires another person to commit a crime. If the person solicited agrees to commit the crime, the same conduct forms a conspiracy and the solicitation merges into that conspiracy. The defendant may be convicted of the conspiracy, but not both solicitation and conspiracy for that same criminal objective.

**Authority floor.** The merger result is consistent with Model Penal Code section 5.05(3), which bars conviction for more than one inchoate offense for conduct designed to culminate in the same crime. Modern statutory analogues state the same rule, including 18 Pa. Cons. Stat. section 906 and Tenn. Code Ann. section 39-12-106(a), each barring multiple convictions among attempt, solicitation, and conspiracy for the same criminal objective. Cornell Wex also summarizes the general inchoate-offense merger frame and the special non-merger rule for conspiracy with the completed target offense.
