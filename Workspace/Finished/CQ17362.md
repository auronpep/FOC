## Full Right-Answer Explanation

**B is correct.** The decisive place is the distant plot, not the cottage. The officers saw the poppies roughly 400 yards from the home, beyond the ravine and locked chain. That is open field, not the home or its curtilage. **Gold Key:** open fields are outside Fourth Amendment protection even when the owner tries to keep people out with signs, fences, chains, or private-property language. **Silver Key:** in a search/no-search call, ask whether the place is protected before asking whether the police trespassed or had a warrant. Because the observation occurred in an open field, it was not a Fourth Amendment search.

## Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule is the Fourth Amendment open-fields doctrine. The Fourth Amendment protects persons, houses, papers, effects, and the home's curtilage; it does not protect open fields beyond the curtilage. In *Hester v. United States*, 265 U.S. 57 (1924), the Supreme Court recognized that Fourth Amendment protection does not extend to open fields. In *Oliver v. United States*, 466 U.S. 170 (1984), the Court reaffirmed the doctrine and applied it even though officers passed a locked gate and no-trespassing sign to view marijuana in a field. In *United States v. Dunn*, 480 U.S. 294 (1987), the Court distinguished curtilage from open fields and identified the usual curtilage factors: proximity to the home, enclosure, domestic use, and steps taken to shield observation.
