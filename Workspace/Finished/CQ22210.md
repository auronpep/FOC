## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

After a late-night gospel concert, sheriff's deputies stopped a van being driven by Daniel and forced him to open the rear cargo compartment. Inside they found a canvas duffel containing several pounds of methamphetamine. They seized the van and the drugs and placed Daniel under arrest. Without advising him of his rights to remain silent and to consult with an attorney, they questioned him about the drugs. During the questioning, Daniel said, "I do not know anything about them. It is not even my van." Daniel was charged with unlawful possession of a controlled substance. Later, Daniel's motion to suppress the methamphetamine was granted, and the charges were dismissed. Daniel then brought an appropriate civil action against the sheriff's department to recover the seized van. During his direct case, Daniel testified that he owned the van but had registered it in Timothy's name for convenience. On cross-examination, the department's lawyer asked, "After your arrest, did you tell the deputies that it was not your van?" If Daniel's lawyer objects to this question, should the objection be sustained?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

In a civil suit to recover a seized vehicle, may the opponent impeach the plaintiff with his earlier unwarned statement denying ownership when he now testifies that he owned the vehicle?

B. No, because Daniel's denial of ownership was a prior inconsistent statement.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

Choice B is the clean impeachment answer. Daniel told the deputies the van was not his, then testified in his civil recovery case that he owned it. That is a classic credibility contradiction, so the cross-examiner may ask about it as a prior inconsistent statement. Gold Key: a voluntary statement taken in violation of Miranda is excluded from the prosecution's criminal case-in-chief, but it may still be used to impeach the defendant's later inconsistent testimony. Silver Key: when the call asks whether an objection to a question should be sustained, lock onto the use being made of the statement. Here the statement is being used to test Daniel's credibility, not as substantive proof that he committed the drug offense.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

Choice A sounds lawyerly because it reaches for a familiar exclusionary-rule phrase. The breaker is that "fruit of the poisonous tree" is an exclusion doctrine, not a reason to let the question in, so it cannot explain why the objection should be overruled.

Choice C tempts students who see that a suppression motion succeeded and want that ruling to do all the work. The breaker is that the granted motion suppressed the methamphetamine in the criminal case, not this later impeachment use of Daniel's own inconsistent statement in the civil recovery action.

Choice D rides the strongest bait in the set because Miranda violations usually signal exclusion. Gold Key: that bar blocks substantive use in the prosecution's case-in-chief, but it does not erase impeachment use of a voluntary inconsistent statement once Daniel takes the stand and tells the opposite story.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome remain unchanged from the source row. Fed. R. Evid. 613 allows examination of a witness about the witness's prior statement, and a prior inconsistent statement is the standard impeachment use. Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), bars unwarned custodial statements from the prosecution's case-in-chief in a criminal prosecution. Harris v. New York, 401 U.S. 222 (1971), and Oregon v. Hass, 420 U.S. 714 (1975), hold that a voluntary statement obtained in violation of Miranda may still be used to impeach the defendant's inconsistent testimony. Because Daniel's cross-examination question targets his credibility after he testified to ownership, the credited answer remains that the objection should not be sustained.

Authority note: the civil-proceeding extension is an inference from the primary authorities above plus the ordinary Rule 613 impeachment rule. I did not locate a Supreme Court case squarely framed as this exact later civil return-of-property posture, so that narrow extension should be treated as attorney-review-worthy even though it does not change the credited outcome.
