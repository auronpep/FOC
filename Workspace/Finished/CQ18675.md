### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** An easement appurtenant "runs with the land" — the benefit passes automatically to successors of the dominant estate, and the burden passes automatically to successors of the servient estate. The deed does not need to say the rights are transferable; transferability is inherent in the nature of the interest.

The original deed from the development company to Barnabas created an easement appurtenant: a private dock right benefiting the cottage (dominant estate) and burdening the lakefront parcel (servient estate). Because the dock right was tied to ownership of the cottage — not to Barnabas personally — it is an easement appurtenant, not an easement in gross. Easements appurtenant are freely alienable and pass automatically with conveyance of the dominant estate. When Barnabas sold the cottage to Lydia, the benefit of the easement passed to Lydia. When the development company sold the lakefront parcel to the resort, the burden passed to the resort. The resort is bound by the easement even though it was not a party to the original deed and even though the deed never mentioned transferability.

**Gold Key:** Easement appurtenant → runs with the land → no express transferability language required.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (the resort, because it was not a party to the original deed):** This is attractive because it feels fair — how can the resort be bound by a deal it never made? But an easement appurtenant is a property right that runs with the land, not a personal contract. Once the easement is created, the burden attaches to the servient parcel itself. Every successor to the servient estate takes subject to the easement, regardless of whether they signed the original deed. The resort bought the lakefront parcel knowing (or should have known) what encumbrances came with it.

**B (the resort, because the deed did not state that the dock rights were transferable):** This is the dominant trap. It preys on the intuition that property rights must be expressly transferable. But the whole point of an easement appurtenant is that it is tied to the dominant estate, not to the person who created it. Transferability is automatic by operation of law — the deed does not need to say so. Students who pick this one are likely confusing easements appurtenant with licenses or easements in gross, which are personal and do not automatically transfer. **Gold Key:** Easement appurtenant → runs with the land → no express transferability language required.

**D (Lydia, because she acquired the dock rights by adverse possession):** This is attractive because adverse possession is the other doctrine students learn for acquiring rights to someone else's land. But adverse possession requires use that is actual, open and notorious, exclusive, continuous, and *hostile* (without permission). Here, Barnabas and Lydia used the dock with the development company's permission — the deed granted the right. Permissive use can never ripen into adverse possession, no matter how long it continues.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** An easement appurtenant is a nonpossessory right that benefits one parcel (the dominant estate) and burdens another (the servient estate). It is created by express grant, implication, necessity, or prescription. Once created, an easement appurtenant "runs with the land," meaning both the benefit and the burden pass automatically to successor owners upon conveyance. No express language of transferability is required.

**Authority:** Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 4.8 (2000) (servitudes are interpreted to carry the intent of the parties; absent express terms to the contrary, an appurtenant easement transfers with the dominant estate). The Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 1.2 defines an easement as a servitude that creates a nonpossessory right of use in another's land. Under § 5.1, the benefit of an appurtenant easement passes to transferees of the dominant estate. See also *Lexawise*, "Easement Appurtenant: Definition, Types & Examples" ("An easement appurtenant runs with the land, meaning it automatically transfers to future owners of both the dominant and servient estates."); *Beresford Booth*, "Appurtenant Easements vs. Easements in Gross" ("The grantor and grantee necessarily can transfer their rights under appurtenant easements to other parties (typically successor owners). The easements are tied to the land, or as commonly stated, 'run with the land.'").

**Verification:** The tested rule (easement appurtenant creation and automatic transfer), the legal issue (whether a successor to the dominant estate can enforce the easement against a successor to the servient estate when the deed is silent on transferability), and the credited outcome (the successor prevails) are all unchanged from the original. Research confirms the official key. **PASS.**
