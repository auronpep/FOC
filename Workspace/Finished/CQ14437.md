## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

On November 29, Lydia, who ran a private Bible-memory tutoring program, received from Barnabas, a merchant who sold devotional craft supplies, an illustrated price sheet and a signed letter. The signed letter stated:

> “For every item listed on the enclosed sheet that you request during the next calendar year, we will sell you as many as you order. We promise that this offer and these listed prices will stay firm for that year.”

Barnabas and Lydia exchanged no further messages until March 29, four months later, when Barnabas received Lydia’s emailed purchase order for “300 olive-wood verse-card boxes, item #40 on your sheet.”

Did Lydia’s March 29 email constitute an effective acceptance of Barnabas’s offer at the prices listed on the sheet?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A merchant promised in a signed writing to keep goods prices firm for one year, no consideration was paid, and the buyer ordered after four months before any revocation. Was the order an effective acceptance at the listed prices?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. Yes, because Barnabas’s letter created a one-year option contract.

B. No, because Lydia did not accept Barnabas’s offer within a reasonable time.

C. Yes, because Barnabas had not revoked the offer before March 29.

D. No, because under applicable law the irrevocability of Barnabas’s offer was limited to three months.

### 5. Correct answer

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is the best answer. Lydia’s email came after the three-month firm-offer protection had expired, so Barnabas was free to revoke. But Barnabas had not revoked, and the offer itself still invited orders during the next calendar year.

**Gold Key:** The three-month firm-offer cap ends irrevocability only. It does not automatically kill the offer.

**Silver Key:** Separate “irrevocable” from “open.” The call asks whether the March 29 order accepted before revocation or lapse, not whether the offer was still firm.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells a familiar label: an option contract keeps an offer open. The breaker is that the signed merchant assurance was not supported by consideration, so it did not create a one-year option contract; it created only limited firm-offer protection.

**B.** This choice sounds careful because four months feels late. The breaker is the stem’s own ordering window: Barnabas invited orders during the next calendar year, and nothing in the stem says that the offer had lapsed before March 29.

**D.** This is the dominant trap because it remembers the hard rule. **Gold Key:** the three-month cap limits irrevocability, not the life of the offer. **Silver Key:** this answer proves only that Barnabas could revoke after month three; it does not prove that he did revoke or that the offer lapsed before Lydia’s order.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

Under UCC § 2-205, a merchant’s signed writing assuring that an offer for goods will be held open is not revocable for lack of consideration during the stated time or a reasonable time, but the period of irrevocability cannot exceed three months. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) Under UCC § 2-206, unless the offer unambiguously says otherwise, an offer invites acceptance in any manner and medium reasonable in the circumstances. ([Legal Information Institute][2])

The correct legal result is unchanged. The offer became revocable after three months because no consideration supported a one-year option. But revocable does not mean revoked. Barnabas did not revoke before March 29, and Lydia accepted within the stated next-calendar-year ordering window. The credited outcome remains yes.
