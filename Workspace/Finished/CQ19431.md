### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The court will say **No** — a standard 14-day TRO is not ordinarily immediately appealable as of right. The federal interlocutory-appeal statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1), allows immediate appeal of orders "granting, continuing, modifying, refusing or dissolving injunctions," but the Supreme Court has read "injunction" in that subsection to mean a preliminary (or permanent) injunction, not a short-term TRO issued under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 65(b). The TRO is a brief, emergency, status-quo-preserving order that does not end the litigation; the restrained party ordinarily must wait until the preliminary-injunction stage (or final judgment) to seek review. If the order's features (length, evidentiary basis, post-hearing character) make it function like a preliminary injunction, an appeal may be available; nothing in the present facts suggests that. **Gold Key:** a standard 14-day Rule 65(b) TRO is not an "injunction" within § 1292(a)(1) and is therefore not immediately appealable as of right. **Silver Key:** when the answer array is 2×2 (Yes/No × overclaim/qualified), name the qualified answer as the residual and let the absolutes knock each other out.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Yes, because final judgment has been entered.**

This answer moves the appealability ground from § 1292(a)(1) (injunctions) to § 1291 (final judgments). The breaker is structural: a TRO does not end the case. The litigation between Mary and Paul continues; the TRO is a status-quo order, not a disposition. A student who reaches for "final judgment" has skipped the basic fact that the underlying suit is still pending and the preliminary-injunction hearing is still ahead.

**C. No, because injunction-related orders are never appealable.**

This is the mirror-image overclaim. The student has registered the correct direction (No) but overreaches the rule. The breaker is the exception: § 1292(a)(1) does make preliminary-injunction orders immediately appealable, and the Gold Key explains that the appeal-as-of-right turns on whether the order functions like a preliminary injunction. "Never appealable" is the same overclaim shape as A's "every order restricting conduct" — just in the opposite direction.

**D. Yes, because every order restricting conduct is appealable.**

This is the dominant trap. The student hears "order restricting conduct" and treats appealability as a default. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: appealability is not a function of how the order feels to the party restrained; it is a function of whether the order fits one of the narrow statutory categories (final judgment under § 1291, injunction under § 1292(a)(1), certified question under § 1292(b), or a collateral order under Cohen). A short-term Rule 65(b) TRO fits none of them on the bare facts given.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 19431, a Civil Procedure item on the appealability of a standard 14-day temporary restraining order. The official key is C: the TRO is not ordinarily immediately appealable absent features making it function like a preliminary injunction. The original explanations confirm that "ordinary short-term TROs usually await later review" and that preliminary-injunction orders are immediately appealable.

The governing statutory authority is 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1), which gives the courts of appeals jurisdiction over appeals from "[i]nterlocutory orders of the district courts ... granting, continuing, modifying, refusing or dissolving injunctions, or refusing to dissolve or modify injunctions." Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 65(b) governs TROs and limits them to 14 days, issued without notice or on shortened notice, to preserve the status quo until a preliminary-injunction hearing can be held.

The Supreme Court has held that a short-term TRO entered under Rule 65(b) is not an "injunction" within the meaning of § 1292(a)(1) and therefore is not immediately appealable as of right. In *Sampson v. Murray*, 415 U.S. 61 (1974), the Court treated a 10-day TRO as outside § 1292(a)(1), emphasizing that TROs are brief, informal, and ex parte in character and that interlocutory appeal would undermine the trial court's case-management role. The Court reaffirmed the principle that immediate appeal under § 1292(a)(1) is reserved for orders functioning as preliminary injunctions, not for short-term status-quo orders. Lower courts have applied the same rule to confirm that an ordinary short-term TRO does not become appealable merely because it constrains the restrained party's conduct.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original. A standard 14-day Rule 65(b) TRO entered to preserve the status quo pending a preliminary-injunction hearing is not immediately appealable as of right. Only when the order's features (length, evidentiary basis, post-hearing character) make it function like a preliminary injunction does the § 1292(a)(1) appeal-as-of-right attach. The present facts — a 14-day order preserving the status quo with a preliminary-injunction hearing still ahead — fit the ordinary case that awaits later review.
