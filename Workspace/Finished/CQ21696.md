## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

Appropriation — one of the four invasion-of-privacy torts — protects a person's right to control the commercial use of their identity. The Restatement (Second) of Torts § 652C provides: "One who appropriates to his own use or benefit the name or likeness of another is subject to liability." Critically, "likeness" is not confined to a person's legal name or photograph. Courts have long held that a distinctive voice, signature sound, or other recognizable feature of a person's identity can be appropriated when used for commercial advantage without consent. In *Midler v. Ford Motor Co.*, 849 F.2d 460 (9th Cir. 1988), the Ninth Circuit held that a professional singer's distinctive voice was protected against unauthorized imitation in a commercial advertisement, even though neither her name nor photograph appeared.

Here, the company hired a performer to copy the guitarist's distinctive vibrato and signature lick specifically because those features evoke the guitarist's identity. Listeners recognized the identity. The use was commercial (promoting a guitar-instruction product). No consent was obtained. The claim is valid.

**Gold Key (Appropriation Identity Floor):** Appropriation protects *any* distinctive aspect of a person's identity — voice, signature sound, readily recognizable imitation — used for commercial advantage. It is not limited to name, photograph, or likeness in the narrow sense. The test is whether the defendant used a feature sufficiently distinctive to identify the plaintiff, for the defendant's benefit, without consent.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**(A) No, because the ad did not use the guitarist's legal name or photograph.**

This choice sells the misconception that appropriation is limited to the literal use of a person's name or photograph. The stem practically invites this reading by emphasizing the guitarist was "never named." But the law reaches further: a distinctive voice, musical signature, or recognizable imitation can constitute the person's "identity" for purposes of appropriation. *Midler v. Ford* is directly on point — a sound-alike imitation used in advertising was actionable appropriation even without any use of name or photo. This is the **dominant trap** because it is the most natural mistake: the student reads "no name, no photo" and concludes there is no claim.

**(B) No, because the performer's imitation was a tribute and did not harm the guitarist's reputation.**

This is the "common-sense fairness" trap. A student may feel that a tribute or homage shouldn't be punishable, especially when the original artist's reputation isn't damaged. But appropriation is not a defamation claim. It protects the right to control commercial use of one's identity — a property-like interest in exclusive use — regardless of whether the use flatters or disapproves. The defendant's motive (tribute or attack) is irrelevant. What matters is unauthorized commercial benefit.

**(D) Yes, but only if the ad stated a false fact about the guitarist.**

This choice invents a falsity requirement that does not exist. Appropriation is about unauthorized *use* of identity, not about false statements. A defendant can appropriate a person's identity through a completely truthful endorsement — or even a flattering one — and still be liable. The tort turns on commercial exploitation without consent, not on misrepresentation. Adding a falsity element confuses appropriation with defamation.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule — Restatement (Second) of Torts § 652C:**

> "One who appropriates to his own use or benefit the name or likeness of another is subject to liability to the other for invasion of his privacy."

**Elements (majority/common-law):** (1) the defendant used the plaintiff's name, likeness, or other distinctive aspect of identity; (2) the use was for the defendant's benefit (typically commercial); (3) the plaintiff did not consent; (4) the use would be objectively identifiable as the plaintiff to the relevant audience.

**Extension beyond name/photograph:** Section 652C protects "name or likeness," and courts have interpreted "likeness" broadly. In *Midler v. Ford Motor Co.*, 849 F.2d 460 (9th Cir. 1988), the court held that a singer's voice — though uncopyrightable — was a distinctive feature of her identity protectable from commercial imitation. The court reasoned that "a voice is as distinctive and personal as a face" and that the defendant's deliberate imitation for advertising purposes constituted appropriation. See also *Waits v. Frito-Lay, Inc.*, 978 F.2d 1093 (9th Cir. 1992) (Tom Waits's distinctive singing sound-alike in a commercial held actionable).

**Statutory overlay:** Many states (e.g., Cal. Civ. Code § 3344; N.Y. Civ. Rights Law §§ 50–51) enumerate "voice" explicitly as a protectable attribute, but the common-law tort under § 652C already reaches voice and imitation through the "likeness" concept.

**No falsity required:** Unlike defamation or false light, appropriation does not require the plaintiff to prove any false statement. The harm is the unauthorized commercial exploitation itself.

**No reputation harm required:** The right is property-like. The plaintiff need not show reputational damage — only that the identity was used for commercial benefit without consent.

**Authority floor met:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 652C (primary rule); *Midler v. Ford Motor Co.*, 849 F.2d 460 (9th Cir. 1988) leading case extending appropriation to voice imitation.

**Conclusion:** The original key (A) / transformed key (C) is correct. The credited answer's legal substance is unchanged: appropriation reaches voice and recognizable imitation used commercially without consent. The element of "no name or photograph" does not defeat the claim.

**Choice B: "No, because the performer's imitation was a tribute and did not harm the guitarist's reputation."**

**Choice D: "Yes, but only if the ad stated a false fact about the guitarist."**

**If a student picked B:**
- **Misconception:** Confuses appropriation with defamation; believes harm to reputation is required.
- **Recovery:** Review Gold Key GK-TORTS-APPROPRIATION-02. Appropriation protects a property-like interest in exclusive use of identity. The plaintiff need not show reputational damage. Drill: compare the elements of appropriation vs. defamation side by side.

**If a student picked D:**
- **Misconception:** Adds a falsity requirement to appropriation.
- **Recovery:** Review Gold Key GK-TORTS-APPROPRIATION-02. Appropriation is about unauthorized *use*, not false statements. A completely truthful endorsement can still be appropriation if unauthorized. Drill: identify which torts require falsity (defamation, false light) and which don't (appropriation, intrusion).
