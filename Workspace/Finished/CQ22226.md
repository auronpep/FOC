7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

B is correct. The privilege question turns on the scope of physician-patient privilege, not on whether Hannah was speaking voluntarily or whether the doctor asked a question that might expose a crime. Gold Key: physician-patient privilege protects confidential information needed for diagnosis or treatment, not an extraneous confession about unrelated wrongdoing. Here, the incriminating detail that Hannah was trying to force open the ticket-office safe was a non-medical matter, so it falls outside the privilege. Silver Key: in a 2-yes/2-no array like this, cut the voluntariness answer and the interrogation answer first, then ask whether the disputed detail helped medical care or just exposed misconduct.

8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A. A feels tempting because Hannah plainly volunteered the admission and begged for secrecy right afterward. The breaker is that voluntariness does not create privilege; the real question is whether the content was treatment-related, and the safe-opening confession was not.

C. C offers a criminal-procedure flavor that can feel sharp because the doctor asked a question that drew out incriminating information. Silver Key: this item is not about interrogation law at all, so an answer asking whether the question was likely to elicit criminal information is solving a different problem.

D. D is the dominant trap because the statement arose in the treatment room right after the doctor asked how the wound happened. Gold Key: privilege protects medical information needed for diagnosis or treatment, not every extra fact blurted out during treatment; the confession about forcing open the safe was the non-medical part that breaks the privilege claim.

9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited outcome remains unchanged from the original row: the objection should not be sustained. The tested issue is the scope of physician-patient privilege in a jurisdiction that recognizes that privilege by statute or rule. Representative statutory formulations limit the privilege to confidential communications made for diagnosis or treatment, or to information reasonably necessary or helpful for those medical purposes. Kansas Statutes Annotated section 60-427(b)(2) protects a communication only when the patient or physician reasonably believed it was necessary or helpful to enable diagnosis or treatment. Utah Rule of Evidence 506(b) protects information communicated in confidence to a physician for the purpose of diagnosing or treating the patient.

Applied here, the medically relevant portion of Hannah's answer was that she had been crouching when shot, because that explained the bullet path. But the added confession that she was trying to force open the ticket-office safe was not needed to diagnose or treat the gunshot wound. That incriminating detail was a non-medical matter, so it falls outside the privilege's protected scope. The defense objection therefore should be overruled.

Voluntariness does not change that result. A voluntary statement can still be privileged if it is treatment-related, and a voluntary statement can still be unprivileged if it is outside the medical purpose. The criminal-interrogation framing in choice C is also irrelevant because this is a civil evidentiary privilege question, not a Miranda question.
