### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Timothy committed an assault by making an immediate battery threat with a raised fist, and that assault caused Hannah's death. The death was unintended, and the facts do not add malice, intent to kill, or a qualifying felony. Gold Key: An unlawful assault or battery that causes an unintended death is the involuntary-manslaughter lane unless the facts add murder-grade malice or felony-murder facts. Silver Key: In a "most serious crime" call, do not stop at the lesser assault once the stem says the victim died; climb to the homicide grade that matches the mental state.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

C. Murder is the dominant trap because a frightening threat caused an actual death, and the answer choice feels like the most serious available label. Gold Key: The missing upgrade is malice or felony-murder facts; trying to recover one's own ring is not robbery, and the assault-based unintended death fits involuntary manslaughter instead.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The governing rule is unchanged. Common-law involuntary manslaughter covers an unintended killing caused by criminal negligence or by an unlawful act not amounting to murder, including the misdemeanor-manslaughter/unlawful-act path. The authority floor supports the grading logic: Model Penal Code sec. 210.1 treats purposely, knowingly, recklessly, or negligently causing death as criminal homicide; MPC sec. 210.3 covers reckless manslaughter and sec. 210.4 covers negligent homicide. Pennsylvania's codified formulation similarly defines involuntary manslaughter as causing death as a direct result of an unlawful act done recklessly or grossly negligently, or a lawful act done recklessly or grossly negligently. The MBE common-law label remains involuntary manslaughter. Nothing in the variant adds intent to kill, malice, a qualifying felony, robbery, or adequate provocation.
