## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Lydia walked up to a private vendor booth at a Christian music festival where Peter was selling study Bibles and hymn journals from a cash lockbox. Before Peter could ask what she wanted to buy, Lydia leaned over the counter and said, "Daniel is on the parking-deck stairwell with a crossbow. He can see your hands. If you do not follow my instructions, he will shoot. Put every bill from the lockbox into this canvas envelope and slide it under the counter."

Peter could not see anyone on the stairwell and did not know whether anyone was there. Not wanting to risk being injured, he put $740 from the lockbox into the envelope and handed it to Lydia. Lydia left with the money, but she was stopped nearby. Daniel did not exist; Lydia had lied and had acted alone.

Of what crime or crimes can Lydia be convicted?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A taker lies about an armed accomplice and uses that immediate threat to make a victim hand over cash; can the taker be convicted of robbery, larceny, both, or a deception-only property crime?

B. Robbery or larceny.

C. Embezzlement.

D. Robbery and larceny.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Lydia can be convicted of robbery because she took another person's money by intimidation: Peter surrendered the cash because Lydia threatened immediate bodily harm through a supposed armed accomplice. Gold Key: robbery is larceny aggravated by force or intimidation, and larceny merges into robbery for the same taking. Silver Key: after identifying the force/fear taking, do not stop at "both elements are present"; check whether one answer improperly stacks the greater and lesser offense.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells the deception fact. Lydia lied about Daniel and the crossbow, so the answer sounds like a fraud-style property crime. The breaker is the way Peter surrendered the money: he yielded to an immediate threat, not to a title-transfer bargain. Gold Key: false pretenses requires obtaining title by deception; this fact pattern gives Lydia possession by intimidation.

C sells the cash-handling label. The money moved from Peter's lockbox into Lydia's hands, so it tempts a student to think any later keeping is embezzlement. The breaker is that Lydia never had lawful possession before the conversion. Gold Key: embezzlement starts with lawful possession; a threat-based taking does not.

D sells the strongest trap. Robbery contains larceny, so it feels precise to list both crimes. The breaker is merger: for the same taking, larceny is the lesser-included offense inside robbery. Silver Key: when the array offers "robbery or larceny" and "robbery and larceny," the fight is not whether larceny elements exist; it is whether the exam permits two convictions for one taking.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. At common law, robbery is larceny from the person or presence of another by force or intimidation. Model Penal Code section 222.1 similarly treats robbery as theft accompanied by infliction, threat, or fear of immediate serious bodily injury. Model Penal Code section 223.2 confirms the theft/larceny core: unlawful taking or control of movable property of another with purpose to deprive. Lydia's lie did not need to be true; Peter surrendered the cash because he feared immediate harm. That supplies intimidation and a trespassory taking. False pretenses does not fit because title did not pass by consent to a bargain. Embezzlement does not fit because Lydia never had lawful possession before the taking. The lesser-included larceny merges into robbery for the same incident under the same-elements principle associated with Blockburger v. United States, 284 U.S. 299 (1932), and Brown v. Ohio, 432 U.S. 161 (1977).
