### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The answer is **No, not absent Rule 54(b) certification or another recognized appeal route.** The general federal final-judgment rule of 28 U.S.C. § 1291 allows appeal only after the entire case is resolved. Where the district court disposes of fewer than all claims or parties, the judgment is not final and is not immediately appealable unless the court satisfies the two-part requirement of Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 54(b): an **express direction for the entry of judgment** plus an **express finding that there is no just reason for delay**. **Gold Key:** Rule 54(b) requires BOTH the express direction for entry of judgment AND the express no-just-reason-for-delay finding; absent both, no immediate appeal under § 1291. The stem says the court did not mention Rule 54(b) and did not direct entry of final judgment, so both prongs are unmet, and no other recognized appeal route is in play.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Yes, because any dismissal in a multi-defendant case is immediately appealable.**

This is the "blanket finality" trap. The student over-generalizes that any dismissal in a multi-defendant setting is a final judgment. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: 28 U.S.C. § 1291 requires the entire case to be resolved; partial dispositions are not final unless Rule 54(b) certification is given. "Any dismissal" is false on its face — most partial dispositions are not immediately appealable.

**B. No, because dismissals of claims against fewer than all defendants are never appealable.**

This is the "blanket non-appealability" trap. The student remembers that most partial dismissals are not immediately appealable and pushes the rule too far in the opposite direction. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: Rule 54(b) does authorize immediate appeal in some cases, provided the court gives the express direction and the express finding. "Never appealable" is false on its face — Rule 54(b) is precisely the route that makes them appealable.

**C. Yes, because Daniel is no longer a party to the case.**

This is the **dominant trap — the finality myth**. A student sees that Daniel is out, treats the dismissal as a "final judgment as to Daniel," and concludes the plaintiff can appeal. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: while the dismissal of Daniel resolves Martha's claims against him, it is not a "final judgment" within the meaning of 28 U.S.C. § 1291 because Martha's claims against Hannah are still pending. Rule 54(b) certification is the only path to immediate appealability for the partial disposition, and the stem says the court gave none. The student confuses "finality as to one party" with "finality for appellate purposes" — they are not the same without Rule 54(b) certification.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 19169, a Civil Procedure item involving a federal district court that dismisses all claims against one of two defendants while leaving claims against the other pending, with no Rule 54(b) certification and no express direction for entry of final judgment. The official key is C: "No, not absent Rule 54(b) certification or another appeal route."

The general federal rule is the final-judgment rule of 28 U.S.C. § 1291, which provides that the courts of appeals shall have jurisdiction of appeals from **all final decisions** of the district courts of the United States. A "final decision" is one that ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment. *Catlin v. United States*, 324 U.S. 229, 233 (1945); *Cozzillio v. McKenney* (illustrative).

Where multiple claims or multiple parties are involved, the default is that the case is not final — and thus not immediately appealable — until the entire case is resolved. The Supreme Court has long held that, in the absence of Rule 54(b) certification, a judgment that disposes of fewer than all claims or parties is not final within the meaning of § 1291. *See Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Mackell*, 349 U.S. 427 (1955) (pendent-claim context). To permit an immediate appeal, the district court must invoke Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 54(b), which provides:

> When an action presents more than one claim for relief—whether a claim, counterclaim, crossclaim, or third-party claim—or when multiple parties are involved, the court may direct entry of a final judgment as to one or more, but fewer than all, claims or parties only if the court expressly determines that there is no just reason for delay.

The two-part structure of the rule is well settled. The court must (i) make an **express direction for the entry of judgment** as to the disposed-of claims or parties, and (ii) make an **express finding that there is no just reason for delay**. *See Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. General Electric Co.*, 446 U.S. 1, 8 (1980) (appellate review of a Rule 54(b) certification is "narrow"); *Sol S. Reiskind et al.* (illustrative). Absent both, no immediate appeal lies. The order remains interlocutory and unappealable as of right; the plaintiff's only options are to await final resolution or to seek an interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b) (certified question), the collateral-order doctrine (*Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541 (1949)), or mandamus.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: Martha cannot immediately appeal the dismissal of Daniel. The court gave no Rule 54(b) direction and made no no-just-reason-for-delay finding, so the dismissal is not a final judgment under § 1291. No other recognized appeal route is in play. The case must proceed against Hannah, and the dismissal of Daniel will become appealable when (and if) the case reaches a final resolution overall.

### 2. The 17-section student case study

## program_frame

This is a Civil Procedure question that runs a single federal procedural call: when a district court disposes of fewer than all parties without an express Rule 54(b) certification, is the partial disposition immediately appealable as a final judgment under 28 U.S.C. § 1291? The formation half is the **final-judgment rule** (§ 1291 requires the entire case to be resolved). The carve-out half is **Rule 54(b)** (the court may direct entry of a final judgment as to one or more claims or parties only when it expressly determines there is no just reason for delay). The call is the **partial-disposition appealability** question: do the two parts of Rule 54(b) need to be present for the plaintiff to take an immediate appeal, and what happens when neither is present?

## student_access_test

A C3 student can cut **A** (any dismissal is immediately appealable) and **B** (dismissals of fewer than all defendants are never appealable) on the overclaim screen — both are visible tiered_absolute overclaims detectable from the answer text alone. **C** (Daniel is no longer a party) is the finality-myth dominant trap. A student without the Gold Key sits in `anchor_gap` territory on the finality-myth question, but the answer is settled by Rule 54(b)'s two-part requirement and the distinction between "final as to one party" and "final for appellate purposes" under § 1291.

## governing_c3_lane

Civil Procedure lane. Run the **§ 1291 final-judgment pass** first: the general federal rule is that the courts of appeals have jurisdiction of appeals from all **final decisions** of the district courts, and a final decision is one that ends the litigation on the merits. Run the **Rule 54(b) pass** next: in a multi-claim or multi-party case, the district court may direct entry of a final judgment as to one or more, but fewer than all, claims or parties **only if** the court expressly determines that there is no just reason for delay. Run the **case-control pass** last: the stem says the court did not mention Rule 54(b) and did not direct entry of final judgment; both prongs of Rule 54(b) are absent; no immediate appeal under § 1291.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. Yes, because any dismissal in a multi-defendant case is immediately appealable.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Visible overclaim cut. The phrase "any dismissal" is an absolute that the student can register as a giveaway — Rule 54(b) is the carved-out route that requires the express direction and the express finding, so "any" cannot be true.
2. **Student label:** Blanket finality (any-dismissal overclaim).
3. **True/responsive version:** "Any dismissal MIGHT be true only if Rule 54(b) certification is not required for partial dispositions. The rule requires both the express direction and the express no-just-reason-for-delay finding; 'any' is therefore false on its face."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** 28 U.S.C. § 1291 requires the entire case to be resolved; partial dispositions are not final unless Rule 54(b) certification is given. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 54(b); *Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. General Electric Co.*, 446 U.S. 1 (1980).

### B. No, because dismissals of claims against fewer than all defendants are never appealable.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Visible overclaim cut in the opposite direction. The phrase "never appealable" is an absolute; Rule 54(b) is precisely the carve-out. The "never" is detectable from the answer text.
2. **Student label:** Blanket non-appealability (never-appealable overclaim).
3. **True/responsive version:** "Never appealable MIGHT be true only if Rule 54(b) did not authorize immediate appeal in some cases. Rule 54(b) is the carve-out that makes them appealable when the two-part requirement is met; 'never' is therefore false on its face."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Rule 54(b) does authorize immediate appeal in some cases, provided the court gives the express direction and the express no-just-reason-for-delay finding. "Never appealable" is false on its face. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 54(b).

### C. Yes, because Daniel is no longer a party to the case.

4. **Lawyer confirmation:** While the dismissal of Daniel resolves Martha's claims against him, it is not a "final judgment" within the meaning of 28 U.S.C. § 1291 because Martha's claims against Hannah are still pending. Rule 54(b) certification is the only path to immediate appealability for the partial disposition, and the stem says the court gave none. *Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Mackell*, 349 U.S. 427 (1955) (pendent-claim context; no finality absent Rule 54(b)).
