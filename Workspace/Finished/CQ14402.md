# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question

Ruth owns a Christian bookstore that regularly buys gift items for resale. Paul operates a studio that makes ceramic Scripture mugs for retailers. Ruth telephoned Paul and offered to buy, at $12 each, a minimum of 600 and a maximum of 1,200 Scripture mugs, to be delivered in 20 days. Paul orally accepted and promptly sent Ruth this signed note, which Ruth received two days later: "This confirms our telephone agreement today to sell you 600 ceramic Scripture mugs for 20-day delivery. /s/ Paul."

Eighteen days later, Paul tendered 1,200 conforming Scripture mugs. Ruth rejected all of them because another supplier had offered a lower price. Because local shops were already stocked for the season, Paul could resell the mugs only at a loss.

In Paul's suit against Ruth, which of the following is the probable decision?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

Two merchants orally agree to a goods sale with a minimum and maximum quantity, the seller sends a signed confirmation stating only the minimum quantity, the buyer does not object, and the seller later tenders the maximum quantity. What quantity can the seller enforce under the UCC Statute of Frauds?

**D.** Paul can enforce a contract for 1,200 mugs, because Ruth ordered and Paul tendered that quantity.

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** Under UCC Section 2-201, a writing for a sale of goods over $500 need not contain every term, but the contract is enforceable only up to the quantity shown in the writing. Between merchants, a signed confirmation sent within a reasonable time satisfies the writing requirement against the recipient if the recipient has reason to know its contents and does not object in writing within 10 days.

**Silver Key:** In a merchant-confirmation question, split the answer array into two issues: "is there any enforceable contract?" and "how much quantity can the writing support?" The silent merchant rule answers the first issue; the quantity term in the memo caps the second.

Ruth and Paul are both merchants for this transaction. Ruth regularly buys gift items for resale, and Paul makes the mugs for retailers. The $12 x 600 minimum is over $500, so the UCC Statute of Frauds applies. Paul sent a signed confirmation shortly after the oral deal, Ruth received it, and the facts give no written objection within 10 days. That confirmation can bind Ruth even though Ruth did not sign it.

But the confirmation stated 600 mugs. The oral range went as high as 1,200, and Paul's tender conformed to that upper end, but the Statute of Frauds writing is enforceable only to the quantity shown. Paul can enforce 600 mugs, not 1,200.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. There is no enforceable agreement, because Ruth never signed a writing.**

*Attractive because:* This answer sells the familiar Statute of Frauds reflex: the party being sued usually must have signed the writing. A student who stops there misses that both sides are merchants and that Paul's signed confirmation can count against Ruth after silence.

*Breaker:* The merchant-confirmation rule defeats the signature trap. **Gold Key:** Between merchants, the sender's signed confirmation can satisfy UCC Section 2-201 against the recipient if the recipient has reason to know its contents and does not object in writing within 10 days. Ruth's missing signature does not destroy enforceability.

**B. There is no enforceable agreement, because Paul's confirmation did not state a price term.**

*Attractive because:* Price feels like a core contract term, and the note does omit the $12 price. Students who treat every important term as Statute-of-Frauds-required will like this.

*Breaker:* UCC Section 2-201 does not require the writing to state every term. The quantity term is the critical ceiling. The missing price term does not prevent enforcement when the writing otherwise indicates a contract and states the quantity.

**D. Paul can enforce a contract for 1,200 mugs, because Ruth ordered and Paul tendered that quantity.**

*Attractive because:* The oral range allowed up to 1,200, and Paul tendered 1,200 conforming mugs. This answer follows the commercial story instead of the Statute of Frauds cap.

*Breaker:* The writing controls the enforceable quantity. **Silver Key:** Once the confirmation states 600, the memo creates a quantity ceiling. The oral maximum and conforming tender do not let Paul enforce beyond the quantity shown in the signed confirmation.

## 8. Black-Letter Legal Verification

**Governing authorities:** UCC Section 2-201(1) provides the Statute of Frauds rule for sales of goods of $500 or more and states that a writing is not insufficient because it omits or incorrectly states a term, but the contract is not enforceable beyond the quantity of goods shown in the writing. UCC Section 2-201(2) provides the between-merchants confirmation rule: a sufficient confirmation received within a reasonable time satisfies the writing requirement against the recipient unless the recipient gives written notice of objection within 10 days. UCC Section 2-104 defines "merchant" and "between merchants." Sources: Cornell LII, UCC Section 2-201, https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-201; Cornell LII, UCC Section 2-104, https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-104.

The credited answer is correct under those authorities. This is a sale of goods over $500 between merchants. Paul's signed confirmation was received by Ruth and stated 600 mugs. Ruth did not object in writing within 10 days. The merchant-confirmation rule supplies the writing against Ruth, but the quantity limitation keeps enforcement at 600 mugs. The tested rule, legal issue, trap structure, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item.
