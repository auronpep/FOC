### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)
D is correct. Lydia's season pass let her enter the private pavilion and sit in a specified seat for the performances, but that right was a license, not an estate or other property interest in the pavilion. A license gives permission to use another's land for a limited purpose. It can be revoked by the landholder, even though a wrongful revocation may leave a contract damages issue. Lydia therefore cannot use property law to force the corporation to keep admitting her to the remaining performances. **Gold Key:** a paid ticket or season pass is a license to enter, not ownership of the seat or an irrevocable right to occupy the venue. **Silver Key:** on a "because" answer set, choose the answer that names the property status of the ticket itself; a broad owner-control answer may sound true, but it is not the precise reason the claimed right to attend fails.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because Lydia paid value for the full season and the corporation canceled before all performances were used, so it feels as though payment should lock in her right to be present. The breaker is that payment for a ticket creates a contractual claim at most; it does not convert the ticket into an irrevocable property interest in the land or seat. **Gold Key:** value paid for admission does not turn a ticket license into an estate in land.

C is attractive because it tries to make the venue justify revocation by proving Lydia's conduct was serious enough to be a nuisance. The breaker is that nuisance is not the gatekeeping doctrine here; the corporation may revoke the license without showing that Lydia committed a nuisance, and any wrongful-revocation issue would sound in contract rather than a property right to remain.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from source row 15013. A ticket to enter and occupy a seat at a private entertainment venue is a license: a personal privilege to enter or use land for a limited purpose, not an estate or possessory interest in the land. The licensor may revoke the license; if the revocation breaches the ticket contract, the ticket holder's remedy is contractual damages or refund-type relief, not a property-law decree forcing continued admission. Here, the private corporation canceled Lydia's remaining season pass and tendered a refund. Because Lydia held only a license, she cannot establish a property right to attend all remaining performances.

Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only): Tyson & Bro. v. Banton, 273 U.S. 418, 440-41 (1927), recognizes that an entertainment ticket may be a revocable license and that, if it is a license, it may be revoked by the proprietor.
