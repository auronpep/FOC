# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question

Lydia, a well-known Christian calligrapher, was scheduled to unveil a hand-lettered Gospel of John mural during a nationally streamed arts gala. A Scripture-memory app that had an exclusive arrangement with Lydia contracted with the streaming platform to send a 30-second sponsored alert on Friday, March 14, at 8:45 p.m. Although the written contract did not say so expressly, both sides understood that the alert would go out during the final segment of Lydia's live unveiling.

The alert promoted the app by advertising Lydia's surprise set of exclusive devotional cards. The day before the gala, Lydia was killed in a hiking accident. The streaming platform offered to send the sponsored alert at the agreed time for a 20 percent discount, but the app refused, explaining that the entire alert and campaign had been built around Lydia's live unveiling.

If the streaming platform sues the Scripture-memory app for breach of contract, how should the court rule?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

Is a buyer excused when an unexpected death destroys the shared event-based purpose of a timed sponsorship, even though the seller could still send the sponsorship at the scheduled time?

## 3. Final Answer Choices

A. For the Scripture-memory app, under the doctrine of frustration of purpose.

B. For the streaming platform, because the contract did not expressly state that the alert had to be sent during Lydia's live unveiling.

C. For the Scripture-memory app, under the doctrine of impossibility.

D. For the streaming platform, because nothing prevented it from sending the alert at the scheduled time.

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

A is correct. The app's remaining duty is discharged because the basic reason for the timed sponsorship was destroyed before performance. The platform could still send an alert at 8:45 p.m., but the point of buying that particular slot was to reach Lydia's live unveiling audience with Lydia-linked devotional cards. Once Lydia died and the live unveiling lost its premise, the app's principal purpose was substantially frustrated through no fault of its own.

Gold Key: Frustration of purpose is not about whether the promised act can physically be done; it is about whether an unexpected event destroys the main shared reason for the contract.

Silver Key: When one answer says performance is still possible and another says the purpose is gone, name the doctrine lane first. Possible performance attacks impossibility, not frustration.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

B is attractive because the written contract does not spell out the final-segment timing in so many words. The breaker is that frustration can rest on the parties' understood basic assumption; the shared event-based purpose does not disappear merely because it was not written as a detailed term.

C is attractive because a death caused the problem and students often pair death with impossibility. The breaker is the Gold Key: sending the alert was still possible, so impossibility is the wrong doctrine. The destroyed thing was the commercial purpose of the timed alert.

D is attractive because it states a true operational fact: the platform could still send the alert at the scheduled minute. The breaker is the Silver Key: literal performance possible is not enough when the buyer's principal purpose, known to both parties, has been almost completely destroyed.

## 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning For The Right Answer

The tested legal issue and credited outcome are unchanged from source question 18427: frustration of purpose excuses a party when a supervening event, through no fault of that party, substantially frustrates the party's principal purpose and the nonoccurrence of that event was a basic assumption of the contract.

Authority floor: Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 265 supplies the governing frustration-of-purpose rule. The American Law Institute identifies frustration of purpose as part of the Restatement Second of Contracts performance and non-performance materials. Secondary public summaries also describe the same section as discharging duties when the principal purpose is substantially frustrated without the party's fault and the event's nonoccurrence was a basic contractual assumption. The classic illustration is the coronation-viewing line of cases: performance may remain physically possible, but the event that supplied the contract's point may be gone.

Authority links: American Law Institute, Restatement of the Law Second, Contracts, https://www.ali.org/publications/restatement-law-second/contracts; Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 265 public outline summary, https://matthewminer.name/law/outlines/1L/2nd%2BSemester/LAW%2B506-002%2B%E2%80%93%2BContracts%2BII/R2C%2B%C2%A7%2B265; Bloomberg Law overview noting Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 265, https://www.bloomberglaw.com/external/document/X9SL32Q4000000/litigation-overview-frustration-of-purpose.

Here, the platform's performance was still technically available: it could send the alert. That makes impossibility the wrong doctrine. The app's principal purpose, however, was to promote Lydia-linked devotional cards to the audience gathered for Lydia's live unveiling. Both sides understood that timing. Lydia's unexpected death destroyed that shared basis and left the alert materially different from the bargain the app purchased. The court should rule for the app.
