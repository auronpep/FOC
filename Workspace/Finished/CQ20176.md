## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Hannah Family Ministries holds a shifting executory interest. The deed gives Lydia Community Homestead a fee simple subject to executory limitation (a defeasible estate that can terminate upon the happening of a specified event — here, racial discrimination). Hannah's interest will divest Lydia's estate only if the triggering event occurs; it will not become possessory automatically upon the natural termination of a preceding estate. That is the hallmark of an executory interest, not a remainder.

Under the common-law Rule Against Perpetuities (RAP), no interest is valid unless it must vest, if at all, not later than 21 years after some life in being at the time the interest is created. Standing alone, Hannah's interest would violate RAP because racial discrimination might first occur decades after the deed, well beyond the perpetuities period. However, the charity-to-charity exception exempts interests shifting from one charity to another charity from RAP. Because both Lydia Community Homestead and Hannah Family Ministries are charitable organizations, the exception applies, and Hannah's executory interest is valid.

> **Gold Key (charity-to-charity RAP exception):** When a future interest shifts from one charity to another charity, the Rule Against Perpetuities does not apply, and the interest is valid regardless of when it might vest.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Classification — executory interest vs. remainder:**

A remainder is a future interest in a grantee that becomes possessory automatically upon the natural and inevitable termination of the preceding estate (e.g., a life estate or term of years). *See* 28 Am. Jur. 2d *Estates* § 257; Restatement (Third) of Property: Wills and Other Donative Transfers § 25.1 cmt. a. An executory interest, by contrast, is a future interest in a grantee that (1) does not follow a naturally terminating estate, or (2) is subject to a condition precedent other than the natural termination of the prior estate, or (3) divests or cuts short a prior estate. *See* Restatement (Third) of Property: Wills and Other Donative Transfers § 25.2.

Here, Lydia receives a fee simple subject to executory limitation — an estate that could last indefinitely. Hannah's interest will become possessory only if racial discrimination occurs, an uncertain divesting event. This is a classic **shifting executory interest** (shifting because it divests a grantee other than the grantor).

**Rule Against Perpetuities and the charity-to-charity exception:**

The common-law RAP, classically stated in *Cadell v. Palmer*, 1 Cl. & Fin. 372 (1833), and *Gray*, The Rule Against Perpetuities § 201 (4th ed. 1942), provides: "No interest is good unless it must vest, if at all, not later than twenty-one years after some life in being at the creation of the interest." Standing alone, Hannah's executory interest could vest at any future time — racial discrimination might not occur for centuries — and so would violate RAP.

However, a well-established exception holds that **the Rule Against Perpetuities does not apply to an interest that shifts from one charity to another charity.** *See* Restatement (Third) of Property: Wills and Other Donative Transfers § 27.1 cmt. e ("A future interest that is limited to take effect upon the termination of a prior charitable interest and that is also charitable is not subject to the rule against perpetuities."); *see also* Nolan Law Firm, "Analysis of the Rule Against Perpetuities" (2025) (noting the foundational exception for shifts from one charitable purpose to another). The rationale is that charities serve the public interest and should not be penalized by a rule designed to prevent excessive "dead hand" control by private donors.

Because both Lydia Community Homestead and Hannah Family Ministries are charitable organizations, the charity-to-charity exception applies, and Hannah's executory interest is valid.

**Conclusion:** The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original. The interest is a valid executory interest. Authority confirmed.

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

#### Program Frame

This item tests two linked concepts in Real Property: (1) the classification of future interests — specifically, the distinction between an executory interest and a remainder — and (2) the Rule Against Perpetuities and its charity-to-charity exception. The item is anchor-assisted: the classification step is structurally visible from the stem, but the validity step requires knowing the charity-to-charity exception (a Gold Key).

#### Student Access Test

A student who has completed the C3 future-interests module and has been taught the Gold Key for the charity-to-charity RAP exception can solve this item. The classification step (executory interest vs. remainder) is hard-structural: the stem facts show a fee simple prior estate and an uncertain divesting event. The validity step (void vs. valid) requires the Gold Key anchor.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** A donor executed a deed conveying her farm, which contained the following clause: To Lydia Community Homestead, for so long as the farm shall be used to house and feed indigent families, but if racial discrimination is practiced in the selection of families to receive aid, to Hannah Family Ministries for so long as the farm shall be used to house and feed indigent families. Lydia Community Homestead and Hannah Family Ministries are both charitable organizations devoted to serving poor families. On the day after the deed is executed, Hannah Family Ministries' interest in the farm is best described as a

**Choices:**
- A: void contingent remainder.
- B: void executory interest.
- C: valid executory interest.
- D: valid contingent remainder.

**Correct Answer:** C

#### Distilled Core Question

A deed conveys land to Charity A for so long as used for a charitable purpose, with a gift over to Charity B if Charity A engages in racial discrimination. What is Charity B's interest, and is it valid under RAP?

#### Call and Prediction

**Call:** "best described as a" — the student must classify the interest and assess its validity.

**Prediction:** Students who remember the charity-to-charity exception will gravitate toward the "valid" choices (C or D). Those who forget it will choose between A and B. The dominant trap is D — students who remember the exception but misclassify the interest type.

#### Trigger Facts

1. **Both grantees are charitable organizations** → triggers the charity-to-charity RAP exception (Gold Key).
2. **The prior estate is a fee simple** (Lydia holds "for so long as" the farm is used charitably — this is a fee simple subject to executory limitation, capable of lasting forever) → establishes that the interest is NOT a remainder (remainders follow naturally terminating estates like life estates).
3. **The gift over is triggered by an uncertain event** (racial discrimination in family selection) → confirms executory interest classification; raises RAP issue.
4. **The triggering event may never occur** → confirms non-automatic vesting; supports executory interest classification.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: void contingent remainder.**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice gets two things wrong. First, the interest is not a remainder — it follows a fee simple, not a life estate or other naturally terminating estate. Second, even if it were a remainder, the charity-to-charity exception would save it from being void. Either defect alone kills this choice.
2. **Student label:** Wrong on both counts.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true-and-responsive version would correctly classify the interest as an executory interest and correctly identify it as valid.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Both the classification (remainder) and the validity conclusion (void) are legally incorrect. *See* Restatement (Third) of Property: Wills and Other Donative Transfers §§ 25.2, 27.1 cmt. e.

**Choice B: void executory interest.**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The classification is right — this is an executory interest. But the "void" conclusion is wrong. The key unlock: both grantees are charities, and the charity-to-charity exception exempts the interest from RAP.
2. **Student label:** Right type, wrong validity.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Valid executory interest" — same classification, correct validity.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The classification is correct (executory interest), but the validity conclusion is wrong. The charity-to-charity exception applies. *See* Restatement (Third) of Property: Wills and Other Donative Transfers § 27.1 cmt. e. **Gold Key:** When a future interest shifts from one charity to another charity, the Rule Against Perpetuities does not apply.

**Choice C: valid executory interest.**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Correct on both counts. The interest is an executory interest (follows a fee simple, triggered by an uncertain divesting event), and it is valid (charity-to-charity exception).
2. **Student label:** Correct.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true-and-responsive answer.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Correct. Shifting executory interest; saved from RAP by charity-to-charity exception. *See* Restatement (Third) of Property: Wills and Other Donative Transfers §§ 25.2, 27.1 cmt. e.

**Choice D: valid contingent remainder.**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The "valid" part is right (the charity exception saves it), but the classification is wrong. A remainder follows a naturally terminating estate like a life estate. Here, Lydia holds a fee simple — it could last forever. The gift over is triggered by an uncertain event, making it an executory interest.
2. **Student label:** Right validity, wrong type (dominant trap).
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Valid executory interest" — same validity conclusion, correct classification.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The validity conclusion is correct (charity-to-charity exception applies), but the classification is wrong. A remainder follows a naturally terminating estate. This interest follows a fee simple and is triggered by an uncertain divesting event — the definition of an executory interest. *See* Restatement (Third) of Property: Wills and Other Donative Transfers § 25.2.

**C** is the only choice that is both true and responsive. It correctly classifies the interest as an executory interest and correctly identifies it as valid under the charity-to-charity RAP exception.

#### Wrong-Answer Recovery Paths

**If the student picked D (valid contingent remainder):**
- **What happened:** The student remembered the charity-to-charity exception (so "valid" is right) but misclassified the interest as a remainder.
- **Recovery step:** Review the definition of a remainder vs. an executory interest. A remainder follows a naturally terminating estate (life estate, term of years). An executory interest follows an estate that could last indefinitely and is triggered by an uncertain divesting event. Lydia holds a fee simple — it could last forever. The gift over is triggered by racial discrimination, an uncertain event. That is an executory interest.

**If the student picked B (void executory interest):**
- **What happened:** The student correctly classified the interest as an executory interest but forgot the charity-to-charity RAP exception.
- **Recovery step:** Review the Gold Key: when a future interest shifts from one charity to another charity, RAP does not apply. Both Lydia Community Homestead and Hannah Family Ministries are charitable organizations. The interest is valid.

**If the student picked A (void contingent remainder):**
- **What happened:** The student got both the classification and the validity wrong.
- **Recovery step:** Two errors to fix. (1) This is not a remainder — it follows a fee simple, not a naturally terminating estate. (2) Even if it were a remainder, the charity-to-charity exception would save it from being void.
