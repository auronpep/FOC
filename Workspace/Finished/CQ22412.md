### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**Gold Key:** A minor's voidable contract becomes fully binding if the former minor **affirms** the contract after reaching the age of majority. Affirmance can be **express** or by **conduct** — any act consistent with an intention to be bound, such as continuing to make payments or retaining the benefit of the contract. Once affirmed, the contract is enforceable and the former minor may no longer disaffirm.

Here, Daniel was 16 when he signed — a minor, so the contract was voidable. But Daniel turned 18 (the age of majority) and then **continued making payments for four more months**. That continued performance after majority constitutes affirmation by conduct. The contract is now binding, and Lydia is entitled to the remaining payments.

---

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A — "No, because Daniel was a minor when he signed the agreement."**
- **Attractive because:** It is literally true that Daniel lacked capacity at the moment of signing, and many students stop there without asking what happened *after* majority. The minor's incapacity is the first thing you learn about infancy doctrine, so it feels like the right hook.
- **Breaker:** The infancy defense is a shield the minor may raise — but only if the minor *disaffirms* within a reasonable time after reaching majority. Here, Daniel did the opposite: he affirmed by continuing to pay. **Gold Key:** affirmance after majority extinguishes the infancy defense entirely.

**Choice C — "No, because Daniel offered to return the laptop."**
- **Attractive because:** Returning the laptop feels like the "right" thing to do, and students sometimes conflate returning the goods with undoing the contract. It also mirrors the language of disaffirmance (returning the benefit).
- **Breaker:** Once a contract is affirmed, the former minor cannot disaffirm. Daniel's offer to return the laptop is legally irrelevant — the contract is already binding. The time to disaffirm was *before* he affirmed by continuing to pay.

**Choice D — "Yes, because Daniel used the laptop to complete his schoolwork."**
- **Attractive because:** Using the laptop for school sounds like it could trigger a "necessities" exception, and the facts support that the laptop was used for an important purpose.
- **Breaker:** The necessities doctrine applies to make a minor *liable in quasi-contract* for the reasonable value of necessaries — it does not make the *contract itself* enforceable. The call asks whether Lydia is entitled to the remaining *contract payments*, not whether Daniel must pay for the value of the laptop. The correct theory is affirmance, not necessaries.

### 9. Black-Letter Verification & Legal Authority

**Governing rule:** At common law, a contract entered into by a minor (infant) is **voidable** at the minor's option. The minor may disaffirm the contract during minority or within a reasonable time after reaching the age of majority. However, if the former minor **affirms** the contract after reaching majority — either by an express statement or by conduct consistent with an intention to be bound (such as continuing to make payments, retaining the benefit, or failing to disaffirm within a reasonable time) — the contract becomes fully binding on both parties, and the former minor may no longer disaffirm.

**Authority:**
- **Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 14, 85** — Section 14 states the infancy-capacity rule: unless a statute provides otherwise, an infant incurs only voidable contractual duties. Section 85 supplies the promise-to-perform-voidable-duty frame for later affirmance.
- **Common-law infancy doctrine:** *Bobby Floars Toyota, Inc. v. Smith*, 269 S.E.2d 320, 48 N.C. App. 580 (1980), applies the classic rule: a minor's conventional contract is voidable during minority or within a reasonable time after majority, but the privilege of disaffirmance may be lost when the former minor affirms or otherwise ratifies after reaching majority. The court held that continued possession and monthly installment payments after age 18 constituted ratification, precluding later disaffirmance.
- **MBE standard:** The NCBE tests the distinction between voidable (minority) and void contracts regularly. The key is that voidable contracts can be ratified; void contracts cannot.

**Verification:** The original credited answer (D) and the transformed credited answer (B) both rest on the same rule — affirmation by conduct after majority. The legal substance, issue, and outcome are unchanged. **PASS.**

**Title:** Infancy Doctrine: Voidable, Not Void
**Signal:** Minor signed a contract, then did something after turning 18.
**Student move:** Ask: did the former minor affirm (by conduct or words) after majority? If yes — contract is binding. If no disaffirmed within reasonable time — contract is void.
