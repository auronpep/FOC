## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**A is correct.** The best defense is not that the President can declare war, and not merely that the President commands the armed forces. The order is strongest when framed as a rapid foreign deployment to carry out an existing mutual-defense treaty obligation at the ally's request. **Gold Key:** On a war-powers item asking for the strongest defense of a rapid foreign deployment, the answer that ties the deployment to an existing defense treaty or foreign-affairs obligation beats bare branch labels. **Silver Key:** In a source-of-power array, do not pick the label whose words merely match the facts; pick the power that answers the exact challenged act.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B.** This choice sells the obvious troop-order hook: if troops are moving, Commander-in-Chief sounds right. The breaker is the call's foreign-policy/treaty posture. **Gold Key:** the strongest support is the existing defense obligation, not the bare fact that the President commands the military once forces are used. **Silver Key:** the answer has to justify this deployment, not just name a military title.

**C.** This choice sells a simple war-powers shortcut: a threatened attack sounds like war, so the President must be able to declare it. The breaker is a branch reversal; Congress has the declare-war power.

**D.** This choice sells the treaty word-match. The breaker is timing and responsiveness: the question does not challenge whether the treaty was validly made; it challenges the later troop order.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are preserved. The strongest support for the President's order is the argument that the President may deploy U.S. forces abroad to carry out an existing mutual-defense treaty or foreign-affairs obligation when immediate defense of the ally is requested.

Authority floor and current-law check: Article I, Section 8, Clause 11 gives Congress the power to declare war, so choice C is not a valid presidential-power answer. Article II, Section 2 makes the President Commander in Chief and gives the President treaty-making power with the Senate's advice and consent, but those clauses by themselves do not make choices B or D the strongest answer to this call. Constitution Annotated's discussion of overseas troop deployments recognizes the historical executive-branch theory that Presidents have used armed forces abroad to implement treaties and foreign policy, while also noting that the Supreme Court has not squarely resolved the full scope of unilateral presidential authority. The War Powers Resolution adds an important statutory caveat: 50 U.S.C. Section 1541(c) states that Commander-in-Chief powers to introduce forces into hostilities are exercised only under a declaration of war, specific statutory authorization, or a national emergency created by an attack on the United States, its territories, possessions, or armed forces; 50 U.S.C. Section 1547(a)(2) also says authority to introduce forces into hostilities may not be inferred from a treaty unless implemented by legislation specifically authorizing that introduction.

That caveat does not make another offered answer stronger on this source row, because the call asks for the strongest argument supporting validity and the treaty-obligation answer is still the only answer tied to the challenged deployment. If the bank intends a treaty alone to be a conclusive modern authorization without implementing legislation or another statutory basis, attorney review is recommended.
