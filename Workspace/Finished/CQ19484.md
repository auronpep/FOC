## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

C is correct. Two independent acts, each on its own sufficient to kill, landed at the same instant. Run the strict but-for test on Caleb and it self-destructs: "but for Caleb's slab, Stephen still dies — from Boaz's slab." Run it on Boaz and you get the mirror image. Strict but-for would free both men even though each delivered an independently fatal blow — an absurd result. **Gold Key:** when two independent causes are *each alone sufficient* and operate together, courts drop strict but-for and apply the **substantial-factor test** — each actor whose conduct was a substantial factor in the result is an actual cause. Caleb's slab independently struck and killed Stephen; it was unquestionably a substantial factor. Caleb is an actual cause, and so is Boaz. C states exactly that, and it is the only choice that keeps the rule's conclusion intact.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

- **A (dominant trap).** A feels airtight because it applies the test you were taught — strict but-for — and the premise is literally true: Stephen *would* have died from Boaz's slab alone. That is the engineered pull: the mechanical but-for move looks complete. **Gold Key:** but the concurrent-sufficient-cause pattern is the one place strict but-for breaks, and courts substitute the substantial-factor test precisely so that a defendant whose act was independently fatal cannot point at the other and walk. The true but-for observation is the *start* of the analysis, not the end.
- **B.** B is attractive because it sounds rigorous — it reaches the right conclusion (Caleb is a cause) and dresses it in a precise-sounding condition (whose slab landed first). The condition is fiction. The stem fixes the strikes as simultaneous, and the substantial-factor test requires no temporal priority at all; either actor whose conduct substantially contributed is an actual cause regardless of which slab landed a heartbeat sooner. B invents a requirement the law does not impose.
- **D.** D is tempting because it correctly spots that strict but-for struggles when two simultaneous causes collide — naming the logical problem feels like solving it. **Silver Key:** but the stem itself says each slab "independently caused his death," so the claim that "neither blow individually caused the death" fights the facts you were given; cut it on the stem before you ever reach the doctrine. Identifying the but-for difficulty is the question's setup, not its answer.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

Tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are **unchanged** from the source item: actual (factual) causation where two independent causes are each independently sufficient and operate simultaneously; the credited answer is that the defendant **is** an actual cause via the substantial-factor test.

Affirmative legal reasoning: actual cause is ordinarily the but-for test — *but for* the defendant's act, the result would not have occurred (MPC § 2.03(1)(a): conduct is the cause of a result when it is "an antecedent but for which the result in question would not have occurred"). Concurrent sufficient causes are the recognized exception: where two forces are each independently sufficient to produce the result, but-for fails to capture either, so each actor whose conduct was a substantial factor in producing the result is an actual cause. Caleb's blow independently produced Stephen's death and was therefore a substantial factor; he is an actual cause, as is Boaz.

Lawyer-confirmation authority (not part of the student path): MPC § 2.03(1)(a) (but-for floor); Restatement (Second) of Torts § 432(2) (when two forces are each by itself sufficient to bring about the harm, each is a substantial factor); *Anderson v. Minneapolis, St. Paul & Sault Ste. Marie Railway Co.*, 146 Minn. 430, 179 N.W. 45 (1920) (leading concurrent-sufficient-cause / merging-forces decision); LaFave, *Substantive Criminal Law* § 6.4 (criminal-law application of concurrent and accelerating causes). The substantial-factor solution is the standard bar treatment of this fact pattern; research confirms rather than contradicts the official key, so the verdict is PASS (no NEEDS_HUMAN). Note: the substantial-factor formulation is borrowed into criminal law from torts; the rule statement is well settled and matches the credited answer.
