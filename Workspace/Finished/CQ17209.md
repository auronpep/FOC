Peter, a church-van volunteer, helped Martha load a sealed lunch cooler into the back of a pickup after a women’s retreat. While Peter was closing the hatch, he negligently jostled the cooler. ## 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct because it answers the duty call with the Palsgraf rule: before the accident, Ruth must be foreseeably within the risk created by Peter’s careless act. The cooler looked ordinary, the dangerous contents were unknown, and Ruth was waiting far away beyond intervening parking-lot objects. That places her outside the apparent risk zone that Peter’s loading conduct created.

Gold Key: In Palsgraf duty framing, the orbit of the danger disclosed to ordinary vigilance is the orbit of the duty; a remote plaintiff outside the apparent risk is not owed a duty for an unforeseeable chain.

Silver Key: When two answers both say No, choose the one that answers the duty call — whether the plaintiff was in the foreseeable risk zone before the accident — not the one that invents a rule about proximate cause.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A. This answer is attractive because it turns one careless act into a universal duty to everyone later affected by the event. The breaker is that Palsgraf rejects duty to unforeseeable remote plaintiffs; later upset or injury does not itself create a duty to the whole world.

B. This answer is attractive because it reaches the same No result and sounds like a clean Palsgraf shortcut. The breaker is that it invents the opposite of the rule: foreseeability is exactly why no duty exists here.

D. This answer is attractive because it follows the timeline from Peter’s careless jostle to Ruth’s later injury. The breaker is that the call asks about duty before the accident, and post-accident injury alone does not show that Ruth was in the foreseeable risk zone.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The governing authority is Palsgraf v. Long Island R.R. Co., 248 N.Y. 339, 162 N.E. 99 (1928). Under the Cardozo majority’s exam-default framing, negligence is relational: the defendant’s duty runs to plaintiffs foreseeably within the zone of risk created by the defendant’s conduct. A plaintiff who is injured by an unforeseeable, remote chain from an apparently harmless act is not owed a duty for that injury merely because the defendant was careless toward someone else.

That confirms the source key and the transformed key. Peter’s negligent jostle may have created a risk to Martha, the cooler, or people near the loading area, but it did not make Ruth — a remote parking-lot visitor beyond ordinary apparent danger from a sealed cooler — a foreseeable plaintiff. The correct answer therefore must be a No answer grounded in pre-accident foreseeability.

The legal issue, outcome, and tested doctrine are unchanged: whether a remote plaintiff is owed a negligence duty under the Palsgraf duty framing when an ordinary-looking object unexpectedly creates harm. No doctrinal-currency hotlist issue is implicated.
