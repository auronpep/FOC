## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation
The motion to suppress should be denied because Sarah lacked a reasonable expectation of privacy in items visible through an uncurtained window and lacked standing to challenge the constables' presence on her neighbor's property. Under the Fourth Amendment, a search occurs only when the government violates a person's subjective expectation of privacy that society recognizes as reasonable. By leaving her kitchen window uncurtained and the stolen silver in plain view of anyone standing in the adjacent yard, Sarah "exposed to the public" what was inside, even if the constables were technically trespassing on Lydia's land. Because Sarah did not own or have a possessory interest in Lydia's yard, she has no standing to complain about the constables' entry onto that property. Thus, the initial observation was legal, and the warrant based on it was valid.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations
**(A) [Original C] [DOMINANT TRAP]** This choice is attractive because it correctly notes that activities in the home are generally protected. However, the Fourth Amendment does not protect what a person knowingly exposes to the public. If a window is uncurtained and visible from a neighbor's property, there is no reasonable expectation of privacy for items left in plain view. Sarah's subjective hope for privacy is not one that society considers reasonable under these circumstances.

**(B) [Original A]** This choice incorrectly suggests that the mere existence of a warrant sanitizes any prior illegal conduct. If the information used to obtain the warrant was itself the fruit of an illegal search, the warrant and the evidence seized under it would typically be suppressed. The reason the evidence is admissible here is that the *initial* observation was not illegal.

**(C) [Original D]** This choice relies on a non-existent legal rule. There is no requirement that evidence be visible from a "public vantage point" (like a street or sidewalk) to be considered in plain view. It is enough that the evidence is visible from a location where the defendant has no reasonable expectation of privacy or standing to challenge the government's presence.

## 8. Black-Letter Verification
**Tested Rule:** Fourth Amendment Standing and the Plain View Doctrine (visibility from non-protected areas).
**Authority:** *Horton v. California*, 496 U.S. 128 (1990) (plain view); *Rakas v. Illinois*, 439 U.S. 128 (1978) (standing depends on the defendant's own privacy interest, not the illegality of the search relative to others).
**Reasoning:** Sarah cannot assert Lydia's Fourth Amendment rights regarding the constables' trespass. Items visible through an uncurtained window from a neighbor's yard are not protected by the Fourth Amendment.

**Gold Key: Fourth Amendment Standing**
