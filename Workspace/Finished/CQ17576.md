### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. The Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches only after the start of adversary judicial proceedings and is offense-specific. Gold Key: counsel on one charged offense does not automatically cover police questioning about a different, uncharged offense; Miranda is the interrogation waiver gate for the separate offense. Silver Key: when the motion says "Sixth Amendment," first match the lawyer to the offense being questioned about. Daniel had counsel on the counterfeit-wristband charge, but the fire had not yet been charged. Because Daniel received Miranda warnings and waived them, the Sixth Amendment did not require suppression of the fire statements.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are preserved. The source row's stored outline code, 73031000, points to burglary, but the actual tested issue is the Sixth Amendment right to counsel in criminal procedure. The deepest matching local outline code is 75090400, Sixth Amendment-Other Rights of the Accused > Right to Counsel.

The Sixth Amendment provides the right to the assistance of counsel in criminal prosecutions. Under McNeil v. Wisconsin, 501 U.S. 171 (1991), that right is offense-specific and attaches only at or after the initiation of adversary judicial criminal proceedings, such as formal charge, indictment, information, arraignment, or preliminary hearing. Texas v. Cobb, 532 U.S. 162 (2001), reaffirms that the right does not extend merely because an uncharged offense is factually related; the Court uses the offense-specific framework. Here, Daniel's right to counsel had attached on the charged counterfeit-wristband case only. The fire offense had not been charged when Detective Martha questioned him. Miranda warnings were given and waived, so the Sixth Amendment did not bar the questioning or require suppression in the later arson case.
