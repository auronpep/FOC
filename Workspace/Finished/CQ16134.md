### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. Conspiracy is an agreement crime, and the agreement must be an agreement to commit the unlawful target offense. Daniel had the murderous purpose, but Lydia did not share it; she was pretending the drops were lethal so Daniel would back away. Gold Key: on the common-law/bilateral conspiracy rule tested here, one real criminal intent plus one feigned helper is not a conspiracy because there is no true meeting of criminal purposes. Silver Key: lock the charge first. The later allergic reaction may matter to a different offense, but it does not create the missing murder agreement.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because the near-death result feels like it proves the murder plan was serious. The breaker is that conspiracy is complete, or absent, at the agreement stage; an accidental dangerous result after the fact does not supply Lydia's missing intent to agree to murder.

C is the dominant trap because Daniel's belief looks like the whole case: he wanted murder and thought Lydia gave him a killing tool. The breaker is the Gold Key: conspiracy needs a real agreement between criminal minds, not one person's murderous belief plus another person's fake cooperation.

D is attractive because it remembers that the planned killing did not succeed. The breaker is that conspiracy does not require completion of the target crime; if there had been a real agreement to murder, Barnabas's survival would not defeat the conspiracy charge.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law and Procedure, Inchoate Crimes, Conspiracy, with verified outline code `71040200`. The common-law conspiracy rule tested by the source item requires an agreement by at least two persons to commit an unlawful act, plus the required criminal intent. The Supreme Court treats conspiracy as centered on the unlawful agreement. See *Iannelli v. United States*, 420 U.S. 770 (1975), https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/420/770/, and *United States v. Shabani*, 513 U.S. 10 (1994), https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/513/10/. The bilateral feigned-agreement point is stated directly in *State v. Horton*, 170 S.E.2d 466 (N.C. 1969), https://law.justia.com/cases/north-carolina/supreme-court/1969/22-1-4.html: where only one person truly agrees and the other merely pretends, there is no conspiracy. The allergic reaction does not change the conspiracy analysis because the charge rises or falls on whether there was a real agreement to murder. The credited answer is unchanged: not guilty of conspiracy because the supposed co-conspirator did not intend or agree that the victim be killed.
