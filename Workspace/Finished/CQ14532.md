# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question

Martha's adult nephew Stephen had purchased a portable sound booth for his van. Martha contracted with Barnabas, a private audio technician, to install and tune the booth before Stephen's audition for a Scripture-audiobook producer. "If the booth is ready by the audition," Martha told Barnabas, "the producer has promised Stephen a job that will pay $72,000 a year."

Barnabas agreed to do the work for $7,400, although the going rate was $8,400. Before the work was to begin and before any payment was made, Barnabas repudiated the contract. Although Martha or Stephen reasonably could have hired, for $8,400, an equally qualified technician to replace Barnabas, neither did so. The booth was not ready for the audition, and the producer refused to employ Stephen. It can be shown that had Barnabas installed and tuned the booth, Stephen would have received the job.

If Martha and Stephen join as plaintiffs and sue Barnabas for breach of contract, how much, if anything, are they entitled to recover?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**C is correct.** Barnabas agreed to do the work for $7,400, and a reasonable substitute technician was available for $8,400. The breach therefore left a $1,000 contract-to-market gap. The lost $72,000 job was serious and was disclosed, but it could have been avoided by hiring the substitute technician. The failure to mitigate cuts off that avoidable salary loss; it does not erase the $1,000 price gap.

**Gold Key:** Failure to mitigate cuts off avoidable loss; it does not wipe out the contract-to-market price difference for a reasonable substitute.

**Silver Key:** Sort the answers into salary loss, total bar, nominal damages, and price gap. The price-gap answer is the only one that both respects mitigation and leaves the plaintiffs with the benefit of the bargain.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because Martha expressly told Barnabas about the $72,000 job, and the facts say Stephen would have received it if the booth had been ready. **B.** This is the dominant trap because it spots the missed mitigation step and then overcorrects. **Gold Key:** failure to mitigate bars avoidable loss, not every dollar of recovery; the $1,000 substitute-price gap remains.

**D.** This choice tries to turn the case into a privity or no-injury problem. The breaker is that Martha made the bargain and Stephen was the named person to receive the promised performance; the damages question still has a concrete $1,000 market-price gap rather than nominal damages only.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning For The Right Answer

The governing rule is unchanged: contract damages are limited by avoidability, but the failure to mitigate does not create a total forfeiture of all expectation damages. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 350 is the authority for the avoidability limitation: losses that the injured party could reasonably avoid are not recoverable. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 347 supplies the expectation-damages frame: the injured party is put in the position performance would have produced, subject to limitations such as avoidability.

Applied here, the reasonable substitute would have cost $8,400 instead of the $7,400 contract price. That $1,000 difference is the unavoidable contract-market loss caused by Barnabas's repudiation. The $72,000 job loss was disclosed and provable, but it was avoidable by hiring the substitute technician, so it is not recoverable. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 302 also supports rejecting the no-promise trap because Stephen was the intended recipient of the promised performance.

- Consequential damages: the salary loss is disclosed but avoidable.
- Third-party beneficiaries: Stephen is the person for whom performance was promised.
- Expectation damages: the remaining number is the price needed to obtain equivalent performance.

## review_truth

An injured contract party who unreasonably skips a reasonable substitute loses avoidable losses but may still recover the contract-to-market price difference.
