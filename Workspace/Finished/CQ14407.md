### 1. Final Question

Barnabas runs a small organic seedling nursery. He emailed Timothy, a farm-supply wholesaler, a purchase order that read: "Please ship 2,000 biodegradable seedling trays at your listed price of $0.50 each." Timothy had the trays in stock. The next day, without replying to Barnabas's email, Timothy loaded the trays onto a delivery truck and shipped them. Barnabas received the trays, inspected them, and began using them in his nursery right away. Three days after the trays arrived, Barnabas found a packing slip inside the shipment bearing Timothy's company name and a printed clause that said: "Seller disclaims all liability for consequential damages." Barnabas had not seen this clause before. Over the next six weeks, Barnabas discovered that the trays cracked when exposed to sunlight, causing 400 seedlings to dry out and die. Barnabas lost $3,200 in expected sales from those seedlings. Barnabas sued Timothy for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability, seeking $3,200 in consequential damages. Timothy raised all appropriate defenses. Is Barnabas likely to recover the $3,200 in consequential damages?

**Call:** Is Barnabas likely to recover the $3,200 in consequential damages from Timothy?

Can the buyer recover consequential damages despite the disclaimer?

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Timothy's packing slip arrived three days after Barnabas had already received, inspected, and begun using the trays. By that point, a contract had already been formed by the parties' conduct — Timothy shipped the trays and Barnabas accepted them — under the principle that conduct recognizing the existence of a contract is sufficient to establish one. The consequential damages disclaimer in Timothy's acknowledgment was therefore not an original term of the contract but rather a proposal to modify it. Under UCC § 2-207(2), additional terms in a confirmation between merchants become part of the contract unless they materially alter it. A disclaimer of consequential damages is a material alteration (it would cause surprise or hardship if injected without assent), so it does NOT automatically become part of the contract. Since Barnabas never agreed to the disclaimer, it is not part of the contract, and Barnabas may recover consequential damages for the defective trays.

**Gold Key:** A contract can be formed by conduct — shipment and acceptance of goods — even when the parties' writings never fully agree. Once that contract exists, a later-arriving form with new terms is a proposal to modify, not an original term. And a consequential damages disclaimer is a material alteration that requires express assent to become part of the contract.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original B) — The "acceptance-of-goods-accepts-all-terms" trap.** This choice sells the idea that by accepting delivery of the trays, Barnabas also accepted every term that later showed up on a packing slip. That's backwards. The contract was already formed by the parties' conduct — Timothy shipped, Barnabas accepted. The packing slip arrived after the contract existed. Acceptance of goods does NOT mean acceptance of new terms that arrive later; those terms are at best a proposal to modify the contract, and a material alteration like a consequential damages disclaimer won't be incorporated without express assent. **Silver Key:** When a contract is already formed by conduct, a later-arriving form cannot unilaterally add new terms — it's a modification proposal, not an original term.

**B (original A) — The "buyers can't recover" overclaim.** This choice states a blanket rule that buyers are "generally not entitled" to consequential damages. That's simply not the law. Under the UCC, buyers CAN recover consequential damages when the damages were foreseeable and not excluded by a valid disclaimer. The default rule favors the buyer's recovery of consequential damages; it is the seller who must properly disclaim or limit them. This choice overstates the restriction and would deny Barnabas a remedy he is legally entitled to. **Gold Key:** The default UCC rule allows buyers to recover foreseeable consequential damages — a seller must properly disclaim or limit them for the exclusion to be effective.

**D (original C) — The "unconscionable" half-truth (DOMINANT TRAP).** This choice gets the right outcome (Barnabas recovers) but for the wrong reason. It claims the disclaimer is "per se unconscionable." That's an overstatement. A consequential damages disclaimer between merchants in a commercial context is NOT per se unconscionable — UCC § 2-719(3) expressly says limitation of consequential damages is not prima facie unconscionable where the loss is commercial. The real reason the disclaimer doesn't bar Barnabas's recovery is that it's a material alteration that never became part of the contract in the first place. The choice conflates two different doctrines: material alteration (which prevents a term from entering the contract) and unconscionability (which can invalidate a term that IS part of the contract). **Gold Key:** A consequential damages disclaimer between merchants is NOT per se unconscionable in a commercial context — the reason it doesn't bar recovery here is that it's a material alteration that never became part of the contract, not that it's unconscionable.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** UCC § 2-207 — Additional Terms in Acceptance or Confirmation.

**UCC § 2-207(1):** A definite and seasonable expression of acceptance operates as an acceptance even though it states additional or different terms, unless acceptance is expressly made conditional on assent to the additional terms.

**UCC § 2-207(2):** Between merchants, additional terms become part of the contract unless (a) the offer expressly limits acceptance to its terms, (b) they materially alter it, or (c) notification of objection is given within a reasonable time.

**UCC § 2-207(3):** Conduct by both parties recognizing the existence of a contract is sufficient to establish a contract for sale, even if writings do not. Terms consist of those on which writings agree, plus supplementary UCC gap-fillers.

**UCC § 2-207, Comment 4:** Examples of material alterations include clauses that would cause "surprise or hardship" — e.g., a disclaimer of consequential damages.

**UCC § 2-719(3):** Consequential damages may be limited or excluded unless the limitation or exclusion is unconscionable. Limitation of consequential damages is NOT prima facie unconscionable where the loss is commercial.

**Application to this fact pattern:**

1. **Contract formation by conduct:** Timothy shipped the trays; Barnabas accepted and used them. Under § 2-207(3), this conduct established a contract. The terms of that contract are those on which the writings agree (Barnabas's purchase order: 2,000 trays at $0.50 each), plus UCC gap-fillers — including the implied warranty of merchantability and the default availability of consequential damages.

2. **The disclaimer arrived after contract formation:** Timothy's packing slip with the consequential damages disclaimer arrived three days after Barnabas had already received and begun using the trays. At that point, the contract already existed. The disclaimer was therefore not an original term but a proposal to modify the contract.

3. **Even if the writings governed:** Under § 2-207(2), the disclaimer would still not become part of the contract because (a) it is a material alteration (disclaimer of consequential damages causes surprise/hardship per Comment 4), and (b) Barnabas never expressly assented to it.

4. **Unconscionability is the wrong frame:** UCC § 2-719(3) says consequential damages disclaimers are NOT per se unconscionable in commercial contexts. The disclaimer fails here not because it's unconscionable but because it never became part of the contract.

**Authority:** UCC §§ 2-207, 2-719(3); LII, Cornell Law School, https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-207.

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

#### Program Frame

This item tests UCC § 2-207 — the "battle of the forms" rule for contracts for the sale of goods. The specific issue is whether a consequential damages disclaimer that arrives after a contract has already been formed by conduct becomes part of the contract. This is a classic Contracts MBE topic: formation, additional terms, and material alteration.

#### Student Access Test

All four choices can be evaluated using only:
- The timeline facts in the stem (who did what, and when)
- The structural rule that a contract formed by conduct cannot be unilaterally modified by a later-arriving form
- The distinction between "material alteration" (a term that doesn't enter the contract automatically) and "unconscionable" (a term a court strikes down)

No outside legal doctrine is needed beyond what the C3 method teaches.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Barnabas (nursery owner) emailed Timothy (wholesaler) a purchase order for 2,000 seedling trays at $0.50 each. Timothy shipped them without replying. Barnabas received, inspected, and used them. Three days later, a packing slip inside the shipment contained a consequential damages disclaimer. Over six weeks, the trays cracked in sunlight, killing 400 seedlings and $3,200 in expected sales. Barnabas sued for breach of implied warranty of merchantability, seeking $3,200 consequential damages.

**Call:** Is Barnabas likely to recover the $3,200 in consequential damages from Timothy?

**Choices:**
- **A:** No — disclaimer became part of the contract when Barnabas accepted delivery
- **B:** No — buyers generally can't recover consequential damages for commercial losses
- **C:** Yes — Timothy's acknowledgment didn't alter the terms of an existing contract
- **D:** Yes — the disclaimer is a material alteration that is per se unconscionable

#### Distilled Core Question

A buyer ordered goods; the seller shipped them; the buyer accepted and used them. After delivery, the seller's form disclaimed consequential damages. The goods were defective, causing lost profits. Can the buyer recover consequential damages despite the disclaimer?

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks whether Barnabas is likely to recover consequential damages. The key structural fact: the contract was already formed by conduct (shipment + acceptance) before the disclaimer arrived. That means the disclaimer is a modification proposal, not an original term. And a consequential damages disclaimer is a material alteration that doesn't become part of the contract without express assent. Prediction: Barnabas recovers. Look for the "Yes" choice with the correct reasoning.

#### Trigger Facts

1. **Timothy shipped without replying** → Acceptance by conduct. The contract is forming through actions, not through matching forms.
2. **Barnabas received, inspected, and used the trays** → Acceptance of goods. The contract is now fully formed by conduct under UCC § 2-207(3).
3. **Packing slip arrived 3 days after delivery** → Post-formation. The contract already existed. The disclaimer is a proposal to modify, not an original term.
4. **Trays cracked in sunlight, killing seedlings** → Breach of implied warranty of merchantability. Foreseeable consequential damages.
5. **$3,200 in lost sales** → Measure of consequential damages claimed.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "No — disclaimer became part of the contract when Barnabas accepted delivery"**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The timeline kills this. The contract was already formed by conduct (shipment + acceptance) before the packing slip arrived. Accepting delivery of goods completes a contract — it doesn't open the door to every new term that shows up later.
2. **Student label:** The "acceptance-of-goods-accepts-all-terms" myth.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Yes, the disclaimer became part of the contract when Barnabas accepted delivery" — but this would only be true if the disclaimer was part of the original contract terms, which it wasn't.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** UCC § 2-207(3): conduct recognizing a contract establishes it. A later-arriving form is a proposal to modify, not an automatic amendment.

**Choice B: "No — buyers generally can't recover consequential damages for commercial losses"**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is an absolute statement ("generally not entitled") that overclaims. The default UCC rule ALLOWS buyers to recover foreseeable consequential damages. The seller has to properly disclaim or limit them.
2. **Student label:** The buyers-can't-recover overclaim.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "No, because the seller validly disclaimed consequential damages before the contract was formed" — but that's not what happened here.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** UCC § 2-719(3) permits consequential damages unless limited or excluded. The default favors buyer recovery.

**Choice C: "Yes — Timothy's acknowledgment didn't alter the terms of an existing contract"**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is correct. The contract was already formed by conduct. The packing slip arrived after. It's a modification proposal, not an original term. And a consequential damages disclaimer is a material alteration that needs express assent.
2. **Student label:** Correct answer.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true and responsive choice.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** UCC § 2-207(2): additional terms between merchants become part of the contract unless they materially alter it. A consequential damages disclaimer is a material alteration (Comment 4). No express assent = not part of the contract.

**Choice D: "Yes — the disclaimer is a material alteration that is per se unconscionable"**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Right outcome, wrong reason. The disclaimer IS a material alteration, but that doesn't make it "per se unconscionable." UCC § 2-719(3) says consequential damages disclaimers are NOT prima facie unconscionable in commercial contexts. The disclaimer fails because it never entered the contract, not because it's unconscionable.
2. **Student label:** The unconscionable half-truth (dominant trap).
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Yes, because the disclaimer is a material alteration that did not become part of the contract" — this would be correct.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** UCC § 2-719(3): limitation of consequential damages is not prima facie unconscionable where the loss is commercial. Material alteration and unconscionability are different doctrines.

#### Residual Answer

**C** is the surviving choice. It is the only choice that is both true (Barnabas recovers) AND responsive (correct legal reasoning: the disclaimer didn't alter an existing contract).
