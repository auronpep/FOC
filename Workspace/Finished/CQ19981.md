### 1. Final question

Police investigating forged prescription slips go to a rented townhome shared by Lydia and Ruth, two students who host a weekly Bible study. Lydia tells the officers they may look in the shared entryway and pantry. She also says Ruth keeps an upstairs art studio locked, and Lydia never goes inside it. The officers unlock Ruth's studio, open a small desk there, and find contraband.

Was the studio search justified by consent?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

A is correct. Lydia's consent covered the shared entryway and pantry, not Ruth's locked upstairs studio. Gold Key: third-party consent turns on actual or apparent common authority over the specific place searched, not general co-tenant status. Silver Key: when the consent answer moves from a shared area to a locked private area, split the map by space before choosing. The officers had clear limits and no reasonable basis to think Lydia could authorize the studio search.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

B is attractive because Ruth's absence feels like the missing permission fact. The breaker is that absence is not the deciding rule; the deciding rule is Lydia's lack of actual or apparent authority over the locked studio.

C is the dominant trap because it turns real shared-occupant consent into a whole-home permission slip. The breaker is the Gold Key: common authority is place-specific, and Lydia's own statement removed any apparent authority over Ruth's locked studio.

D is attractive because the officers had real permission to enter some parts of the townhome. The breaker is that consent to shared entry and pantry spaces does not carry into a separate locked private studio.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. Third-party consent is a Fourth Amendment warrant exception only when the consenting person has actual common authority over the place or effects searched, or when officers reasonably believe that authority exists. United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974), grounds third-party consent in mutual use and joint access or control. Illinois v. Rodriguez, 497 U.S. 177 (1990), permits searches based on apparent authority only when the officers' belief in authority is reasonable under the facts known at the time. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248 (1991), confirms that consent-search scope is measured by objective reasonableness. Here Lydia expressly limited consent to shared spaces and said she never used Ruth's locked studio. That defeats both actual authority and apparent authority over the specific place searched, so the consent did not justify the studio or desk search.

Sources: United States v. Matlock, https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/415/164/; Illinois v. Rodriguez, https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/497/177/; Florida v. Jimeno, https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/90-622.ZS.html.
