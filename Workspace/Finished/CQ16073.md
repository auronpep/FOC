## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question

Martha worked as the stockroom coordinator for Lydia's private Christian conference-supply company, which sold music equipment, classroom supplies, and event materials. One afternoon, while doing her assigned shelf reset and comparing stockroom labels against the company's inventory spreadsheet, Martha rolled a boxed digital piano to a staging alcove near the employee freight elevator. After she moved it, Martha noticed that the piano did not appear anywhere in the inventory spreadsheet. Realizing that no one would probably miss it, Martha decided to steal it. She planned to roll the piano onto the freight elevator just before closing and then load it into her van after everyone left. Before moving it again, however, Martha changed her mind, left the piano in the staging alcove, and added it to the company inventory.

Of which of the following crimes may Martha properly be convicted?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Martha's first movement of the boxed digital piano was authorized inventory work, and at that time she had not yet decided to steal it. Her later decision to steal did not become larceny because she changed her mind before any post-intent taking or carrying away. It also did not become embezzlement because she never converted the piano or exercised control over it inconsistently with Lydia's company rights after forming the plan.

Gold Key: For larceny, the theft intent must line up with a trespassory taking and carrying away; authorized movement before the theft intent forms does not become larceny by hindsight.

Gold Key: Embezzlement requires conversion of property already lawfully possessed; an unacted plan to convert is not conversion.

Silver Key: Timestamp the act, not just the thought. Ask what Martha did after the theft intent formed.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

B is the dominant trap because Martha physically rolled the piano near an exit and then decided to steal it. The breaker is timing: the rolling happened while she was doing assigned inventory work, and she never moved the piano again after forming theft intent.

D looks attractive because Martha had work access to the stockroom and was trusted with company property. The breaker is conversion: embezzlement needs serious interference with the owner's rights, and Martha changed her mind before using the piano as her own or moving it under the theft plan.

A sells the combined-crime overclaim. The same missing post-intent act defeats larceny, and the same missing conversion defeats embezzlement, so stacking both labels does not repair either one.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. Common-law larceny requires a trespassory taking and carrying away of another's personal property with intent to permanently deprive the owner. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), confirms the traditional stealing/conversion focus on wrongful taking with intent to deprive, and Model Penal Code section 223.2 treats theft by unlawful taking as unlawful taking or control over another's movable property with purpose to deprive. Here, Martha's only movement occurred before theft intent and under company authorization. She did not take or carry away the piano after deciding to steal it.

Embezzlement requires fraudulent conversion or serious interference with property already lawfully possessed. Georgia Code section 16-8-4 states the conversion idea in statutory form: a person who lawfully obtains another's property commits theft by conversion when she knowingly converts it to her own use in violation of the obligation. Martha never converted the piano or acted inconsistently with the company's rights after forming the plan. Authority checked: Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/342/246/; Model Penal Code section 223.2 text as reproduced in CALI's Criminal Law: An Integrated Approach, https://ristrophcriminallaw.lawbooks.cali.org/chapter/chapter-5-property-crimes/; Georgia Code section 16-8-4, https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/2020/title-16/chapter-8/article-1/section-16-8-4/.
