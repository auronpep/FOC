## Right-answer explanation (exam-register)
Peter's strongest argument is D. Pinkerton liability does not automatically make every conspirator liable for every crime a co-conspirator commits. 

**Gold Key: Pinkerton Limits**  
Pinkerton v. United States requires two elements for a conspirator to be liable for a co-conspirator's substantive offense: (1) the offense must have been committed in furtherance of the conspiracy, and (2) it must have been reasonably foreseeable as a natural and probable consequence of the unlawful agreement. A purely personal crime that does not advance the agreed criminal objective and has no connection to the scope of the conspiracy does not bind the other conspirators.

The murder was a private act between John and his former romantic partner. It occurred in a parking lot during an unrelated argument. It did nothing to further the fake detailing deposits. Peter knew nothing about it. Because both the "in furtherance" and "reasonably foreseeable from this conspiracy's scope" requirements fail, Peter has a complete defense to Pinkerton murder liability.

## Wrong-answer explanations
**A (original B mechanic - timing/ongoing alone suffices)**: Attractive because students often think "if we were still in the conspiracy when the crime happened, everyone is on the hook." This sells the misconception that the ongoing nature of the agreement is enough by itself. The breaker is that timing is irrelevant without furtherance of the specific conspiracy and reasonable foreseeability tied to its scope. The murder was a personal act that did not advance the deposit scheme at all.

**B (original D mechanic - specific item of fraud)**: Attractive because it sounds like a narrow factual defense ("this particular business"). This is the "wrong element" or "sounds lawyerly but misses the test" trap. The doctrinal reason Peter is not liable has nothing to do with the fact that the scheme involved car detailing rather than tickets or any other good; the reason is the complete absence of furtherance and foreseeability. Changing the merchandise would not change the outcome.

**C (original C mechanic - murder always foreseeable in any conspiracy - DOMINANT TRAP)**: Attractive because it offers a sweeping, easy-to-remember rule that feels morally satisfying ("conspirators are responsible for everything their partners do"). This is the classic overbroad_rule / common_student_myth trap. The breaker is that foreseeability is not presumed for every crime in every conspiracy; it is judged by the specific nature and scope of the agreed criminal project. A personal killing over a romantic dispute is not a natural consequence of a fake auto-detailing deposit scam.

## Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer
The credited answer is correct. Under the governing authority, a conspirator is liable for substantive offenses committed by a co-conspirator only when those offenses are (1) in furtherance of the conspiracy, (2) within the scope of the unlawful project, and (3) reasonably foreseeable as a necessary or natural consequence of the agreement. Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640 (1946). 

Here the murder was a personal act arising from a private romantic dispute. It bore no relationship to the fake mobile auto detailing deposit scheme, did not advance or protect that scheme, and was not a reasonably foreseeable consequence of two men taking deposits for services they never intended to perform. The original question's facts (unrelated personal killing during an ongoing but narrow fraud conspiracy) are preserved exactly in legal substance. No research contradicted the supplied official key.
