## 1. Final Question

At Grace Community Shelter, the deacons were seeking a way to increase the number of hot evening meals the kitchen could serve each night. On March 1, they posted the following notice on the bulletin board in the volunteers' break room:

The deacons of this shelter are offering a cash award of $200 to any volunteer who develops a modification of the current meal-preparation and serving workflow that will double the number of hot meals the kitchen can serve during the 6:00–7:30 p.m. dinner service. Detailed workflow proposals should be submitted to the head of Kitchen Ministry prior to June 1. In the event that proposals are submitted by more than one volunteer, the award will go to the volunteer who submits the plan which, in the opinion of the Kitchen Ministry, can be implemented most economically with the shelter's current volunteers and equipment.

A regular volunteer cook named Peter read the notice on March 5 and immediately began experimenting with new prep-station layouts and volunteer task assignments during his off hours. On March 8, he wrote and signed a memo that said, “I accept the deacons' offer of a $200 award for redesigning the kitchen workflow. I am hard at work on the project and expect to submit my workflow plan within a week or two.” Peter sent the note to the head of the Kitchen Ministry by the church office inter-office mail system, but it was somehow diverted and was never received.

What is the best characterization of the notice that was posted on March 1?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**C** is correct. The notice is an offer for a unilateral contract because the deacons promised the award only upon actual submission of a qualifying workflow plan; they did not seek or invite a return promise. Acceptance occurs by rendering the invited performance (submitting the plan), not by promising to submit one or by communicating a promise. Peter's memo, even if received, would not have formed a contract because it was a promise rather than the requested act of submission, and the company's obligation was conditioned on receipt and selection of a plan that met the criteria.

**Gold Key:** In a unilateral contract, the offeree accepts by performing the act the offer requests (here, submitting the design/plan). Beginning performance creates an option contract that prevents revocation (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 45), but the contract itself is not formed until the full invited performance is rendered and any conditions (receipt + selection) are satisfied. Mailing a promise does not constitute acceptance when the offer calls for an act.

**Silver Key:** Read the call for the precise characterization requested, then isolate the exact act the notice conditions payment upon (submission of the plan). Any choice that treats a mailed promise as sufficient acceptance or that converts the offer into a search for a promise fails the call.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A** is attractive because students sometimes assume every offer seeks a promise in return (bilateral default). It is defeated because the notice promises payment only if a plan is actually submitted and selected; it asks for no promise in exchange for the promise to pay. The deacons' obligation arises only upon receipt of a qualifying submission.

**B** is attractive because reward notices can resemble ads. It is defeated because the notice creates an immediate power of acceptance in any volunteer who submits a plan meeting the criteria; it is not a mere invitation to negotiate or a request for volunteers. The key difference between an offer and an invitation is whether the statement confers power of acceptance on the recipient.

**D** is the dominant trap because it combines two appealing errors: (1) treating the offer as seeking a promise (so the memo "accepts"), and (2) assuming the mailbox rule saves a lost communication. It is defeated because even a received memo would not have formed a contract—the offer called for the act of submission, not a promise to submit. The mailbox rule applies to acceptances by promise in bilateral contracts; it has no application when acceptance is by performance of a requested act.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited answer is correct and unchanged from the source. A posted notice that promises a prize or award only to the person who actually submits a design, plan, or other specified performance is a classic unilateral offer. The offeror seeks the act of submission, not a return promise. Acceptance is complete upon tender or completion of the invited performance (subject to any express conditions such as receipt and judging), not upon communication of a promise to perform.

Governing authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):

- Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 45 (1981): "Where an offer invites an offeree to accept by rendering a performance and does not invite a promissory acceptance, an option contract is created when the offeree tenders or begins the invited performance or tenders a beginning of it." The rule protects the offeree who has begun performance; the offeror cannot revoke once performance has started. Full formation still requires completion of the invited act and satisfaction of any conditions (here, actual submission that the Kitchen Ministry can use economically).

- Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 32: In case of doubt an offer is interpreted as inviting acceptance either by promise or by performance, but the language here removes doubt by conditioning payment solely on submission and selection of a plan.

- Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 50 and § 56 (acceptance by performance; notification not required unless the offer so provides or the offeree knows the offeror has no adequate means of learning of performance).

- Common-law reward-offer cases (persuasive): performance of the requested act (finding the lost dog, using the smoke ball and catching influenza, submitting the qualifying entry) constitutes acceptance. See, e.g., the reasoning in Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. (1893) 1 QB 256 (Eng.) (advertised reward accepted by performance; no prior communication required).

The source explanation correctly distinguishes unilateral (promise in exchange for an act) from bilateral (promise in exchange for a promise). The company's notice made clear it would pay only one prize and only upon actual receipt of a usable design/plan. Peter's memo was therefore irrelevant even if received. The notice created power of acceptance in every volunteer; it was therefore an offer, not an invitation to deal. No authority contradicts the official key A (now C after shuffle).
