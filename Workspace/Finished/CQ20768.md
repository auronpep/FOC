# CQ20768 — The Charter Sailboat's Keel Joint

## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question — stem + call

Barnabas is a licensed marine engineer. A charter company designed and built a new charter sailboat for paid coastal excursions. The company retained Barnabas for a single purpose: to design the auxiliary engine's raw-water cooling system for the vessel. To obtain the sailboat's certificate of inspection, maritime regulations required a licensed marine engineer to file a stamped general-arrangement drawing of the vessel with the maritime authority. Barnabas prepared that filing and submitted it with the certificate application. As the regulations required, the general-arrangement drawing depicted the entire vessel and was signed and sealed by Barnabas as a licensed marine engineer.

After the sailboat entered service, the keel-to-hull joint failed in calm water on a routine excursion. The vessel took on water and capsized, injuring a passenger, Ruth. During discovery in Ruth's action against Barnabas, it was established that the capsizing was caused by a design defect in the keel-to-hull joint, which was unrelated to the cooling system Barnabas designed. The defect was, however, present in the general-arrangement drawing Barnabas signed.

In that action, will Ruth prevail?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A licensed professional is hired to design only one component of a larger project. To get a government permit, she must file a stamped drawing that shows the whole project, and the stamped drawing contains a defect in a part she was not hired to design. That part fails and injures someone, who sues her directly. Does she owe a duty as to that defect?

**D.** Yes, because everyone who designed any part of the vessel is jointly and severally liable for the defect.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**B is correct.** This reads like a products-liability problem, but it is professional negligence. The charter company designed and built the vessel; Barnabas was retained for one narrow service — designing the engine's raw-water cooling system. His duty in tort ran only to the exercise of professional skill on that task. The capsizing came from the keel-to-hull joint, a part he was never hired to design, so he owed Ruth no duty to guard against that particular risk. Filing a stamped general-arrangement drawing to obtain the certificate did not enlarge his duty to the whole vessel: a permit filing is permission to proceed, not a warranty that every part of the project is defect-free.

**Gold Key:** A licensed professional's tort duty extends only to the specific task she was retained to perform; sealing a permit drawing certifies *her own* work, not a guarantee that the rest of the project is sound. **Silver Key:** B and C both say "No" — credit the one whose *reason* actually does the work; the deciding ground is the absence of a duty, not Barnabas's contractor status.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A — Yes, because Barnabas stamped and filed the drawing that depicted the defective keel joint.** This is the engineered pull: a vivid, literally true fact — his seal sits on the very drawing showing the defect — invites you to treat the stamp as a warranty of the whole vessel. The breaker is scope: stamping a drawing for a permit certifies the work he was retained to do, and there is no fact that he undertook to inspect or vouch for the keel design. **Gold Key:** the seal certifies his own task, not the entire project; a true fact that proves a non-dispositive point still loses.

**C — No, because Barnabas served as an independent contractor.** This one lands the right outcome, which makes it feel safe, and "independent contractor" sounds like a real liability rule. But contractor status is a shield for the *hiring party* against vicarious liability — it answers a different question. Ruth is suing Barnabas directly, so his status as an independent contractor neither creates nor defeats his own duty. **Silver Key:** when two choices share the verdict, the one citing the wrong reason still fails.

**D — Yes, because everyone who designed any part of the vessel is jointly and severally liable for the defect.** The lawyerly phrase "jointly and severally liable" invites you to sweep every participant into one liability pool. The word doing the damage is "everyone": joint and several liability attaches only to those who are *first* established as tortfeasors acting in concert or causing one indivisible harm. Barnabas neither acted in concert with the vessel's designers nor caused the keel defect, so there is nothing for joint-and-several apportionment to operate on.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested rule (unchanged from the original):** One who undertakes to render professional services is held to the skill and knowledge of the profession, but the *scope* of that duty is defined by the task actually undertaken; a defect outside that undertaking is outside the duty.

- **Restatement (Second) of Torts § 299A** — a person who undertakes to render services in a profession is required to exercise the skill and knowledge normally possessed by members of that profession; this is a *negligence* standard, not a warranty or strict-liability standard, and it is measured against the service the professional actually undertook.
- **Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability § 19** — services are not "products"; the provision of a service is governed by negligence, defeating any strict-products-liability theory (kills choice A's implicit warranty premise).
- **Restatement (Second) of Torts § 324A** — liability for negligent performance of an undertaking to a third person extends only to the scope of the undertaking; one does not assume a duty as to matters one did not undertake.
- **Duty as a question of law:** whether a defendant owes the plaintiff a duty, and the scope of any such duty, is decided by the court; the professional's duty here is bounded by the cooling-system retention.
