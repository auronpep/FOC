The governing authority is *New York v. Quarles*, 467 U.S. 649 (1984), which recognized a public-safety exception to the requirement that Miranda warnings precede admissible custodial answers when officers ask questions reasonably prompted by concern for public safety. The Court held that the exception does not turn on the officer's subjective motivation and is limited by the exigency that justifies it. See https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/467/649/. The baseline Miranda rule remains that custodial interrogation generally requires warnings before the prosecution may use the suspect's statement. See *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/384/436/.

Applied here, the question, "Where did you put the pistol?" was directed to locating a loaded gun in a crowded public area. That is the same legal issue and credited outcome as the source item. The officer did not ask for an account of the robbery or a broad confession. The statement identifying the pistol's location is admissible under the narrow public-safety exception. No doctrinal-currency hotlist issue changes the result.
