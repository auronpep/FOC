### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The strongest Equal Protection argument is that the rule **may penalize the fundamental right to interstate travel and therefore triggers strict scrutiny**. The constitutional right to travel has three components: (1) the right to enter and leave a state, (2) the right to be treated as a welcome visitor while temporarily in another state, and (3) the right to be treated equally to other residents once the traveler has established residence in the new state. The third component — the "equal-treatment" or "newcomer" right — is squarely at issue here. A state rule that conditions full Medicaid coverage on a one-year residency period, or that limits new residents to the lower of their prior state's level or the current state's level, draws a classification between established residents and new residents. That classification penalizes the right to travel because it operates as a financial disincentive to relocate and conditions the receipt of a basic public benefit on the exercise of the constitutional right to move. The Supreme Court has held that durational-residency classifications that burden the right to travel are subject to strict scrutiny and are generally invalid. **Gold Key:** the constitutional right at issue is INTERSTATE travel, not international travel; durational-residency rules that disadvantage new residents because they recently moved trigger strict scrutiny.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Rational basis applies because public benefits are always discretionary and the state may allocate them as it wishes.**

This is the **dominant trap**. The argument is that welfare/Medicaid is a public benefit, that public benefits are discretionary, and that the state may therefore set whatever eligibility rules it wants under rational-basis review. The argument is wrong because **public-benefit rules can still burden fundamental rights**. The right to interstate travel is a fundamental right protected by the Constitution, and durational-residency classifications that burden the right to travel are subject to strict scrutiny — not rational-basis review. The "discretionary benefits" framing collapses when the eligibility rule operates as a penalty on the exercise of a constitutional right. **Gold Key:** durational-residency rules that disadvantage new residents because they recently moved are not subject to mere rational-basis review; they trigger strict scrutiny because they burden the fundamental right to travel.

**B. The rule is valid because states may always favor long-term residents over newcomers.**

This is the "general police power" trap. It is true that states have broad police power to make classifications among residents, but the constitutional right to travel limits that power when the classification penalizes the exercise of the right to move. The right to be treated equally to other residents once the traveler has established residence in the new state is a component of the constitutional right to travel, and a durational-residency rule that disadvantages new residents is a textbook violation of that component. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: the right to travel limits the state's general police power when the classification operates as a penalty on the right to move.

**C. Strict scrutiny applies only if the new resident crossed an international border.**

This is the "international travel" misframing. The constitutional right at issue is **interstate** travel, not international travel. The right to travel from one state to another is one of the most fundamental rights protected by the Constitution; it is a component of the Privileges and Immunities Clause of Article IV, § 2, and is also protected by the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment and by the Equal Protection Clause. Shapiro v. Thompson, 394 U.S. 618 (1969) (striking down one-year residency requirements for welfare); Saenz v. Roe, 526 U.S. 489 (1999) (recognizing the right to be treated equally to other residents once the traveler has established residence). The "international border" framing is wrong; this is an interstate-mover case.

### B. The rule is valid because states may always favor long-term residents over newcomers.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The "states may always favor long-term residents" framing is a general-police-power claim that the constitutional right to travel overrides. 4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The right to be treated equally to other residents once the traveler has established residence in the new state is a component of the constitutional right to travel. Saenz v. Roe, 526 U.S. 489, 500–03 (1999). A durational-residency classification is a textbook violation of that component.

### C. Strict scrutiny applies only if the new resident crossed an international border.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The "international border" framing is wrong. The constitutional right at issue is INTERSTATE travel; Hannah and David moved from State B to State A — an interstate move, not an international one.
2. **Student label:** International-border misframing.
