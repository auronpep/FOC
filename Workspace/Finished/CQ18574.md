### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Common-law burglary is not just any unlawful entry with theft intent. Gold Key: common-law burglary keeps the old elements: breaking and entering, another person's dwelling, at nighttime, with intent to commit a felony inside. Silver Key: when the call says common-law, do not let a modern-statute answer replace the antique elements. Peter entered a retail shop at midday. ### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells the modern-statute shortcut. Many jurisdictions do treat business spaces and daytime entries as burglary, but that answers a different call. Silver Key: the phrase "common-law burglary" locks the older element set, so modern expansion cannot make Peter guilty here.

C sells a value-threshold detour. The question does not give a jurisdictional theft threshold, and the decisive problem is not the amount stolen. The burglary charge fails because the place was not a dwelling and the entry was not at nighttime.

D sells the half-built common-law formula. Breaking, entering, and theft intent are part of the rule, but they do not complete it. Gold Key: the old rule also requires another person's dwelling and nighttime entry; those two missing facts defeat burglary even if theft intent is present.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is unchanged. Common-law burglary requires breaking and entering the dwelling house of another at nighttime with intent to commit a felony inside. In Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575 (1990), the Supreme Court distinguished modern generic burglary from the classic common-law definition and identified the common-law formulation as breaking and entering a dwelling at night with felony intent. Taylor also notes that modern statutes have expanded beyond dwelling, nighttime, breaking, and felony-intent limitations, which is why a modern-statute answer does not fit this call. Blackstone's Commentaries, drawing on Coke, likewise frames burglary around night entry into a mansion house with felonious intent. Peter's shop entry occurred at 12:30 p.m. and the shop was not used as a dwelling. Those missing elements independently defeat common-law burglary.

Authority layer: Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575, 592-94 & n.3 (1990); 4 Blackstone, Commentaries *224.
