# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question
In the crowded marketplace of Ephesus, Silas met with a man he believed to be a rogue merchant. The merchant offered to sell Silas a pouch of "sacred temple incense," the private possession of which was strictly forbidden by local decree. Silas, believing the pouch contained the forbidden incense, paid the merchant several silver coins. In reality, the merchant was a disguised temple official, and the pouch contained nothing but common road dust. Silas was arrested as he reached for the pouch and was subsequently charged with attempted possession of forbidden incense.

What is the most likely outcome of Silas's case regarding the impossibility of the crime?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
Silas tries to buy forbidden incense from an undercover official, but the incense is actually common dust. Is he guilty of attempt?

### 3. Final Answer Choices
A. Silas is not guilty because the presence of a disguised official constitutes legal entrapment.
B. Silas is guilty only if he had previously tested the substance to confirm its sacred properties.
C. Silas is guilty of attempt because he intended to possess the forbidden incense and took a substantial step based on that belief.
D. Silas is not guilty because it was factually impossible for him to possess forbidden incense when the pouch contained only dust.

### 4. Correct Answer

### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation
Attempt liability focuses on the defendant's intent and the steps taken toward completing the crime, evaluated based on the facts as the defendant believed them to be. Silas intended to possess the forbidden incense (specific intent) and performed a substantial step by meeting the merchant and paying for the pouch. The fact that the pouch actually contained common dust is a "factual impossibility." At common law and under modern statutes, factual impossibility is not a defense to a charge of attempt. Had the facts been as Silas believed, he would have completed the crime of possessing forbidden incense.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations
- **A:** This choice introduces entrapment, which is a misfit doctrine. Entrapment occurs when the government induces a person to commit a crime they were not otherwise predisposed to commit. Merely providing an opportunity (like an undercover sale) does not constitute entrapment, and the question specifically asks about the *impossibility* of the crime, making this choice non-responsive to the call.
- **B:** This choice suggests a "tested substance" requirement, which is a fabricated rule. The prosecution must prove the defendant's *intent* and *belief*, but the law does not require the defendant to have personal scientific or ritualistic confirmation of the contraband's nature before attempt liability attaches.
- **D:** This is the dominant trap. It plays on the common misconception that if a crime *cannot* be completed (because the "incense" isn't real), the defendant cannot be guilty of trying. This correctly identifies the "factual impossibility" but incorrectly asserts it as a valid defense. Factual impossibility is famously not a defense to attempt.

### 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification
**Authority:** *United States v. Quijada*, 588 F.2d 1253 (9th Cir. 1978); MPC § 5.01(1)(a).
The "factual impossibility" rule holds that if a defendant's intended conduct would constitute a crime if the attendant circumstances were as he believed them to be, he is guilty of attempt. Selling "non-narcotic" substances as narcotics to a buyer who believes they are real is the standard illustration of this rule. The transform preserves the rule (attempt/impossibility), the issue (fake contraband/undercover sale), and the credited outcome.
