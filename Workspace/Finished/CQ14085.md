## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Lydia's award is far too low — the jury found a breach but gave her $4.50 on a $188,000 claim. She wants the award *increased*. In state court, State A would let her seek additur (a court-ordered increase as an alternative to a new trial). But this is a federal diversity case, and additur is unconstitutional in federal court under the Seventh Amendment. That federal constitutional rule is on point and controls, even though a federal court sitting in diversity ordinarily applies state substantive law. Because additur is off the table and remittitur only *reduces* an excessive award (the opposite of what Lydia needs), her single available route is a motion for a **new trial on the ground that the verdict is inadequate**.

**Gold Key:** Additur — raising an inadequate jury award as an alternative to a new trial — is unconstitutional in federal court under the Seventh Amendment, even in diversity cases. A federal plaintiff who believes the award is too low can only move for a new trial on the ground that the verdict is inadequate. **Silver Key:** On a diversity Erie question, first ask whether a controlling federal rule or constitutional provision is on point; if so, it controls and you never reach the state-law option — here the Seventh-Amendment additur bar is on point, so State A's additur is irrelevant.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule (additur in federal court):** Additur — a trial court's offer to the defendant of a higher damages figure as the alternative to a new trial, used to cure an inadequate verdict — violates the Seventh Amendment's reexamination clause and is not available in federal court. *Dimick v. Schiedt*, 293 U.S. 474 (1935) (holding additur unconstitutional under the Seventh Amendment). Remittitur, by contrast, is permitted because reducing an excessive award to a figure the evidence supports does not deny the parties a jury trial on facts already found.

**Governing rule (remittitur / new trial for damages):** Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 59, a court may grant a new trial on numerous grounds, including that the verdict is excessive *or* inadequate. For an excessive award, the court may order a new trial or offer the plaintiff remittitur (acceptance of a reduced award in lieu of a new trial); the court may not simply reduce the award without offering the new-trial alternative. *Hetzel v. Prince William County*, 523 U.S. 208 (1998) (per curiam). For an inadequate award in federal court, the only remedy is a new trial — additur being unavailable.

**Governing rule (Erie overlay):** A federal court sitting in diversity must apply state substantive law (*Erie R.R. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938)), but the threshold question is always whether a controlling federal statute, Federal Rule, or constitutional provision is on point; if so, the federal rule governs and there is no Erie substantive/procedural balancing. Here the Seventh-Amendment bar on additur is on point and controls, even though State A's additur rule would otherwise be the applicable damages remedy. On the related question of the standard for reviewing verdict size, *Gasperini v. Center for Humanities, Inc.*, 518 U.S. 415 (1996), requires a federal diversity court to apply the state standard for excessiveness — but it does not, and cannot, resurrect additur in federal court.

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

### Program Frame

This item tests **Civil Procedure → Law Applied by Federal Courts (Erie) → Erie Doctrine** (outline code 96060100), through the lens of post-trial damages motions. The core conflict: a federal diversity jury returns a verdict for the plaintiff but awards grossly inadequate damages, and the question is which motion is available. The answer turns on the Erie threshold — a controlling federal rule on point prevails over state law — and the specific federal rule that additur is unconstitutional under the Seventh Amendment. This is a Core-difficulty item requiring the student to (1) recognize the diversity/Erie posture, (2) apply the additur bar even though State A allows additur, and (3) reject the wrong-direction (remittitur) and wrong-standard ("shocks the conscience") options.

### Student Access Test

### Question Data

**Stem:** Lydia, an olive-oil presser in State A, contracted with Barnabas, a jar-maker in State B, for storage jars; Barnabas failed to deliver conforming jars. Lydia filed a diversity action against him in federal court in State A for breach of contract, seeking $188,000. After a five-day trial the jury returned a verdict for Lydia but awarded only $4.50. Federal law: a "shocks the conscience" excessiveness standard supports a new trial or remittitur. State A law: "excessive" -> new trial or remittitur; "inadequate" -> new trial or additur.

**Call:** Which motion should Lydia file to challenge the jury's damages award?

**Choices:**

- D: Motion for a new trial or additur, on the ground that the jury's damage award is inadequate.

### Distilled Core Question

In a federal diversity case, a jury returns a verdict for the plaintiff but awards grossly inadequate compensatory damages. Which post-trial motion may the plaintiff file to challenge the inadequate award?

### Call and Prediction

The call asks which motion the prevailing-but-under-compensated plaintiff should file. The award is far too low ($4.50 on a $188,000 claim), so the plaintiff wants it raised. In state court that would mean additur, but additur is unconstitutional in federal court, and this is a federal diversity case. Remittitur only reduces an excessive award, so it is the wrong direction. Prediction: a motion for a new trial on the ground that the verdict is inadequate. This points to C. The student must then reject the additur options (B, D) and the remittitur option (A).
