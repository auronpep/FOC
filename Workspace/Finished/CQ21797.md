### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. Daniel's strongest defense is not that Mary also contributed to Peter's death; one death can have more than one factual cause. His strongest defense is that Mary's refusal to unlock the RV or call for help was an unforeseeable intervening act. If a later act is so unforeseeable that it becomes a superseding intervening cause, Daniel's knife wound is no longer the legal or proximate cause of Peter's death for murder liability.

**Gold Key:** In homicide causation, separate factual cause from legal cause. A defendant can be a factual cause even when someone else also contributes, but an unforeseeable superseding intervening cause can break legal causation.

**Silver Key:** On a "most effective causation defense" call, do not stop at "someone else also caused it." Look for the answer that says the later cause was unforeseeable, because that is the chain-breaking argument.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
**A is attractive** because a forearm wound sounds less naturally fatal than a wound to the chest, head, or abdomen. The breaker is that "not usually fatal" is not the causation test. A rare result may still be foreseeable enough for legal causation, especially where the wound produced heavy bleeding. Silver Key: the defense needs a chain-breaking fact, not just a statistical-sounding observation about the body part.

**C is attractive** because it correctly spots that Mary mattered. Her refusal was a substantial factor; if she had called for help, Peter would have lived. The breaker is that this does not answer Daniel's best defense. Multiple causes can coexist. Mary's conduct helps Daniel only if it was superseding, and the answer does not say that.

**D is attractive** because it uses clean but-for language: Peter would not have died if Mary had obtained prompt medical care. The breaker is the same multiple-cause trap. That fact makes Mary a factual cause; it does not prove Daniel was not also a factual and legal cause. Gold Key: homicide causation separates factual cause from legal cause, and the only chain-breaking argument here is unforeseeable superseding intervention.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule is unchanged. Murder requires the prosecution to prove that the defendant caused the victim's death. Causation in homicide has two pieces: factual cause and legal or proximate cause. Daniel's knife wound is a factual cause because Peter would not have been bleeding without it. Mary may also be a factual cause because Peter would have survived had she obtained help. But the existence of a second factual cause does not erase the first. The defense with real force is legal cause: if Mary's later refusal to help was unforeseeable, it may be a superseding intervening cause that breaks the chain between Daniel's wound and Peter's death.

The authority floor is satisfied by Model Penal Code Section 2.03, which treats causation in result offenses as requiring but-for causation plus any additional causal requirement imposed by the offense, and by the criminal proximate-cause rule stated in People v. Kibbe, 35 N.Y.2d 407 (1974), later discussed in Henderson v. Kibbe, 431 U.S. 145 (1977): the ultimate harm must be foreseeable as reasonably related to the accused's conduct. California's current homicide causation instruction, CALCRIM No. 620 (2026), likewise states that there may be more than one cause of death and that an act need not be the only factor causing death. It also treats third-party negligence or omission as non-exculpatory when the defendant's act remains a substantial factor. None of the current-law hotlist doctrines is implicated. The official key is consistent with the research: the unforeseeability of Mary's refusal is Daniel's most effective defense argument.

#### review_truth
A later act breaks homicide causation only when it is a superseding intervening cause; merely adding another factual cause does not erase the defendant's causal role.
