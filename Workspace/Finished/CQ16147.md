## Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**A is correct.** The statute requires "breaking and entering" a building or structure with intent to commit a felony or to steal. When a store is open to the public during its regular business hours, entry by a member of the public is privileged and consensual. There is therefore no "breaking." The open-to-the-public status directly negates the breaking element, so Peter cannot be guilty of burglary no matter what his intent was once inside. 

**Gold Key:** Premises open to the public (or the entrant is licensed/privileged) at the time of entry = no "breaking" for burglary. This is the precise distinction that defeats any claim that walking through an unlocked door during business hours satisfies the element.

The killing of the passerby and the dropped toy gun are irrelevant to the burglary charge. The crime of burglary (under this statute) is complete upon the breaking and entering with the requisite intent; actual commission of the intended felony is unnecessary.

## Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The governing rule is the common-law-derived definition of burglary embedded in the statute: breaking and entering any building or structure with intent to commit a felony or to steal therein. 

"Breaking" at common law (and under statutes using this language) requires a trespassory entry — an entry without consent, license, or privilege. Entry during regular business hours into a store that is open to the public is privileged and consensual; members of the public are invited in. Therefore there is no breaking. See, e.g., the black-letter statement in standard references and case law applying the open-to-the-public rule: "It is not considered breaking and entering if the premises are at the time open to the public or the person is licensed or privileged to enter." (Cornell LII, Breaking and Entering, summarizing common-law and modern statutory application; consistent with MBE-tested explanations in the source row.)

The statute's use of the traditional "breaking and entering" phrase incorporates this limitation. The credited answer (now A) directly negates the breaking element with a fact the stem supplies: the store was open, the sign was up, customers were coming and going, and Peter simply walked through the front door during business hours.
