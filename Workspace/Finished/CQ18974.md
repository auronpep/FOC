### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**C** is correct. A district-court order granting or denying class certification is **not** appealable as of right. The route to the court of appeals is **Rule 23(f) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure**, which lets a party **petition** the court of appeals for permission to appeal. The court of appeals has discretion whether to grant the petition; permission is not guaranteed. Neither the importance of the class-certification question nor the presence of a federal question changes the route.

**Silver Key:** when the call asks "what is the appellate route" for a non-final order, scan for a permission-based statute or rule (Rule 23(f), 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b), mandamus, collateral order). The default is no interlocutory appeal as of right.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Immediate appeal only if the case also contains a federal question.**

The "federal question" hook misstates the rule. The class-certification appellate route does not turn on the presence or absence of a federal question. A state-law consumer class action with no federal question can still be appealed via a Rule 23(f) petition; a federal-question consumer class action is **not** appealable as of right simply because a federal question is in the case. The procedural posture controls, not the substantive theory.

**B. Automatic appeal as of right because class certification is important.**

This is the dominant trap. "Importance" of the underlying question is the rhetorical hook the parties typically invoke; it does not create appellate jurisdiction. The final-judgment rule of 28 U.S.C. § 1291 is the default, and Rule 23(f) carves out a narrow, **discretionary** permission-based exception. "Important" + "the court of appeals must hear it" is the wrong rule stated in confident language. **Silver Key:** when a non-final order is the subject, the default is no appeal as of right; locate the specific permission-granting rule or statute before picking an as-of-right answer.

**D. No possible review ever, even after final judgment.**

This is the overclaim trap. Class-certification rulings are reviewable (a) on appeal from final judgment under 28 U.S.C. § 1291, and (b) in some circumstances through Rule 23(f) immediately. The "no possible review ever" framing is a flat misstatement. The breaker is that interlocutory review of a class-certification order **does** exist — it just requires a Rule 23(f) petition rather than an as-of-right appeal.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18974, a Civil Procedure item involving a district court's certification of a consumer class action and the defendant's immediate notice of appeal. The official key is B: a discretionary Rule 23(f) petition for permission to appeal.

The default rule of federal appellate jurisdiction is the final-judgment rule: a party may appeal only from a "final decision" of the district court. 28 U.S.C. § 1291. A class-certification order is interlocutory; it does not end the case. Absent a specific statutory or rule-based exception, immediate appellate review is unavailable.

Rule 23(f) supplies the relevant exception. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23(f), added in 1998, provides that a court of appeals "may permit an appeal from an order granting or denying class-action certification" if a petition for permission to appeal is filed within fourteen days of the order. The decision whether to grant the petition is committed to the discretion of the court of appeals. Rule 23(f) is the **only** mechanism for immediate federal appellate review of a class-certification order in the usual case; it is permission-based, not as-of-right. The rule reflects the Advisory Committee's view that class-certification rulings often have substantial practical consequences and may warrant immediate review, but that an automatic right of appeal would be costly and inefficient.

Other paths to immediate review exist in narrow circumstances. A party may seek interlocutory review under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b) (certified question), or mandamus, or under the collateral-order doctrine, but none of these is the **standard** route for a class-certification order, and the question presented here is the standard route. The court of appeals' decision whether to grant a Rule 23(f) petition is reviewed for abuse of discretion.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: the defendant must use Rule 23(f), not an as-of-right appeal. Authority: Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23(f); 28 U.S.C. § 1291; see generally *Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay*, 437 U.S. 463 (1978) (interlocutory review of class certification historically limited).

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. Immediate appeal only if the case also contains a federal question.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The student treats "federal question" as the procedural gate. The Rule 23(f) route is the same whether or not the case has a federal question. The answer text itself states a rule that is not the law.

4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The appellate route for a class-certification order is Rule 23(f), which is not conditioned on the presence of a federal question. Federal-question jurisdiction is a subject-matter-jurisdiction concept, not a procedural-route trigger.

### B. Automatic appeal as of right because class certification is important.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the dominant trap. The student accepts the importance argument and concludes that an as-of-right appeal must exist. The breaker is the final-judgment rule plus Rule 23(f)'s narrow, permission-based carve-out; importance is not a statutory hook.

4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23(f) provides that a court of appeals may permit an appeal from an order granting or denying class-action certification if a petition is filed within fourteen days. The decision is discretionary, not as-of-right. Importance does not create appellate jurisdiction.

### C. A discretionary Rule 23(f) petition for permission to appeal.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the residual answer. After the dominant trap (B) is recognized as the importance-as-jurisdiction myth and the two single-filter distractors (A, D) are cut, the residual is the discretionary Rule 23(f) petition.
2. **Student label:** Residual answer (Rule 23(f)).
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23(f) is the narrow, permission-based carve-out for immediate federal appellate review of a class-certification order. The court of appeals has discretion whether to grant the petition; importance of the question does not change the route.

### D. No possible review ever, even after final judgment.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The "no possible review ever, even after final judgment" framing is a flat overclaim. The breaker is that final-judgment review under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 is always available at the end of the case, and Rule 23(f) interlocutory review is available now.
