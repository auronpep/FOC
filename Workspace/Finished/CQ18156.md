## 1. Final Question

Stephen approaches elderly Naomi at a roadside farmers' market and tells her: "I'll harvest your apple orchard this October for $1,200." Naomi pays Stephen $1,200 in cash on the spot. Stephen has no intention of doing the harvest and never returns. He spends the money within days. There was no misrepresentation about Stephen's farming experience, equipment, or current availability — only the promise of future work. Is Stephen guilty of false pretenses at common law?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**D is correct.**

Common-law false pretenses has a present-fact requirement: the defendant must make a knowingly false representation of a *presently existing* material fact. Stephen's statement — "I'll harvest your orchard this October" — is a promise about future conduct. It describes what Stephen will do, not what is currently true. Even though Stephen knew at the moment he spoke that he had no intention of performing, his statement was grammatically and legally about the future.

**Gold Key:** The traditional common-law rule holds that a false promise, even one made with a secret fraudulent intent, is a statement about the future, not the present. A present mental state (hidden intent not to perform) does not convert a future-oriented promise into a false representation of presently existing fact under the common-law definition. No present fact = no common-law false pretenses.

Stephen may have civil liability for breach of contract and could be prosecuted under modern theft-by-deception statutes, but the common-law false pretenses charge fails on the present-fact element.

**Silver Key:** Lock D by naming the axis: A and D fight on whether Stephen's hidden intent at the moment of the promise transforms the promise into a statement of present fact. The common-law rule is that it does not — the promise is future-oriented regardless of the defendant's secret state of mind.

## 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A — dominant trap.** The pull: if Stephen accepted money while secretly knowing he would never perform, he was lying about something right now — his willingness to deal honestly. That reasoning locates a present mental state and makes A feel like a present-fact representation. The breaker: the traditional common-law rule draws the line at what the *statement* describes, not at what the defendant privately knew. Stephen's statement described future conduct. Some modern courts and the MPC accept the implicit-current-intent theory, but the bar-exam common-law rule rejects it. **Gold Key:** a secret fraudulent intent does not convert a future promise into a false representation of presently existing fact at common law.

**B.** The pull: Naomi was careless — no contract, no references, cash upfront — and a student may feel that her carelessness contributed to her loss. The breaker: criminal liability never depends on the victim's failure to take precautions. A defendant's guilt is measured by the defendant's conduct and mental state, not by the victim's due diligence. This choice answers the wrong question entirely.

**C.** The pull: false pretenses does require that the defendant *knew* the representation was false at the time he made it — and Stephen obviously did. C correctly identifies the scienter requirement. The breaker: false pretenses requires both knowing falsity AND a presently existing fact. C supplies the first element but ignores the second. Stephen's knowingly false statement was still a promise about the future; meeting the scienter prong cannot substitute for meeting the present-fact prong. **Gold Key:** even a deliberately broken promise remains a promise about the future — it does not become a false statement of present fact just because the defendant knew he would not perform.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Tested rule confirmed.** Common-law false pretenses has five elements: (1) a false representation (2) of a presently existing material fact (3) made knowingly (4) with intent to defraud (5) that causes the victim to pass title to property. The present-fact element (element 2) is the decisive one here. A false promise of future performance does not satisfy it.

**Authority:** LaFave, Substantive Criminal Law § 19.7 (3d ed.) states the traditional common-law elements and confirms that false promises do not satisfy the present-fact requirement. The Model Penal Code § 223.3 deliberately expands the offense to cover false promises of future performance (theft by deception), departing from the common-law rule — this contrast confirms that the common-law rule bars prosecution on a future promise alone. No case name fabricated; rule statement based on settled black-letter.

**Credited outcome unchanged:** the original key C maps to new key D; the legal substance — not guilty of common-law false pretenses because the present-fact element is not met by a future promise — is identical.

#### § 15 · Outline Mastery Map

**Placement:** 73030300 — Crimes against Property > False pretenses

**This item teaches:** The present-fact element is the most commonly tested and most commonly confused element of common-law false pretenses. It is what distinguishes false pretenses from breach of contract, theft by deception, and fraud under the MPC.

**Adjacent nodes to master:**

**Fills:** The present-fact requirement is the key distinction between false pretenses and larceny by trick (possession vs. title) and between false pretenses and breach of contract (crime vs. civil wrong). Future promises → civil; present-fact falsehood → potential criminal.

- **Contracts:** A false future promise is a breach of contract (or potentially promissory fraud under Restatement Second § 530 in civil law), not criminal false pretenses. Students who think in contract terms will be drawn to A.

Here Naomi gave $1,200 cash — title passes — so the correct charge frame is false pretenses (not larceny by trick), but the issue is whether the present-fact element is met, not which property crime applies.
