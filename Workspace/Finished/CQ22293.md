## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct** under the attempted battery theory of criminal assault. Criminal assault has two distinct forms: (1) an attempt to commit battery, and (2) intentionally placing the victim in fear of imminent harmful contact. The attempted battery form requires only (a) specific intent to commit battery and (b) a substantial step toward completing it. Here, Peter specifically intended to kill Timothy (which necessarily includes intent to apply unlawful force — the mens rea for battery), and he took a substantial step by firing three crossbow bolts at Timothy's cart. That none of the bolts penetrated the iron plating is irrelevant — attempt does not require completion. Timothy's awareness is also irrelevant because this is the attempted battery form, not the fear-inducing form. Additionally, the dual sovereignty doctrine holds that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar separate prosecutions by different sovereigns (federal and state) arising from the same conduct. Peter's federal acquittal of attempted murder therefore does not bar a state prosecution for criminal assault.

**Gold Key:** Criminal assault as attempted battery requires only specific intent to commit battery plus a substantial step — the victim need not be aware of the attempt. This is distinct from the fear-based form of assault, which does require victim awareness and apprehension.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A: No, because Timothy was unaware that any bolts had been fired.**

This choice is attractive because students learn that one form of criminal assault requires the victim to be placed in fear, and fear requires awareness. The trap is applying the wrong form of assault to the facts. Timothy's awareness matters only for the fear-based theory of assault. Here, the prosecution can proceed on the attempted battery theory, which requires no victim awareness at all. **Gold Key:** The attempted battery form of assault does not require the victim to know about the attempt — only specific intent and a substantial step.

**B: No, because Peter has already been acquitted in the federal court.**

This choice appeals to the intuitive notion that one cannot be tried twice for the same conduct. The trap is misapplying the Double Jeopardy Clause. Under the dual sovereignty doctrine, the federal and state governments are separate sovereigns, each with independent power to prosecute. A federal acquittal does not bar a state prosecution for a different offense arising from the same act. The constitutional protection against double jeopardy does not apply across sovereign lines.

**C: Yes, because Peter's conduct would cause a reasonable person to be placed in fear of his or her life.**

This choice is attractive because it states a correct legal principle — assault can be committed by intentionally placing the victim in reasonable apprehension of imminent harmful contact. However, that form of assault requires the victim to actually be aware of the defendant's conduct and to experience apprehension as a result. Here, Timothy was unaware of the bolts because of the street preacher's noise. The reasonable-person-fear standard is therefore inapplicable on these facts. The correct theory is attempted battery, not fear induction.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Criminal Assault — Two Forms:**

1. **Attempted battery assault**: Specific intent to commit battery (intentional or reckless application of unlawful force to another) + substantial step toward completion. Victim awareness is *not* required. See *Commonwealth v. Slaney*, 345 Mass. 135 (1962); MPC § 211.1(1)(a).

2. **Fear-based assault**: Intentional act that places the victim in reasonable apprehension of imminent harmful or offensive contact. Requires (a) victim awareness of the defendant's conduct and (b) actual or apparent ability to carry out the threat. See MPC § 211.1(1)(c); *I de S et Ux v. W de S*, Y.B. Lib. Ass. fol. 99, pl. 60 (1348) (early common law recognition).

**Dual Sovereignty Doctrine:**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment does not bar successive prosecutions by separate sovereigns (federal and state governments) for the same conduct. *Gamble v. United States*, 587 U.S. 678 (2019) (reaffirming dual sovereignty exception); *Abbate v. United States*, 359 U.S. 187 (1959); *Bartkus v. Illinois*, 359 U.S. 121 (1959). The doctrine treats each sovereign's law enforcement power as independent.
