## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

On May 4, a private company called Lydia House, which operated a Christian-themed lifetime care residence for older adults, admitted Naomi, a 91-year-old retired hymn teacher, for a six-week trial stay. On June 12, Naomi and Lydia House signed a written lifetime care agreement to begin on June 20. The agreement required Naomi to prepay the full contract price of $36,000, which she paid on June 12. Naomi died unexpectedly of a heart attack on June 22.

In a restitutionary action, may Martha, the personal representative of Naomi's estate and Naomi's surviving sister, recover all or part of the $36,000 paid to Lydia House?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

Can an estate recover a prepaid lifetime-care fee when the resident dies shortly after a valid contract begins?

### 3. Final Answer Choices

A. Yes, under the doctrine of frustration of purpose.

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. The contract sold lifetime care, so the length of Naomi's life was the central risk the parties priced into the agreement. The later death makes the bargain look one-sided in hindsight, but that is not enough to recover restitution when the payment was made under a valid, binding contract. Gold Key: restitution does not reopen a valid contract merely because the agreed risk later produces a harsh-looking result. Silver Key: when two choices say "No," choose the reason tied to the contract's risk allocation, not a reason that adds unnecessary proof.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because it reaches for a real excuse doctrine when the expected benefit collapses quickly. The breaker is that frustration of purpose does not apply when the event was the very risk allocated by the lifetime-care bargain.

C is attractive because the short stay makes the prepaid amount feel unfair. The breaker is the Gold Key: unjust enrichment does not displace a valid contract just because hindsight makes the exchange uneven.

D is attractive because the company turning away another applicant sounds like reliance and makes the no-refund result feel earned. The breaker is the Silver Key: the company does not need to prove detrimental reliance when the contract itself is valid and allocates the lifespan risk.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The official key is confirmed. Restitution is generally unavailable to recover a benefit conferred under an enforceable contract unless a recognized ground makes the duty discharged, voidable, or otherwise unenforceable. Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 208 measures unconscionability at contract formation; section 265 excuses performance for supervening frustration only when the event frustrates a basic assumption and the party has not assumed that risk; section 377 allows restitution after discharge for impracticability, frustration, nonoccurrence of condition, or disclaimer. Here, the lifetime-care agreement expressly turns on the resident's lifespan, so early death was an allocated risk rather than a frustration that discharges the bargain. The tested issue, rule, and credited outcome remain unchanged.
