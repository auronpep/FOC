In the pastureland straddling the line between State A and State B, Barnabas drove off Stephen's flock of sheep from a field in State A and herded the animals across the border into State B. The act violated the livestock-theft statutes of both states, which are identical. ## 2. Distilled Core Question

A single course of conduct violates the identical laws of two different states; the defendant is convicted in the first state, and the second state then prosecutes him for the same conduct. Does the Double Jeopardy Clause bar the second state's prosecution?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (C)

The motion asks the court to dismiss on double jeopardy grounds. Granting it would treat State A's conviction as a bar to State B's case. **Gold Key:** under the **separate-sovereignties (dual sovereignty) doctrine**, when a single course of conduct violates the laws of two different sovereigns, each sovereign may prosecute under its own laws, and the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar the second prosecution — no matter how the first sovereign's case came out. State A and State B are two distinct sovereigns; State B is vindicating its own laws, not relitigating State A's. **Silver Key:** before applying the ordinary "no reprosecution after conviction" rule, ask *who* is prosecuting now — a different sovereign means the bar does not run. The court should **not** grant the motion.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A — "DJ bars a second prosecution after a conviction for the same offense" (DOMINANT TRAP).** This sells the single most familiar double jeopardy rule, and it is a true rule — *between the same sovereign*, a conviction does bar reprosecution. The breaker: **Gold Key** — two *different* states are prosecuting here, and the separate-sovereignties doctrine means State A's conviction does not bar State B from enforcing its own identical statute.

**B — "the government is collaterally estopped."** This sells a lawyerly-sounding preclusion label and the instinct that one government's judgment should bind the next prosecution. The breaker: collateral estoppel is an issue-preclusion concept that does not operate to bar a separate sovereign's prosecution, and the controlling question is double jeopardy — which the separate-sovereignties doctrine resolves in State B's favor.

**C —** credited (see right-answer explanation).

**D — "DJ bars a second prosecution only following an acquittal."** This sells the right bottom line ("No, don't dismiss") and a tidy-sounding limiting rule. The breaker: **Silver Key** — the rule is false, because double jeopardy also bars reprosecution after a *conviction* by the same sovereign; the reason State B may proceed is not that the first case ended in a conviction, but that a *different sovereign* is now prosecuting.

## 9. Black-letter verification + legal reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

The credited answer is correct under the separate-sovereignties doctrine. The Double Jeopardy Clause (U.S. Const. amend. V, applied to the states through the Fourteenth) bars a second prosecution for the same offense by the *same sovereign* after acquittal or conviction. But two states are **separate sovereigns**, so each may prosecute the same conduct under its own laws without offending double jeopardy. *Authority:* *Heath v. Alabama*, 474 U.S. 82 (1985) (defendant convicted of a cross-border kidnapping-murder in one state may be separately prosecuted by the other state); *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922) (an act offending two sovereigns "may be punished by each"). **Doctrinal-currency check:** the dual-sovereignty doctrine was reaffirmed in *Gamble v. United States*, 587 U.S. 678 (2019); the framing and outcome remain current. The tested rule (separate-sovereignties exception to double jeopardy), the legal issue, and the credited outcome (the second sovereign may prosecute; motion denied) are **unchanged** from the source item. Collateral estoppel in the criminal-double-jeopardy setting (*Ashe v. Swenson*, 397 U.S. 436 (1970)) is issue preclusion within one sovereign's case and does not bar a separate sovereign, so it does not change the result for choice B. Authority confidence: high (named Supreme Court cases and the constitutional provision).
