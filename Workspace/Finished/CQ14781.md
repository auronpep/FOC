## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Daniel's statement to his wife is admissible as a statement against interest under FRE 804(b)(3). First, the statement was against Daniel's penal (and potentially pecuniary) interest when made — admitting that he was paid to commit arson would expose him to criminal prosecution for arson and conspiracy, as well as civil liability. Second, Daniel had direct personal knowledge: he was describing his own act of setting the fire and the fact that Peter paid him to do it. Third, Daniel made the statement in the presence of an attending chaplain, a third-party witness, which shows he was aware the statement could be used against him and had no motive to fabricate — a person does not falsely confess to a crime in front of a witness. Fourth, Daniel died from his burns, rendering him unavailable as a witness under FRE 804(a)(4). All four requirements of FRE 804(b)(3) are satisfied.

**Gold Key:** A statement against interest under FRE 804(b)(3) requires that the statement was "so far contrary to the declarant's pecuniary or proprietary interest, or so far tended to subject the declarant to civil or criminal liability, that a reasonable person in the declarant's position would not have made the statement unless he believed it to be true." The declarant must also be unavailable. The presence of a third-party witness negates any claim that the statement was a confidential marital communication.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect.** This choice states a true rule of law — the marital communications privilege does survive the communicating spouse's death — but the privilege never attached here. The marital communications privilege only protects *confidential* communications between spouses. Daniel made his statement to Ruth in the presence of an attending chaplain, a known third party. A communication made in the presence of a stranger is not confidential and therefore not privileged. The privilege is real; it simply does not apply to these facts.

**B is incorrect.** This choice correctly identifies that a co-conspirator's statement is only admissible if made during and in furtherance of the conspiracy, and that the conspiracy would have ended by the time Daniel made the statement. However, this choice answers "No" — it says the testimony is *not* admissible. That conclusion is wrong. The testimony *is* admissible, just not under the co-conspirator exemption. It is admissible as a statement against interest under FRE 804(b)(3). This choice is a trap for students who correctly identify that the co-conspirator exemption doesn't apply but then incorrectly conclude the testimony is inadmissible altogether.

**D is incorrect.** This is the dominant trap. It reaches the correct outcome — the testimony is admissible — but for the wrong reason. The co-conspirator exemption (FRE 801(d)(2)(E)) requires that the statement be made (1) during the course of and (2) in furtherance of the conspiracy. Daniel's statement to his wife was a confession about a past act, not a statement made to advance the conspiracy's objectives. By the time Daniel was in the hospital, the arson was complete and the conspiracy had ended. A post-conspiracy confession to a spouse is not "in furtherance of" the conspiracy. The statement is admissible, but under the statement-against-interest exception, not the co-conspirator exemption.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule — FRE 804(b)(3): Statement Against Interest.**

A statement is not excluded by the hearsay rule if the declarant is unavailable as a witness and the statement was, at the time of its making, so far contrary to the declarant's pecuniary or proprietary interest, or so far tended to subject the declarant to civil or criminal liability, or to render invalid a claim by the declarant against another, that a reasonable person in the declarant's position would not have made the statement unless he believed it to be true. Fed. R. Evid. 804(b)(3).

**Requirements:**
1. The declarant must be unavailable (FRE 804(a)) — here, Daniel died, satisfying FRE 804(a)(4).
2. The statement must have been against the declarant's interest when made — admitting to arson for hire subjects Daniel to criminal prosecution and civil liability.
3. The declarant must have had personal knowledge — Daniel described his own criminal act.
4. The declarant must have been aware the statement was against his interest and had no motive to misrepresent — the presence of a chaplain as witness confirms this.

**Authority:** Fed. R. Evid. 804(b)(3); *Williamson v. United States*, 512 U.S. 594 (1994) (interpreting the scope of "statement" under 804(b)(3)); 5 J. Wigmore, Evidence § 1476 (J. Chadbourn rev. ed. 1974); C. McCormick, McCormick's Handbook of the Law of Evidence § 277 (E. Cleary 2d ed. 1972).

**Marital Privilege (distractor A):** The confidential marital communications privilege protects only communications made in confidence between spouses during a valid marriage. A communication made in the known presence of a third party is not confidential and therefore not privileged. *Trammel v. United States*, 445 U.S. 40 (1980); LII Wex, "spousal communications privilege," https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/spousal_communications_privilege.

**Co-Conspirator Exemption (distractors B and D):** Under FRE 801(d)(2)(E), a statement by a coconspirator is not hearsay if made during the course of and in furtherance of the conspiracy. A conspiracy ends when its central criminal purposes are accomplished. *Grunewald v. United States*, 353 U.S. 391, 401–02 (1957). Post-conspiracy confessions are not "in furtherance of" the conspiracy. *Krulewitch v. United States*, 336 U.S. 440, 443–44 (1949).

**Verification:** The tested rule (statement against interest), the legal issue (admissibility of an unavailable declarant's statement), and the credited outcome (admissible under FRE 804(b)(3)) are all unchanged from the original. The transform preserves the original rigor and difficulty.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Peter owns a 40-acre wheat farm. After a suspicious fire destroys his harvested grain, he files an insurance claim. The insurer denies it, alleging Peter paid his neighbor Daniel to set the fire. Daniel, burned in the fire, tells his wife Ruth in a chaplain's presence: "Peter paid me to torch the barn." Daniel dies two weeks later. The insurer calls Ruth to testify. Peter objects.

**Call:** Is Ruth's testimony admissible?

**Choices:**
- A: No, marital privilege survives death.
- B: No, statement made after conspiracy ended.
- C: Yes, statement against interest.
- D: Yes, statement by coconspirator.

#### Distilled Core Question

#### Review Truth

A statement by an unavailable declarant is admissible as a statement against interest under FRE 804(b)(3) if it was against the declarant's pecuniary, proprietary, or penal interest when made, the declarant had personal knowledge, was aware the statement was against his interest with no motive to misrepresent, and is now unavailable.

The presence of a third-party witness negates any claim that a spousal statement was a confidential marital communication.
