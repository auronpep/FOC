Lydia is indicted for forgery for falsifying a business license, and counsel is appointed. Detectives later suspect Lydia of an unrelated home-invasion burglary that has not been charged. **Should the burglary statement be suppressed on Sixth Amendment grounds?**

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Choice C)

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel is **offense-specific**. It attaches only when formal adversarial judicial proceedings have begun for a particular offense — here, the forgery indictment. Because no proceedings had been initiated for the separate, uncharged burglary, the Sixth Amendment right to counsel had not attached to that offense. Police were therefore free to question Lydia about the burglary after she validly waived her Miranda rights. The statement is admissible; the motion to suppress should be denied.

**Gold Key:** The Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches *offense by offense* — it is not a blanket right that covers every crime once you have a lawyer for one charge. Formal proceedings (indictment, information, arraignment, preliminary hearing) must begin for *each specific offense* before the Sixth Amendment right attaches to that offense.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### Choice A (was B) — Dominant Trap

**What makes it attractive:** This choice sells a robust, intuitive vision of the right to counsel — once you have a lawyer, the police can't question you about anything. It feels like the right should work that way, and many students overgeneralize constitutional protections.

**The breaker:** The Sixth Amendment right is offense-specific, not universal. *McNeil v. Wisconsin*, 501 U.S. 171 (1991); *Texas v. Cobb*, 532 U.S. 162 (2001). Having counsel on the forgery charge does not trigger Sixth Amendment protection for the separate, uncharged burglary. **Gold Key:** The right attaches offense by offense — formal proceedings must begin for each specific crime.

### Choice B (was D) — Conflation Trap

**What makes it attractive:** This choice conflates two different constitutional rights. Students who know that Miranda waives the Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination may incorrectly assume it also waives the Sixth Amendment right to counsel. The reasoning sounds plausible because Miranda warnings *were* given and waived.

**The breaker:** Miranda warnings govern the Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination during custodial interrogation. They do not "always waive" the Sixth Amendment right to counsel, which is a separate right that attaches through formal judicial proceedings, not through police warnings. The better analysis is that the Sixth Amendment right had not attached to the burglary in the first place — the waiver question is secondary.

### Choice D (was C) — Severity Misfit

**What makes it attractive:** Both crimes being felonies feels legally relevant. Students may think that the seriousness of the offenses triggers heightened protections, or that the Sixth Amendment applies more broadly when both crimes are serious.

**The breaker:** Offense specificity does not turn on the severity classification of the crimes. The Sixth Amendment analysis asks whether formal proceedings began for the specific offense questioned — not whether both crimes are felonies, misdemeanors, or any other classification. The felony label is irrelevant to the attachment question.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule:** The Sixth Amendment right to counsel is offense-specific. It attaches only to the specific offense for which formal adversarial judicial proceedings have been initiated. *McNeil v. Wisconsin*, 501 U.S. 171 (1991); *Texas v. Cobb*, 532 U.S. 162 (2001).

**Application:** Lydia was indicted for forgery — formal proceedings began for that offense, and the Sixth Amendment right attached. No proceedings had been initiated for the burglary. The Sixth Amendment therefore did not bar police from questioning her about the burglary after a valid Miranda waiver.

**Authority:**
- *McNeil v. Wisconsin*, 501 U.S. 171 (1991): Invocation of Sixth Amendment right to counsel for a charged offense does not constitute invocation of Fifth Amendment right to counsel for unrelated, uncharged offenses.
- *Texas v. Cobb*, 532 U.S. 162 (2001): Sixth Amendment right to counsel is offense-specific and does not extend to offenses that are merely "factually related" to the charged offense.
- U.S. Const. amend. VI: "In all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right...to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence."
