## 1. Final question — revised stem + call

Before he took office, a presidential candidate hired Lydia, a craftswoman who hand-sews
rally banners and canopy tents, to produce custom banners for his campaign. They signed a

inaugurated and was serving as President, Lydia sued him personally to recover the unpaid

completely immune from every private civil lawsuit for as long as he holds office.

What is the best answer?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A sitting President is sued personally for a private debt he incurred before taking office.
Does presidential immunity bar the suit?

## 3. Final answer choices (A–D, post-shuffle)

- **D.** The suit is barred only if Congress approves the contract.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register) — choice C

The contract was for campaign banners the candidate ordered **before** he held any office —
ordinary private commercial conduct, not the exercise of any presidential power. Presidential
immunity from civil damages is **absolute only for official acts**; it never reaches private
or pre-office conduct. So official-act immunity does not bar Lydia's suit, and C is correct.
**Gold Key:** absolute presidential civil immunity covers *official acts only* — a sitting
President can still be sued for unofficial private conduct. **Silver Key:** when one choice
says immunity bars *all* civil suits and another limits it to *official* acts, the "all"
word is the overclaim tell — cut it and lock the limited choice unless the stem actually
shows official conduct.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

- **A — timing bar.** It feels intuitive that suing only *after* inauguration is what creates
  the immunity problem, so the date of filing looks decisive. But the deciding axis is whether
  the conduct was official or private, not when the plaintiff filed; **Silver Key:** the
  official-vs-unofficial axis is what resolves this clash, and timing of suit never touches it.
- **B — absolute immunity from all civil litigation.** It trades on a real doctrine
  (Presidents do get absolute civil immunity) and stretches it to cover *everything*, which
  reads as strong and lawyerly. The word "all" is the overclaim: **Gold Key —** that immunity
  is limited to **official acts**, so it cannot bar a suit over a private pre-office banner
  contract.
- **D — barred unless Congress approves the contract.** It borrows the separation-of-powers
  mood of the question and invents a congressional sign-off step that sounds like a check on
  the executive. No such requirement exists; congressional approval has nothing to do with
  whether the conduct was official or private for immunity purposes.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification + legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested rule (unchanged):** A President's immunity from civil liability is *absolute* but
extends only to acts within the "outer perimeter" of **official** responsibility; it does not
shield unofficial, private, or pre-office conduct.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer):**
- *Nixon v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 731 (1982) — absolute presidential immunity from civil
  damages liability is confined to acts within the outer perimeter of official duties.
- *Clinton v. Jones*, 520 U.S. 681 (1997) — a sitting President has **no** immunity from
  civil litigation for acts committed before taking office and unrelated to the office;
  such a suit may proceed during the presidency.

Applied: the banner contract is private, pre-office, commercial conduct, squarely within
*Clinton v. Jones*. Credited outcome (suit not barred) is unchanged from the original.

**Doctrinal-currency check:** *Trump v. United States* (2024) addressed **criminal** immunity
for official acts and does **not** disturb the **civil** rule of *Clinton v. Jones* /
*Nixon v. Fitzgerald* governing this item. Framing and outcome current. No attorney flag needed.
