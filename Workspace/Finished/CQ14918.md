## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**A is correct.** Peter’s claim is about the physical condition of an existing residence: the well pump, septic line, and skylight. Lydia promised a general warranty deed, but the deed’s covenants protect title. They do not promise that the cottage’s systems and roof components will keep working. **Gold Key:** an existing-home seller is not generally treated as warranting that the dwelling is fit or defect-free unless the facts give an express condition warranty, a new-home/builder-vendor warranty, a statute, or known concealment. **Silver Key:** read the array as a title/condition split; physical breakdown facts stay in the condition-warranty lane.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B.** The attraction is the remembered housing-fitness rule: “residential property” sounds like habitability, so the answer feels protective of the buyer. The breaker is the Gold Key: this is an existing-home sale with no express condition promise, no statute, no known defect, and no builder-vendor/new-home trigger. The implied-fitness idea is being pushed beyond its lane.

**C.** The attraction is the post-closing posture: once a deed is delivered and accepted, “merger” sounds like the right real-property word. The breaker is the Silver Key title/condition split. Merger is a title-lane doctrine. Peter is not complaining that Lydia failed to convey title; he is complaining that physical parts of the cottage failed.

**D.** The attraction is the phrase “general warranty deed,” which sounds like a broad warranty of the property. The breaker is the Gold Key title distinction. Covenants of title deal with ownership, right to convey, encumbrances, quiet enjoyment, warranty of title, and further assurances. They do not warrant the cottage’s well pump, septic line, skylight, or loft.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The source row for Q14918 gives Real Property / Contracts, a warranty-deed land sale, later physical defects, a genuinely surprised seller, no statute, and official key C: no seller liability because the seller made no condition warranty and had no duty to disclose unknown facts. The official wrong-answer notes identify the exact preserved traps: implied fitness does not apply, warranty-deed covenants relate to title rather than physical condition, and merger is relevant only to title issues. 

Black-letter verification: an implied warranty of fitness/habitability is not a general rule for all existing-home conveyances; the common tested exception is new construction by a builder-vendor or statute. Cornell Wex states that a real-property sale contract has no implied warranties of fitness or habitability unless it is new construction by a builder, and the Virginia new-home statute illustrates how new-dwelling warranties are specifically bounded. ([Legal Information Institute][1]) A warranty deed supplies covenants of title; Cornell Wex lists the traditional covenants as seisin, right to convey, against encumbrances, quiet enjoyment, warranty, and further assurances, all title-focused. ([Legal Information Institute][2]) Nondisclosure liability also requires a known fact plus a disclosure duty; the Fifth Circuit’s quotation of Restatement (Second) of Torts § 551 frames nondisclosure around facts the party knows and is under a duty to disclose. ([Justia Law][3])

Credited outcome verified: Peter loses because the facts give no condition warranty, no statute, and no knowledge-based nondisclosure claim. Nothing in the transform changes the tested issue or outcome.

### Choice B

**(2) Student label:** Overextended implied-fitness warranty.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** Merger doctrine is relevant to title obligations after delivery and acceptance of a deed. It does not supply the reason Peter loses on a physical-condition claim.

### Choice D

**(1) Student-accessible C3 signal:** D says covenants of title. The stem says well pump, septic line, skylight, and loft.

**(2) Student label:** Title covenants used for condition defects.

**(3) What a true/responsive version would look like:** A responsive title-covenant answer would point to a defect in ownership, right to convey, encumbrance, quiet enjoyment, warranty of title, or further assurances.

**(4) Lawyer confirmation:** Warranty-deed covenants protect title, not the physical condition of the cottage.

## final_student_script

Physical defects after closing. Not title. Warranty deed and merger are title-lane bait. No condition warranty, no statute, no known defect, no builder/new-home trigger. Buyer loses. Pick A.

## remediation_card

**Card:** RP-REMEDY-CONDITION-TITLE-01
**Title:** Separate condition defects from title defects.
**Signal:** Home systems fail, but answers mention deed, title covenants, or merger.
**Move:** Ask whether the problem is ownership/title or the building’s physical condition.
**Tiny rule:** Warranty deed protects title; existing-home condition defects need a separate warranty/statute/known-concealment path.
**Trap:** Treating “warranty deed” as a warranty of the whole building.

## Gold Key(s)

**GK-REAL_PROPERTY-MARANATHA-COTTAGE-01**
Existing-home sale: no general implied warranty that the dwelling is fit or defect-free. Look for an express condition warranty, a builder-vendor/new-home warranty, a statute, or known concealment before charging the seller for physical defects.

**GK-REAL_PROPERTY-MARANATHA-COTTAGE-02**
Warranty-deed covenants are title covenants. Do not use seisin, right to convey, encumbrances, quiet enjoyment, warranty, or further assurances to repair a pump, roof, septic line, furnace, or other physical condition defect.

## Silver Key(s)

**SK-REAL_PROPERTY-MARANATHA-COTTAGE-01**
Read the answer array as a title/condition split. Physical breakdown facts call for the condition-warranty lane; deed covenants and merger are title-lane bait unless the stem gives a title defect.
