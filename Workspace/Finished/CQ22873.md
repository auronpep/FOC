### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** In a class action brought under traditional diversity jurisdiction (28 U.S.C. § 1332(a)), only the citizenship of the named class representatives — not the absent class members — is considered when determining whether complete diversity of citizenship exists. *See Snyder v. Harris*, 394 U.S. 332 (1969); *Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble*, 255 U.S. 356 (1921).

Here, the two named class representatives are citizens of State B, and Peter (the defendant) is a citizen of State A. That is complete diversity. The fact that most pantry locations (and thus most absent class members) are in State A does not destroy jurisdiction. The court should deny the motion.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (Grant — State A pantries suffered the most damage):** This choice tempts students who conflate the amount of damage with the jurisdictional analysis. The distribution of damages has no bearing on whether the parties are diverse. The relevant inquiry is citizenship, not the severity of harm on either side. **Silver Key:** When a jurisdiction question asks about citizenship, any choice focusing on damage amount or who suffered most is answering the wrong question — cut it.

**B (Grant — majority of pantry locations are in State A) — DOMINANT TRAP:** This is the strongest distractor because it mirrors how students naturally think about class composition: "most of the class is from the same state as the defendant, so there's no diversity." But the rule is narrow — only the named representatives' citizenship counts. The majority's citizenship is irrelevant. **Gold Key:** For diversity in class actions, the test looks exclusively at the named representatives, not the class as a whole.

**D (Deny — supplies destined for both sides of the state line):** This choice sounds plausible because it references the interstate nature of the facts. But the geographic scope of the occurrence or the distribution of goods does not establish subject-matter jurisdiction. Diversity jurisdiction turns on party citizenship, not on whether the underlying events crossed state lines.

### 9. Black-Letter Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** Under 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a), federal courts have diversity jurisdiction when the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000 and the dispute is between citizens of different states. In the class action context, the Supreme Court has long held that only the citizenship of the named class representatives — not the absent class members — is considered for purposes of determining complete diversity. *Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble*, 255 U.S. 356 (1921); *Snyder v. Harris*, 394 U.S. 332 (1969).

**Application:** The named class representatives (two pantry directors from State B) are citizens of a different state than the defendant Peter (citizen of State A). Complete diversity exists. The citizenship of the absent class members — i.e., the other pantry locations in State A — is irrelevant.

**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a); *Snyder v. Harris*, 394 U.S. 332 (1969); *Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble*, 255 U.S. 356 (1921).

**Note:** This analysis applies the traditional diversity rule, not the Class Action Fairness Act (CAFA), 28 U.S.C. § 1332(d), which employs a "minimal diversity" standard for qualifying large class actions. The MBE tests the traditional rule unless CAFA is explicitly invoked.

#### Wrong-Answer Recovery Paths

**If the student picked A (damage amount):**
- Recovery: "The call asks about jurisdiction. Jurisdiction turns on citizenship, not on who suffered more damage. When you see a choice about damage amount on a jurisdiction question, it's answering the wrong question. Cut it."

**If the student picked B (majority citizenship) — DOMINANT TRAP:**
- Recovery: 'This is the most common miss. You thought the majority of the class determines citizenship. But the rule is narrower: only the named class representatives count. Go back to the stem — who are the named representatives? They're from State B. The defendant is from State A. That's complete diversity.'

**If the student picked D (geographic scope):**
- Recovery: "The supplies going to both states sounds like it creates a federal issue. But diversity jurisdiction doesn't care about where the goods went — it cares about who the parties are. Citizenship of the parties, not geography."

#### Review Truth

In a diversity class action under 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a), only the citizenship of the named class representatives — not the absent class members — determines whether complete diversity exists.
