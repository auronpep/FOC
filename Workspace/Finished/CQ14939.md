### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** Under the Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act (12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3), a due-on-sale clause in a mortgage to a federally chartered bank is valid and enforceable. The Act preempts any state law that would restrict such clauses. An installment land contract is a transfer of an interest in the property — it does not matter that the form is an installment contract rather than a deed. Because Daniel is transferring the stable to Hannah without the bank's prior written consent, the bank may exercise its contractual option to declare the entire loan immediately due and payable. If the full amount is not then paid, the bank may foreclose. The fact that mortgage payments are current and Hannah's credit is excellent is legally irrelevant to the bank's right to enforce the clause.

**C is correct.** Even though the transfer is structured as an installment land contract, it is still a transfer triggering the due-on-sale clause. The bank may accelerate and foreclose if the debt is not paid in full.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect.** This choice sells the attractive idea that the bank's refusal to consent is an illegal restraint on alienation. Restraints on alienation are indeed a real doctrine, and students may be tempted to invoke it here. But the Garn-St. Germain Act (12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3) expressly validates due-on-sale clauses in mortgages to federally chartered banks and preempts contrary state law. The clause is not an illegal restraint — it is a contractual condition the borrower agreed to. **Gold Key:** The Garn-St. Germain Act preempts state-law challenges to due-on-sale clauses for federally chartered banks.

**B is incorrect.** This choice exploits the common misconception that transferring property subject to a mortgage automatically shifts personal liability to the transferee. It does not. Hannah would be personally liable on the mortgage debt only if she expressly assumed it. The due-on-sale clause gives the bank the *option* to accelerate — it does not by operation of law make the buyer liable. The bank's remedy is acceleration and foreclosure, not automatic liability.

**D is incorrect.** This choice plays on the idea that keeping payments current should protect the borrower. But the due-on-sale clause is triggered by the *transfer itself*, not by any default in payments. The form of the transfer (installment land contract) and the timeliness of payments are both irrelevant. Once Daniel transfers any interest in the stable without the bank's consent, the bank may accelerate regardless of payment history.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing authority:** Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982, 12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3.

**Rule:** A due-on-sale clause is a contract provision authorizing a lender to declare the entire loan due and payable if all or any part of the mortgaged property, or an interest therein, is sold or transferred without the lender's prior written consent. Under 12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3, such clauses are enforceable for loans made by federally chartered (nationally chartered) lenders, and the Act preempts any state law that would prohibit or restrict their enforcement.

**Application:** The mortgage between Daniel and the federally chartered bank contains a valid due-on-sale clause. Daniel's proposed installment land contract with Hannah is a transfer of an interest in the property — the installment form does not change its character as a conveyance. Because the bank has not consented, the bank may exercise its option to accelerate the debt. If the accelerated amount is not paid, the bank may foreclose. The current status of payments and Hannah's creditworthiness are irrelevant to the bank's contractual right.

**Confirmed:** The tested rule (due-on-sale enforceability), legal issue (whether the transfer can proceed despite bank refusal), and credited outcome (bank may accelerate) are all unchanged from the original question.

**Authority:** 12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3 (Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982); see also *Fidelity Federal Savings & Loan Ass'n v. de la Cuesta*, 458 U.S. 141 (1982) (federal preemption of state restrictions on due-on-sale clauses).
