## 7. Right-Answer Explanation (B)

Lydia struck Esther with a hive smoker intending to kill her. The blow alone would not have been fatal, so but-for causation is debatable — Esther was already dying from the alkaloid. But the law does not require but-for causation as the sole test. Under the **substantial-factor test**, an act that significantly accelerates a victim's death is a cause-in-fact of that death. Lydia's blow moved Esther's death from hours to 15 minutes — a massive acceleration. That makes Lydia a substantial factor in causing Esther's death. Combined with her clear intent to kill (malice aforethought), all elements of murder are satisfied.

**Gold Key: GK-CRIMINAL-SUBSTANTIAL-FACTOR-01** — When a defendant's act is not a but-for cause of death because the victim would have died from another cause, the defendant is still liable if the act was a "substantial factor" in causing the death — such as significantly accelerating it.

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

### Choice A: No, because Esther would have died from the alkaloid regardless.

This is the dominant trap. It sells the cognitive move that "would have died anyway" eliminates all causation. The choice is a half-truth: right that but-for fails, wrong that this ends the analysis. **Gold Key: GK-CRIMINAL-SUBSTANTIAL-FACTOR-01** — the substantial-factor test defeats this trap by supplying the alternative causation pathway the choice omits.

### Choice C: No, because the blow alone would not have been fatal.

This choice invents a legal requirement that does not exist — the idea that an act must be independently lethal to support murder liability. The stem explicitly states the blow alone would not have killed Esther, and this choice takes that fact and builds a fabricated threshold around it. Criminal law has no independent-lethality requirement. The substantial-factor test asks whether the act significantly contributed to the death, not whether it could have caused the death alone. The blow moved death from hours to 15 minutes. That contribution is enough. **Gold Key: GK-CRIMINAL-SUBSTANTIAL-FACTOR-01** — the substantial-factor test requires only significant contribution, not independent sufficiency.

### Choice D: Yes, because Lydia was trespassing when she struck Esther.

This reaches the right result (yes, Lydia can be convicted) but for an entirely irrelevant reason. The call asks about murder — and murder turns on intent, act, and causation. Trespass is a property offense. Lydia's forced entry into the shed might support a separate burglary charge, but it has nothing to do with whether she caused Esther's death. A student can cut this without any doctrinal knowledge: trespass and murder causation are different legal questions.

## 9. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Tested rule:** Cause-in-fact for murder — specifically, the substantial-factor test as an alternative to but-for causation when the defendant's act accelerates death from an independent lethal cause.

**Governing authority:** At common law, a defendant who hastens or accelerates the death of another is a cause of that death, even when the victim would have died from a separate cause. The substantial-factor test supplements but-for causation in concurrent-cause scenarios.

*People v. Lewis*, 124 Cal. 551, 57 P. 470 (1899): "The test is — or at least one test — whether, when the death occurred, the wound inflicted by the defendant did contribute to the event. If it did, although other independent causes also contributed, the causal relation between the unlawful acts of the defendant and the death has been made out." The court articulated the broader principle that "one whose wrongful act hastens or accelerates the death of another, or contributes to its cause, is guilty of homicide, though other causes co-operate."

The acceleration principle is universally accepted: an act that speeds up death satisfies factual causation. This holds even when the victim was already fatally wounded or poisoned.
