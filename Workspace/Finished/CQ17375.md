## 7. Right-Answer Explanation

**B is correct.**

The call asks for the most appropriate charge under **strict common-law kidnapping doctrine**. That doctrine has a critical two-part structure: (1) unlawful confinement of another person AND (2) **asportation** — physical movement of the victim from one location to another.

Mary was unquestionably confined. But she was never moved. She entered the below-deck cabin to stow gear and remained there throughout. Peter never relocated her to any other part of the vessel or ashore. The asportation element fails entirely.

**Gold Key GK-CRIM-ASPORTATION-01:** Under strict common-law kidnapping, the victim must be physically moved (asportation) — secret or forcible confinement without movement, however long or inaccessible, is not kidnapping at common law.

Because asportation is absent, kidnapping is off the table. But Peter did intentionally confine Mary within fixed boundaries (the locked below-deck cabin) against her will, with no means of escape. That is the definition of false imprisonment: intentional confinement of another within boundaries against their will. B correctly identifies the charge and gives the accurate reason.

**Silver Key SK-CRIM-ASPORTATION-01:** When the stem locks in "strict common-law kidnapping" and says the victim was never moved, name the axis (asportation vs. confinement-only) — any answer calling it kidnapping automatically fails on that axis, and the residual is false imprisonment.

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A — Kidnapping, because Peter used force (drugging) to establish control over Mary**

The drugging confirms the confinement was non-consensual — and that matters for false imprisonment. But the method of establishing control is not a substitute for asportation under common-law doctrine. A student might reason: force → control → kidnapping. That's the trap. Force is one element of wrongfulness, but it replaces nothing in the asportation requirement. Even the most deliberate pharmacological confinement is not common-law kidnapping if the victim was never moved.

**C — No crime was committed, because Mary boarded the vessel and entered the cabin voluntarily**

Initial consent to enter a space does not carry forward as consent to everything that happens inside it. Mary agreed to go below deck to stow gear. She did not consent to being drugged, locked in, and rendered unconscious. The stem says Peter locked the hatch "so that no one can enter and Mary cannot leave" — this is visible stem evidence of deliberate confinement against her will. Voluntary initial entry is legally irrelevant once intentional confinement begins.

**D — Kidnapping, because Peter secretly confined Mary against her will in an enclosed space that was inaccessible to others** ← DOMINANT TRAP

Secret, forcible confinement in an inaccessible location sounds like everything kidnapping requires. Some modern statutes define it that way. But the stem specifies **strict common-law kidnapping doctrine**, which adds one mandatory element not satisfied here: **asportation** — physical movement of the victim.

**Gold Key GK-CRIM-ASPORTATION-01:** Under strict common-law kidnapping, secret or forcible confinement without movement — no matter how long or inaccessible — is not kidnapping at common law because asportation is a required independent element. Mary was never moved. D omits that element entirely and overclaims what the specified doctrine requires.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Tested rule:** Common-law kidnapping requires (1) unlawful confinement (2) of another person (3) accompanied by asportation — movement of the victim from one location to another. Confinement without asportation does not satisfy common-law kidnapping; it may constitute false imprisonment.

**False imprisonment:** Intentional confinement of another within fixed boundaries against their will, without lawful justification.

**Lawyer-confirmation layer:**
- Asportation requirement: LaFave, Substantive Criminal Law § 18.1 (3d ed.) ("The common law crime required both the act of confining the victim and the act of asportation — movement of the victim.").
- MPC § 212.1 partially relaxed this by specifying substantial distance or confinement to commit another felony; some statutes replace asportation with secret confinement. The question specifies *strict common-law doctrine*, placing this squarely in the asportation-required category.
- False imprisonment: Restatement (Second) of Torts § 35 (civil analog); MPC § 212.3 (criminal restraint).
- The credited outcome (false imprisonment) is unchanged from the original question. Rule, issue, and credited outcome are identical.

**Doctrinal currency:** No hotlist items apply. This tests settled common-law kidnapping doctrine; no 2019–2025 Supreme Court decision altered the common-law asportation requirement.
