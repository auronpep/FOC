## 1. Final Question — Stem + Call

Lydia owned a purple-dye workshop — a stone building on a city lot in the cloth-market quarter. On March 3, 2016, Lydia borrowed $28,000 from the bank, signing a note secured by a mortgage on the workshop. On November 20, 2016, Lydia executed and delivered to Timothy a deed that contained a covenant against encumbrances. On November 21, 2016, the bank duly recorded its mortgage on the workshop. On November 25, 2016, Timothy recorded his deed. A statute in the jurisdiction states: "In the event of a dispute between parties claiming conflicting interests in realty, the interest which shall first have been recorded shall have priority." **Call:** If Timothy institutes an appropriate action against Lydia for breach of the covenant against encumbrances, what is Timothy's likely relief?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (B)

A covenant against encumbrances is a present covenant: it promises that no liens burden the title, and it is breached — if ever — at the single instant the deed changes hands. On November 20, Lydia's mortgage was alive (she did not pay it off until the 27th), so the deed she delivered carried an encumbrance and the covenant broke on delivery. A breach therefore exists. But the measure of relief is loss, and Timothy lost nothing: Lydia satisfied the mortgage out of his own purchase money, the bank's interest was discharged, and Timothy never had to spend a cent to clear title. With a proven breach but zero actual damages, the law gives the smallest non-zero remedy — nominal damages.

**Gold Key:** a present covenant against encumbrances is breached at delivery if a lien then exists, and a *later* satisfaction does not erase that breach — it only caps the recovery; where the grantee paid nothing to remove the lien, recovery is nominal. **Silver Key:** read A→D as a remedy spectrum (nothing / nominal / contract damages / rescission) and split "was there a breach?" from "how much harm?" — once you see a breach with no loss, the answer is the smallest non-zero rung.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A — Nothing, since there has been no breach.** The pull is moral common sense: Lydia cleaned up her own mess with the buyer's money, the lien is gone, no one is out of pocket — so it feels like nothing happened. The breaker is timing: a present covenant is tested at the *moment of delivery*, and a mortgage indisputably encumbered the title that day; later payoff cannot reach back and un-break a covenant that already broke. **Gold Key:** the covenant against encumbrances breaks at delivery if a lien then exists — no-loss is not no-breach.

**C — Rescission of the deed.** Its premise is actually correct ("the covenant was breached at the time the deed was delivered") and that true clause makes the whole choice sound authoritative; a student who confirms the timing nods along to the remedy. The breaker is remedy law: breach of a title covenant sounds in damages, not in unwinding the conveyance — rescission undoes the sale, it does not compensate for a lien, and nothing here gives Timothy the extraordinary right to hand the workshop back.

**D — Damages for breach of contract, since the covenant was breached at the time the contract was made.** The contract did promise title free of encumbrances, so suing "on the contract" feels natural and the September date looks like the first promise broken. The breaker is the instrument: the covenant Timothy sued on lives in the *deed*, which was not delivered until November — the deed covenant did not exist in September and could not be breached then; the contract's title promise merged into the deed at closing.

## 9. Black-Letter Verification + Legal Reasoning for B (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule (confirmed unchanged):** The covenant against encumbrances is one of the six covenants of title and is a *present* covenant — breached, if at all, at the instant of delivery of the deed. An outstanding mortgage is a classic encumbrance. A present covenant breached at delivery is not retroactively cured by a later discharge of the lien; however, the grantee's damages are measured by actual loss, so a grantee who expended nothing to remove the encumbrance and suffered no diminution recovers only nominal damages.

**Affirmative reasoning for B:** (1) An encumbrance (the bank's mortgage) existed at delivery on November 20 → the present covenant was breached then. (2) Lydia later satisfied the mortgage with Timothy's purchase funds; Timothy paid nothing extra and lost no value → no actual (compensable) damages. (3) Breach + no actual loss = nominal damages. Outcome is identical to the source item's credited answer.

**Authority:** Present-vs-future covenant timing — *Brown v. Lober*, 75 Ill. 2d 547, 389 N.E.2d 1188 (1979) (present covenants, e.g., seisin, breach at delivery; future covenants breach on later disturbance); 11 Stoebuck & Whitman, *The Law of Property* (covenants of title; present covenants breach at conveyance; measure of damages limited to loss actually sustained). Nominal-damages-where-no-loss principle is the general rule (see, e.g., Minn. Stat. § 507.21 framing liability as "all damages sustained in removing" an encumbrance — a grantee who removed nothing has no substantial damages). `attorney_review: true` for the precise nominal-measure citation: the rule is hornbook-settled and statutorily illustrated, but it rests on treatise/general authority rather than a single controlling MBE case.
