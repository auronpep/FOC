### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** Under FRE 803(3), a statement of the declarant's then-existing mental state — including intent, motive, or plan — is admissible as an exception to the hearsay rule. The declarant's availability is completely immaterial. Under the *Hillmon* doctrine, such a statement of intent is admissible to prove that the declarant subsequently acted in accordance with that stated intent. Here, Peter said, "I'm riding over to Lydia's farm right now," which is a clear statement of his then-existing intent to go to Lydia's farm. This is admissible to prove Peter intended to go (and by inference, that he did go). The correct answer is **C**.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original C):** This choice is attractive because students may think that hearsay exceptions require the declarant to be unavailable. That is true for *some* exceptions under FRE 804, but FRE 803 exceptions — including the then-existing state of mind exception — do not require unavailability. The availability of the declarant is immaterial. **Gold Key:** FRE 803 exceptions do not require the declarant to be available; only FRE 804 exceptions require a showing of unavailability.

**B (original D):** This choice is attractive because the statement *is* hearsay — it is an out-of-court statement offered for the truth of the matter asserted. However, the choice ignores that the statement falls within a recognized hearsay exception (FRE 803(3)). Calling it "inadmissible hearsay" is wrong because the exception makes it admissible. Students who stop at "it's hearsay" without checking for exceptions will pick this trap.

**D (original A) — DOMINANT TRAP:** This is the most attractive wrong answer. Peter's language — "I can't take this anymore!" — sounds excited, and students may be tempted to classify this as an excited utterance under FRE 803(2). However, an excited utterance requires (1) a startling event or condition, (2) a statement that *relates to* that startling event, and (3) the declarant must be under the stress of excitement caused by the event. Here, there is no startling event. Peter is venting frustration, not reacting to a sudden, shocking occurrence. The statement does not relate to any startling event. **Gold Key:** Excited utterance (FRE 803(2)) requires a startling event; mere emotional venting without a triggering event does not qualify.

### 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**FRE 803(3) — Then-Existing Mental, Emotional, or Physical Condition:**
A statement of the declarant's then-existing state of mind (such as motive, intent, or plan) or emotional, sensory, or physical condition (such as mental feeling, pain, or bodily health), but not including a statement of memory or belief to prove the fact remembered or believed. *See* FRE 803(3), Federal Rules of Evidence; *Mutual Life Ins. Co. v. Hillmon*, 145 U.S. 285 (1892) (statements of intent admissible to prove declarant's subsequent conduct). Availability of the declarant is immaterial under FRE 803.

**FRE 803(2) — Excited Utterance:**
A statement relating to a startling event or condition made while the declarant was under the stress of excitement caused by the event or condition. *See* FRE 803(2); *Cornell Law School, Legal Information Institute*, https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_803.

**Analysis:** The credited answer (B in the original, now C) is correct. Peter's statement "I'm riding over to Lydia's farm right now" is a statement of his then-existing intent to perform a future act. Under FRE 803(3) and the *Hillmon* doctrine, this is admissible to prove Peter acted in accordance with that intent. The statement is not an excited utterance because there is no startling event. The declarant's availability is irrelevant under FRE 803. The legal issue, tested rule, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original.
