## 7. Right-Answer Explanation

**D is correct.** The creamery is not vicariously liable.

**Gold Key — GK-TORTS-FROLIC-01:** An employer is not vicariously liable for an employee's tort committed during a substantial personal **frolic** — a major deviation from assigned work for purely personal reasons. Remaining "on the clock" or using the employer's vehicle does *not* keep the conduct within scope once the employee has substantially abandoned the assigned work; only a slight **detour** stays within scope.

Stephen left the delivery zone by 18 miles, switched off the route tracker, skipped three scheduled stops, and was still driving *toward* the auction — a personal errand serving no creamery purpose — when the collision occurred. The deviation was substantial and personal, and the tort happened *before* he resumed the route. Scope of employment does not attach.

**Silver Key — SK-TORTS-FROLIC-01:** Two choices say "No" (C and D) and two say "Yes" (A and B); the exam is testing the **reason**, not the result. Reject the "No" that rests on an absolute ("never") — employers *can* be liable within scope, so "never" self-destructs — leaving the "No" that names the actual frolic. Likewise, a true-sounding fact ("on a paid shift," "company van") is not the test.

## 9. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule — respondeat superior / scope of employment (lawyer-confirmation layer):**
Under respondeat superior, an employer is vicariously liable for an employee's torts committed within the scope of employment. Conduct falls outside the scope when the employee substantially departs from assigned work to pursue a purely personal objective — a "frolic." A minor deviation (a "detour") remains within scope; a substantial, personal deviation does not, and the employer is not liable for torts committed during the frolic before the employee returns to the employer's business.

**Authority:** Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07(2) (an employee's act is not within the scope of employment "when it occurs within an independent course of conduct not intended by the employee to serve any purpose of the employer"); Restatement (Second) of Agency § 228 (scope-of-employment test: conduct of the kind employed to perform, substantially within authorized time and space limits, actuated at least in part by a purpose to serve the employer); the classic frolic-and-detour case, *Joel v. Morison*, 172 Eng. Rep. 1338 (1834) ("on a frolic of his own"). The doctrine is common-law and applied at the state level; courts divide on whether to apply the Second or Third Restatement framing, but both yield the same result on a substantial personal frolic.

**Key agreement:** The credited answer (original B, now D) is confirmed correct. The tested rule (frolic-and-detour limit on respondeat superior), the legal issue (whether the employer is vicariously liable for the employee's negligent driving), and the credited outcome (no — substantial frolic) are all unchanged from the original item. No NEEDS_HUMAN flag warranted. Respondeat superior is not on the doctrinal-currency hotlist; no superseded-framing issue.

## Gold Key(s)

**GK-TORTS-FROLIC-01 — Frolic-and-Detour Limit on Respondeat Superior**

An employer is not vicariously liable for an employee's tort committed during a substantial personal **frolic** — a major deviation from assigned work for purely personal reasons. Staying on the clock or using the employer's vehicle does NOT preserve liability once the employee has substantially abandoned the assigned work; only a slight **detour** remains within scope.

- **Type:** distinction
- **Unlocks:** Frolic-and-detour limit; defeats the on-the-clock/company-van distractor (B) and confirms D.
- **Trigger:** Employee on an assigned route substantially leaves it for a personal errand and commits a tort before returning.
- **Authority (lawyer-confirmation only):** Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07(2); Restatement (Second) of Agency § 228; *Joel v. Morison*, 172 Eng. Rep. 1338 (1834).
