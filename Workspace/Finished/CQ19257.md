### 1. Final question

Ruth, a Christian woman who coordinates a community food-preservation ministry at her church, was severely burned when a home pressure canner she had been using to prepare jars of food for the ministry's distribution program exploded in her kitchen. ### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The judge should **order a new trial on damages**. Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 59(d), a district court may on its own order a new trial "for any reason that would justify granting one on a party's motion." Inadequate damages — particularly when the inadequacy reflects jury prejudice — is a classic ground for a new trial. **Gold Key:** the 7th Amendment bars additur (and conditional additur) in federal court, so the judge cannot increase the verdict, and judgment as a matter of law is unavailable on these facts because Rule 50 requires a party's pre-submission motion. **Silver Key:** when the call asks what the judge should do in response to a jury verdict, run two filters — (a) is the action constitutionally available (7A bar on additur in any form), and (b) is it procedurally available sua sponte (Rule 59 yes; Rule 50 no). Only the new trial survives both filters.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Offer the defendant a choice between a higher damage award or a new trial on damages.**

This is the dominant trap. The conditional framing — "give the defendant a choice" — sounds like a procedural compromise that respects both the jury verdict and the defendant's autonomy. The student thinks: if the defendant can opt for a new trial, the 7A jury-trial problem is solved. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: under *Dimick v. Schiedt*, 293 U.S. 474 (1935), the Supreme Court held that even conditional additur violates the 7th Amendment. The Court rejected the workaround precisely because reweighing the evidence or augmenting the damages award is a jury function that the trial judge cannot perform, and offering the defendant a "choice" does not cure the constitutional defect. The judge's only available response to inadequate damages is a new trial, not a re-doctrine of the damages calculation in any form.

**B. Enter judgment as a matter of law.**

This is the procedural-defect distractor. The student registers "inadequate damages" and reaches for the most powerful judicial tool — judgment as a matter of law — and assumes the judge can deploy it whenever the verdict is wrong. The breaker is FRCP 50: a motion for JMOL must be (i) made by a **party** (not the court sua sponte) and (ii) made **before the case is submitted to the jury**. By the time the jury has returned a verdict, the JMOL window has closed; the proper post-verdict device is a Rule 59 motion for new trial, not a JMOL.

**C. Amend the judgment to increase the damages by additur.**

This is the bare additur. Many students know that additur is unconstitutional in federal court and will rule this out quickly. The breaker is *Dimick v. Schiedt*: the 7th Amendment right to jury trial bars a federal court from increasing the damages award, and the only available judicial response to an inadequate verdict is a new trial. Additur is the named bar-exam enemy; the conditional version in A is the more subtle trap.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 19257, a Civil Procedure item in which a federal civil jury has returned a verdict for the plaintiff but the trial judge believes the damages are inadequate because of jury prejudice. The official key is C: order a new trial.

The governing rule is Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 59(d), which provides that "[o]n its own, the court may order a new trial for any reason that would justify granting one on a party's motion." The Advisory Committee's note and the standard treatises confirm that inadequate damages is a paradigmatic ground for a new trial. See 11 Charles Alan Wright, Arthur R. Miller & Mary Kay Kane, Federal Practice and Procedure § 2806 (3d ed.).

The constitutional limit on the alternative remedy — additur — comes from the Supreme Court's decision in *Dimick v. Schiedt*, 293 U.S. 474 (1935). The Court there held that "[u]nder the Seventh Amendment, a federal court, finding a verdict inadequate, is without power to add to it by refusing to grant the plaintiff a new trial." The same reasoning forecloses **conditional** additur (offering the defendant a choice between a higher verdict and a new trial) because the constitutional defect — the trial judge, rather than the jury, performing the damages-reweighing function — is the same. *See also* James, **Additur and Remittitur**, 9 Vand. L. Rev. 337 (1956) (the conditional-additur workaround was specifically rejected in *Dimick*).

The procedural defect in choice B (JMOL) is governed by Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 50(a), which provides that "[a] motion for judgment as a matter of law may be made at any time before the case is submitted to the jury" and that the motion must be made by a party. Rule 50(d) allows the court to enter JMOL against a party that has been fully heard on a motion under Rule 50(a), but the predicate is a party's pre-submission motion — not the court's own initiative. After a verdict, the only sua sponte judicial tool for an inadequate-damages problem is the new trial under Rule 59(d).

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: the judge orders a new trial on damages.

### 2. The 17-section student case study

## program_frame

This is a Civil Procedure question that runs the post-verdict judicial-action selection. The fact pattern is a federal civil jury verdict for the plaintiff, with the trial judge believing the damages are inadequate because of jury prejudice. The call is what judicial action the judge should take. The four choices are four different judicial actions, and the test is whether each action is (a) constitutionally available under the 7th Amendment and (b) procedurally available sua sponte on a jury verdict.

A student without the *Dimick* anchor sits in `anchor_gap` territory on the conditional-additur dominant trap, but the answer is settled by the constitutional rule and the Rule 59(d) sua sponte mechanism.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. Offer the defendant a choice between a higher damage award or a new trial on damages.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the dominant trap. The student sees "offer the defendant a choice" as a procedural compromise that respects the jury verdict and the defendant's autonomy. The Gold Key tells them *Dimick v. Schiedt* rejected this exact workaround: the constitutional defect (the trial judge, rather than the jury, performing the damages-reweighing function) is the same as for bare additur.
2. **Student label:** Conditional additur dressed as compromise.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Offer the defendant a choice between a higher damage award or a new trial MIGHT be constitutional if the trial judge were not the one performing the damages-reweighing function. Under *Dimick*, the trial judge cannot perform that function in any form — bare or conditional — and offering the defendant a 'choice' does not cure the defect."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** *Dimick v. Schiedt*, 293 U.S. 474 (1935), held that even conditional additur violates the 7th Amendment. The Court's reasoning was that the reweighing of evidence or augmentation of the damages award is a jury function that the trial judge cannot perform; the defendant's "choice" does not cure the constitutional defect.

### B. Enter judgment as a matter of law.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Procedural-defect cut. The stem supplies the post-verdict posture, which closes the Rule 50 sua sponte-JMOL window. The student reached for the most powerful judicial tool (JMOL) and assumed the judge can deploy it whenever the verdict is wrong.
2. **Student label:** JMOL — wrong timing and wrong party.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Enter JMOL MIGHT be correct if the motion were made by a party before the case were submitted to the jury. The stem describes a post-verdict posture; Rule 50's sua sponte window is closed."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRCP 50(a) requires (i) a party's motion and (ii) the motion to be made before the case is submitted to the jury. Rule 50(d) allows the court to enter JMOL against a party that has been fully heard on a Rule 50(a) motion, but the predicate is a party's pre-submission motion — not the court's own initiative. By the time the jury has returned a verdict, the JMOL window is closed.

### C. Amend the judgment to increase the damages by additur.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Constitutional-bar cut. The student who knows *Dimick* rules this out on first read. A student who does not is in `anchor_gap` territory; the Gold Key resolves the cut.
2. **Student label:** Bare additur (named bar-exam enemy).
3. **True/responsive version:** "Amend the judgment to increase the damages by additur MIGHT be constitutional if the 7A jury trial right did not bar the trial judge from augmenting the damages award. Under *Dimick*, the 7A bars this action in any form."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** *Dimick v. Schiedt*, 293 U.S. 474 (1935), held that "[u]nder the Seventh Amendment, a federal court, finding a verdict inadequate, is without power to add to it by refusing to grant the plaintiff a new trial." The named bar-exam enemy.

### D. Order a new trial on damages.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the residual answer. After the additur pair is cut by the 7A bar and the JMOL distractor is cut by Rule 50 timing/party, the residual is the only constitutionally and procedurally available action.
2. **Student label:** Residual answer (new trial sua sponte).
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRCP 59(d) expressly authorizes the court to order a new trial sua sponte for any reason that would justify granting one on a party's motion. Inadequate damages — particularly when the inadequacy reflects jury prejudice — is a paradigmatic ground. *See* 11 Charles Alan Wright, Arthur R. Miller & Mary Kay Kane, Federal Practice and Procedure § 2806 (3d ed.).
