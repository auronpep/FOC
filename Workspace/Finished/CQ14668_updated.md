### FINAL QUESTION

At common law, first-degree murder is defined as murder committed (1) by means of poison, or (2) with premeditation and deliberation. All other murder is second-degree murder. Manslaughter is defined as at common law.

Hannah is a church custodian. She has worked at the church for years, but the new youth pastor, Timothy, recently criticized her cleaning in front of the congregation. Humiliated and angry, Hannah decided to teach Timothy a lesson. She went to a pharmacy and bought a bottle of non-prescription sleep-aid tablets. That Sunday, while preparing the fellowship-hall coffee before services, Hannah crushed two tablets and stirred them into Timothy's mug. She intended merely to make Timothy drowsy during the service so he would embarrass himself in front of the church. Timothy drank the coffee. Because Timothy was already taking a prescribed medication that interacted with the sleep aids, he lost consciousness, struck his head on a table, and died from the head injury.

If the jurisdiction charges Hannah with first-degree murder, should she be convicted?

**A.** A: Yes, only because she acted with premeditation and deliberation.
**B.** B: only because she used poison.
**C.** C: No.
**D.** D: Yes, both because she used poison and because she acted with premeditation and deliberation.

### DISTILLED CORE QUESTION

### FULL RIGHT-ANSWER EXPLANATION

**C is correct.** To convict of first-degree murder, the prosecution must first establish that the defendant committed murder — which requires malice aforethought. Malice aforethought exists when a defendant: (1) intends to kill, (2) intends to inflict serious bodily harm, (3) acts with reckless indifference to an unjustifiably high risk to human life (depraved heart), or (4) causes death during the commission of an inherently dangerous felony (felony murder).

Here, Hannah intended only to make Timothy drowsy — she wanted to embarrass him, not kill him or seriously injure him. Giving someone non-prescription sleep aids in a normal dose does not constitute reckless indifference to human life. And she was not committing any felony. Because Hannah lacked malice aforethought, she is not guilty of murder at all. Without an underlying murder, the first-degree enhancements (poison and premeditation) are irrelevant — they elevate second-degree murder to first-degree murder, but they cannot create murder from a killing that lacks malice.

*Key distinction (Gold Key):* The "premeditation" that elevates murder to first-degree must be premeditation **to kill**. Hannah premeditated putting the tablets in the coffee — but she premeditated an embarrassing prank, not a killing. Premeditation of the act is not the same as premeditation to murder.

### FULL WRONG-ANSWER EXPLANATIONS

**B (Yes, only because she used poison) [DOMINANT TRAP].** This choice is attractive because Hannah did secretly put a substance into Timothy's drink, and focusing students read "poison" broadly to mean any harmful chemical. But over-the-counter sleep-aid tablets are not "poison" in the legal sense used by first-degree murder statutes. More fundamentally, even if the substance were poison, first-degree murder still requires an underlying murder — malice aforethought — which Hannah lacked. The choice traps students who seize on the surface fact of a secreted substance without examining whether a murder was committed at all. *Gold Key: Poison in this context means a substance that is harmful by nature; non-prescription sleep aids taken in normal doses are not poison.*

**A (Yes, only because she acted with premeditation and deliberation).** This choice is attractive because Hannah clearly planned the act in advance — she bought the tablets, crushed them, and timed her actions. Planning equals premeditation, right? No. Premeditation for first-degree murder requires planning the **killing**, not merely planning the act. Hannah planned to cause drowsiness and embarrassment, not death. Students who conflate "premeditation of any act" with "premeditation to commit murder" fall for this trap. *Gold Key: Premeditation-and-deliberation for first-degree murder means reflection on the decision to kill — not merely thinking ahead about any action.*

**D (Yes, both because she used poison and because she acted with premeditation and deliberation).** This is the double-bait answer. It combines the two independent errors from A and B into a compound claim. A student who is unsure about whether either ground is sufficient may gravitate toward the看起来 more comprehensive answer. The breaker is the same for both: no poison, and no premeditation to kill — and more fundamentally, no malice aforethought means no murder to elevate. *Gold Key: The same Gold Keys that kill A and B independently kill D together — D is the compounded version of both traps.*

### BLACK-LETTER VERIFICATION

**Authority:** Common-law murder requires malice aforethought, which encompasses four mental states: (1) intent to kill, (2) intent to cause serious bodily harm, (3) depraved-heart recklessness, and (4) felony murder. *See* Cal. Penal Code § 187 et seq.; CA Bar Exam Content Map, Criminal Law — Homicide § II.A.1 (listing "intent to kill," "intent to inflict serious bodily harm," and "reckless disregard for risk to human life" as the three non-felony-murder bases for malice aforethought); NYU Criminal Law Attack Outline at 3 (defining malice aforethought and noting premeditation is a *degree* issue, not a murder element).

For first-degree murder by poison, the poison must be a substance noxious or dangerous in its nature — not an ordinary non-prescription medication in normal dosage. *See People v. Bergenthal* (Cal. 2023) (poison requires deliberate administration of a noxious substance). 

For premeditation and deliberation, the defendant must have reflected on the decision to kill — not merely planned an act. *See* CA Bar Exam Content Map, Criminal Law — Homicide § II.A.1.b (first-degree murder = "willful, deliberate, and premeditated" killing); CA10 Pattern Jury Instructions (premeditation requires forming intent to kill and being "fully conscious of that intent").

## 8. Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

### Choice A: "Yes, only because she acted with premeditation and deliberation."

**1. Student-accessible C3 signal:** Hannah clearly planned ahead — she bought the tablets, chose the moment, crushed them into the coffee. But the statute requires premeditation *to kill*. The facts say she wanted to embarrass Timothy. Planning a prank is not planning a murder. If you can see the difference between "premeditated putting tablets in coffee" and "premeditated killing," this choice falls.

**2. Student label:** "Sounds Planned"

**3. What a true-and-responsive version would look like:** "Yes, because Hannah reflected on and planned Timothy's death over a period of time before carrying out the killing." That would require facts showing Hannah thought about and decided to kill him.

**4. Lawyer confirmation:** Premeditation and deliberation for first-degree murder requires the defendant to have reflected on the decision to kill — forming the intent to kill and being fully conscious of that intent before acting. Hannah formed the intent to cause drowsiness, not death. See CA Bar Exam Content Map, Crim Law § II.A.1.b.

**C3 verdict: CUT — NOT_TRUE (DISTORTION/half_truth).**

---

### Choice B: "Yes, only because she used poison." [DOMINANT TRAP — 15%]

**1. Student-accessible C3 signal:** Hannah put something in Timothy's coffee secretly. But "poison" doesn't mean "any substance." Sleep-aid tablets from a pharmacy that anyone can buy — that's not poison. Poison is a substance that's harmful by its nature, like arsenic or cyanide. You know the difference between medicine and poison.

**2. Student label:** "Secret Substance = Poison?"

**3. What a true-and-responsive version would look like:** "Yes, because Hannah put arsenic in Timothy's coffee, and arsenic is poison."

**4. Lawyer confirmation:** "Poison" in first-degree murder statutes refers to substances noxious or dangerous in their nature. Non-prescription sleep-aid tablets in ordinary doses are not poison. The statute targets toxins, lethal chemicals — not OTC medications. Cf. People v. Bergenthal (Cal. 2023).

**C3 verdict: CUT — NOT_TRUE (FALSITY/flat_misstatement).**

---

### Choice C: "No."

**1. Student-accessible C3 signal:** Hannah didn't want to kill Timothy. She didn't want to seriously hurt him. She wasn't acting with reckless disregard for his life (she gave him normal OTC tablets). And she wasn't committing a felony. Without any of those mental states, there's no malice aforethought. Without malice, there's no murder. Without murder, there's no first-degree murder.

**2. Student label:** "No Murder at All"

**3. What a true-and-responsive version looks like:** This IS the true-and-responsive choice.

**4. Lawyer confirmation:** Malice aforethought requires: (1) intent to kill, or (2) intent to cause serious bodily harm, or (3) depraved-heart recklessness, or (4) felony murder. Hannah intended to cause drowsiness and embarrassment — none of these four forms of malice are satisfied. No malice → no murder → no first-degree murder. The statutory aggravators (poison/premeditation) elevate second-degree murder to first-degree; they do not create murder. See CA Bar Exam Content Map, Criminal Law § II.A.1.a.

**C3 verdict: CORRECT — neither filter broken.**

---

### Choice D: "Yes, both because she used poison and because she acted with premeditation and deliberation."

**1. Student-accessible C3 signal:** This combines both A and B. If neither poison nor premeditation-to-kill is satisfied on its own, then putting them together doesn't help. It's like saying "wrong for two reasons" when each reason is independently wrong.

**2. Student label:** "Double Bait"

**3. What a true-and-responsive version would look like:** "Yes, because Hannah used actual poison (e.g., a lethal toxin) AND premeditated Timothy's death."

**4. Lawyer confirmation:** D is the compound of A and B. Both independent prongs fail: the substance was not poison, and Hannah did not premeditate a killing. The compound claim fails because neither leg is satisfied. Moreover, even if one prong were satisfied, the underlying murder (malice aforethought) is absent — so no degree-of-murder analysis is even reached.

**C3 verdict: CUT — NOT_TRUE (FALSITY/flat_misstatement).**

## 12. Remediation Card

**Card ID:** REM-CRIM-HOMICIDE-01
**Title:** First-Degree Murder Requires Murder First
**Signal:** Any question asking about first-degree (or second-degree) murder
**Student move:** Before analyzing degree (poison, premeditation, felony murder), confirm that murder exists. Check for malice aforethought: intent to kill? intent to seriously harm? depraved heart? felony murder?
**Tiny rule:** "No malice = no murder = forget the degree, the defendant isn't guilty of murder."
**Trap:** Jumping straight to the degree analysis — reading that someone used poison or planned ahead and choosing "yes" without checking malice.
**Diagnostic question:** "If the defendant intended to play a prank, not to kill, can they still be guilty of first-degree murder?" Answer: No, because without malice aforethought, there's no murder to elevate.
