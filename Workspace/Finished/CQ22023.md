# CQ22023 — Jesse's Carmel Ridge Vineyard (Rule Against Perpetuities)

## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question (stem + call)

Jesse died, leaving a will that contained the following clause:

> **CLAUSE SEVEN —** I devise my vineyard on the eastern slope of Carmel Ridge to my wife Hannah for life, remainder to those of my children who achieve the age of 25 years. If any child of mine shall predecease me, or if any child of mine shall survive me but shall die before achieving the age of 25 years, that child's share shall be distributed equally among any of that child's children who shall marry; but if such child of mine shall die without issue, then his or her share shall be distributed among my children who achieve the age of 25 years.

At the time of Jesse's death he had no grandchildren and was survived by three children — Caleb, Lydia, and his youngest daughter Ruth. Three years after Jesse's death, Ruth gave birth to a daughter, Esther. One week after Esther's birth, Ruth died at the age of 22.

**Call:** If Esther marries at the age of 17, will she be entitled to share in the Carmel Ridge vineyard?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A will gives land to a life tenant, then to the testator's children at an age; if a child dies before that age, that child's share goes to *that child's children who marry*. A grandchild is born after the testator dies and later marries young. Tested at the testator's death, is the grandchild's interest valid under the common-law Rule Against Perpetuities?

### 3. Final answer choices (A–D, post-shuffle)

- **A.** Yes, because Esther's interest vested within 21 years after Ruth's death.
- **B.** No, because at the time of Jesse's death, it was possible that Esther's interest would not vest until more than 21 years after the deaths of Jesse's three children.
- **C.** No, because at the time of Ruth's death, it was possible that another grandchild would subsequently be born who would marry more than 21 years after the deaths of Jesse's three children.
- **D.** Yes, because Esther's interest vested within 21 years after Jesse's death.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**B is correct.** Common-law RAP asks one question at the moment the interest is created — here, Jesse's death: *must* this interest vest or fail within 21 years of a life in being then? The gift-over runs to a deceased child's children "who marry." Esther was not alive at Jesse's death (she is an afterborn), and marriage is an event with no time limit. So at Jesse's death it was entirely possible that the taker would marry — and thus vest — more than 21 years after Jesse's three children (the only relevant measuring lives) had all died. An interest that *might* vest too late is void from the start.

**Gold Key:** *Common-law RAP is judged at creation and ignores what actually happened* — an interest is void if, when created, it merely **might** vest later than 21 years after a life in being. **Gold Key:** *A gift conditioned on an open-ended event (marriage) can vest at any time*, so it violates RAP unless a validating life guarantees timely vesting; an afterborn grandchild cannot be a measuring life. **Silver Key:** read the 2×2 — kill both "Yes" choices with the at-creation rule, then pick the "No" whose reason is the true ground.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

- **D (dominant trap).** It feels airtight: Esther *did* marry young, comfortably inside any 21-year window measured from Jesse's death — the correct starting date — so the interest "obviously vested in time." That is the single most common RAP error. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: common-law RAP tests *possibilities at creation*, not outcomes. Because at Jesse's death the gift *could* have vested too late, it was void then — and what later happened cannot resurrect it.
- **A.** It offers the same comforting move — the interest "vested in time" — and dresses it up by measuring from Ruth's death, the life tenant's parent who actually produced the taker. But the date is doubly off: the controlling test is certainty *at creation*, not actual vesting, and the measuring life that would have to validate the gift is one in being at Jesse's death, not Ruth's later death.
- **C.** It reaches the right destination (No) and even speaks fluent RAP — "an afterborn grandchild could marry more than 21 years after the children die." The pull is that it sounds like the perpetuities answer. The breaker is the **Silver Key**: when two choices share the correct outcome, the exam is testing the *reason*. C's reason is the wrong ground — the will channels a deceased child's share only to *that child's own* issue, and Ruth (dead at 22) can have no afterborn children, so C's hypothetical is foreclosed. Esther's interest already fails under B's reasoning, independent of any other grandchild.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule (unchanged from the original):** the common-law Rule Against Perpetuities — *"No interest is good unless it must vest, if at all, not later than twenty-one years after some life in being at the creation of the interest"* (John Chipman Gray's canonical formulation). For a testamentary gift the perpetuities period begins at the **testator's death**.

**Application:** The executory/contingent gift to a deceased child's "children who shall marry" is contingent on (a) the child predeceasing or dying under the stated age, and (b) the grandchild *marrying*. Grandchildren born after the testator's death are afterborns and cannot serve as measuring lives. Marriage is not bounded by any life in being plus 21 years, so at creation there was no validating life guaranteeing the interest would vest or fail within the period — the classic violation. The interest is void at common law.

**Authority floor (real):** *Jee v. Audley*, 1 Cox 324 (1787) — RAP is tested on the **possibilities existing at creation**, however remote (the "fertile octogenarian"); an interest that *might* vest too late is void regardless of actual events. *The Duke of Norfolk's Case* (1682) (origin of the Rule). Gray, *The Rule Against Perpetuities* (canonical rule statement — treatise support only).

**Credited outcome:** UNCHANGED. The interest violates RAP → "No." (Original key C ≡ post-shuffle B.) C3 residual agrees with the official key → **PASS**.

**Doctrinal-currency note (framing, not outcome):** RAP is not on the currency hotlist, but it carries a known reform overlay — under USRAP / wait-and-see jurisdictions (and the Uniform Statutory Rule Against Perpetuities' 90-year period), an interest that in fact vests in time is *saved*, which would flip this answer. The MBE default, and what this item tests, is **common-law RAP**, under which the credited answer stands.
