**BARMATRIX Q#: 15259_peter_sparkling_cider_stopper**

At a Christian law school’s spring banquet, **Peter**, a caterer, was removing the restraining wire from a bottle of sparkling grape juice produced and bottled by **Lydia Cellars, Inc.** The plastic stopper suddenly shot out of the bottle and struck Peter in the eye.

Peter had opened other bottles of sparkling grape juice before, and occasionally stoppers had shot out with great force, but Peter had not previously been injured. Peter brought an action against Lydia Cellars, Inc., alleging that the bottle that caused his injury was defective and unreasonably dangerous because its label did not warn that the stopper might suddenly shoot out during opening.

The state has merged contributory negligence and unreasonable assumption of risk into a pure comparative fault system that is applied in strict products liability actions. A jury made the following findings of fact: that the bottle was defective and unreasonably dangerous because it lacked a warning, that a legally sufficient warning would not have prevented Peter’s injury, and that a reasonable caterer would have realized that a stopper could eject from the bottle and hit his eye.

**Will Peter recover a judgment in his favor?**

## 6. Full right-answer explanation

**A is correct.** This is a warning-defect products-liability question. The jury already found a defect: the bottle lacked a legally sufficient warning. But defect alone is not enough. The injury still must be caused by the missing warning.

**Gold Key:** In a warning-defect products case, no warning-causation means no recovery. If the factfinder says an adequate warning would not have prevented the injury, the plaintiff loses before comparative fault matters.

**Silver Key:** Read the answer array as threshold first, damages second. A uses the threshold fact: “warning would not have prevented injury.” C and D jump to damage reduction or no reduction. Those are late unless liability is first established.

The same causation requirement appears in modern warning-defect formulations: a plaintiff must prove that the lack of sufficient warning was a substantial factor in causing harm, and California’s CACI warning instruction lists causation as an essential element. ([Justia][1]) A California products-liability practice source states the same point more directly: strict-liability failure-to-warn requires both inadequate warnings and that an adequate warning would have prevented the injury. 

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B is wrong.**
B uses the risk-awareness fact. That fact is tempting because Peter had opened similar bottles and a reasonable caterer would know a stopper might eject. But this answer makes risk awareness the total reason for no recovery. The better first-pass move is to ask: “What fact decides a warning-defect claim?” The decisive fact is not merely that Peter knew the risk. It is that a warning would not have prevented the injury. **Gold Key:** no warning-causation means no recovery; risk awareness is not the controlling answer here.

**C is wrong.**
C jumps to comparative fault and damage reduction. That is the classic late-layer trap. Comparative fault matters only after the plaintiff has a recoverable claim. Here the causation finding stops the claim first. **Silver Key:** threshold before damages. Do not reduce a recovery that never gets past warning causation.

**D is wrong.**
D sounds policy-aware: warnings exist because people may be careless. But the answer ignores the jury’s specific causation finding. It also says “with no reduction” even though the jurisdiction uses comparative fault. The immediate defect is that D answers warning policy and damages, not the threshold causation problem. **Gold Key:** a warning-defect claim fails when the warning would not have changed the outcome.
