## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested issue is the Eighth Amendment limit on sentencing juveniles to life without parole for nonhomicide crimes. The credited outcome is preserved: the sentence violates the Eighth Amendment because the defendant was under 18 and was convicted of a nonhomicide offense.

The governing authority is the Eighth Amendment, applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, and *Graham v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 48 (2010). *Graham* held that the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause does not permit life without parole for a juvenile offender who did not commit homicide, while allowing states to deny release later if the offender does not show maturity and rehabilitation. Source: Library of Congress U.S. Reports PDF for *Graham v. Florida*, https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep560/usrep560048/usrep560048.pdf.

Current-law check: *Jones v. Mississippi*, 593 U.S. 98 (2021), did not disturb the *Graham* categorical bar for juvenile nonhomicide offenders. *Jones* addressed homicide offenders under 18 and held that a sentencer need not make a separate finding of permanent incorrigibility before imposing discretionary life without parole in a juvenile homicide case. It expressly describes *Graham* as barring life without parole for offenders under 18 who committed nonhomicide offenses. Source: Supreme Court PDF for *Jones v. Mississippi*, https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/18-1259_8njq.pdf.

Applied here, Timothy was 15 and the conviction was for armed carjacking, a serious violent offense but not homicide. The violent nature of the crime and Timothy's juvenile history may matter in ordinary sentencing or later parole decisions, but they do not permit a no-parole life sentence for a juvenile nonhomicide offender.
