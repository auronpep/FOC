### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**Gold Key: The jury's role in assessing the credibility and weight of admitted evidence is separate from, and survives, the court's gatekeeping ruling on admissibility.**

C is correct. Under FRE 702, the court serves as gatekeeper and decides whether a witness is qualified to testify as an expert. But once the court admits the expert's testimony, the jury becomes the factfinder and must determine how much weight to give that testimony. Credibility is the jury's domain. For an expert, the jury cannot meaningfully assess credibility without knowing the expert's qualifications — especially where opposing experts will offer conflicting opinions and the jury must compare them. The opposing party's concession does not eliminate the relevance of qualification evidence; it merely removes the *formal dispute* over whether the threshold is met. The plaintiff's attorney is therefore entitled to continue questioning the expert about her qualifications.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A (original B)** — This is wrong because it mistakes the court's gatekeeping finding for a complete resolution of all issues relating to the expert's testimony. When a court finds a witness qualified under FRE 702, it has decided a threshold admissibility question. But the jury's separate function — assessing credibility and weight — requires that it too have access to the expert's qualifications. The court's satisfaction does not render qualification evidence irrelevant to the jury's deliberative task. *(This is the **dominant trap:** it sounds correct because the court really did find the expert qualified, but it conflates admissibility with weight.)* **Gold Key:** The jury's credibility assessment is independent of the court's admissibility ruling.

**Choice B (original A)** — This is wrong because a party concession (or even a stipulation) on an expert's qualifications does not make the qualifications irrelevant to the jury's credibility determination. If both parties stipulate that a witness is qualified, the court may accept that stipulation and dispense with formal proof of threshold qualification. But the offering party is not *barred* from presenting qualification evidence to the jury. The concession removes the formal dispute, not the factual basis for the jury's assessment.

**Choice D (original C)** — This is wrong because, while the court does have an independent gatekeeping obligation under FRE 702, the premise that a stipulation *cannot* satisfy that obligation is too strong. A court may accept a stipulation as the basis for finding a witness qualified. The real reason the attorney may continue with qualification evidence is not that the court "must" rehear everything — it's that the jury needs the information for its own credibility assessment.

### 9. Black-Letter Legal Verification

**Authority:** FRE 702 (as amended, effective Dec. 1, 2023) provides that a witness qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education may testify if the proponent demonstrates it is more likely than not that the testimony meets the rule's requirements. The trial court serves as the gatekeeping decision-maker on admissibility.

**Credibility vs. admissibility distinction:** Under the Federal Rules and the general division of labor between judge and jury, the court decides admissibility and competency; the jury decides credibility and weight. This is a foundational principle of trial practice reflected throughout the FRE and case law. If the jury is to weigh expert opinions — particularly competing expert opinions — it must know the qualifications of each expert. Expert qualification evidence remains relevant to credibility even after the court has ruled that the expert is qualified to testify.

**No key disagreement identified.** The original credited answer (D) correctly identifies the jury-credibility rationale. The transform preserves that same legal substance exactly. Verdict: **PASS**.
