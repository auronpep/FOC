# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

Under a written agreement, Lydia, an artisan who made glow-in-the-dark Scripture-memory wristbands, promised to sell her entire output of the wristbands at a specified unit price to Daniel, who operated a private Christian gift booth at youth festivals, for one year. Daniel promised that his booth would not sell any other supplier’s Scripture-memory wristbands. Before Lydia tendered any wristbands, Daniel learned that youth groups had abruptly shifted from wristbands to phone lock-screen cards, sharply reducing customer demand for the wristbands. Daniel renounced the agreement, and Lydia sues for breach of contract.

Which of the following will the court probably decide?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

Seller promised all output of Bible-verse wristbands to an exclusive retailer for one year; the retailer quit before delivery because demand dropped. Who wins?

## 3. Final answer choices

A. Daniel wins, because the agreement was void for indefiniteness of quantity and total price for the year.

B. Lydia wins, because both parties to a sale-of-goods contract impliedly assume the risk of changes in price and customer demand.

C. Daniel wins, because there was no mutuality of obligation since Daniel made no express promise to buy any of Lydia’s Scripture-memory wristbands.

D. Lydia wins, because Daniel’s promise to retail Lydia’s Scripture-memory wristbands exclusively, if he sold such wristbands at all, implied a promise to use his best efforts to sell Lydia’s one-year output of wristbands.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**D is correct.** Lydia wins because the written deal did more than leave Daniel free to buy nothing. The agreement gave Daniel an exclusive retail position for Lydia’s wristbands. **Gold Key:** in an exclusive-dealing sale-of-goods contract, the law supplies a best-efforts duty. Daniel’s exclusivity promise implied that he would use best efforts to promote the sale of Lydia’s one-year output.

**Silver Key:** when two answers give Lydia the win, choose the reason that uses the special fact in the stem. The special fact is Daniel’s promise not to sell any other supplier’s Scripture-memory wristbands. D uses that fact. B gives the right winner but not the right legal hook.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells the familiar anxiety that a contract with no fixed annual quantity and no fixed total yearly price is too indefinite to enforce. The breaker is the output/exclusive-dealing frame: the agreement measures quantity by Lydia’s output and adds Daniel’s exclusivity promise, so the missing fixed total is not the defect.

**B.** This is the dominant trap. It gives Lydia the win and speaks directly to the demand-crash fact, so it feels commercially sensible. **Silver Key:** same-result answers are not interchangeable; the credited answer must use the fact that creates the duty. B talks about market risk. It does not identify the exclusive-dealing best-efforts rule. **Gold Key:** the rule that defeats the trap is the implied best-efforts duty from exclusive dealing.

**C.** This choice sells the surface grammar: Daniel never wrote the words “I promise to buy all of them.” The breaker is the supplied legal obligation. In this output-plus-exclusivity frame, the law implies a best-efforts duty, so the absence of that express sentence does not make the contract fail for lack of mutuality.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule is unchanged: an exclusive sale-of-goods arrangement that measures quantity by the seller’s output and gives the buyer an exclusive retail position is not void merely because the buyer did not expressly promise a fixed quantity; the buyer’s exclusivity promise triggers an implied best-efforts duty. UCC § 2-306(1) validates output and requirements quantity terms when they occur in good faith, and UCC § 2-306(2) provides that a lawful exclusive-dealing agreement imposes, unless otherwise agreed, best-efforts duties on the seller to supply and the buyer to promote sale. ([Legal Information Institute][1])

The credited result is also unchanged. The seller wins because the buyer renounced before any tender solely because demand declined, while the buyer had promised exclusivity. The legal issue remains UCC output/exclusive-dealing enforceability and implied best efforts, not commercial impracticability, price-risk allocation, or a no-mutuality defect. The source row’s official explanation identifies the same rule: the retailer had an implied obligation to use best efforts to promote the seller’s goods.
