### 1. Final question

Esther told Timothy to come with her into Ruth's unlocked backyard greenhouse and retrieve an expensive olivewood nativity carving that Esther said she had lent to Ruth for a Christmas table display. Timothy went with Esther to Ruth's greenhouse, lifted the latch, found the carving on a shelf above the potting bench, and climbed a step stool to reach it. The jurisdiction follows the common law, except that burglary covers structures in addition to dwellings and the nighttime element has been eliminated. Which, if any, of these individuals is guilty of burglary?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Esther entered Ruth's greenhouse intending to take Ruth's carving as her own. The jurisdiction has removed the dwelling and nighttime limits and includes structures, so the greenhouse entry can satisfy the entry-place part of burglary. Gold Key: burglary still needs the felony plan at entry, and the felony here is larceny; the person who honestly believes she owns the item or has permission to take it lacks larceny intent. Silver Key: sort each person by the intent carried through the doorway, not by who touched the property later.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A. Only Ruth is attractive because Ruth is the one who used a pry bar to enter a back room. The breaker is intent: she entered to recover her own carving, so the larceny felony plan that burglary needs is missing.

C. Esther and Timothy is the dominant trap because Timothy opened the door, climbed up, and handed down the carving. The breaker is Timothy's mental state: he acted on Esther's statement that she had lent the carving to Ruth, so he lacked the intent to steal property of another. Gold Key: honest permission belief defeats larceny intent even if the helper is physically inside the structure.

D. All of them is attractive because every person made an entry connected to the carving. The breaker is the same intent screen: burglary is not entry-plus-property movement; it is entry with intent to commit the felony inside.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. Burglary requires an unlawful or unprivileged entry into a protected structure with intent to commit a crime inside; the source jurisdiction keeps common-law burglary but expands it to structures and removes the nighttime element. Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575 (1990), identifies both the common-law burglary formulation and the modern generic formulation requiring unlawful entry of a building or structure with criminal intent. The larceny predicate requires intent to steal property of another. People v. Navarro, 99 Cal. App. 3d Supp. 1, 160 Cal. Rptr. 692 (1979), confirms the claim-of-right principle: a good-faith belief in ownership or permission negates theft intent.

Esther is guilty because she entered Ruth's greenhouse with the plan to commit larceny. Timothy is not guilty because he entered under an honest permission story. Ruth is not guilty because she entered to take back her own carving, not to steal property of another. Authority checked: Taylor v. United States, https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/495/575/; People v. Navarro, https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/people-v-navarro-cr-891962723.
