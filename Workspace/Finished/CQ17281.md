## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**D is correct.** The Necessary and Proper Clause is not an
independent source of regulatory authority. It is a "carry into
execution" clause — it empowers Congress to enact laws that are
**reasonably adapted** to putting some **other enumerated
power** into effect. The federal government is a government of
enumerated powers; outside the powers actually enumerated, the
federal government (including Congress) has none. So when a
federal statute recites N&P as its authority but does not tie the
requirement to any other enumerated power being "carried into
execution," N&P alone cannot validate the statute. On these
facts, the statute identifies no other enumerated power that the
hospitality-course requirement helps implement. The best
objection is therefore the **structural** one: the N&P Clause
does not stand alone.

> **Silver Key:** on a "best objection" question about a federal
> statute, the "best objection" is the one that targets the
> **structural** defect, not the most familiar-sounding
> doctrine. Here, the structural defect is the missing tie to an
> enumerated power, not viewpoint discrimination, not a federal
> police power, not "anything useful is N&P." When a federal
> statute is constitutionally defective, ask: *what is the
> actual constitutional defect?* Match the choice to the defect,
> not the choice to the most familiar-sounding doctrine.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations (one per distractor)

### Choice A (dominant trap) — "The statute is valid because anything useful to the national community is necessary and proper."

The cognitive move this sells is the **utility-equals-authority
fallacy**: the hospitality course is plainly useful (more
neighborly care is good; more social cohesion is good), and
Marshall's *McCulloch* opinion did describe "necessary" as
meaning "appropriate and legitimate." The breaker is that
"appropriate and legitimate" is not a synonym for "useful to
the national community." Marshall's "appropriate and legitimate"
language was *contextual* — it described the *means* Congress
could use to **carry into execution** *another* enumerated
power. The N&P Clause is not a general-utility grant. It does
not authorize Congress to legislate on every subject that
benefits the country. It authorizes only those laws that are
reasonably adapted to executing some other enumerated power
that the Constitution has actually given Congress. On these
facts, no such other enumerated power is identified. C3 cut:
the N&P Clause's bridging function is not a stand-in for
enumeration. Filter broken: **NOT_TRUE**.

> **Silver Key:** the four choices attack four different
> constitutional defects. Three of them (A utility/overbreadth,
> B federal police power, C viewpoint-only) attack the **wrong**
> constitutional defect. Only D attacks the actual defect: that
> the N&P Clause is not a standalone power. When a federal
> statute recites N&P but ties to no other enumerated power,

### Choice B — "The statute is valid because Congress has a general police power over community welfare."

The cognitive move this sells is the **federal-police-power
myth**: it sounds right to say that "community welfare" is
something a government should regulate, and federal regulation
of community welfare sounds like a normal exercise of
government authority. The breaker is that the **federal**
government has no general police power. The federal government
is a government of enumerated powers; whatever "police power"
exists in the U.S. system belongs to the **states** under
their general jurisdiction (and even state police power has
constitutional limits). Congress cannot reach an
otherwise-unenumerated subject by labeling it "community
welfare" or "general welfare." C3 cut: federal
community-welfare legislation requires an enumerated power;
this statute names none. Filter broken: **NOT_TRUE**.

### Choice C — "The statute is invalid only if it discriminates by viewpoint."

The cognitive move this sells is the **single-defect
narrowing**: the First Amendment's viewpoint-discrimination
rule is a real and important constitutional limit, and a
student who has studied constitutional law will recognize
that "viewpoint discrimination" is the most familiar way an
educational requirement runs into a constitutional problem.
The breaker is that **a statute can have multiple independent
constitutional defects, and a single defect is enough to
invalidate it.** Even if the hospitality-course statute
contained no viewpoint discrimination, it would still need an
enumerated power to authorize it — and on these facts it
identifies none. The structural defect is independently
fatal; whether the statute *also* discriminates by viewpoint
is a separate question that the call does not ask. C3 cut:
the question is "best objection," and the structural
missing-enumerated-power objection is the cleanest, most
fundamental defect. Filter broken: **NOT_TRUE**.

## 9. Black-letter verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**The rule.** The federal government is a government of
**enumerated** powers. Article I, Section 8 of the U.S.
Constitution lists Congress's affirmative powers (the
"enumerated powers" or "foregoing powers"). The last clause
of Article I, Section 8 — the **Necessary and Proper
Clause** — provides that Congress has power

> "To make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for
> carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers, and all
> other Powers vested by this Constitution in the Government
> of the United States, or in any Department or Officer
> thereof."

That text is structural, not freestanding. It authorizes
Congress to enact **incidental** laws — laws that are
**reasonably adapted** to executing some **other** enumerated
power. It does not authorize Congress to legislate on every
subject that is "useful," "convenient," or "appropriate" in
the abstract. *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.)
316 (1819), is the foundational case: Chief Justice John
Marshall described the Clause's word "necessary" as meaning
"appropriate and legitimate" — but that language was used
to describe the *means* Congress could use to **carry into
execution** an enumerated power. The Court upheld a
national bank as N&P because the bank was reasonably adapted
to carrying into execution the **taxing, borrowing, and
commerce** powers — three enumerated powers in Article I,
Section 8. The bank itself was not enumerated; what made it
valid was that it was a means of executing enumerated powers.
The N&P Clause has never been read as a stand-alone source of
federal regulatory authority.

The corollary — the federal government has **no general
police power** — flows from the same enumeration structure.
The "police power" (general authority to regulate health,
safety, welfare, morals, and education) is reserved to the
states under the Tenth Amendment and the structural design
of dual sovereignty. *See, e.g., United States v. Lopez*,
514 U.S. 549 (1995) (reaffirming the limited, enumerated
character of federal authority; striking a federal Gun-Free
School Zones Act on the ground that possession of a firearm
in a school zone is not an economic activity substantially
affecting interstate commerce).

A third principle — the **independent-defects rule** —
completes the picture: a statute can be invalid for more
than one reason, and a single constitutional defect is
sufficient to invalidate it. Even a statute that survives
one form of review (e.g., viewpoint-discrimination review)
still must satisfy the threshold requirement that Congress
have affirmative authority to enact it. The federal
government must point to an enumerated power as the source
of its authority before any other constitutional analysis
becomes necessary.

**Application to the variant facts.** Congress has enacted a
statute requiring every adult to attend an annual federal
"Hospitality and Community Welcome" course. The statute
recites the N&P Clause and describes the statute's purpose
as "improve national social cohesion and neighborly care."
It identifies no other enumerated power being carried into
execution. The structural defect is fatal:

- **No tie to an enumerated power.** The hospitality-course
  requirement is not, on its face, an exercise of the taxing
  power, the spending power (it imposes a training requirement,
  not a spending condition), the commerce power (it does not
  regulate economic activity, channel interstate channels, or
  substantially affect interstate commerce under *Lopez* /
  *Morrison*), the war power, the property power, the
  investigatory power, the 13th/14th/15th-Amendment
  enforcement power, or any other enumerated power. "Improve
  national social cohesion and neighborly care" is not itself
  an enumerated power; it is a description of a generally
  beneficial social outcome.
- **N&P is not a stand-alone power.** The N&P Clause can
  authorize a law only as a means of executing an enumerated
  power. *McCulloch*'s "appropriate and legitimate" language
  is contextual, not freestanding. A federal statute that
  recites N&P but ties to no other enumerated power exceeds
  Congress's authority, regardless of how useful the statute
  might be.
- **No federal police power.** The federal government has no
  general police power. Whatever authority exists to regulate
  "community welfare" through mandatory training belongs to
  the states. Congress cannot reach an unenumerated subject
  by labeling it "community welfare" or "general welfare" —
  those labels do not create federal jurisdiction.
- **Viewpoint discrimination is irrelevant to this defect.**
  Even if the hospitality-course statute contained no
  viewpoint discrimination (and the facts do not suggest
  any), it would still need an enumerated power. The
  structural defect is independently fatal.

**Authority floor (lawyer confirmation).**
