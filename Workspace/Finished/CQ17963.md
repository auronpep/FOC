## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

False imprisonment at common law is the intentional, unlawful confinement of another within fixed boundaries, of which the victim is conscious or by which the victim is harmed. **Restatement (Second) of Torts § 35**. Confinement is complete where a credible threat of immediate harm prevents the victim from using an otherwise available means of escape. The question is whether the victim was effectively confined, not whether physical escape was literally possible.

Daniel told Esther she could not leave the editing office and backed the command with a credible, immediate threat of arrest witnessed by her parents and her brother Timothy. Esther remained in the office for ninety minutes because she reasonably believed she had no lawful choice. That duration of compelled presence is more than enough to constitute confinement. The fact that the door was physically unlocked does not negate confinement achieved by duress — under the Restatement, a threat of immediate harm can satisfy the confinement element even where a physical exit exists, and § 36(2)'s "reasonable means of escape" rule is not the doctrinal hook for threat-of-immediate-harm cases. **Silver Key:** when a stem gives you a physical-fact tell (an unlocked door, an open window), don't end the analysis there — ask whether a credible threat prevented the victim from using it.

**C — "Yes, but only if Daniel's underlying accusation of theft was false."**

This choice sells the cognitive move: "False imprisonment requires the underlying accusation to be false; if she actually stole, then the employer had a right to detain her." It sounds lawyerly because it ties the tort to the merits of the underlying accusation. But the truth or falsity of the underlying accusation is irrelevant to whether false imprisonment occurred. A private employer has no privilege to detain an employee for interrogation the way law enforcement might, and even police must follow constitutional rules. The shopkeeper's privilege, which requires reasonable grounds, reasonable duration, and reasonable manner, applies only to merchant-detained suspected shoplifters; it does not extend to general employer-employee interrogations of this kind. Esther could be the worst thief in the city and Daniel's confinement of her would still be false imprisonment.

**D — "No, because Daniel's threat was to call the police — a lawful act — not to use physical force against Esther."**

This choice sells the cognitive move: "The threatened act (calling the police) is itself lawful, so it cannot be duress. If Daniel was going to do something legal, Esther wasn't coerced." It feels intuitive — how can a lawful threat be unlawful confinement? But even a threat of a technically lawful act can constitute the coercive force required for false imprisonment if it is used to compel the victim to remain against her will. The key question is whether the victim was unreasonably confined, not whether the threatened act was itself lawful. Confinement by moral pressure or threat of immediate harm (including threatened police involvement used coercively) satisfies the element. **Silver Key (SK-TORTS-FI-LAWFUL-01):** when the threat is a "lawful act" (calling police, filing a lawsuit, reporting to authorities), don't conclude that confinement is impossible — ask whether the lawful act was used coercively to compel presence.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

**Governing rule.** False imprisonment at common law is the intentional, unlawful confinement of another within fixed boundaries, of which the victim is conscious or by which the victim is harmed. **Restatement (Second) of Torts § 35** ("An actor is subject to liability to another for false imprisonment if (a) he acts intending to confine the other or a third person within boundaries fixed by the actor, and (b) his act directly or indirectly results in such a confinement of the other, and (c) the other is conscious of the confinement or is harmed by it").

**Confinement by threat.** Section 36(2) of the Restatement provides that "[t]he confinement is complete although there is a reasonable means of escape, unless the other knows of it." Although § 36(2) speaks of a known reasonable means of escape, the broader rule — well settled in the case law and reflected in the Restatement's discussion of threat-based confinement — is that a credible threat of immediate harm can itself prevent the victim from using an available means of escape, and the existence of that physical means is then not dispositive. Cornell's Wex summary of the tort captures the same principle: "If escape would cause risk of physical harm, or if threats are made against the plaintiff or family members, the area is deemed bounded." (https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/false_imprisonment)

**The shopkeeper's privilege.** The shopkeeper's privilege (often codified by state statute, e.g., S.C. Code § 16-13-140; Ky. Rev. Stat. § 433.236) authorizes a merchant, merchant's employee, or security agent to detain a person whom they have probable cause to believe has shoplifted, in a reasonable manner and for a reasonable time, on or near the merchant's premises. The privilege is narrow on its face: it requires probable cause of shoplifting (not generalized workplace theft), reasonableness of manner and duration, and physical proximity to the alleged offense. The privilege does **not** extend to general employer-employee interrogations of suspected office or back-office theft, and it does not authorize coercive interrogation behind closed doors with threats of family shame. (See generally https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/false_imprisonment; https://www.collinsandlacy.com/applicability-of-the-shopkeepers-statute-in-defending-a-claim-for-false-imprisonment)

**Truth of underlying accusation is irrelevant.** Whether Esther actually stole the cash advances is irrelevant to whether Daniel committed false imprisonment. The tort is concerned with the act of confining, not with the truth of any underlying accusation that motivated the confinement. Even a guilty employee can be falsely imprisoned by her employer.

**Application.** Daniel acted with intent to confine Esther within the editing office (the boundaries he fixed). His threat of immediate arrest, witnessed by her family, directly caused her to remain in the office for ninety minutes. Esther was conscious of the confinement throughout. The confinement was not privileged: Daniel is not a merchant detaining a suspected shoplifter, and the manner (closed-door interrogation under threat of family shame) is not the "reasonable manner" the shopkeeper's privilege contemplates. The unlocked door is irrelevant because the threat prevented her from using it. The credited answer (A) follows from these elements and from the well-settled rule that a credible threat of immediate harm satisfies the confinement element of false imprisonment.
