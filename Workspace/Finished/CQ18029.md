Under M'Naghten, is an insanity claim viable when mental disease makes the defendant know the physical motion but not that she is striking a person?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

A is correct. Gold Key: M'Naghten is satisfied when, because of mental disease, the defendant did not know the nature and quality of the act. Lydia knew the body movement, but her delusion prevented her from understanding that she was hitting a person rather than an object. Silver Key: do not stop at "she knew she was swinging." ### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

B. This is the dominant trap because it captures one true fact: Lydia knew she was swinging the cross. The breaker is that M'Naghten's nature-and-quality prong asks more than whether she knew the body movement; it asks whether mental disease kept her from understanding what she was actually doing.

C. The ordinary-mistake answer overclaims. A mistake of fact is not automatically insanity; the defense works here only because the mistaken perception came from mental disease and defeated knowledge of the act's nature and quality.

D. The control answer imports a volitional test. M'Naghten is a cognitive test, so the issue is Lydia's understanding, not whether she could resist an impulse.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule is unchanged: under M'Naghten, insanity may be established if, because of disease of the mind, the accused did not know the nature and quality of the act or did not know that the act was wrong. M'Naghten's Case is reported at 8 English Reports 718 and 10 Clark & Finnelly 200. The University of Minnesota Law Library summary states the rule in those terms and identifies the nature-and-quality prong. See https://lawlibrarycollections.umn.edu/classic-cases-criminal-mnaghten-rule.

Lydia's delusion is not an ordinary factual mistake. It is caused by mental disease. She understood the gross physical movement, but not the nature and quality of the act because she believed the target was an empty candle stand rather than a human being. That fits the M'Naghten cognitive prong supplied by the question.
