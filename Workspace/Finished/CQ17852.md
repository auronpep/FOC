Timothy, who had recently lost his job at the church bookstore, promised to repay Paul as soon as he was back on his feet. Weeks passed. ### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

Paul is **not** guilty of larceny, because the common-law claim-of-right doctrine negates the trespassory-taking element.

> **Gold Key — Claim of Right (Larceny):** A defendant who genuinely (even if mistakenly) believes he has a legal right to the specific property he takes — or to its equivalent value in satisfaction of a debt — cannot form the mens rea for a trespassory taking. The belief must be *subjectively sincere*; it need not be reasonable or legally correct. The prosecution must disprove this honest belief beyond a reasonable doubt.

Here, Paul sincerely believed he was legally entitled to hold the lathe as security for the $400 debt. Even though civil law would not recognize a self-help "offset" right in this situation, Paul's honest (if legally wrong) belief defeats the trespassory-taking element of larceny. The claim of right does not need to be a *good* argument — it needs to be an *honest* one.

The property taken does not need to be the exact coins or goods the defendant owns; taking property of equivalent value to satisfy an honest debt claim falls within the doctrine (*Ilich v R* (1987) 162 CLR 110; *R v Fuge* (2001) 123 A Crim R 310 at 314–315).

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice D (Yes, because taking property from another's premises without consent is trespassory as a matter of law):**

This is the most attractive wrong answer because it completes a clean story: Paul walked into Timothy's open carport and took something that wasn't his. That *feels* like the whole analysis. But lack of consent is only *one element* of a trespassory taking. The claim-of-right doctrine provides that a genuine (even mistaken) belief in a legal right to the property negates the "trespassory" character of the taking — the very element this choice assumes is settled. This is a classic overclaim: it says consent is dispositive "as a matter of law," which is exactly what the claim-of-right exception denies.

> **Gold Key:** Honest belief in legal entitlement defeats trespassory taking — the lack-of-consent element alone does not close the analysis.

**Choice A (Yes, because Paul's self-help remedy is unlawful under civil law, so his conduct cannot be innocent under criminal law):**

This choice tempts students who know that self-help remedies are restricted and conflate civil wrongfulness with criminal liability. Civil law certainly does not authorize a creditor to seize a debtor's property without process. But criminal larceny has its own specific elements and defenses. The claim-of-right doctrine operates within *criminal* law independently of whether the civil law would support the same self-help conduct. A person can be civilly liable for conversion while simultaneously lacking the criminal intent required for larceny.

**Choice C (No, because the lathe's value does not exceed the amount Timothy owed Paul):**

This answer reaches the right outcome (not guilty) but for the wrong reason. It offers a value-equivalence theory — essentially, "you can't steal what's owed to you" based on net value. That is not the doctrinal basis for acquittal. The correct reason is the claim-of-right doctrine (subjective belief), not an objective value-matching test. A person who takes property worth *more* than the debt could still have a valid claim of right on the portion they believed was owed. The defense turns on the defendant's mental state, not a balance-sheet calculation.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Elements of larceny (common law):** (1) trespassory taking and (2) asportation of (3) personal property (4) of another (5) with intent to permanently deprive.

**Claim-of-right doctrine (the governing rule):** When a defendant honestly believes — even if incorrectly — that he has a legal right to the specific property taken (or to property of equivalent value to satisfy a debt owed to him), the "trespassory" element of larceny is negated. The belief must be genuinely held; it need not be reasonable or legally well-founded. The prosecution bears the burden of disproving the honest claim of right beyond a reasonable doubt.

**Authority:**
- *Ilich v R* (1987) 162 CLR 110 (HCA) — dishonesty is an element of larceny; claim of right negates dishonesty/trespassory taking.
- *R v Fuge* (2001) 123 A Crim R 310 at 314–315 (NSWCCA) — comprehensive statement of claim-of-right principles, including that the belief need not be reasonable and extends to equivalent value.
- Judicial Commission of NSW, *Bench Book — Criminal*, Larceny, §5-6110 (claim of right).
- *Peters v The Queen* (1998) 192 CLR 493 — dishonesty assessed by standards of ordinary decent people.

**Application to this variant:** Paul's belief that he could hold the lathe as security for the $400 debt, even though legally incorrect under civil law, was subjectively sincere. The claim-of-right doctrine therefore negates the trespassory-taking element. The legal issue (claim of right / larceny), the tested rule, and the credited outcome (not guilty) are **unchanged** from the original.

#### Gold Key(s)

> **Gold Key — Claim of Right (Larceny):** A defendant who genuinely (even if mistakenly) believes he has a legal right to the specific property he takes — or to its equivalent value in satisfaction of a debt — cannot form the mens rea for a trespassory taking. The belief must be *subjectively sincere*; it need not be reasonable or legally correct. The prosecution must disprove this honest belief beyond a reasonable doubt. (*Ilich v R* (1987) 162 CLR 110; *R v Fuge* (2001) 123 A Crim R 310 at 314–315)
