## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Robbery is larceny plus force or threat. That means the state still must prove the larcenous taking of the property of another. Naomi used a threat, but the charge still fails if she honestly believed she was reclaiming her own specific stolen suitcase. Her belief can be foolish and still negate the intent to steal.

**Gold Key:** a good-faith claim of ownership to specific property defeats the larcenous intent required for robbery, even when the belief is mistaken and unreasonable.

**Silver Key:** when one answer leans on force and another leans on ownership or steal-intent, test the base larceny element first. Force cannot turn a nonlarcenous retaking into robbery.

Because Naomi honestly believed the suitcase was hers, she lacked the intent to steal the property of another. That defeats robbery.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.**
This is the dominant trap because the threat is loud, ugly, and unmistakable. Students see the robbery force element and stop there. The breaker is that robbery is not force by itself. **Silver Key:** test larceny first. If Naomi honestly believed she was reclaiming her own specific suitcase, the added threat does not create robbery.

**C.**
This answer feels technical because it attacks the threat itself and sounds like it is tightening the prosecution's proof burden. The breaker is that robbery does not require proof that Naomi could immediately carry out the threat. That is a made-up requirement, so this choice misses the doctrine entirely.

**D.**
This answer is attractive because "unreasonable" sounds like bad faith. The trap is smuggling in an objective reasonableness test. **Gold Key:** for a claim-of-right retaking of specific property, the issue is honest belief, not reasonable belief. An unreasonable mistake can still negate larcenous intent.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

I infer from the source row's general bar framing that the bank is testing the common-law robbery rule: robbery is larceny accomplished by force or intimidation, so the prosecution must still prove a taking of the property of another with larcenous intent.

The official key checks out. In *People v. Tufunga*, the California Supreme Court explained that a good-faith claim of right to specific property negates the felonious intent required for theft and therefore for robbery, even when the belief is mistaken. The court also rooted that rule in the common-law understanding of robbery as an aggravated form of larceny. https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/21/935.html

The same property-of-another principle appears in *State v. Smith*, where the Connecticut Supreme Court held that robbery incorporates larceny and therefore requires proof that the defendant was taking property of another rather than her own property. https://law.justia.com/cases/connecticut/supreme-court/2015/sc19314.html

Applying that rule here, Naomi's threat satisfies the force or fear component, but the charge still fails if she honestly believed she was reclaiming her own specific suitcase. The source row expressly says Naomi proved honest belief, and the prosecution proved only that the belief was unreasonable. That does not supply the missing larcenous intent. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome remain the same as the source question.
