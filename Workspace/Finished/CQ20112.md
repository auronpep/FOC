1. Final question — the revised question stem + call.

Samuel struck and injured a customer during a bar fight; the state charged Samuel with simple assault and prosecuted. Samuel was acquitted of simple assault. The state then charged Samuel with aggravated battery arising from the same incident, asserting that aggravated battery requires proof of serious bodily injury — an element not required for simple assault. Samuel moves to bar the aggravated battery prosecution on double jeopardy grounds. Under the Blockburger same-offense test, is the successive aggravated battery prosecution barred?

A. Yes, because simple assault is a lesser-included offense of aggravated battery, and an acquittal on the lesser bars prosecution for the greater
B. No, because aggravated battery requires proof of serious bodily injury, an element not required for simple assault, so they are different offenses under Blockburger
C. Yes, because the charges arise from the same conduct and the Double Jeopardy Clause bars all successive prosecutions based on the same incident
D. No, because Samuel was acquitted rather than convicted and the Double Jeopardy Clause's bar applies only after conviction

7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register).

Blockburger compares statutory elements: if each offense requires proof of an element the other does not, they are distinct. But when the greater offense necessarily includes all elements of the lesser (i.e., the lesser is a lesser-included offense), an acquittal on the lesser precludes later prosecution for the greater based on the same conduct because the prosecution would be required to relitigate elements already resolved. Simple assault is typically subsumed within aggravated battery; because Samuel was acquitted of the assault, double jeopardy bars the aggravated-battery prosecution in this fact pattern.

8. Full wrong-answer explanations — one per distractor.

B: Attractive because aggravated battery sometimes contains an extra element (serious bodily injury); however, Blockburger requires a two-way elements comparison. If the lesser offense's elements are subsumed within the greater, the offenses are the same for double jeopardy purposes despite an additional aggravating element.

C: Attractive via overbroad reading of "same conduct" — but double jeopardy hinges on elements, not merely overlapping conduct. Saying the Clause bars all successive prosecutions based on the same incident is too broad and misstates Blockburger.

D: Attractive to those who conflate acquittal and conviction effects, but incorrect: an acquittal is an absolute bar to retrial for the same offense; double jeopardy protections apply with force after acquittal.

9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer.

Rule: Blockburger v. United States, 284 U.S. 299 (1932): Two offenses are the same for double jeopardy unless each requires proof of an element the other does not. If the greater necessarily proves the lesser, the lesser is a lesser-included offense and an earlier acquittal on the lesser bars later prosecution for the greater when based on the same conduct. Authority: Blockburger v. United States (1932). Note: statutory definitions vary by jurisdiction; courts apply the elements test rather than factual overlap. Attorney review not required for this black-letter mapping; Blockburger is controlling precedent for the elements test.
