### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The strongest basis for denial is **untimeliness combined with prejudice to the existing parties**. Mary's Mercy followed the case from the day it was filed, then waited until the eve of trial — after summary judgment briefing had closed — to move to intervene, and the proposed intervention would require reopening discovery and delaying trial. Both Rule 24(a) (intervention of right) and Rule 24(b) (permissive intervention) require the motion to be timely; courts weigh how far the case has progressed, the movant's reason for the delay, and the prejudice that intervention would cause existing parties. **Silver Key:** when the movant knew of the case from day one, run the Rule 24 timeliness factors first; the public-importance choice is a misstatement of the mandatory-intervention rule (public interest alone is not a magic override of the timeliness requirement).

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B. Intervention is required because zoning decisions affect the public.**

This is the dominant trap. Zoning does affect the public, and that is exactly why courts police the timeliness gate — otherwise any community group could parachute in at the eve of trial and force a do-over. The breaker is the **Silver Key**: the public importance of zoning is not a magic override of Rule 24's timeliness requirement; "intervention is required" misstates the mandatory-intervention rule, which requires a separate Rule 24(a)(2) showing of impairment of a legally protected interest, not merely a general public concern.

**C. Intervention must be denied because only original parties may raise traffic-impact arguments.**

This choice is a misstatement of who may intervene. Rule 24 expressly allows non-parties to raise arguments; the question is whether the motion is timely and whether Rule 24(a) or (b) requirements are met — not whether the movant is a named party. The choice is a wrong-rule / wrong-element cut: it points at a defect (non-parties raising arguments) that does not exist.

**D. Mary's Mercy has no possible interest because it is not a property owner.**

This is the "no interest at all" trap. Rule 24(a)(2) requires an interest that may be impaired by the disposition — it does not require property ownership. A mobile-medical ministry that operates clinics in the affected neighborhood has a cognizable interest in traffic, parking, and patient access. The choice misstates the Rule 24(a)(2) interest test.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17711, a Civil Procedure item involving a non-party that followed a zoning lawsuit from the start, waited until the eve of trial to move to intervene, and offered arguments it could have raised earlier. The official key is C: the motion is untimely, and late intervention would prejudice existing parties.

Rule 24 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure governs intervention. Rule 24(a) provides for intervention of right on a timely motion by a non-party who (1) is given a conditional right by federal statute, or (2) claims an interest relating to the property or transaction that may be impaired by the disposition and is not adequately represented by existing parties. Rule 24(b) provides for permissive intervention on a timely motion when a statute of the United States confers a conditional right, or when a non-party has a claim or defense sharing a common question of law or fact with the main action. Both branches require timeliness, and courts evaluate timeliness by reference to how far the case has progressed, the movant's reason for the delay, and the prejudice that intervention would impose on existing parties. The leading articulation of these factors is set out in *NAACP v. New York*, 413 U.S. 345 (1973), and is commonly taught alongside the four-factor test articulated in *Stallworth v. Monsanto Co.*, 558 F.2d 741 (5th Cir. 1977), and similar appellate formulations.

The supplied facts map onto the timeliness-plus-prejudice holding: the movant knew of the case from the day it was filed, waited until briefing on summary judgment had closed and trial was scheduled, and the proposed intervention would require reopening discovery and delaying trial. Each of those facts weighs against the movant on the timeliness factors and weighs in favor of the existing parties' prejudice argument. The transformed outcome is identical to the original: the strongest basis for denying the motion is the combination of untimeliness and prejudice to existing parties.
