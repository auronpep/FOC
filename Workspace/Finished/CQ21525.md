### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** The incapacity defense belongs to the minor alone. A minor may disaffirm a contract because he or she lacks capacity, but the adult party who voluntarily entered the contract cannot turn around and use the minor's incapacity as an excuse not to perform. The risk of dealing with a minor falls on the adult, not the minor.

**C is correct.** When an adult enters into a contract with a minor, the adult is bound by the contract. The minor has the power to disaffirm, but that power belongs exclusively to the minor. Lydia contracted with Timothy and now must perform. Her refusal to deliver the laptop unless Timothy proves his age or produces a co-signer is a breach of the agreement.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original B) — Dominant trap.** This choice is attractive because it states a true rule — minors lack contractual capacity — and students may reflexively pick it without noticing that the question asks whether *Lydia* can use Timothy's incapacity as *her* defense. The capacity defense is the minor's shield, not the adult's sword. **Gold Key:** The incapacity defense belongs to the minor alone; the adult cannot invoke it to escape her own obligations.

**B (original A).** This choice plays on a genuine concern — yes, a minor *can* disaffirm and demand a refund. But that risk is the adult's to bear when choosing to contract with a minor. The possibility that Timothy *might* later disaffirm does not excuse Lydia's current breach. The law places the risk of minority on the adult party, not the minor.

**D (original C).** This choice tempts students who spot Timothy's reliance (buying the sleeve and mouse) and jump to detrimental reliance / promissory estoppel. But the defendant's promise was supported by consideration — Timothy's promise to pay $850 on delivery. Detrimental reliance is a doctrine for enforcing promises *lacking* consideration. Since this contract has consideration, reliance is irrelevant to the enforcement question.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** Under the common law and the Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 12–14, a natural person under 18 incurs only voidable contractual duties. The minor may disaffirm the contract during minority or within a reasonable time after reaching majority. However, the contract is binding on the other party. The adult cannot use the minor's lack of capacity as a defense to nonperformance.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 14 ("Infants"): "Unless a statute provides otherwise, a natural person has the capacity to incur only voidable contractual duties until the beginning of the day before the person's eighteenth birthday." See also § 12 (general capacity rule). The rule is further confirmed by the weight of common-law authority: "contracts made with a minor are voidable at the minor's option: they don't bind the minor but do bind the other contracting party" (5 Pump Court, *Contracting Capacity of Minors*, 2022); "adults must contract with minors at their own peril" (DR&A Law Firm, *Approaching Contracts with the Boardroom*, 2017).

**Verification:** The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original. The official key (D in the original, now C post-shuffle) is correct. The minor prevails; the adult cannot use the minor's incapacity as a defense.

#### Wrong-Answer Recovery Paths

**If you picked B:** You correctly remembered that minors can disaffirm — but you confused a real risk with a valid defense. The risk of disaffirmance falls on the adult who chose to deal with the minor. It doesn't excuse the adult's own breach.
