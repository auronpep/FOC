### 1. Final question

### 6. Student black-letter-law answer explanation
B is correct. Stephen's payment schedule did not give him money at 25 percent completion, and the unfinished pavilion was completely destroyed before Lydia received any usable benefit from the work. Restitution does not pay the contractor for effort in the abstract. It asks what benefit the owner kept. Gold Key: for restitution after part performance, look for benefit retained by the defendant, not the claimant's labor cost or completion percentage. Silver Key: when every money answer uses a different math frame, first ask whether the owner kept anything valuable from the partial work.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct because Stephen conferred no retained benefit on Lydia. The contract called for the first progress payment only at 50 percent completion, but Stephen reached only 25 percent. More importantly, the partially built new structure was completely destroyed before Lydia used it or kept any improvement. Whether Stephen's refusal to rebuild is treated as a breach or whether the destruction is treated as an excuse problem, Stephen's possible recovery is restitutionary, and restitution is limited to the value of the benefit Lydia received. Lydia received none. The lower replacement price helps Lydia, not Stephen; the 25 percent completion number does not create a payment right; and the reasonable value of Stephen's work is not the measure when the owner retained no benefit.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because it turns the 25 percent completion fact into clean arithmetic. The breaker is that the contract did not promise a 25 percent progress payment, and restitution does not award a percentage of the contract price just because work reached that percentage.

C is attractive because Lydia eventually obtained the completed pavilion for less than the original contract price. The breaker is the remedial frame. That price difference is not Stephen's restitution claim against Lydia; it imports a damages idea from a different setting and ignores the no-benefit fact.

D is attractive because "reasonable value of work performed" sounds like quantum meruit. The breaker is the Gold Key: restitution measures the benefit Lydia retained, not Stephen's effort standing alone. Because the new structure was completely destroyed before Lydia received a benefit, this answer pays the wrong element.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Contracts, Remedies, Legal Restitution, and the verified outline code is `51060201`.

Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 374 provides the restitution rule for a party in breach: the party in breach may recover only for a benefit conferred by part performance or reliance, reduced by the loss caused by the breach. Source: https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/11720-bruckner-howard-law-contracts-2024/resources/13.5.2-restatement-second-of-contracts-374/

Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 377 provides the same benefit-conferred frame when a duty is discharged by impracticability, frustration, non-occurrence of a condition, or disclaimer. Source: https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/11720-bruckner-howard-law-contracts-2024/resources/13.5.5-restatement-second-of-contracts-377/

Those authorities confirm the official key. If the fire did not excuse Stephen, his refusal to continue was a breach, but Section 374 still limits any restitution claim to benefits conferred on Lydia. If the fire did excuse further performance, Section 377 still asks whether Stephen conferred a benefit on Lydia. On these facts, the partially constructed new pavilion was completely destroyed before any payment was due and before Lydia received any retained improvement. Stephen therefore has no restitution recovery. The credited outcome is unchanged.
