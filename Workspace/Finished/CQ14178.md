## 1. Final question

Mary runs a Christian music-lesson studio. John organized a private youth choir. Mary made a rush order of choir robes for John's choir after John told her, in writing, that the choir had $9,000 in its music fund and no unpaid vendor balances. Mary relied on those statements and extended 60-day credit. John never paid.

In federal court, Mary's first count alleged fraudulent inducement: John knowingly made false statements about the choir's finances, and Mary relied on them to her detriment in extending credit. The second count alleged that, although John's statements were not false and Mary did not rely on them when extending credit, John is in default on the robe account and liable for the unpaid balance. Payment was due before the complaint was filed.

Is a court likely to find that Mary's complaint was pleaded in good faith?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

Can a plaintiff plead both fraud and contract theories when one theory says she relied on the same statements and the other says she did not rely on them?

## 3. Final answer choices (post-shuffle)

C. No, because Mary must have made one of the two allegations in bad faith.

D. Yes, because alternative and inconsistent allegations are permitted when the pleading party does not have knowledge that only one of the allegations is true.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Gold Key: Rule 8 permits alternative and inconsistent claims, but Rule 11 still requires a good-faith factual basis for the factual contentions a pleader puts before the court.

Silver Key: Read the call. It asks whether the complaint was pleaded in good faith, not whether at least one theory states a claim.

Choice C wins because Mary alleged both reliance and non-reliance on the same financial statements when she extended credit. Those are facts about Mary's own conduct. A pleader may plead alternative legal theories, but the pleader cannot in good faith assert two mutually exclusive factual accounts of her own reliance when she knows both cannot be true. The fraud count says Mary relied; the contract/default count says Mary did not rely. That internal good-faith problem makes C the correct no answer.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

### Choice A

The pull is that the complaint does contain an inconsistency, and a no conclusion feels plausible. The breaker is that the fatal inconsistency is not merely about whether John lied; a pleader may be uncertain about an opponent's truthfulness. The good-faith problem is Mary's own mutually exclusive allegations about reliance.

### Choice B

The pull is that pleading at least one viable theory feels enough to keep the complaint alive. The breaker is that claim sufficiency is not the call. A complaint can state a claim and still be attacked for a Rule 11 good-faith problem when it asserts facts the pleader cannot in good faith believe.

### Choice D

The pull is that Rule 8 expressly allows inconsistent claims, so a yes answer sounds technically elegant. Gold Key: that permission is not absolute; Rule 11 still requires good-faith factual support. Silver Key: when the yes answer says “permitted” without a good-faith limit, it is over-applying Rule 8. Mary cannot rely on Rule 8 to plead two mutually exclusive facts about her own reliance.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

Authority floor: Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 8(d)(2) permits alternative statements of a claim or defense, and Rule 8(d)(3) permits inconsistent claims or defenses. Cornell LII rule text: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_8. Rule 11(b)(3) separately requires that factual contentions have evidentiary support or, if specifically identified, will likely have evidentiary support after a reasonable opportunity for further investigation or discovery. Cornell LII rule text: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_11.

The credited answer is unchanged: the item tests the limit of alternative pleading when the pleader asserts mutually exclusive facts about her own reliance. Rule 8(d)(3) allows inconsistent claims, but Rule 11(b)(3) requires a good-faith evidentiary basis. The complaint alleges both that Mary relied on John's statements and that Mary did not rely on those same statements. Because those are facts about the pleader's own conduct, the complaint is likely not pleaded in good faith.
