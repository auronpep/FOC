## Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)
**B is correct.** Under Fed. R. Civ. P. 55(b)(2), if the party against whom default judgment is sought has appeared personally or by representative, that party must be served with written notice of the application for default judgment at least **7 days** before the hearing on the application. Barnabas appeared in the action by filing his motion to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction. Filing any paper that shows an intent to defend the case constitutes an appearance for Rule 55 purposes. The church served notice only three days before the hearing—four days short of the required seven. That defect alone requires that the default judgment be set aside upon a timely motion showing good cause.

**Silver Key (call focus):** When the stem gives you a short number of days (here, "three days") next to a rule that uses the same unit ("seven days"), lock the call on whether the noticed party received the full period the rule demands after an appearance has occurred. Do not let the "jurisdiction motion" or "no answer after denial" facts pull you into a different analysis.

## Full wrong-answer explanations
**A (new letter for original D) is incorrect.** The seven-day period in Rule 55(b)(2) is the plaintiff's obligation to give notice to a party who has appeared. It is not a deadline for the defendant to file a motion to set aside the judgment. A motion under Rule 55(c) or 60(b) must be made within a reasonable time (and, for excusable neglect, not more than one year). Ten days is well within any reasonable-time window.

**C is incorrect.** Once a defendant has appeared (by filing the motion to dismiss), the plaintiff cannot simply take a default judgment without giving the required seven days' notice. The fact that Barnabas filed nothing after the denial does not erase the notice defect created by the church's three-day service. The appearance had already happened.

**D is incorrect.** Filing a motion to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction constitutes an appearance for purposes of Rule 55(b)(2). The rule does not carve out an exception for jurisdictional challenges. After the court denied the motion, Barnabas was required to respond on the merits or suffer default, but the church still had to give him the full seven days' notice before the default-judgment hearing because the appearance had already occurred.

## Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule is the appearance-triggered notice requirement of Fed. R. Civ. P. 55(b)(2). The text of the rule is unambiguous: "If the party against whom a default judgment is sought has appeared personally or by a representative, that party or its representative must be served with written notice of the application at least 7 days before the hearing." 

Courts uniformly hold that filing a pre-answer motion, including a motion to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction under Rule 12(b)(2), constitutes an "appearance" that triggers the notice obligation. See, e.g., *Roland v. W & L Motor Lines, Inc.*, 32 N.C. App. 288 (1977) (appearance may arise by implication from any step beneficial to defendant or detrimental to plaintiff); see also *Strauss v. Hunt*, 140 N.C. App. 345 (2000) (motion to dismiss for improper service counted as appearance). The three-day notice given here was legally insufficient. The default judgment was therefore entered in violation of the rule. A motion to set aside under Rule 55(c) or 60(b) is the proper vehicle to correct that procedural defect when good cause is shown. The outcome on these facts is unchanged from the source question.

**Authority floor (lawyer-confirmation layer only):** Fed. R. Civ. P. 55(b)(2) (7-day written notice after appearance). No leading case is required; the rule text and the consistent construction that any paper showing intent to defend = appearance are sufficient and current. No doctrinal-currency issue (the 7-day period has been stable since the 2009 amendment; no relevant change since).
