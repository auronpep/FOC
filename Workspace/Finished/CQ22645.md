## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. The amendment draws a state-created line between people with in-state felony convictions and everyone else, then denies the burdened group access to a state business permit. That is an Equal Protection argument. It may be a hard argument to win because the classification is not suspect and ordinary occupational licensing is usually reviewed deferentially, but the question asks for Lydia's strongest listed argument, not for a guaranteed winner.

Gold Key: A classification claim belongs under Equal Protection even when the class gets only rational basis review; the clause is still the correct hook.

Silver Key: In a strongest-argument call, first cut choices with no fact hook, then choose the constitutional label that actually matches the state action.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because the words "doing business in the state" can sound like a border-crossing or mobility problem. The breaker is that the amendment does not penalize entering, leaving, visiting, or becoming a resident of another state; it classifies by in-state felony conviction and permit eligibility, so the right-to-travel hook is not responsive.

C is attractive because the fresh-start point feels morally powerful and sympathetic. The breaker is that moral fairness is not a constitutional clause, standard, or remedy; it does not answer the call for the strongest constitutional argument.

D is attractive because a business permit and a small food cart can make students think in contract language. The breaker is the Gold Key's classification hook: the amendment does not impair an existing contractual obligation, and the Contracts Clause is not a general right-to-operate-a-business clause.

## 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning For The Right Answer

The credited outcome is unchanged: the Equal Protection Clause is the only listed argument with a real constitutional hook. The Fourteenth Amendment provides that a state may not deny any person within its jurisdiction equal protection of the laws. The Supreme Court describes the clause as a command that the state govern impartially and recognizes that a special rule burdening a subset of persons raises an equal-protection question. See New York City Transit Authority v. Beazer, 440 U.S. 568 (1979), https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/440/568/.

Because the amendment classifies applicants by felony-conviction status and restricts business licensing for that class, Equal Protection is the responsive argument. The argument is not necessarily strong on the merits. For most classifications that do not involve a suspect class or a fundamental right, rational basis review applies. See Constitution Annotated, Equal Protection and Rational Basis Review Generally, https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/amdt14-S1-8-1-2/ALDE_00000817/. Economic and occupational regulations are highly deferential under Williamson v. Lee Optical, Inc., 348 U.S. 483 (1955), https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/348/483/. Richardson v. Ramirez, 418 U.S. 24 (1974), also confirms that completed felony sentences do not automatically make an Equal Protection challenge prevail. See https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/418/24/.

The distractors fail for the same reasons as in the source item. A right-to-travel claim requires an actual burden on interstate movement, temporary presence, or new-resident equality; Saenz v. Roe identifies those travel interests, but this amendment is keyed to in-state felony conviction and business licensing, not interstate movement. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-97.ZO.html. The Contracts Clause protects existing contractual obligations from state impairment; it is not a general liberty to do business after a felony conviction. See Constitution Annotated, Overview of Contract Clause, https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/artI-S10-C1-4-1/ALDE_00013037/.
