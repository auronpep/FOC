# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## Final question

A young woman named Ruth applied to join a short-term mission team organized by the Antioch Outreach Society, a Christian nonprofit that sends service teams abroad. She was not selected. Ruth sued the Society in federal district court for race discrimination in its team selection process. During discovery, Ruth requested “all items concerning the Society’s team selection practices.”

Several years earlier, a staff member named Stephen had written a highly critical internal memorandum to the board about the Society’s team selection criteria. The memorandum warned that the criteria appeared to disadvantage certain racial groups and could create legal exposure. After the board received the memorandum, the Society immediately turned it over to the Society’s outside attorneys, who were now representing the Society in Ruth’s action and used the memorandum in preparing the Society’s defense. Due to its inflammatory nature and the amount of hearsay it contained, the memorandum was inadmissible at trial.

Must the Society provide the memorandum to Ruth?

## Distilled Core Question

## Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**B is correct.** Under the discovery rules, a party must produce relevant, nonprivileged matter. The memorandum is relevant to Ruth’s race discrimination claim about team selection practices. It need not be admissible itself. The fact that the Society later gave it to its attorneys for use in this lawsuit does not convert it into work product, because the document was created years earlier by a staff member with no indication it was prepared in anticipation of this (or any) litigation.

**Silver Key: Work-product timing tell.** When a choice claims “privileged work product” after lawyers received and used an old memo, check the creation date in the stem. Protection attaches (or fails) at the time the document was prepared, not when counsel later received it.

## Full wrong-answer explanations

**A is attractive** because students often think “if it can’t come in at trial, it is not discoverable” (the moral/common-sense pull that inadmissible = secret).  
**Breaker:** FRCP 26(b)(1) explicitly states that information “need not be admissible in evidence” if it is likely to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. The choice misstates the black-letter scope of discovery.

**C is attractive** because the memo was turned over to the Society’s attorneys and used in preparing the defense (classic work-product bait; students stop at “lawyers touched it”).  
**Breaker (Silver Key):** Work product protection requires that the document was prepared in anticipation of litigation. Stephen wrote the memorandum several years earlier as an internal staff critique; the suit had not been filed and there is no stem fact showing anticipation at creation time. Handing a pre-existing document to counsel later does not create retroactive protection.

**D is attractive** because it sounds like a fairness or necessity rule (“if she can’t get it anywhere else, she should get it here”).  
**Breaker:** FRCP 26(b)(1) imposes no requirement that the requesting party prove it would be impossible or even difficult to obtain the information from another source. The choice fabricates a threshold that does not exist in the rule.

## Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited outcome is unchanged from the original. The tested black-letter rule is the scope of discovery under Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(b)(1): a party must produce “any nonprivileged matter that is relevant to any party’s claim or defense,” and information “need not be admissible in evidence” if it is “reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” (The 2015 amendment tightened the language slightly to “relevant to any party’s claim or defense and proportional to the needs of the case,” but the core principle that inadmissible material remains discoverable when it may lead to admissible evidence is intact.)

Work-product protection is governed by Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(b)(3) and the doctrine originating in *Hickman v. Taylor*, 329 U.S. 495 (1947). It protects “documents and tangible things that are prepared in anticipation of litigation or for trial by or for another party or its representative.” The protection is not triggered merely because a pre-existing document is later reviewed by counsel or used in defense preparation. The critical timing inquiry is whether the document was created because of the prospect of litigation. Here, the memorandum was written “several years earlier” by a staff member as an internal critique of selection criteria; the lawsuit had not yet been filed. Therefore it is not work product.

The original key (C) and the legal issue (scope of discovery / work-product timing mismatch) are preserved exactly. No research contradicted the supplied official key or explanations. Authority: Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(b)(1) and 26(b)(3).
