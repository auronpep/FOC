### 1. Final Question

Lydia, a well-known Christian singer-songwriter, kept a password-protected archive of personal voice notes on her own tablet. Barnabas, her paid scheduling assistant, watched from behind a sound booth as Lydia entered the password to open the archive before a hymn retreat.

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

D is the best answer. Barnabas did more than hear public facts or repeat true information. He secretly watched Lydia enter a password, used that password to enter a private archive, listened to private voice notes, and exported transcripts without permission. Gold Key: intrusion upon seclusion is complete when the defendant intentionally pries into private affairs in a highly offensive way; publication, falsity, and lack of newsworthiness are not required. Silver Key: separate how the information was obtained from what was later published. This stem is about the secret access first.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A. This answer sells a familiar defense: true information on a matter of public interest usually sounds protected. The breaker is that the claim can be based on the private, unauthorized access itself, so truth and newsworthiness do not defeat the intrusion theory.

B. This answer is attractive because it lands in the right privacy neighborhood and reaches the same yes/no outcome. The breaker is the theory: public disclosure of private facts turns on publicity of private facts and newsworthiness limits, while this stem is cleanly won on Barnabas's secret access to Lydia's private archive.

C. This answer uses a real fact in the stem: Barnabas was allowed to work around Lydia's studio. The breaker is scope. Permission to work in the studio did not include permission to watch Lydia's password, open her private archive later, listen to voice notes, or export transcript snippets.

### 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. The source item tests invasion of privacy by intrusion upon seclusion, not defamation and not public disclosure of private facts. Restatement (Second) of Torts Section 652B states the governing privacy-tort rule: intentional intrusion, physical or otherwise, into another's solitude, seclusion, or private affairs creates liability if the intrusion would be highly offensive to a reasonable person. See https://cyber.harvard.edu/privacy/Privacy_R2d_Torts_Sections.htm. State jury-instruction authorities also summarize intrusion as not requiring physical entry, publicity, or general communication to the public, and they cite Restatement Section 652B for that proposition. See https://www.coloradojudicial.gov/media/8687. Shulman v. Group W Productions, Inc., 18 Cal. 4th 200 (1998), likewise separates offensive newsgathering intrusion from later publication defenses. See https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/shulman-v-group-w-productions-inc-31911.

Barnabas intentionally pried into a password-protected personal archive. Lydia kept the archive private, and Barnabas acquired access only by watching the password and later using it without permission. That supports intrusion upon seclusion. The fact that later reports were true and addressed matters of public interest may matter to a publication-of-private-facts theory, but it does not erase the earlier intrusion. The permission-to-work fact also fails because the invitation did not cover private digital access. The official key is consistent with the researched rule.
