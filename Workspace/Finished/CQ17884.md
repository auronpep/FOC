# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question — revised stem and call

Daniel is convicted by a jury of second-degree criminal mischief for smashing the sound equipment trailer that Lydia used for a neighborhood Bible-study outreach. The offense carries a maximum sentence of four years. At sentencing, the judge finds by a preponderance of the evidence that Daniel targeted the trailer because it was used for Christian worship and teaching, a fact not submitted to the jury. Under the state's faith-targeting sentencing enhancement statute, the judge sentences Daniel to seven years, which exceeds the four-year maximum for second-degree criminal mischief but falls within the nine-year maximum for the faith-targeting enhanced offense. Daniel argues that the sentence violates the Sixth Amendment. Is Daniel's Sixth Amendment challenge correct?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A jury verdict authorizes a four-year maximum, but the judge finds a new enhancement fact and imposes seven years. Does the Sixth Amendment require the enhancement fact to go to the jury?

## 3. Final answer choices

A. No, because the judge was resolving a sentencing factor after conviction, and the Sixth Amendment jury-trial right does not control facts found only at sentencing.

B. Yes, because the faith-targeting finding increased the sentence above the four-year maximum authorized by the jury's verdict, so that finding had to be submitted to the jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt.

C. Yes, but only if Daniel can show that the judge's faith-targeting finding was inaccurate; if the finding was factually correct, no Sixth Amendment violation occurs.

D. No, because the judge found the faith-targeting fact by a preponderance of the evidence, which is permissible for sentencing findings that do not exceed the maximum authorized by the verdict.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

**B is the best answer.** The Sixth Amendment challenge is correct. The jury's verdict alone authorized only a four-year maximum for second-degree criminal mischief. The judge's separate faith-targeting finding moved Daniel's lawful sentencing ceiling from four years to nine years and produced a seven-year sentence. **Gold Key:** other than a prior conviction, a fact that increases the punishment above the maximum authorized by the jury's verdict must be submitted to a jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt. **Silver Key:** use the jury-authorized maximum, not the enhanced-offense maximum after the judge finds the extra fact.

Because the faith-targeting fact was neither submitted to the jury nor proved beyond a reasonable doubt, the judge could not use that fact to impose a sentence above four years. The constitutional problem is the allocation and standard of fact-finding, not whether the judge believed the fact by a preponderance.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This answer sells a familiar sentencing shortcut: judges often find facts at sentencing. The breaker is the ceiling. **Gold Key:** judicial fact-finding is allowed only within the punishment range already authorized by the jury's verdict; once the finding raises the maximum, the Sixth Amendment requires jury finding beyond a reasonable doubt.

**C.** This answer is attractive because it keeps the defendant's challenge alive and makes factual accuracy feel like the sensible line. The breaker is that Apprendi is a procedural jury-right rule. Even an accurate judge-found enhancement fact cannot raise the maximum sentence unless the jury found it beyond a reasonable doubt.

**D.** This is the dominant trap. It uses a true sentencing principle in the wrong box: judges may use a preponderance standard for many facts that guide a sentence inside the authorized range. **Silver Key:** first identify the jury-authorized maximum. Here the seven-year sentence crossed the four-year ceiling, so the preponderance standard cannot do the work.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested black-letter rule (unchanged):** Other than the fact of a prior conviction, any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the statutory maximum authorized by the jury's verdict must be submitted to the jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt. The relevant maximum is the maximum a judge may impose based only on the jury's verdict or the defendant's admissions, not the higher maximum available after the judge finds an extra fact.

**Governing authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):** Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), held that a judge-found bias motive could not increase the sentence beyond the otherwise authorized maximum. Blakely v. Washington, 542 U.S. 296 (2004), confirmed that the Apprendi maximum is the verdict-authorized maximum, not the maximum available after additional judicial findings. Erlinger v. United States, 602 U.S. ___ (2024), confirms the current Apprendi line: facts that increase the prescribed punishment range belong to the jury, subject to the prior-conviction exception. Research sources checked: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/530/466/ ; https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/542/296/ ; https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/23-370_i4dj.pdf ; https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/amdt6-5-3-4/ALDE_00013129/
