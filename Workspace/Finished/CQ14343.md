Testing and maintaining residential smoke alarms and carbon-monoxide detectors has traditionally been handled by private electricians, landlords, and local fire departments. ## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**A is correct.** The commission is advisory, the campaign is public education, Congress has not forbidden the action, and Congress has appropriated broad funds to the Office of the President. The President does not need a statute naming this exact commission when the action stays inside executive administration and uses funds Congress made available. **Gold Key:** When the President acts with express or implied congressional support, especially through an appropriation and no statutory prohibition, presidential authority is at its strongest. **Silver Key:** In a separation-of-powers array, do not stop at "no specific statute"; ask whether Congress has forbidden, funded, or left room for the executive action.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B.** This choice sells the formal-authorization instinct: no statute names the commission, so the commission must be invalid. The breaker is the invented requirement. A specific statute is not always required for an advisory commission that falls within executive authority, faces no statutory prohibition, and is funded by a broad congressional appropriation. **Gold Key:** congressional support can be implied through funding and statutory silence; the Constitution does not demand a tailor-made statute for every advisory executive act. **Silver Key:** the authorization axis is not "specific statute or nothing."

**C.** This choice sells the federalism reflex: fire readiness sounds local, so the federal government must stay out. The breaker is exclusivity. A state police-power tradition does not make the field exclusively state territory when the federal government is using valid federal authority for a public-education campaign.

**D.** This choice reaches the right outcome with the wrong source of power. The President has no plenary national police power over health, safety, and welfare. The valid path is executive authority plus Congress's funding posture, not a freestanding presidential general-welfare power.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are preserved. The item tests the President's chief-executive authority when Congress has neither prohibited the action nor withheld funds, and when Congress has appropriated broad funds that can support the executive activity.

Authority floor and current-law check: Article II vests executive power in the President and Article II, Section 3 includes the duty to take care that the laws are faithfully executed. The Constitution Annotated explains that the Take Care duty can include powers conferred directly by Article II and powers congressional acts confer on the President or executive agencies. Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer, 343 U.S. 579 (1952), supplies the controlling separation-of-powers frame: presidential authority is strongest when the President acts with express or implied congressional authorization, and weakest when contrary to Congress's will. Article I, Section 8, Clause 1 gives Congress the spending power for the common defense and general welfare, so Congress's broad appropriation matters; it does not make the President a holder of general police power. Nothing in the current doctrinal hotlist changes this executive-power analysis.

The plenary-police-power answer remains wrong because the President does not possess a general federal police power. The Tenth Amendment answer remains wrong because a traditional local safety role does not make the area exclusively state-controlled when the federal government acts through valid federal powers. The no-specific-statute answer remains wrong because the separation-of-powers inquiry is not that rigid under Youngstown's first category.
