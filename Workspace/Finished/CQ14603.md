### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.** Lydia's motion should be granted because the first constitutional problem happened before the arrest and before the tote was opened. The deputy did not observe Lydia from a public aisle or some ordinary vantage. She was hidden above closed changing booths and could see inside only because she occupied a concealed position.

**Gold Key:** a temporary private enclosure can carry a reasonable expectation of privacy even in a public commercial setting. A closed changing booth is not plain view from a hidden police perch.

**Silver Key:** when one answer attacks the later bag search and another attacks the earlier hidden observation, lock onto the first illegality that generated the probable cause. If the police only had probable cause because of the unlawful surveillance, the later arrest-and-seizure rationale does not save the evidence.

Because Lydia had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the changing booth, the concealed surveillance was an unlawful search. The arrest and tote seizure that followed were fruits of that search, so the jacket must be suppressed.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This is the dominant trap because search incident to arrest is a real and familiar exception, and the officer did arrest Lydia before opening the tote. The breaker is causation: the only probable cause came from the unlawful hidden surveillance inside the changing booth, so the later seizure does not cleanse the taint. **Silver Key:** find the first Fourth Amendment break.

**B.** This choice sounds clean because the deputy did see the theft happen and the words "plain view" feel natural when an officer personally observes conduct. **Gold Key:** plain view requires that the officer have a right to be in the position that produced the view. A hidden perch above a private changing booth is not that kind of lawful vantage.

**D.** This choice is attractive because the jacket was found inside a tote, and students often jump to warrant language when a bag is opened. The breaker is that the tote search is not the first constitutional defect. Once the arrest was driven by the unlawful surveillance, the suppression analysis turns on the hidden search inside the booth, not on a separate warrant requirement for the tote.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The source row supplies QID 14603, measured pick rates, official key **B**, and an explanation that the hidden fitting-room surveillance was the illegal search. That key checks out.

The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures, and **Katz v. United States** holds that the Amendment reaches government conduct that invades a person's reasonable expectation of privacy, not just physical trespasses. In Katz, the Court explained that the Amendment protects people rather than places and covers warrantless surveillance that intrudes on justified privacy expectations. ([Justia][1]; [Congress.gov][2]) A closed changing booth fits that framework: it is a temporarily private enclosure where a shopper expects not to be secretly watched from a hidden police perch.

California authority points in the same direction. **People v. Randazzo** said that if government officers had watched a fitting room by lying in a hidden adjacent position, the evidence would have been inadmissible; the case ultimately turned on the absence of state action because the watcher there was a private store detective. ([Justia][3]) **People v. Triggs** then made the related plain-view point explicit: observation is not plain view unless the officer had a right to be in the position that produced the view, and an officer may not retreat to a clandestine position to peer into a private enclosure. ([Justia][4]) I am inferring from those authorities that hidden overhead surveillance into a private changing booth is likewise outside plain view and constitutionally infirm.

Once the hidden observation is unlawful, the suppression remedy follows. **Mapp v. Ohio** applies the exclusionary rule in state criminal trials, and **Wong Sun v. United States** bars evidence derived from unconstitutional police conduct unless the taint is purged. ([Justia][5]; [Justia][6]) Here the jacket was seized only because the hidden surveillance gave officers the probable cause to stop and arrest Lydia, so the credited answer remains that suppression is granted.

Doctrinal-currency check: this is a standard Fourth Amendment privacy and exclusion issue, not on the hotlist. No authority contradicts the official key. **bank_validation_verdict: PASS.**
