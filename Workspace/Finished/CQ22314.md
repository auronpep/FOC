### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law and Procedure, Homicide, Murder, with verified outline code `74020101`. The source item asks whether a murder charge survives a self-defense claim when the defendant used deadly force under a reasonable factual mistake about an imminent shooting. The credited outcome is unchanged: a reasonable mistake about the need for self-defense preserves the justification and defeats murder.

The governing black-letter rule is that deadly force in self-defense is justified when the actor reasonably believes such force is necessary to prevent imminent death or serious bodily harm. A mistake about the threat does not defeat the defense if the mistake was reasonable. *People v. Goetz*, 68 N.Y.2d 96 (1986), explains that deadly force may be justified even when the actor's belief about the other's intentions turns out wrong, so long as the belief has an objective reasonable basis; it also rejects a purely subjective fear standard. The New York court's historical discussion ties that rule to common-law self-defense and the reasonable-ground requirement. https://www.nycourts.gov/reporter/archives/p_goetz.htm. Model Penal Code section 3.04 likewise frames self-protection around force the actor believes is immediately necessary, and section 3.04(2)(b) specifically limits deadly force to threats such as death or serious bodily harm; section 3.09 addresses mistaken justification. https://www.criminallawweb.net/web1/mpc/PART1/snippets/3_04-3_07.htm.

Here, the facts that matter are the late hour, recent armed robberies, metal object pressed into Stephen's back, express statement "I have a pistol," threat to shoot, and demand for the deposit pouch. Those facts make the mistaken belief in imminent deadly force reasonable. The later revelation that Hannah was playing a prank with a handbell handle does not retroactively make Stephen guilty of murder.
