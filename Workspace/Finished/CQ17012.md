# Pass-1 Transform Report — Q17012

## 1. Final Question

Hannah agreed to purchase a used sailboat from Peter for $12,000. Hannah paid Peter a $3,000 deposit at signing. Before the closing date, Hannah separately spent $2,500 hiring a marine surveyor and a mechanic to inspect the boat. Peter then refused to transfer the boat and kept the deposit. The boat's resale market is thin and volatile, so Hannah cannot establish expectation damages with reasonable certainty. Which statement best separates Hannah's remedy interests?

**Answer A:** Hannah has no remedy because expectation damages cannot be proven.

**Answer B:** The $2,500 inspection costs are expectation damages because Hannah expected to buy the boat.

**Answer C:** The $3,000 deposit is restitutionary because it was a benefit conferred on Peter; the $2,500 inspection and survey costs are reliance expenditures.

**Answer D:** The $3,000 deposit and the $2,500 inspection costs are both restitution because Hannah spent them.

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C** is correct. When a seller breaches a contract to sell property, the buyer may pursue restitution and reliance as alternative remedy interests when expectation damages are too speculative.

The $3,000 deposit is restitutionary. A deposit paid to the seller is a **benefit conferred on the seller**—money that the seller received and now holds. Restitution focuses on the **defendant's gain**, not the plaintiff's outlay. The buyer can recover the deposit because the seller was unjustly enriched by retaining it without performing.

The $2,500 in inspection and survey costs are **reliance expenditures**. These are out-of-pocket costs the buyer incurred **in reliance on the contract**—preparing to complete the purchase. Reliance damages aim to put the buyer back in the position she would have been in had she never entered the contract, by reimbursing wasted expenditures.

These two categories are distinct even though both may be recoverable. Confusing them is exactly what the question tests.

**Gold Key:** *Restitution = defendant's benefit. Reliance = plaintiff's wasted expenditure. Same dollar can be both only if it both enriched the defendant AND was spent in reliance—but the legal categories are defined by different questions.*

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original D):** This is attractive because students learn that expectation damages are the "default" remedy and may wrongly conclude that if the default fails, nothing is left. The breaker: the law provides alternative remedy interests—restitution and reliance—precisely for cases where expectation damages are too speculative. The absence of provable expectation damages does not eliminate other claims.

**B (original C):** This is attractive because the inspection costs are indeed connected to the expected purchase—Hannah wouldn't have spent the money but for the contract. The breaker: expectation damages measure the **value of the promised performance** (the boat), not the costs of preparing to receive it. Inspection costs are reliance expenditures, not a substitute for the bargain's value.

**D (original A) — DOMINANT TRAP:** This is attractive because both sums flowed from Hannah to the transaction, and "restitution" sounds like a general "give it back" remedy. The breaker: restitution is not defined by who spent the money; it is defined by **what the defendant received as a benefit**. The $2,500 went to third-party inspectors, not to Peter. Only the $3,000 deposit enriched Peter and is restitutionary. Calling both sums "restitution" collapses two distinct remedy categories into one.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Rule:** Contract remedies protect three distinct interests: (1) the **expectation interest** (the benefit of the bargain—put the plaintiff in the position she would have been in had the contract been performed); (2) the **reliance interest** (wasted expenditures incurred in reliance on the contract—restore the plaintiff to her pre-contract position); and (3) the **restitution interest** (benefit conferred on the defendant—prevent unjust enrichment).

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 346, 349, 371. Section 349 provides that the injured party may recover reliance damages "including expenditures made in preparation for performance or in performance." Section 371 provides that restitution is measured by "the extent to which the other party's property has been increased in value or his other interests advanced." *See also* Anglia Television Ltd. v. Reed [1972] 1 QB 60 (allowing reliance expenditures when expectation damages too speculative); Chicago Coliseum Club v. Dempsey, 265 Ill. App. 542 (1932) (distinguishing pre-contract expenses, reliance expenses, and expectation).

**Application:** The deposit is a direct monetary benefit conferred on Peter—restitutionary. The inspection costs are out-of-pocket reliance expenditures—reliance. Both are recoverable, but they represent different remedy interests. The credited answer (C) is correct. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original.

#### Program Frame

This item tests whether you can distinguish between **restitution** and **reliance** as remedy interests when expectation damages are unavailable. The key insight: these categories ask different questions. Restitution asks "what did the defendant gain?" Reliance asks "what did the plaintiff waste?" Both may be recoverable, but they are not the same.
