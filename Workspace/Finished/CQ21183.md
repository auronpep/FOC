## 6. Right-Answer Explanation

**B is correct.** Claim preclusion (res judicata) bars a plaintiff from splitting a single claim into multiple lawsuits. Once a final judgment on the merits is entered, the plaintiff cannot bring a second suit on any claim that **arose from the same transaction or occurrence** and **could have been raised** in the first action. Here, both the fence damage and the medical expenses arose from the same tree-fall incident. The woman knew about her medical injuries before the first judgment but chose not to include them. Because she could have raised the medical-expense claim in the first suit, claim preclusion bars the second suit entirely.

> **Gold Key:** Claim preclusion bars all claims from the same transaction that were brought **or could have been brought** in the first suit. Claim splitting is not allowed.

## 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect.** This choice is attractive because students think "property damage" and "personal injury" are different legal claims. But under the transactional test used by most courts (and the federal common law of preclusion), all injuries from one incident form a **single claim** regardless of the type of damages. The woman cannot split her tree-fall claim into two lawsuits just because she seeks different categories of recovery.

**C is incorrect (dominant trap).** This choice confuses **claim preclusion** with **issue preclusion** (collateral estoppel). Claim preclusion requires only: (1) same parties, (2) final judgment on the merits, (3) same claim (transaction). It does **not** require that the specific issue or damage category was actually litigated. Issue preclusion has an actual-litigation requirement, but that doctrine applies to re-litigating specific issues, not to splitting claims. Here, claim preclusion applies, and the medical bills need not have been actually litigated to be barred.

> **Silver Key:** Claim preclusion = could-have-been-raised (no actual litigation required). Issue preclusion = actually-litigated-and-decided.

**D is incorrect.** Preclusion doctrine has nothing to do with diversity of citizenship. Diversity jurisdiction determines whether a federal court can hear a case under 28 U.S.C. § 1332, but preclusion applies regardless of whether the parties are diverse, non-diverse, or in state court. The woman's citizenship relative to her neighbor is irrelevant to whether claim preclusion bars the second suit.

## 8. Black-Letter Verification (Lawyer Confirmation)

**Tested rule:** Claim preclusion (res judicata) bars a plaintiff from splitting a single transactional claim into multiple lawsuits. All claims arising from the same transaction that were or could have been raised in the first suit are merged into the judgment (if plaintiff won) or barred (if plaintiff lost).

**Authority floor:**
- *Restatement (Second) of Judgments* § 24 (1982) — "when a valid and final judgment rendered in an action extinguishes the plaintiff's claim pursuant to the rules of merger or bar... the claim extinguished includes all rights of the plaintiff to remedies against the defendant with respect to all or any part of the transaction, or series of connected transactions, out of which the action arose."
- *Migra v. Warren City School District Board of Education*, 465 U.S. 75, 77 (1984) — federal common law of res judicata applies; claim preclusion bars claims that could have been raised.
- *Federated Department Stores, Inc. v. Moitie*, 452 U.S. 394, 398 (1981) — "a final judgment on the merits of an action precludes the parties or their privies from relitigating issues that were or could have been raised in that action."
- *Cromwell v. County of Sac*, 94 U.S. 351, 352–53 (1877) — classic distinction between claim preclusion and issue preclusion.

**Doctrinal currency check:** No superseding authority affects the transactional test or the could-have-been-raised rule. The black-letter rule remains stable.

**Outcome confirmation:** The woman's property damage and medical expenses arose from the same tree-fall transaction. She knew about both before judgment. Claim preclusion bars the second suit. B is correct.
