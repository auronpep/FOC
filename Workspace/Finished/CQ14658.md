### 5. Correct answer

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

A is correct. **Gold Key:** extreme recklessness plus foreseeable death can supply murder liability even when the defendant did not personally shoot the victim and did not specifically intend that victim’s death. **Silver Key:** in a “guilty/not guilty because X” array, the reason matters. The right guilty answer is not just “guilty”; it must give the right route to guilt.

Paul knowingly used Timothy’s jealousy and violent temper to send him armed into a guarded office. That created a grave and foreseeable risk that Timothy or someone else would be shot. John’s shooting did not erase Paul’s causal role because the guard’s response was the kind of foreseeable consequence Paul set in motion.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B is wrong.** Transferred intent is the dominant trap. **Gold Key:** transferred intent moves the defendant’s own intent from an intended victim to an unintended victim when the defendant’s own conduct causes the unintended death. Here, John killed Timothy. Paul’s better route to guilt is extreme recklessness and foreseeable causation, not transferred intent.

**C is wrong.** This proves the wrong mental-state point. Paul did not need to intend for Timothy to be shot by John if Paul created a deadly risk with extreme recklessness and the death was foreseeable.

**D is wrong.** This answers the wrong liability route. Paul did not need to be the shooter or John’s accomplice. The charge can rest on Paul’s own reckless causal conduct.

### 8. Black-letter verification and legal reasoning

The original row supplies QID 14658, the 47/20/14/19 selection data, the original homicide stem, key A, and the official explanation that the actor’s extreme recklessness and foreseeable causation support murder liability.  The prompt also required legal research to stay in lawyer-confirmation and not leak into the student C3 path. 

Lawyer confirmation: murder can be based on reckless conduct under circumstances manifesting extreme indifference to human life; the MPC formulation uses that language, and the official row uses the common MBE phrase “extreme recklessness” / implied malice. ([Open Casebook][1]) Proximate cause generally asks whether the harm is legally attributable; foreseeability is a common test, and intervening acts do not automatically sever causation when they are foreseeable consequences of the defendant-created situation. ([Legal Information Institute][2]) Henderson v. Kibbe confirms that a later third-party act does not necessarily relieve a defendant of homicide responsibility when the defendant created the fatal risk. ([Justia Law][3]) Transferred intent, by contrast, is the “intent follows the bullet” type rule: the defendant’s own intended harm to A results in actual harm to B. That does not describe this answer’s best theory because John, not Paul, fired the fatal shot. ([North Carolina Criminal Law Blog][4])
