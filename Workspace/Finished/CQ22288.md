### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** Two crimes are at issue: receiving stolen property and larceny.

*Receiving stolen property* requires (1) receiving control of stolen property (2) knowing it was stolen **at the time of receipt** (3) with intent to permanently deprive. At the moment Ruth handed Peter the guitar, Peter had no idea it was stolen — he accepted it as a genuine gift from a coworker. His subsequent discovery, weeks later, cannot supply retroactive knowledge. The crime was not committed.

*Larceny* requires a trespassory taking and carrying away of personal property of another, knowing it is another's property, with intent to permanently deprive. Here, Ruth *voluntarily handed* the guitar to Peter — there was no trespassory taking, and at the moment Peter took the guitar he believed it was Ruth's to give. Even setting aside the consent issue, Peter did not know the guitar belonged to someone else when he took possession. Larceny was not committed.

**Gold Key:** Knowledge that property is stolen must exist at the moment of acquisition — you cannot "receive" stolen property you already received in ignorance weeks earlier. This is what defeats both crimes in one move.

**Silver Key:** When a stem tells you the defendant *later* learned the item was stolen, check the clock: did knowledge arrive at acquisition time or after? If after, receiving stolen property fails automatically on the knowledge element.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. "Receiving stolen property and larceny."** This is attractive because it stacks both crimes and feels comprehensive — Peter kept the guitar after learning the truth, so surely he must have committed *some* crime, right? But both crimes fail on the same element: knowledge at the time of acquisition. Peter didn't know the guitar was stolen when Ruth gave it to him. Receiving stolen property requires knowing the property was stolen *when you received it* — a later discovery doesn't convert innocent ownership into a crime. And larceny requires a trespassory taking with knowledge that the property belonged to another — but Ruth voluntarily handed it over, and Peter believed it was hers to give. Both crimes fail together. **Gold Key** (Knowledge-at-Acquisition Rule): the knowledge element is locked to the moment of receipt, not the moment of discovery.

**B. "Receiving stolen property only."** **(DOMINANT TRAP)** This is the most attractive wrong answer because the story *tells* you Peter found out the guitar was stolen — the store receipt is right there in the facts — and he kept it. It feels like he must be guilty of *something*. But receiving stolen property requires that Peter knew the guitar was stolen *at the time he received it*. He didn't. He accepted it as a genuine gift from a trusted coworker. Learning it was stolen weeks later doesn't retroactively transform an innocent receipt into a crime. The trap here is using Peter's *later* knowledge as a substitute for the *at-acquisition* knowledge the crime requires. **Silver Key:** When the stem front-loads a discovery story ("later found out," "weeks later learned"), ask yourself: did the defendant know *at acquisition*? If the discovery comes after, receiving stolen property fails on timing alone.

**C. "Larceny only."** This choice is appealing because it feels like the "moderate" answer — not too broad (not both crimes), not too narrow (not no crime). Larceny *does* target theft-like conduct, and keeping stolen property *feels* larcenous. But larceny requires a trespassory taking — an unlawful taking from another's possession. Here, Ruth *voluntarily gave* Peter the guitar. There was no trespass. And at the time Peter took the guitar, he believed it was Ruth's property to give. He didn't know it belonged to a music store. Larceny fails on both the trespassory-taking element and the knowledge element simultaneously.

### 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Receiving stolen property** (common law / modern statutory formulation): The offense requires (1) receiving possession or control of stolen personal property (2) knowing at the time of receiving that the property was stolen (3) with the intent to permanently deprive the owner. *See* 3 W. LaFave, Substantive Criminal Law § 19.5 (3d ed.); Model Penal Code § 223.6 (receiving stolen property requires "knowing that it has been stolen"). The key timing requirement is that knowledge must exist at acquisition — subsequent discovery does not suffice for the completed crime of receiving. *See also* People v. Lopez, 2013 IL App (1st) 111119 (knowledge at time of receipt required).

**Larceny** (common law): (1) Trespassory taking and (2) asportation (carrying away) of (3) personal property of another (4) knowing it to be another's property (5) with the intent to permanently deprive. *See* 3 W. LaFave, Substantive Criminal Law § 19.2 (3d ed.). "Trespassory" means without the owner's consent — a voluntary delivery by someone (even someone without title) generally negates trespassory taking as to the recipient. Knowledge that the property belongs to another must exist at the time of taking.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Peter's coworker Ruth gave him a brand-new guitar as a thank-you gift for helping her move into a new apartment. Peter thanked Ruth and took the guitar home. Two weeks later, while tuning the guitar, Peter found a store receipt tucked under the strings showing the guitar had been shoplifted from a music store. Despite learning this, Peter decided to keep the guitar and continued playing it.

**Call:** What crime, if any, has Peter committed?

- B. Receiving stolen property only.
- C. Larceny only.
- D. No crime.

#### Distilled Core Question

A person receives a gift and later discovers it was stolen property but keeps it. Guilty of any crime?

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks what crime Peter committed — it's an open-call (not "EXCEPT" or "LEAST"). The "if any" signals that "no crime" is a live option. Before reading choices, a student should predict: Peter didn't know the guitar was stolen when he got it, so he probably didn't commit either crime. The answer is likely "no crime."

#### Trigger Facts

1. **Ruth voluntarily gave Peter the guitar** — This defeats the trespassory-taking element of larceny. A voluntary delivery is not a trespass.
2. **Peter had no knowledge the guitar was stolen when received** — This is the key fact. Both receiving stolen property and larceny require knowledge at the time of acquisition.
3. **Peter discovered the receipt two weeks later** — This creates the trap. After-acquired knowledge feels legally significant but isn't.
4. **Peter kept the guitar after discovery** — This creates moral pressure but is legally irrelevant to both crimes' elements.
