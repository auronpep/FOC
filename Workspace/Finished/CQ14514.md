## 1. Final Question

Peter is a restoration contractor who buys and resells vintage instruments. ## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**C is correct.** Peter rightfully rejected the damaged guitar, so the prompt assumes his later sale was not an acceptance of the goods. After rejection, Peter could hold the guitar for Lydia, but he could not treat it as his own. Selling and delivering it to Timothy was an exercise of ownership over Lydia's property.

Gold Key: after rightful rejection, the buyer's exercise of ownership over the rejected goods is wrongful against the seller; if that wrongful exercise is a sale, the seller's remedy is conversion damages measured by the fair market value of the goods at the time and place of conversion. Silver Key: once the call tells you the later sale was not acceptance, stop pricing the original contract and price the later wrong.

The best evidence of the guitar's fair market value at the time Peter converted it is Peter's own cash sale to Timothy for $8,500. Lydia may recover $8,500.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### A. Contract-price trap - 36% inherited

**What makes it attractive.** This choice points to the cleanest number in the contract and feels like the natural cap on Lydia's recovery. Students who stay in ordinary contract-pricing mode after the rejection may stop here.

**The breaker.** The Gold Key defeats the trap: after rightful rejection, Peter's sale was not a contract-price event; it was a wrongful exercise of ownership over Lydia's goods. ### B. Volume-seller lost-profit trap - 1% inherited

**What makes it attractive.** This choice uses a real remedies phrase and sounds sophisticated because it treats Lydia as a seller seeking profit rather than the full resale price. It may appeal to students who remember UCC seller remedies but do not check the facts.

**The breaker.** Lydia is not a volume dealer. She is selling a one-of-a-kind personal guitar. More importantly, the call is not asking for ordinary lost-profit damages on an uncompleted sale; it is asking what Lydia can recover after Peter converted the rejected guitar.

### D. Excess-over-contract trap - 2% inherited

**What makes it attractive.** This choice notices that the $8,500 resale is higher than the $8,000 contract price and tries to award only the difference. That feels like a tidy damages formula.

**The breaker.** The Silver Key steers away from a delta calculation: price the later wrong, not the original bargain. Conversion damages are the full fair market value of the chattel at the time of conversion, so the correct measure is $8,500, not $500.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning For The Right Answer

**Tested rule.** Under UCC Section 2-601, if goods or tender fail in any respect to conform to the contract, the buyer may reject the whole. Under UCC Section 2-602, rejection must be timely and seasonably noticed, and after rejection any exercise of ownership by the buyer over the rejected commercial unit is wrongful against the seller. Under UCC Section 2-401(4), rejection or refusal revests title in the seller by operation of law. UCC Section 1-103(b) allows common-law and equitable principles to supplement the Code unless displaced. Conversion law treats a serious wrongful exercise of dominion over another's chattel as conversion, with damages measured by the chattel's full value at the time and place of conversion.
