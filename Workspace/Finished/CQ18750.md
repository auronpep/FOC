## 7. Full right-answer explanation

A is the correct answer. The complaint states a fraud claim, so Rule 9(b) controls the pleading detail. Rule 9(b) requires the circumstances of fraud to be pleaded with particularity; it does not let a plaintiff plead fraud in broad strokes and then rely only on a general intent allegation.

Gold Key: Rule 9(b) separates two things: the circumstances of fraud must be particular, while malice, intent, knowledge, and similar conditions of mind may be alleged generally.

Silver Key: When the call attacks a fraud pleading, first ask whether the complaint supplies the circumstances of fraud. If it does not, the motion should be granted even though intent may be alleged generally.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

B sells the comfort of the familiar Rule 8 notice-pleading baseline. The breaker is that fraud is a special pleading matter: Rule 9(b) adds particularity for the circumstances of fraud, so a Rule 8-only answer overstates the pleading rule.

C borrows jurisdiction language and makes the item feel procedural. The breaker is that the stem gives no personal-jurisdiction facts and asks only about fraud particularity; intentional conduct does not turn a pleading-defect motion into a minimum-contacts motion.

D is the strongest trap because the intent sentence is real. The breaker is that it answers only the mind-state half of Rule 9(b). Gold Key: Rule 9(b) lets intent be alleged generally, but it still requires the circumstances of fraud to be pleaded with particularity.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

Verified rule: Fed. R. Civ. P. 9(b) provides that, in alleging fraud or mistake, a party must state with particularity the circumstances constituting fraud or mistake, while malice, intent, knowledge, and other conditions of mind may be alleged generally. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_9. The motion posture is a pretrial pleading motion; Rule 12(c) allows judgment on the pleadings after pleadings are closed and early enough not to delay trial. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_12.
