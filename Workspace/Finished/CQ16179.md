## 1. Final Question

Stephen was charged with felony arson after a fire damaged the delivery van behind a privately owned Christian bookstore. Stephen had left school after the seventh grade and had no courtroom experience. At a pretrial hearing, Stephen told the judge that he did not trust the public defender appointed for him and wanted a different lawyer. The judge refused to appoint another lawyer and, without saying anything more about what self-representation would involve, told Stephen that his only other choice was to handle the trial himself. Stephen replied that he would do that because "anything is better than this lawyer." Stephen did not bring up the issue again. He represented himself at trial. After hearing overwhelming evidence of Stephen's guilt, the jury convicted him. On appeal, Stephen asks for a new trial on the ground that he was deprived of his right to counsel.

Should the appellate court grant Stephen's request?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

If a criminal defendant asks for a different appointed lawyer and then accepts self-representation only after the judge gives no warning about the dangers of proceeding without counsel, is the waiver valid?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. A criminal defendant may choose to represent himself, but the record must show that the waiver of counsel was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent. The judge did not explain the dangers and disadvantages of self-representation. Stephen's frustrated statement was not enough to create a valid waiver record.

Gold Key: A valid trial self-representation waiver needs more than "I will do it myself"; the record must show that the defendant understood the risks of giving up counsel.

Silver Key: Separate the defendant's choice from the judge's record. The question is not whether Stephen was guilty, angry, or bad at lawyering; it is whether the court made the waiver record valid before allowing self-representation.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the strongest appellate-sounding escape hatch: if the evidence was overwhelming, maybe the conviction should stand. The breaker is that the claim concerns the structure of the trial and the waiver record, not the weight of the evidence. Silver Key: when the call asks whether counsel was lost at trial, do not let "overwhelming evidence" answer a different remedy question.

C feels compassionate because Stephen had little schooling and no courtroom experience. The breaker is that the waiver question is not whether Stephen would perform like a lawyer. Gold Key: the court must ensure a knowing and intelligent waiver, but the defendant's technical legal skill is not the deciding test.

D has the dominant trap. Stephen did choose self-representation after the judge refused to appoint a new lawyer, so the choice captures one necessary part of waiver. The breaker is that voluntariness alone is incomplete; the record still must show that Stephen was warned about the dangers and disadvantages of self-representation.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning

The governing authority is the Sixth Amendment right to assistance of counsel and the correlative right of self-representation. In [Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806 (1975)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/422/806/), the Supreme Court recognized a criminal defendant's right to conduct his own defense, but required the defendant to knowingly and intelligently forgo the benefits of counsel after being made aware of the dangers and disadvantages of self-representation. In [Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/509/389/), the Court reaffirmed that competence to waive counsel is not the same as ability to perform effectively as one's own lawyer, and that the waiver must still be knowing and voluntary. Structural-right cases such as [McKaskle v. Wiggins, 465 U.S. 168 (1984)](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep465/usrep465168/usrep465168.pdf) and [United States v. Gonzalez-Lopez, 548 U.S. 140 (2006)](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep548/usrep548140/usrep548140.pdf) support treating the loss or misallocation of the trial counsel/self-representation framework as a trial-structure problem rather than a simple sufficiency-of-evidence problem.
