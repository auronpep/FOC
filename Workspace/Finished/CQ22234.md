## 1. Final Question

At the trial of a breach-of-contract action brought by Miriam against Nathan, Miriam's lawyer called Miriam as a witness on her own behalf. Miriam was an expert in pipe-organ acoustics. On direct examination, Miriam's lawyer asked, "Do you own a walnut chapel organ with a cracked ivory stop?" Miriam said that she did. No other questions were asked on direct examination. On cross-examination, Nathan's lawyer asked Miriam, "Your organ is generally known to have a mellow tone, isn't that correct?"

If Miriam's lawyer objects to the question, should the objection be sustained?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

On direct, a witness says only that she owns a specific item. On cross, opposing counsel asks a leading question about that item's general reputation or quality. Should the objection be sustained because the cross question goes beyond the scope of direct examination?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

Choice B is correct. Miriam's direct examination established only one narrow fact: that she owned a walnut chapel organ with a cracked ivory stop. The cross-examination question asked about an entirely different subject, namely whether that organ was generally known to have a mellow tone. That new topic was outside the subject matter of the direct examination and did not bear on Miriam's credibility.

Gold Key: leading questions are ordinarily allowed on cross-examination. So the problem with this question is not its form. The problem is that it jumps to a new subject after a one-question direct.

Silver Key: when two answers both say the objection should be sustained, separate form from scope. A cross question may suggest the answer and still be proper, but it cannot roam to a new topic unless the court permits broader inquiry.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### Choice A

Attractive move: the stem tells you Miriam is an expert in pipe-organ acoustics, so it is tempting to think her expertise makes the cross-question permissible.

Breaker: the objection is not about whether Miriam knows enough to answer. The defect is that the question reaches beyond the tiny slice opened on direct examination, so expert status does not fix the scope problem.

### Choice C

Attractive move: students sometimes associate cross-examination with hostile witnesses and treat hostility as a reason the question may be allowed.

Breaker: nothing in the facts shows that Miriam is hostile to Nathan's lawyer in the evidentiary sense. The witness is simply the plaintiff, and the real issue is still that the question exceeds the scope of direct.

### Choice D

Attractive move: the question is obviously leading, so this answer feels like a quick and familiar objection.

Breaker: Gold Key: leading questions are ordinarily allowed on cross-examination under the usual evidence rule. The better objection is scope, because the question moves from ownership of a specific organ to the organ's general reputation for tone.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning For The Right Answer

The transformed item preserves the original tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome. The governing rule is the scope of cross-examination.

- Fed. R. Evid. 611(b) provides that cross-examination should not go beyond the subject matter of the direct examination and matters affecting the witness's credibility, though the court may allow additional inquiry as if on direct examination. Here, direct examination covered only ownership of a particular organ. Asking whether the organ was generally known to have a mellow tone moved to a different subject and did not target Miriam's credibility. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_611
- Fed. R. Evid. 611(c) also says that leading questions ordinarily should be allowed on cross-examination. That defeats the dominant trap answer that treats leading form, by itself, as the reason to sustain the objection. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_611
- The hostile-witness distractor is also defeated by Rule 611(c), which separately allows leading questions when a party calls a hostile witness, an adverse party, or a witness identified with an adverse party. Nothing in the facts says Miriam was hostile in fact.
- The expert-status distractor stays nonresponsive for the same reason as in the source row. The question asks whether the organ is generally known to have a mellow tone, not for Miriam's expert opinion about acoustic design. Even if expertise made the answer easier, it would not cure the scope defect.
