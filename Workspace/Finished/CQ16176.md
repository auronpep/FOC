## 1. Final Question

Halfway through the first afternoon of Daniel's criminal mischief trial, the jury had been sworn and the first witness had begun testifying about damage to a Christian bookstore's front window. During a short sidebar in chambers about a photograph, the judge received a call that his adult son had been taken to the emergency room after a highway accident.

Without asking the prosecutor or Daniel's lawyer whether they wanted a pause, and without considering whether the trial could simply resume the next morning, the judge returned to the courtroom, declared a mistrial, dismissed the jury, and left for the hospital. The next morning, a new jury was sworn before a different judge. Daniel objected to the second trial on double jeopardy grounds.

Would the second trial violate the prohibition against double jeopardy?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Once the jury was sworn, jeopardy attached. A mistrial declared without the defendant's consent does not automatically permit the government to start again. Gold Key: after jeopardy attaches, retrial after a mistrial over the defendant's objection requires manifest necessity. Silver Key: do not stop at the judge's reason for leaving; check whether the judge considered a less drastic alternative or heard from counsel. Here, the judge did neither. A short continuance was obvious, so the second trial violates double jeopardy.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the sympathy shortcut. The judge may have had a sincere family emergency, but good faith is not the Double Jeopardy test after jeopardy attaches. The Gold Key requires manifest necessity for the mistrial, not merely an understandable reason for the judge to leave.

B reaches the right result through the wrong door. A second judge's possible evidentiary ruling is not what makes retrial unconstitutional. The breaker is the first judge's unsupported mistrial after jeopardy attached, not the chance of different photograph rulings.

D sells the dominant no-verdict myth. Double jeopardy protection can matter before a verdict once the jury has been sworn. Silver Key: ask whether jeopardy attached and whether manifest necessity justified ending the first trial; the absence of a verdict does not answer the question.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: in a jury trial, jeopardy attaches after the jury is sworn. If the court declares a mistrial over the defendant's objection after attachment, retrial is barred unless the mistrial was supported by manifest necessity or the ends of public justice would otherwise be defeated.

United States v. Perez, 22 U.S. 579 (1824), supplies the manifest-necessity foundation for discharging a jury without barring retrial. United States v. Jorn, 400 U.S. 470 (1971), confirms that an abrupt sua sponte mistrial without adequate concern for the defendant's right to complete the trial before the first tribunal can bar retrial. Arizona v. Washington, 434 U.S. 497 (1978), confirms the prosecutor must show manifest necessity for a mistrial over the defendant's objection, while also recognizing deference when the record shows deliberate consideration. Sources checked: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/22/579/, https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-supreme-court/400/470.html, and https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/434/497/.

Daniel's first jury had been sworn, so jeopardy attached. The judge declared a mistrial sua sponte, without asking the lawyers for their positions and without considering the obvious alternative of a short continuance until the next morning. The family emergency explains why the judge needed to leave, but it does not show that the trial itself had to be terminated. Because manifest necessity is absent, the second trial violates double jeopardy.
