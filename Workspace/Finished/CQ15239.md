## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** Strict products liability applies only to defendants who are *commercial sellers* — those engaged in the business of selling the type of product at issue. A one-time auctioneer liquidating another's property is not "in the business of selling" that product and therefore falls outside the scope of strict liability.

Timothy was hired to liquidate Barnabas's foreclosed equipment — a one-time engagement. He is not in the business of selling woodworking tools. Under Restatement (Second) of Torts § 402A, strict liability attaches only to sellers engaged in the business of selling the product. Because Timothy does not meet that threshold, the strict liability claim fails regardless of whether the saw was defective or whether Lydia was injured. The defect and injury are necessary elements, but the threshold requirement — that the defendant be a commercial seller — is not met.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (plaintiff, because Timothy sold a defective product):** This is the dominant trap. It states a broadly correct principle — a seller of a defective product can be strictly liable — but it silently drops the critical limitation: the seller must be *engaged in the business of selling* that type of product. Timothy sold the saw, yes, but a one-time foreclosure auction does not make him a commercial seller of woodworking equipment. **Gold Key:** The "commercial seller" requirement is the gatekeeper element — without it, the rest of the strict liability analysis never gets off the ground.

**C (plaintiff, because Timothy failed to inspect):** This distractor assumes that Timothy had a duty to inspect the saw. A commercial seller of woodworking equipment might have such a duty, but Timothy is not a commercial seller. The duty to inspect attaches to those in the business of selling the product, not to one-time auctioneers. The choice is attractive because it sounds like a reasonable safety expectation, but it overstates the obligations of a non-commercial seller.

**D (defendant, because Lydia's own negligence contributed):** This distractor reaches the correct party (defendant) but for the wrong reason. Contributory negligence is not a defense in strict products liability in this jurisdiction — and more fundamentally, the negligence issue is never reached because Timothy is not a commercial seller. The choice tempts students who know that contributory negligence doesn't bar strict liability recovery and flip it into a reason to rule for the defendant. **Silver Key:** When a choice reaches the right outcome for the wrong reason, check whether the reasoning actually applies — here, the contributory negligence analysis is irrelevant because the threshold commercial-seller element fails first.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 402A (1965). Strict products liability imposes liability on a seller who is *engaged in the business of selling* a defective product that causes injury to a user. The key elements are: (1) the defendant is a commercial seller engaged in the business of selling the product; (2) the product is in a defective condition unreasonably dangerous to the user; (3) the product reaches the user without substantial change; and (4) the defect causes physical harm.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 402A(1) ("One who sells any product in a defective condition unreasonably dangerous to the user or consumer or to his property is subject to liability for physical harm thereby caused to the ultimate user or consumer..."). Comment f clarifies that the rule applies to any person in the business of selling products — the seller need not be the manufacturer, but must be engaged in the business of selling such products. Courts have consistently held that casual sellers, one-time sellers, and auctioneers selling another's goods at foreclosure are not "engaged in the business" and therefore are not subject to strict liability. *See, e.g.*, *Tauber-Arons Auctioneers, Inc. v. Superior Court*, 101 Cal. App. 3d 268 (1980) (auctioneer not strictly liable for defective product sold at auction); *Riley v. Ford Motor Co.*, 442 F.2d 670 (5th Cir. 1971) (casual seller distinction).
