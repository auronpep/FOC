## 1. Final Question

Lydia's Little Shepherd Workshop has its headquarters and only production room in one state. It makes a children's tabletop learning kit called "The Good Shepherd Trail," in which children move small wooden sheep through hills, wells, and a sheepfold while learning basic animal-care facts. The kit accurately depicts common grazing conditions, sheep behavior, and simple shepherding equipment. It is high quality, safe, durable, and has sold very well.

Several other kit makers, all located outside the state, have developed lower-priced Good Shepherd-style kits. Those makers have contracts to sell their kits to homeschool stores and toy stores in the state. Their kits are also safe and durable, but they depict grazing conditions and sheep behavior less realistically than Lydia's kit. Because of the price difference, sales of those outside kits have severely reduced sales of Lydia's in-state kit.

The state legislature recently enacted a law "to protect the children of this state from faulty animal science and to protect in-state learning-kit makers from unfair competition." The law forbids the sale in the state of any children's learning kit that purports to represent shepherding or sheep care and does not satisfy specified animal-science criteria. Lydia's kit satisfies all of the criteria; none of the competing outside kits satisfy them.

Is the state law constitutional?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

D is correct. The law does not merely set a neutral safety rule with incidental effects. It blocks all competing out-of-state kits from being sold in the state while the in-state kit satisfies the criteria, and the statute itself names protection of in-state makers from unfair competition as one of its purposes. That is the red flag under the Dormant Commerce Clause. A state may pursue a real child-protection or product-accuracy interest, but it may not protect local businesses by excluding safe out-of-state products when reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives are available. **Gold Key:** a protectionist state law that benefits in-state economic interests and burdens out-of-state competitors is invalid unless the state shows that a legitimate local purpose cannot be served by reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives. **Silver Key:** when the stem gives both a public-interest purpose and an in-state-protection purpose, do not stop at good motive; compare who is helped and who is blocked.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A.** The local-sales answer is attractive because the law applies only to stores inside the state, and local sales can sound like a local matter. The breaker is that the law controls access to the in-state market for out-of-state goods. ## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification AND Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. The governing doctrine is the Dormant Commerce Clause, not a Tenth Amendment anti-commandeering rule. The source row listed `41030401`, but the deepest matching local outline node is `41030402`, "Dormant Commerce Clause," and that code appears verbatim in `OUTLINE_CODES_COMPLETE.md`.

The Commerce Clause gives Congress power to regulate commerce among the states. U.S. Const. art. I, section 8, clause 3. The Supreme Court's Dormant Commerce Clause doctrine limits state laws that discriminate against interstate commerce or unduly burden it even when Congress has not acted. The Constitution Annotated overview states the modern two-principle frame: states may not discriminate against interstate commerce, and facially neutral actions may not unduly burden it. Source: https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/artI-S8-C3-7-1/ALDE_00013307/

For discriminatory burdens, the governing cases are stricter than ordinary rational-basis review. In *Oregon Waste Systems, Inc. v. Department of Environmental Quality of Oregon*, 511 U.S. 93 (1994), the Court treated discrimination favoring in-state economic interests over out-of-state interests as virtually per se invalid and required a legitimate local purpose that could not be served by reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/511/93/ The same core rule appears in *Maine v. Taylor*, 477 U.S. 131 (1986), and the nondiscriminatory-burden baseline comes from *Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc.*, 397 U.S. 137 (1970). Sources: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/477/131/ and https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/397/137/

Those authorities confirm the official key. The state has a legitimate interest in protecting children from faulty animal-science claims, but the law's effect and stated purpose protect in-state makers by excluding competing out-of-state goods from the local market. The outside goods are safe and durable; the state could pursue accuracy through labeling, disclosure, neutral standards, grading, or enforcement rules that do not functionally clear the market for the local producer. Because the law imposes a protectionist burden on interstate commerce and reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives are available, the state law is unconstitutional. The credited answer remains "No, because the law imposes an undue burden on interstate commerce."
