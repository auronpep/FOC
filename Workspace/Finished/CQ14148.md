## 1. Final Question

Barnabas owns a private vineyard estate and regularly directs invited guests to a neighboring farmer's adjacent meadow — land Barnabas neither owns nor leases, but has historically used for overflow gatherings. During one such gathering, Hannah, a guest Barnabas had personally directed to the meadow, was assaulted by third parties. Hannah's attorney filed a negligence complaint alleging that Barnabas owed her a duty of care as an invitee on his "premises" and had failed to warn of the dangers posed by third parties in the area. At the time the complaint was filed, it was an unresolved question under applicable law whether a landowner's "premises" could extend to adjacent land the landowner did not own but regularly directed invitees onto. Barnabas moved to dismiss the complaint for failure to state a claim, contending the meadow was not his "premises" and that he owed Hannah no duty. Along with the motion to dismiss, Barnabas also moved for sanctions, asserting that the legal theory was not warranted by existing law or by any nonfrivolous argument for extending it.

Should the court grant the motion for sanctions?

## 7. Right-Answer Explanation

**A is correct.**

**Gold Key:** Under FRCP 11(c)(2), a motion for sanctions must be made separately from any other motion, served on the opposing party, and may not be filed with the court until 21 days after service — the "safe harbor" period during which the opposing party may withdraw or correct the challenged paper. Here, Barnabas filed the sanctions motion simultaneously with the motion to dismiss, not separately. Hannah's attorney was never separately served and had no opportunity to withdraw or correct the complaint before the sanctions motion was presented to the court. Both procedural prerequisites were violated, and the motion must be denied.

**Silver Key:** When two "No" answers both survive elimination, lock the reason: which denial ground tracks what FRCP 11(c)(2) actually requires? Choice C says "No" because Barnabas never challenged the factual accuracy of Hannah's complaint — but factual accuracy is not a prerequisite for this denial, and its absence doesn't drive the outcome. A gives the enforceable procedural basis: the motion was not separate and no safe harbor ran.

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice B** (dominant trap — "complaint didn't make its legal basis apparent"):

Rule 11 requires that a signing attorney certify the legal contention is warranted by existing law or a nonfrivolous argument — but that certification is implicit in the signature; the complaint does not need to affirmatively display its legal basis on its face. B invents a showing requirement that Rule 11 does not impose on the pleader. **Gold Key:** FRCP 11(c)(2)'s procedural prerequisites — not Rule 11(b)'s substantive certification test — control whether a sanctions motion is proper at all. B has the wrong provision and the wrong question.

**Choice C** (wrong-element "No"):

**Silver Key:** A student who correctly concludes "No" but selects C is assigning the denial to the wrong legal ground. C notes that Barnabas never alleged Hannah's factual statements were inaccurate — true, but irrelevant. FRCP 11(c)(2) requires denial because of how the motion was filed (not separately, no safe harbor), not because of what the motion alleged or failed to allege about factual accuracy.

**Choice D** (flat misstatement):

D states Hannah had no good-faith basis under any existing law at the time of filing. But the stem explicitly says the legal question was "unresolved" — it was an open question whether Barnabas's "premises" extended to the adjacent meadow. D converts an uncertain legal question into a definitive finding of no legal basis, flatly contradicting the stem.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Governing rule:** Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, Rule 11(c)(2).

FRCP 11(c)(2) provides: "A motion for sanctions must be made separately from any other motion and must describe the specific conduct that allegedly violates Rule 11(b). The motion must be served under Rule 5, but it must not be filed or be presented to the court if the challenged paper, claim, defense, contention, or denial is withdrawn or appropriately corrected within 21 days after service or within another time the court sets."

Two mandatory requirements: (1) the sanctions motion must be a separate filing, not bundled with any other motion; (2) the movant must serve the motion on the opposing party and allow a 21-day safe harbor before filing. Courts hold these are non-waivable prerequisites — failure to comply requires denial regardless of whether the underlying legal theory is or is not warranted. *See, e.g.,* Hadges v. Yonkers Racing Corp., 48 F.3d 1320, 1328 (2d Cir. 1995); Nisenbaum v. Milwaukee County, 333 F.3d 804 (7th Cir. 2003).

The tested rule (FRCP 11(c)(2) separate-motion + safe-harbor requirements), the legal issue (whether a Rule 11 sanctions motion filed simultaneously with a motion to dismiss satisfies Rule 11's procedural prerequisites), and the credited outcome (motion denied) are unchanged from the original item.
