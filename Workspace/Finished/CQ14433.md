### 1. Final Question
Simon Peter said to his brother Andrew, "Because you have worked so hard beside me on the lake, I am going to give you my fishing boat." Andrew replied, "Thank you for the boat. In return, I am going to give you my best fishing net." The boat was worth 500 denarii; the net was worth 5 denarii. Andrew delivered the net to Simon Peter, but Simon Peter refused to deliver the boat. If Andrew sues Simon Peter to recover the value of the boat, how should the court rule?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
Does a promise made as a gift, without seeking a return promise or performance, create an enforceable contract when the promisee delivers something of unequal value in response?

### 6. Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)
**C: For Simon Peter, because there was no bargained-for exchange to support his promise.**

Gold Key: **GK-CONTRACTS-BARGAINED-EXCHANGE-01** - "Consideration requires that a return promise or performance be sought by the promisor in exchange for his promise and given by the promisee in exchange for that promise; a promise made as a gift, without seeking a return, is not enforceable as a contract."

Simon Peter's promise to give Andrew the fishing boat was made as a gift ("Because you have worked so hard..."), not as part of a bargained-for exchange. He did not seek Andrew's promise of the fishing net in exchange for his promise of the boat. Since there was no bargain—the promise was not given in exchange for a requested return promise or performance—there is no consideration, and thus no enforceable contract. Andrew's delivery of the net does not retroactively create consideration for Simon Peter's prior gift promise.

### 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A: For Andrew, because he conferred a benefit on Simon Peter by delivering the fishing net.**

This choice is attractive because it appeals to moral common sense: Andrew did give Simon Peter something of value. However, consideration is not merely about conferring a benefit. The benefit must be given *in a bargained-for exchange*. Andrew's delivery of the net was not sought by Simon Peter as the price for his promise of the boat. 

* breaker: The benefit was not bargained for.

**B: For Simon Peter, because the consideration given for his promise was inadequate.**

This choice mistakes a non-issue for the real issue. Under contract law, courts generally do not inquire into the adequacy of consideration. Even if the net (5 denarii) was objectively inadequate compared to the boat (500 denarii), inadequacy alone is not a defense to contract formation. Simon Peter cannot avoid his promise on this ground.

* breaker: Adequacy of consideration is irrelevant; the issue is whether there was a bargained-for exchange at all.

**D: For Andrew, because he gave the fishing net to Simon Peter in reliance on receiving the boat.**

Silver Key: **SK-CONTRACTS-RELIANCE-TRAP-01** - "When a promise is made as a gift without a bargained exchange, promissory estoppel does not apply absent clear, foreseeable reliance that the promisor should have expected to induce."

This choice is attractive because it invokes the doctrine of promissory estoppel, which can sometimes enforce promises without consideration. However, promissory estoppel requires that the promise was reasonably expected to induce definite and substantial action or forbearance, and that such action was indeed induced. Simon Peter's gift promise did not seek Andrew's reliance, and the facts do not show the type of definite, foreseeable reliance that would trigger promissory estoppel. The dispositive issue remains the lack of bargained-for consideration.

* breaker: Promissory estoppel does not apply here because Simon Peter's promise was not made to induce Andrew's action, and the reliance was not of the foreseeable type that would justify enforcing the promise.
* **Gold Key:** GK-CONTRACTS-BARGAINED-EXCHANGE-01 defeats this trap.

### 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 71 - "Requirement of Exchange; Types of Exchange"

> (1) To constitute consideration, a performance or return promise must be bargained for.
> (2) A performance or return promise is bargained for if it is sought by the promisor in exchange for his promise and is given by the promisee in exchange for that promise.

**Legal Reasoning:** Simon Peter's promise was unilateral and gratuitous—he promised the boat "because you have worked so hard," which is the language of a gift, not a bargain. He did not condition his promise on Andrew's promise or performance. Andrew's promise to give the net was not sought by Simon Peter as the price for the boat. Therefore, there was no bargained-for exchange, and Simon Peter's promise is unenforceable for lack of consideration.

**Verification of Outcome:** This matches the original question's credited outcome (A: For the man, because there was no bargained-for exchange). The legal issue (bargained-for exchange requirement) and the credited answer's legal substance are **UNCHANGED**.

**Authority:** 
- Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 71
- *Batsakis v. Demotsis*, 226 S.W.2d 673 (Tex. 1950) ("A promise of gift, made without consideration, is not enforceable.")
- *Fiege v. Boehm*, 210 A.2d 242 (Md. 1965) (promises made as gifts are not contracts)
