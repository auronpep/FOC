### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam register)

**The court should rule the prior conviction admissible as evidence of a distinctive method of operation (post-shuffle A).** The general rule under FRE 404(b)(1) bars evidence of a person's character offered to prove conduct in conformity with that character — i.e., propensity. But FRE 404(b)(2) carves out a list of non-propensity uses for which prior-acts evidence is admissible, including **identity**. To come in for identity, the prior act must show a **distinctive modus operandi** — a method so particular that its reappearance acts as a signature identifying the same hand.

Here, the prior conviction shares not just the same category of crime (heroin importation) but the *same specific signature*: a small olive-wood crucifix pendant from Jerusalem with a hollowed cross chamber used to conceal heroin, on the same Newark-from-Tel Aviv route. That is a definite, particular, and strong inference that the same person committed the precise act charged. The defense argument is that this is propensity evidence dressed in a thin disguise, but the disguise is not thin — the prior act's distinctive features (the pendant type, the hollowed-chamber location, the foil-wrapped brick) are *unusual* in the universe of heroin-importation cases. The MO exception, drawn from the FRE 404(b)(2) identity category, is the framework the prosecution is invoking. Although a real court would still conduct FRE 403 balancing and, on request, deliver a FRE 105 limiting instruction, the answer-architecture question is doctrinal: of the four answer frames, only the MO frame survives.

**Gold Key:** Under FRE 404(b)(2), a single prior act that bears a *distinctive* method (not just the same *category* of crime) can come in to prove identity. Distinctiveness — not numerosity — is the test.

**Silver Key:** Three of the four answers describe different doctrinal categories (habit under FRE 406, the categorical propensity ban, and the FRE 609(b) 10-year impeachment rule). Only the distinctive-MO answer addresses the precise way the prosecution is actually offering the prior. The right answer is whichever frame *matches the offer* — and the prosecution is offering the prior for identity, not for impeachment or habit.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations (one per distractor)

**Post-shuffle B — "inadmissible, because the prior conviction occurred more than 10 years before the trial."**
What makes it attractive: students remember FRE 609(b) — the 10-year rule on impeachment by prior conviction — and see "12 years ago" on the face of the stem. The 10-year number is the right number; the prior is even more remote than 10 years; this *feels* textbook-correct. The breaker: FRE 609(b) is an impeachment rule. It governs using a prior conviction to attack a *witness's credibility*. The prosecution here is not attacking Daniel's credibility — it is offering the prior substantively, to prove identity through a distinctive method. The 10-year rule is in the wrong doctrine. (The "bait_doctrine" mold: right area + true rule, but the rule is for impeachment, not substantive use.) **Silver Key:** Ask "what purpose is the prosecution offering the prior for?" If the answer is "to prove identity via a distinctive MO" — the 10-year impeachment rule is the wrong rule.

**Post-shuffle C — "inadmissible, because evidence of previous conduct by a defendant may not be used against him or her." [DOMINANT TRAP]**
What makes it attractive: the "no prior bad acts" rule is the headline FRE 404(a) / 404(b)(1) principle every student memorizes first. The instinct is "prior smuggling conviction = propensity = inadmissible, end of analysis." It is the *most common student myth* on this archetype. The breaker: the rule is *not* categorical. FRE 404(b)(2) lists non-propensity purposes for which prior-acts evidence is admissible — motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, **knowledge, identity, absence of mistake, lack of accident**. The MIMIC framework. The prosecution here is not offering the prior for propensity; it is offering it for identity, where the prior act's distinctive features make it a signature, not a tendency. The "tiered_absolute" mold: a never/always framing where the rule has well-defined exceptions. **Gold Key:** "May not be used to *prove character*" is not the same as "may never be used." The MIMIC exceptions are the entire reason 404(b) is divided into (b)(1) and (b)(2).

**Post-shuffle D — "admissible, as evidence of habit."**
What makes it attractive: FRE 406 (habit; routine practice) lets in evidence of a person's habit to prove conduct in conformity with the habit on a particular occasion. Smuggling-into-the-U.S. *feels* like a routine practice. The "he did it once before, so it's his habit" move is intuitive. The breaker: FRE 406 requires a *regular, semi-automatic pattern* of conduct — enough repetitions to make the behavior predictable in response to a recurring situation. A single prior + the current act is not a pattern; it is two events. One prior is not a habit. The "bait_doctrine" mold: the right rule is in play, but the *variant* of the rule (one prior = habit) is wrong. Worse, this answer misses the cleaner non-propensity path — distinctive MO under FRE 404(b)(2) — and applies the wrong doctrinal frame. **Gold Key:** Habit (FRE 406) requires a regular, repeated pattern. Distinctive MO (FRE 404(b)(2) identity) requires only a *signature* — distinctiveness, not numerosity. One prior is not a habit, but it can be a signature.

### 9. Full Black-Letter Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

**Governing rule.** Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b)(1) bars evidence of a crime, wrong, or other act "to prove a person's character in order to show that on a particular occasion the person acted in accordance with the character." Rule 404(b)(2) provides that such evidence "may be admissible for other purposes, such as proving motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, **identity**, absence of mistake, or lack of accident." The Supreme Court's controlling four-part test for admitting Rule 404(b) evidence is *Huddleston v. United States*, 485 U.S. 681 (1988): (1) proper non-propensity purpose, (2) relevance to that purpose, (3) probative value not substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice under FRE 403, and (4) limiting instruction on request under FRE 105.

**Application to the credited answer (post-shuffle A; original B).** The prior conviction shares not only the same crime (heroin importation) but the *same distinctive signature*: a small olive-wood crucifix pendant from Jerusalem, a hollowed cross chamber at the base, a foil-wrapped brick of heroin, on the same Newark-from-Tel Aviv route, twelve years earlier. That is precisely the kind of "particular, unusual, and distinctive" method that supports a Rule 404(b)(2) identity inference. The brass-statue/false-bottom analogy in the source row is on all fours: the prior is being offered to show that the same *unusual method* identifies Daniel as the actor, not to argue that Daniel is the kind of person who smuggles heroin. The defendant is free to argue FRE 403 unfair prejudice (and the remoteness of twelve years) and to demand a FRE 105 limiting instruction — those are real-world admissibility challenges that do not affect the MBE answer.

### wrong_answer_recovery_paths
- **Post-shuffle B (10-year rule) — recovery path:** When a stem gives you a "10-year" number and a prior conviction, do not stop at "10 years = barred." Ask: *what purpose is the prior being offered for?* If the offer is for *impeachment* under FRE 609, the 10-year rule applies (subject to the "interest of justice" escape hatch). If the offer is *substantive* (e.g., FRE 404(b) identity / MO), the 10-year rule is in the wrong lane — FRE 403 remoteness may be a real-world argument, but the doctrinal bar is not FRE 609(b).
- **Post-shuffle C (categorical ban) — recovery path [DOMINANT TRAP]:** When a choice states "previous conduct may not be used against the defendant" as a flat absolute, recognize the *tiered_absolute* mold. The actual rule is layered: FRE 404(b)(1) bars *propensity* use, but FRE 404(b)(2) carves out non-propensity uses (MIMIC). Identify the prosecution's stated offer — is it for propensity, or for one of the MIMIC purposes? If the latter, the categorical bar is overbroad.
- **Post-shuffle D (habit) — recovery path:** When a choice frames a prior-acts offer as "habit" (FRE 406), ask: *is there a regular pattern?* One prior + one current act is two data points, not a pattern. Habit requires *numerosity*. If the pattern is thin, the right frame is usually *distinctive MO / identity* under FRE 404(b)(2), where *distinctiveness* — not numerosity — is the test.
