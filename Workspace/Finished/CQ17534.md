## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Lydia believes it is a felony to import a box of paper Scripture bookmarks without a special federal devotional-goods license. In reality, no law restricts importing those bookmarks. Lydia orders the bookmarks from overseas, intending to violate what she thinks is the licensing law. Customs agents intercept the package and charge her with attempted illegal importation. What is Lydia's best defense?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

Can a person be guilty of attempt when she intended to complete conduct that would not be a crime?

### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

D is the best defense because the law does not criminalize the completed conduct Lydia intended. She meant to break a licensing law, but there was no licensing law for the paper bookmarks. Gold Key: pure legal impossibility defeats attempt when the actor's completed intended conduct would not be a crime, even though the actor believed it was unlawful. Silver Key: test the completed act first; if completion would still be lawful, intent alone does not supply the missing crime.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because interception sounds like the fact that turned success into failure. The breaker is that delivery would not have made the conduct criminal; the package fact does not answer the legal-impossibility problem.

B sells a concrete shipping distinction, and the stem does involve an overseas package. The breaker is that the shipping method is irrelevant when the supposed federal offense does not exist.

C is the dominant trap because Lydia's criminal purpose is obvious. Gold Key: attempt requires intent to commit an actual crime, so a mistaken belief that lawful conduct is a felony does not create the missing offense.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The credited answer remains unchanged in substance. Attempt liability requires intent to commit a target crime plus conduct beyond preparation. Pure legal impossibility is a defense under the traditional bar-exam formulation: when the actor's completed intended conduct would not be criminal, the actor's belief that it is criminal does not create attempt liability. That differs from factual impossibility, where the target crime exists but an unknown factual circumstance prevents completion.

Authority floor: People v. Jaffe, 185 N.Y. 497 (1906), treated attempted receipt of stolen property as legally impossible where the goods had lost their stolen character; the court explained that if all the accused intended to do would constitute no crime, it cannot be a crime to attempt part of it. United States v. Oviedo, 525 F.2d 881 (5th Cir. 1976), likewise discussed the traditional rule recognizing legal impossibility while rejecting conviction based on intent alone where the objective acts did not mark criminal conduct. Source checked: https://www.nycourts.gov/reporter/archives/p_jaffe.htm and https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/525/881/169895/. I also checked Cornell Wex's impossibility overview for the legal/factual impossibility distinction: https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/impossibility.
