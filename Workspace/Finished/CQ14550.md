### 5. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct.** Timothy is an incidental beneficiary of the contract between Peter and Lydia. The contract requires Lydia to set up and maintain a tent system that will benefit retreat attendees generally, but nothing in the facts indicates that Peter (the promisee) intended to confer a direct, enforceable right on Timothy or any other individual attendee. Under the Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 302, a third party is an intended beneficiary only if recognition of a right to performance in the beneficiary is appropriate to effectuate the intention of the parties, and either (a) the performance will satisfy an obligation of the promisee to pay money to the beneficiary, or (b) the circumstances indicate that the promisee intends to give the beneficiary the benefit of the promised performance. Here, Peter's purpose in hiring Lydia was to fulfill his own obligations to provide a venue — not to create individual enforcement rights in each attendee. Timothy benefits from the contract, but only incidentally. An incidental beneficiary cannot sue to enforce the contract.

**Gold Key:** A third party who merely benefits from contract performance — even foreseeably and directly — is an incidental beneficiary unless the promisee intended to create an enforceable right in that party. Benefit alone does not make a party an intended beneficiary.

### 6. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (Dominant Trap) is attractive but wrong.** This choice sells the most tempting misconception: because the tent service was obviously for the benefit of retreat attendees, each attendee looks like an intended beneficiary. The cognitive move is "the contract was for their benefit, so they can enforce it." The breaker is the distinction between *benefit* and *intent to create an enforceable right*. The contract between Peter and Lydia was for Peter's benefit (to make his venue functional); the attendees' benefit was a foreseeable side effect. Without evidence that Peter intended Timothy to have a direct right of enforcement, Timothy remains an incidental beneficiary. **Gold Key:** Benefit alone ≠ intended beneficiary. The promisee must intend to create an enforceable right in the third party.

**B is attractive but wrong.** This choice reframes the claim as a public-policy matter: Lydia violated the fire-safety code, so she should be liable. The cognitive move is "wrongdoer who broke the code must pay." The breaker is that the call asks about *breach of contract* liability, not tort or regulatory liability. Even if Lydia violated the code, Timothy has no contract claim because he lacks third-party beneficiary status. Public policy does not create a contract enforcement right in a stranger to the contract.

**C is attractive but wrong.** This choice invents a naming requirement: Timothy cannot sue because he was not named in the contract. The cognitive move is "if you're not in the contract, you can't sue on it." The breaker is that an intended beneficiary does NOT need to be named in the contract for enforcement rights to vest. Naming is one way to establish intent to benefit, but it is not required. This choice states the rule backwards and would deny enforcement even to parties who are clearly intended beneficiaries.

### 7. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 302 (1979) governs the distinction between intended and incidental third-party beneficiaries.

Under § 302(1), a beneficiary is an *intended* beneficiary if:
- Recognition of a right to performance in the beneficiary is appropriate to effectuate the intention of the parties, **and**
- Either (a) the performance will satisfy an obligation of the promisee to pay money to the beneficiary, or (b) the circumstances indicate that the promisee intends to give the beneficiary the benefit of the promised performance.

Under § 302(2), an *incidental* beneficiary is a beneficiary who is not an intended beneficiary. An incidental beneficiary cannot enforce the contract.

**Application:** The contract between Peter and Lydia is for tent services at Peter's pavilion. Timothy, a retreat attendee, benefits from the tent but there is no indication that Peter intended to give Timothy an enforceable right against Lydia. Neither § 302(1)(a) nor (b) is satisfied. Timothy is an incidental beneficiary and cannot sue for breach of contract.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 302 (1979). See also *Harris v. Waikane Corp.*, 484 F. Supp. 372, 379 (D. Haw. 1980) (intent to benefit a class requires that performance run to the class, not merely that the class benefits incidentally).

**Verification:** The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original. The official key is confirmed.
