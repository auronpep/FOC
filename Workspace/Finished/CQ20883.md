### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**D is correct** because Barnabas's notice promised to pay $300 to any person who performed a specific act — delivering a written soil amendment plan by July 1. A unilateral contract is a promise exchanged for performance, not a promise exchanged for a promise. Barnabas did not ask anyone to promise to prepare a plan; he asked for the completed plan itself. Acceptance occurs by delivering the plan, not by sending a memo promising to do so.

**Gold Key:** A reward or prize offer that calls for a completed performance (not a promise to perform) is an offer for a unilateral contract. The offeree accepts by completing the act, not by replying "I accept."

**Silver Key:** When the call asks what the notice "constituted," lock the call to the moment the notice was posted — before anyone acted. The question is about the character of the offer itself, not what happened after.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original B) — an offer for a bilateral contract.**
This is attractive because most contracts students encounter are bilateral — an exchange of promises. The student sees a notice, sees someone "accepting," and defaults to the familiar bilateral frame. The breaker: a bilateral contract requires an exchange of promises, each serving as consideration for the other. Barnabas's notice asked for a completed plan, not a promise to prepare one. No employee was asked to promise anything. The offer was perform-to-accept, which is the hallmark of a unilateral contract. **Gold Key:** A reward offer that asks for performance, not a promise, is unilateral — not bilateral.

**B (original C) — a unilateral contract that ripened into a bilateral contract when Timothy wrote the note.**
This is attractive because Timothy did in fact write and send a memo accepting the offer, and the student wants to treat that memo as a meaningful contractual event. The breaker: a unilateral offer is accepted by performance, not by a promise. Timothy's memo was a promise to perform — which is not what Barnabas asked for. A unilateral offer cannot "ripen" into a bilateral contract merely because the offeree sends a note promising to act. The offeror never requested a promise, and the offeree's unsolicited promise does not change the character of the offer. **Gold Key:** Acceptance of a unilateral offer requires completing the requested act — a promise to perform is not acceptance.

**C (original D) — a preliminary invitation to deal.**
This is attractive because the notice was posted publicly and directed at no one in particular, which feels like an advertisement. The breaker: the key difference between an offer and an invitation to negotiate is that an offer creates an immediate power of acceptance in the offeree. Barnabas's notice was definite in its terms ($300, a written plan, a July 1 deadline, a selection criterion). Any person could accept by delivering a qualifying plan. That is an offer — not an invitation to negotiate. **Silver Key:** Definiteness of terms + the power to accept by acting = an offer, even if directed to the general public.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Rule:** A unilateral contract is formed when one party makes a promise in exchange for the performance of a specified act by the other party. Acceptance occurs through complete performance of the act, not through a promise to perform. *See* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 30 (defining unilateral contracts); *see also* Cornell Law School Legal Information Institute, *Unilateral Contract*, https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/unilateral_contract ("A unilateral contract is a contract in which one party makes a promise in exchange for the performance of a specified act by the other party").

**Application:** Barnabas promised $300 to "any person who delivers" a written soil amendment plan before July 1. The notice called for a completed deliverable — a written plan — not a promise to prepare one. Timothy's memo saying "I am already working on it" was a promise to perform, which is not the acceptance Barnabas requested. The notice was therefore an offer for a unilateral contract.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 30, 45 (once performance begins, the offer becomes irrevocable, but the contract is still accepted by completed performance, not by the promise to perform). The classic reward cases (*e.g.*, *Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.* [1893] 1 QB 256) illustrate the same principle: a public reward offer is a unilateral contract accepted by performing the requested act.

**Confirmed:** The tested rule (unilateral vs. bilateral contract classification), the legal issue (character of a reward-type offer), and the credited outcome (unilateral contract) are unchanged from the original.

#### Distilled Core Question

A person posts a public notice offering to pay money to anyone who performs a specified act. A second person begins working and sends a note of acceptance that never arrives. Was the original notice an offer for a unilateral contract?

#### Call and Prediction

**Call:** "The notice … constituted" — this asks the student to classify the legal character of the notice at the moment it was posted.

**Prediction:** The student who recognizes that the notice promised money in exchange for a completed act (delivering a plan) will identify it as a unilateral contract (D). 2. **Timothy wrote a note saying "I accept" and gave it to a messenger** — This creates the illusion of bilateral acceptance, but a unilateral offer is accepted by performance, not by a promise.
3. **The note never arrived** — Eliminates any argument that the note formed a contract or changed the offer's character.
4. **Definite terms: $300, written plan, July 1, lowest cost** — Distinguishes the notice from a preliminary invitation to negotiate.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "an offer for a bilateral contract."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The notice asked for a completed plan, not a promise to prepare one. Barnabas did not ask anyone to promise anything — he asked for the plan itself.
2. **Student label:** Bilateral contract.
3. **What a true-and-responsive version would look like:** "An offer for a bilateral contract is an exchange of promises. If Barnabas had asked employees to promise to prepare a plan by a deadline, and they had promised, that would be bilateral. But he asked for the plan itself — performance, not a promise."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** A bilateral contract requires mutual promises, each serving as consideration for the other. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 3. Barnabas's notice requested performance (delivering a plan), not a promise. **Gold Key:** A reward offer that asks for performance, not a promise, is unilateral — not bilateral.

**Choice B: "an offer for a unilateral contract that ripened into a bilateral contract when Timothy wrote the note."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Timothy's memo was a promise to perform, not performance itself. A unilateral offer is accepted by completing the act, not by sending a note saying "I accept."
2. **Student label:** Ripened into bilateral.
3. **What a true-and-responsive version would look like:** "If Timothy had delivered the completed plan, that would have been acceptance by performance — the unilateral contract would have been fulfilled. But a promise to perform is not acceptance of a unilateral offer."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** A unilateral offer cannot be transformed into a bilateral contract by an unsolicited promise to perform. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 45. The offeror is bound to keep the offer open once performance begins, but the contract is still accepted by completed performance.

**Choice C: "a preliminary invitation to deal."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The notice had definite terms ($300, written plan, July 1, lowest cost) and any person could accept by delivering a plan. That is an offer, not an invitation to negotiate.
2. **Student label:** Preliminary invitation.
3. **What a true-and-responsive version would look like:** "A preliminary invitation to deal lacks definite terms and does not create a power of acceptance. Barnabas's notice was specific about what was required, the deadline, and the selection criterion. Any person could accept by performing."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** An offer creates an immediate power of acceptance in the offeree. The definiteness of terms distinguishes an offer from an invitation to negotiate. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 26.

**Choice D: "an offer for a unilateral contract."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Barnabas promised $300 in exchange for a completed act (delivering a written plan). Promise for performance = unilateral contract.
2. **Student label:** Unilateral contract.
3. **What a true-and-responsive version would look like:** This is the correct answer. A unilateral contract is a promise exchanged for performance. Barnabas promised money; the offeree accepts by delivering the plan.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** A unilateral contract is formed when one party makes a promise in exchange for the performance of a specified act. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 30. The classic example is a reward offer — exactly what Barnabas posted.
