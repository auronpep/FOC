### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** A conspirator is vicariously liable for substantive crimes committed by a co-conspirator in furtherance of the conspiracy — even if the act was not specifically agreed upon, was unnecessary to the plan, or was unforeseeable — so long as it was done to advance the shared criminal objective.

Timothy and Lydia agreed to steal a tractor and sell it. That agreement established a conspiracy. When Lydia drove the tractor across the state line to escape what she believed was a police car, she did so to advance their shared criminal objective — getting away with the stolen property. Under the Pinkerton doctrine, the act of one conspirator in furtherance of the conspiracy is attributable to all conspirators. Timothy does not need to have foreseen the interstate crossing, does not need to have specifically agreed to it, and the transportation does not need to have been necessary to the theft's success. The only requirement is that the act was done in furtherance of the conspiracy, and fleeing with stolen goods to avoid arrest is plainly in furtherance of the agreed-upon theft. Therefore, Timothy is guilty of violating the National Stolen Property Act.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### 8. Black-Letter Legal Verification

**Governing Rule:** Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640 (1946). The Supreme Court held that a conspirator may be held liable for substantive crimes committed by a co-conspirator in furtherance of the conspiracy, even if the defendant did not personally participate in or direct the substantive offense. The Court stated: "The criminal intent to do an illegal act by one of the conspirators in furtherance of the unlawful project is established by the formation of the conspiracy. Each conspirator instigated the commission of the crime. The unlawful agreement contemplated what was done." *Id.* at 647-48.

The Court specifically noted that a different case would arise if the substantive offense "was not in fact done in furtherance of the conspiracy, did not fall within the scope of the unlawful project, or was merely a part of the ramifications of the plan which could not be reasonably foreseen as a necessary or natural consequence of the unlawful agreement." *Id.* at 648. But so long as the act was in furtherance of the conspiracy, liability attaches.

**Application to this variant:** Lydia's act of driving the tractor across the state line to evade law enforcement was done in furtherance of the agreed-upon theft — it was the natural and expected means of escaping with stolen goods. Under Pinkerton, Timothy is liable for that substantive offense regardless of whether he foresaw it, specifically agreed to it, or it was necessary to the theft's success.

**Credited outcome unchanged:** The correct answer remains "Yes" — the defendant is guilty. The legal substance, rule, and outcome are identical to the original.

**Authority:** Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640 (1946); see also 18 U.S.C. § 371 (general conspiracy statute); United States v. Perez, 489 F.3d 51 (2d Cir. 2007) (applying Pinkerton liability for substantive offenses committed in furtherance of conspiracy).
