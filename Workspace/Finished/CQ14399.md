### 1. Final Question

Peter, a worship leader at a church, agreed to buy a two-year-old male breeding ram from Daniel, a fellow church member and sheep farmer, for $1,200. No express warranty was made about the ram's fertility. Eighteen months later, a veterinarian confirmed that the ram had been born with a congenital defect rendering it permanently sterile — a condition that would have limited its value to roughly $150. Both Peter and Daniel stipulate that they were aware, at the time of the sale, that the earliest a ram's fertility can be reliably tested is at about 14 months of age. Peter sues Daniel to rescind the contract and recover the purchase price.

Which of the following will the court probably decide?

**A:** Peter wins, because Daniel impliedly warranted that the ram was fit for breeding purposes.

**B:** Peter wins, because both parties were mutually mistaken about the ram's fertility when they made the agreement.

**C:** Daniel wins, because Peter assumed the risk of the ram's sterility by contracting despite knowing the fertility was untestable at the time of sale.

**D:** Daniel wins, because any mistake involved was unilateral, not mutual.

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** When both parties to a contract know that a material fact is unknowable at the time of contracting, neither is "mistaken" — they are operating under uncertainty. A party who proceeds with a contract despite conscious ignorance of a material fact has assumed the risk of that uncertainty and cannot later avoid the contract on the ground of mutual mistake. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 154(a).

Here, both Peter and Daniel knew the ram's fertility could not be tested until about 14 months of age. Peter bought the ram anyway, at a price reflecting a breeding-age male. The fact that the ram turned out to be congenitally sterile was a risk Peter took on by contracting with conscious ignorance. Because Peter bore the risk of the mistake, the mutual-mistake defense under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 152 is unavailable to him. Daniel wins.

---

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (Peter wins — implied warranty):** This choice is attractive because the UCC does imply a warranty of fitness for a particular purpose under § 2-315 when the seller knows the buyer's particular purpose and the buyer relies on the seller's expertise. But here, both parties knew the fertility was untestable — there was no reliance on Daniel's special knowledge about this specific ram's fertility. No implied warranty of fitness arises when the buyer knows the relevant fact is unknown. **Gold Key:** An implied warranty of fitness requires the buyer to rely on the seller's expertise; reliance is impossible when both parties know the fact is unknowable.

**B (Peter wins — mutual mistake) — DOMINANT TRAP:** This is the most attractive wrong answer because the facts do involve a shared false assumption (both assumed the ram was fertile). But the critical distinction is that mutual mistake requires a mistaken belief about an existing fact — not a conscious gamble on an unknown outcome. Both parties knew fertility was untestable; they didn't share a mistaken belief, they shared uncertainty. By contracting anyway, Peter assumed the risk. **Gold Key:** Conscious ignorance of a material fact is not "mistake" — it is assumption of risk, which bars the mutual-mistake defense.

**D (Daniel wins — unilateral mistake):** This choice reaches the correct party but for the wrong reason. It is attractive because it correctly identifies that the contract should not be rescinded. However, the problem was not a unilateral mistake — both parties were in the same position regarding the ram's fertility. The real reason Daniel wins is that Peter assumed the risk of the uncertainty, not that the mistake was unilateral. **Silver Key:** When a wrong answer reaches the right outcome but misidentifies the legal reason, check whether the stated doctrine actually fits the facts — a doctrine that doesn't apply cannot be the right reason even if the conclusion is correct.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule — Mutual Mistake and Assumption of Risk:**

Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 152 provides that a contract is voidable by the adversely affected party when both parties were mistaken about a basic assumption on which the contract was made, the mistake has a material effect on the agreed exchange, **and** the adversely affected party does not bear the risk of the mistake.

Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 154 allocates risk when a party (a) knows they lack knowledge of a relevant fact but treats their limited knowledge as sufficient (conscious ignorance), (b) the contract allocates the risk, or (c) the court allocates the risk as reasonable.

**Application:**

Both Peter and Daniel knew the ram was too young for fertility testing. Neither had a mistaken belief — they had a hope or expectation. Peter, by contracting with full knowledge that fertility was unknowable, assumed the risk under § 154(a) (conscious ignorance). The mutual-mistake defense fails at the third element: Peter bore the risk.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 152, 154 (American Law Institute). See also *Sherwood v. Walker*, 66 Mich. 568, 33 N.W. 919 (1887) (classic mutual-mistake case — distinguished because in *Sherwood* both parties shared an affirmative mistaken belief, not conscious ignorance). LII Wex, "mutual material mistake," Cornell Law School.

**Verification:** The tested rule (mutual mistake + assumption of risk bars rescission), the legal issue (whether the buyer can avoid the contract), and the credited outcome (seller wins) are all unchanged from the original.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "Peter wins, because Daniel impliedly warranted that the ram was fit for breeding purposes."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The stem says both parties knew fertility was untestable. An implied warranty of fitness requires the buyer to rely on the seller's expertise. If both know the fact is unknown, there's no reliance.
2. **Student label:** No implied warranty where both parties knew the fact was unknowable.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Peter wins, because Daniel, as a sheep farmer, knew Peter was relying on Daniel's expertise about this specific ram's fertility, and the ram turned out to be defective." (But that's not what happened — both knew it was untestable.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** UCC § 2-315 requires buyer reliance on the seller's special knowledge. When both parties know a material fact is unknowable, reliance is impossible and no implied warranty of fitness arises.

**Choice B: "Peter wins, because both parties were mutually mistaken about the ram's fertility when they made the agreement."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Both parties *knew* they didn't know the fertility. That's not a mistaken belief — it's conscious ignorance. Mutual mistake requires an actual mistaken belief about an existing fact.
2. **Student label:** This looks like mutual mistake but is actually conscious ignorance with assumed risk.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Peter wins, because both parties genuinely believed the ram was fertile, and that belief was a basic assumption of the contract." (But the facts say they *knew* fertility was untestable — they didn't believe either way.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 154(a) — a party who contracts with conscious ignorance of a material fact bears the risk of that mistake. This bars the § 152 mutual-mistake defense.

**Gold Key — GK-CONTRACTS-MISTAKE-01:** When both parties to a contract know that a material fact is unknowable at the time of contracting, neither is "mistaken" — they are operating under uncertainty. A party who proceeds despite conscious ignorance has assumed the risk and cannot later avoid the contract on the ground of mutual mistake. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 154(a).

**Choice C: "Daniel wins, because Peter assumed the risk of the ram's sterility by contracting despite knowing the fertility was untestable at the time of sale."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Both knew fertility was untestable. Peter proceeded anyway. That's conscious ignorance = assumed risk.
2. **Student label:** Peter assumed the risk; no rescission.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This choice IS true and responsive. It correctly identifies the doctrine (assumed risk) and applies it to the facts (Peter knew fertility was untestable and bought anyway).
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 154(a). Peter's conscious ignorance allocates the risk to him.

**Choice D: "Daniel wins, because any mistake involved was unilateral, not mutual."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Both parties were equally aware of the uncertainty. This wasn't a unilateral-mistake situation — both were in the same boat.
2. **Student label:** Right party, wrong reason. Unilateral mistake doesn't apply here.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** "Daniel wins, because only Peter was mistaken about the ram's fertility, and Daniel had no reason to know of Peter's mistake." (But both parties shared the same knowledge state.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Unilateral mistake (Restatement § 153) requires that only one party be mistaken. Here, both parties were equally aware of the uncertainty, so unilateral mistake is the wrong doctrine — even though Daniel does win.
