## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** Daniel began the confrontation, so he initially lost the ordinary right to claim self-defense. But the stem gives both reset facts: he stepped back and expressly said he was finished and going home. That is withdrawal plus communicated withdrawal. Peter then changed the threat by charging with a metal tire iron. At that point the operative threat was not the earlier shoving match; it was an imminent deadly-force threat. Daniel may claim self-defense because he withdrew, communicated withdrawal, and then faced a deadly escalation.

**Gold Key:** An initial aggressor is not permanently locked out of self-defense. If he withdraws in good faith and clearly communicates the withdrawal, he may regain the defense if the other person continues or escalates the fight.

**Silver Key:** Freeze the call at the moment defensive force is used. Do not answer from the first shove if the stem later gives a withdrawal signal and a new deadly threat.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### Choice D - Dominant Trap

This choice is attractive because Daniel did start the confrontation, and bar students often remember the initial-aggressor rule as a flat disqualification. The word "never" is the tell. **Gold Key:** the initial-aggressor bar has a reset: good-faith withdrawal plus communication can restore the self-defense claim. Daniel did both before Peter charged with a tire iron, so the absolute version of the rule overclaims.

### Choice A

This choice is attractive because a gun seems disproportionate to a shoving match. The breaker is the timeline. Daniel did not fire while answering ordinary shoves; he fired after stepping back, saying he was leaving, and then facing Peter's tire-iron charge. **Silver Key:** answer from the threat at the moment of force, not from the first physical contact in the stem.

### Choice C

This choice is attractive because it reaches the correct "yes" result and keeps the fatality in view. But it gives a made-up reason. Self-defense depends on threat, necessity, proportionality, and aggressor status at the time force is used. It does not turn on the bare fact that the person who died was the other participant.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Tested rule:** An initial aggressor generally loses the privilege of self-defense, but may regain it by withdrawing in good faith and clearly communicating that withdrawal. If the other person then continues the attack or escalates to an imminent deadly threat, defensive force proportionate to that new threat may be justified.

**Authority floor:** *United States v. Peterson*, 483 F.2d 1222 (D.C. Cir. 1973), explains the common-law necessity foundation of deadly self-defense and states that an aggressor is restored to self-defense only by communicating an intent to withdraw and attempting withdrawal in good faith. Model Penal Code Section 3.04(1) also frames defensive force around immediate necessity against unlawful force. Research sources consulted: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/483/1222/155740/ and https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/6141-tanaka-criminal-law-casebook/resources/12.2.1-model-penal-code-mpc-304-use-of-force-in-self-protection/.

**Application:** Daniel's shoving made him the initial aggressor. He then stepped back, raised his hands, and said he was going home. Those facts satisfy withdrawal and communication. Peter's tire-iron charge created an imminent deadly threat. A revolver shot may be proportionate to that deadly threat. The credited answer is therefore doctrinally correct.
