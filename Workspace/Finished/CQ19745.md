## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Ruth bought a countertop soup warmer from a kitchen-supply company to use at a Bible-study supper in her home. While the warmer was heating soup, the lid popped open and hot soup splashed Ruth. Three weeks later, the manufacturer changed the same model by adding a vented locking lid and a warning label about steam pressure. In a products-liability action against the manufacturer, Ruth offers the post-incident changes to prove that the original warmer was defectively designed and should have carried a warning.

How should the court rule?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

Can a plaintiff use a manufacturer's later safety change to prove the original product had a design defect and needed a warning?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. Admit the evidence because Rule 407 bars negligence proof, not products-liability proof.

B. Exclude the evidence only if the manufacturer admits responsibility for Ruth's injury.

C. Exclude the evidence because subsequent remedial measures are not admissible to prove negligence, culpable conduct, a product defect, a design defect, or a need for a warning.

D. Admit the evidence because later safety upgrades always show that the earlier product was defective.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. Ruth is offering the later vented locking lid and warning label for the exact barred inference: that the original soup warmer was defectively designed and should have had a warning. Gold Key: Federal Rule of Evidence 407 covers product cases; it bars later remedial measures when offered to prove negligence, culpable conduct, product defect, design defect, or need for a warning. Silver Key: first ask why the later fix is being offered. Here the purpose is not impeachment, ownership, control, or disputed feasibility; it is the forbidden defect-and-warning proof.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells the dominant product-case myth. It remembers the negligence version of Rule 407 and then leaves product-liability cases outside the rule. The breaker is the Gold Key: Rule 407 expressly includes product defect, design defect, and need for a warning.

B sells a fake admission condition. It makes exclusion depend on the manufacturer admitting responsibility, but the rule does not require any admission of liability before the barred-purpose exclusion applies. The breaker is the offered purpose: Ruth is using the post-incident changes to prove defect and warning need, so the evidence is excluded even without an admission.

D sells raw relevance. Later safety upgrades feel powerful because they make the earlier design look unsafe. The breaker is that Rule 407 blocks that exact hindsight inference; later remedial measures do not become admissible just because they are persuasive.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: under Federal Rule of Evidence 407, when a later measure would have made an earlier injury or harm less likely, evidence of that later measure is not admissible to prove negligence, culpable conduct, a defect in a product or its design, or a need for a warning or instruction. The rule may permit the evidence for another purpose, such as impeachment or, if disputed, ownership, control, or feasibility, but Ruth offers the changes for the barred product-defect and warning purposes.

Authority checked: Fed. R. Evid. 407, current U.S. Code text at https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title28a-node232-article4-rule407. The 1997 advisory note confirms that Rule 407 was amended to cover product defect, design defect, and warning-instruction uses in products-liability actions. The researched authority does not contradict the supplied official key.

Ruth offers the manufacturer's later vented locking lid and warning label to prove that the earlier warmer was defectively designed and should have warned users. That is the forbidden inference, so exclusion is the best ruling.
