## 1. Final Question

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** Under the objective theory of contract formation, whether an offer exists depends on whether a reasonable recipient would understand the communication as a present commitment to be bound. Here, the Rate Summary expressly disclaimed present commitment: it said it was "not a commitment to teach until I confirm my fall schedule and approve the lesson plan." That language is unambiguous. A reasonable person in Lydia's position would understand that Peter was not making an offer — he was inviting further negotiation. Because there was no offer, Lydia's signature could not operate as acceptance, and no contract was formed.

**Gold Key:** *Objective-language controls over subjective intent.* When a document's express words disclaim present commitment, the signer's subjective belief that a contract exists is irrelevant. The objective manifestation — what a reasonable person would understand from the words — governs. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 26 cmt. b.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**D (Original A) — Signature Formalism.** This choice sells the misconception that the act of signing a document is itself sufficient to create a contract, regardless of what the document says. It is attractive because students intuit that signatures carry legal weight — and they do, but only when the document signed is an offer or agreement. Here, the document was not an offer; it was a preliminary summary that expressly disclaimed commitment. Signing a non-offer does not conjure one into existence. **Gold Key:** A signature operates as acceptance only when there is an offer to accept. No offer, no contract — regardless of the signature.

**A (Original B) — UCC/Common-Law Swap.** This is the dominant trap. It reframes the transaction as one involving "goods" and invokes Article 2's gap-filling provisions. It is attractive because students overapply the UCC to any transaction involving tangible items, and a guitar is tangible. But this is a *services* contract — guitar *instruction* — and common law governs. Even under the UCC, the open-terms gap fillers (§ 2-204) apply only when there is a contract to fill gaps in. Here, there is no contract at all because there was no offer. **Gold Key:** The UCC applies to transactions in goods, not services. Instruction is a service. Common law governs formation questions for service contracts.

**C (Original D) — Consideration Adequacy Review.** This choice tempts students into evaluating whether $900 is "enough" for 12 weeks of lessons. It is attractive because students conflate sufficiency of consideration with adequacy of consideration. Courts do not police the adequacy of consideration — a bargain with even nominal consideration is enforceable. The real issue is not whether $900 is enough but whether there was an offer in the first place. **Gold Key:** Courts do not inquire into the adequacy of consideration. The formation question (was there an offer?) precedes the consideration question.

## 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 26 (1981): "A manifestation of willingness to enter into a bargain is not an offer if the person to whom it is addressed knows or has reason to know that the person making it does not intend to conclude a bargain until he has made a further manifestation of assent."

**Application:** The Rate Summary is a classic preliminary negotiation. Its title ("Rate Summary," not "Contract" or "Agreement"), its qualifying language ("estimated fee," "roughly $900"), and its express disclaimer ("not a commitment to teach until I confirm my fall schedule and approve the lesson plan") all objectively communicate that Peter does not presently intend to be bound. Under the objective theory of assent, what matters is how a reasonable recipient would interpret the communication — not what Lydia subjectively hoped or believed. *See also* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 33 (certainty of terms — a manifestation of intention that omits a material term and provides no basis for supplying it is too indefinite to be an offer).

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 26, 33 (1981). The objective theory is the universal standard in American contract law. *See, e.g.*, **Lonergan v. Scolnick**, 129 Cal. App. 2d 179 (1954) (preliminary correspondence with disclaimers is not an offer); **Texaco Inc. v. Pennzoil Co.**, 729 S.W.2d 768 (Tex. App. 1987) (objective manifestations control intent to be bound).

**Verification:** The tested rule (objective theory controls; express disclaimer negates offer), the legal issue (whether a preliminary document constitutes an offer), and the credited outcome (no contract) are all unchanged from the original. Research confirms the official key. **bank_validation_verdict: PASS.**
