# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

During a weekend youth retreat at a mountain Christian conference center, 15-year-old Esther and 15-year-old Paul were expected to remain with their small-group Bible study after the evening session. Esther had brought a water bottle containing a strong homemade relaxation tonic from her family's pantry that included a significant amount of alcohol. Paul initially refused Esther's suggestion that they slip away to a storage building behind the lodge, saying he did not want to break the retreat rules. Esther kept offering him the tonic and saying it would help him relax and enjoy the evening more. After Paul drank most of the bottle and became heavily intoxicated, he agreed to go with her. Once inside the building, Esther began to undress Paul and attempted to have sexual intercourse with him. Because of the intoxication, Paul was physically unable to perform. A statute in the jurisdiction provides that “A person is guilty of rape in the third degree when, being 17 years of age or more, he or she engages in sexual intercourse with a person under the age of 16 years.” If Esther is charged with attempting to commit rape in the third degree as a result of her attempt to have intercourse with Paul, she should be found

A: guilty, because Paul was under the legal age of consent.

B: not guilty, because Esther was a female.

C: guilty, because she overcame his resistance by the use of an intoxicating substance and would have completed the act of intercourse but for Paul's physical inability to perform.

D: not guilty, because Esther was under the age of 17.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Esther should be found not guilty because she was only 15 years old. The statute defines third-degree rape as committed only when "a person" who is "17 years of age or more" engages in sexual intercourse with a person under 16. Because Esther lacks the status the statute requires of the perpetrator, she is legally incapable of committing the completed offense. Attempt liability does not attach when the defendant could not have completed the crime even if every fact had unfolded exactly as she intended. The fact that she used an intoxicating substance to overcome Paul's initial reluctance, or that Paul was under 16, does not change Esther's own age or the statute's limitation on who can commit this particular offense.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A (dominant trap):** Students are powerfully drawn to the facts that Paul was 15 (under the age of consent) and that Esther used an intoxicating substance to overcome his reluctance—the classic markers of statutory rape and common-law rape by intoxicants. This choice sells the appealing but incomplete idea that "if the victim is too young and the defendant used intoxicants, it must be the crime." It is defeated because the statute expressly limits the class of persons who can commit third-degree rape to those "being 17 years of age or more." Esther's age makes it definitionally impossible for her to be "a person" guilty under the statute, regardless of what she did to or with Paul.

**B:** This choice is attractive because many students carry a strong background assumption that rape is a crime only a male can commit against a female, so a female defendant "cannot" be guilty. It is defeated because the statute itself uses explicitly gender-neutral language ("he or she") and therefore applies to female perpetrators.

**C:** This choice feels correct because, at common law and under many modern rape statutes, using an intoxicating substance to overcome a victim's resistance is sufficient to establish the offense, and the "but for" the victim's physical inability language tracks standard attempt doctrine. It is defeated because this particular statute adds a strict status requirement on the perpetrator ("being 17 years of age or more") that Esther does not meet. The common-law intoxicant rule cannot create liability for an offense the legislature has defined so that this defendant cannot commit it.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited answer is correct. The statute on its face requires that the person who "engages in sexual intercourse" be "17 years of age or more." Esther is 15. A defendant cannot be guilty of attempting a crime that the governing statute makes it legally impossible for her to commit because she lacks a status element the legislature has written into the definition of the offense itself. This is legal impossibility, not factual impossibility. The intoxicant facts and the victim's age are relevant to whether the elements of force/overcoming resistance and victim age would be satisfied if the perpetrator-age gate were met, but they do not satisfy or bypass the gate. The official explanation in the source row is accurate and is confirmed by the statutory text supplied in the question.

Lawyer-confirmation layer (research verified): The supplied statute language tracks real age-gated statutory rape provisions in multiple U.S. jurisdictions (e.g., older formulations in New York and analogous "being X years or more" perpetrator requirements in other states). Standard criminal law doctrine holds that attempt does not lie where the completed crime is legally impossible for the actor due to a status or definitional limitation in the statute (see, e.g., MPC § 5.01 comment on legal impossibility; case law recognizing that an unmarried person cannot attempt bigamy, a non-merchant cannot attempt certain merchant-only offenses, and here a person under the statutory minimum age cannot attempt the age-gated sex offense). No research contradicted the official key of "not guilty because the girl was under the age of 17." No doctrinal-currency hotlist issue applies.
