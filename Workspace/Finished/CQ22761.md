## Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)
The stem explicitly states that the ministry workers "received no notice of the earlier lawsuit nor were they represented in the prior claim." Under the governing rule, a judgment binds only the parties to the litigation and those in privity with them or who were adequately represented. Because these facts are absent, the prior judgment does not bar the ministry workers.

**Silver Key: Call focus on the specific plaintiffs.** The call asks whether *these* ministry workers are barred. The stem directly negates the two facts (notice + representation) required for binding effect. The class-action label does not supply either fact.

The two "yes" choices are attractive because they invoke the familiar res judicata / claim preclusion language on an identical underlying levy, but both fail the stem facts. The "class action" choice is attractive because it suggests a fresh procedural vehicle, but the form of the new suit does not create notice or representation that was missing.

Lawyer confirmation (for verification only): The credited outcome is identical to the source. Richards v. Jefferson County, 517 U.S. 793 (1996) (due process forbids binding absent persons who had neither notice nor adequate representation in the prior suit; the earlier Alliance suit did not purport to represent employee interests and gave them no notice). Later cases such as Taylor v. Sturgell, 553 U.S. 880 (2008) continue to cite Richards for the constitutional floor on non-party preclusion. No doctrinal drift; the rule remains good law.

## Full wrong-answer explanations
**A (class action):** Attractive because the new suit is brought as a class action, suggesting a "fresh start" or different posture that might evade the prior loss. Breaker: The preclusion question turns on whether *these plaintiffs* were parties to or adequately represented in the earlier suit. The stem says they were not. The procedural vehicle they now choose (class action) does not retroactively create notice or representation that never occurred. This choice answers a different question (what form is the suit in?) than the call (are these specific people bound?).

**C (res judicata):** Dominant trap. Attractive because the underlying levy is identical and a court already ruled it lawful; students over-apply the shorthand "same claim, already decided, game over." Breaker: Res judicata requires that the second suit be between the same parties or their privies. The stem expressly states the ministry workers received no notice and were not represented. Without those facts, the prior judgment has no preclusive effect against them as a matter of due process. (Gold Key: non-party preclusion limit.)

**D (claim preclusion):** Attractive as a more "precise" or modern-sounding label for the same bar, or as a synonym trap that feels lawyerly. Breaker: "Claim preclusion" is simply another name for res judicata. It carries the identical party/privity/representation requirement. The stem facts (no notice, no representation) defeat it for the same reason they defeat choice C. This is the synonym variant of the dominant trap.

## Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested black-letter rule is the due-process limitation on the preclusive effect of a judgment against non-parties: a prior judgment binds only parties and those in privity or who received adequate representation; absent persons who had no notice are not bound. The credited answer is correct because the stem supplies the exact disqualifying facts (no notice + not represented). The outcome, issue, and trap structure are unchanged from the source question.

Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only): Richards v. Jefferson County, 517 U.S. 793, 797–805 (1996) (extreme application of state res judicata that binds non-parties without notice or adequate representation violates due process; the prior taxpayer/city suit did not adequately represent the later employee class). The rule has been reaffirmed in the virtual-representation context. See Taylor v. Sturgell, 553 U.S. 880, 892–93 (2008) (citing Richards for the constitutional floor). No superseding authority; the principle is stable.

The prior suit was brought by a business-owners alliance; the later plaintiffs are a distinct group of workers who were never parties and received no notice. Therefore they are not barred.
