### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C** is correct. By stepping into a contact sport like dodgeball, a participant impliedly consents to bodily contacts that are customary and within the ordinary rules and scope of the game. Here, the throw was customary, violated no safety rule, and occurred during ordinary play. The fact that the plaintiff fell does not defeat the consent defense.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. No, because the plaintiff was injured.**
This is attractive because the plaintiff did fall and may have been hurt. The breaker is that injury, by itself, does not defeat consent when the contact was within the scope of the sport the plaintiff agreed to play.

**B. Yes, but only because the player did not intend any contact.**
This is attractive because it reaches the right result — "yes" — but for the wrong reason. The breaker is that the defense is consent, not absence of intent. In a game like dodgeball, the throw was intentional; the privilege comes from the plaintiff's implied consent to such contacts.

**D. No, because consent to a sport never covers intentional contact.**
This is the dominant trap because it states an absolute rule that sounds like a principled boundary around battery. The breaker is that many contact sports, including dodgeball, involve intentional contacts that are within the scope of implied consent. The word "never" overclaims.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The original row tests a Torts item on the consent defense to battery in the context of contact sports. The black-letter rule is that a participant in a contact sport impliedly consents to bodily contacts that are customary and within the ordinary rules and scope of the game, but not to conduct that falls outside that scope. The transform tests the identical rule: the thrown ball was customary, within the rules, and part of ordinary dodgeball play, so the throwing player may rely on consent.

For lawyer confirmation, the Restatement (Second) of Torts § 892 recognizes that consent may be express or implied, and that implied consent may be inferred from conduct — including participation in a contact sport under accepted customs. Cases applying this principle consistently hold that customary game contacts are privileged, while conduct outside the ordinary scope of play (e.g., a fight or a flagrant foul) is not protected.

The transformed outcome is unchanged: the correct answer is the choice recognizing consent to contacts within the ordinary scope of the sport.
