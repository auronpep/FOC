### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Under FRE 607, any party may attack a witness's credibility. One well-established method of impeachment is extrinsic evidence showing that the witness was incapable of perceiving the events he or she testified about. Here, Peter testified he identified the defendant by the light of a full moon. The expert astronomer's testimony that there was no moon at all that night directly undermines Peter's capacity to observe and identify the defendant. This is classic impeachment by contradiction on a sensory-deficiency theory — the testimony tends to show Peter's identification is unworthy of belief because he could not have seen what he claimed.

> **Gold Key (Capacity-to-Perceive Exception):** A witness's ability to perceive or remember is *never* treated as a collateral matter. The collateral-fact rule bars extrinsic evidence only on points that are irrelevant to any issue in the case. Capacity to observe goes to the heart of the witness's credibility and is always material. This is why the bar in answer B fails.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original B) — "admitted, because it is part of the res gestae":** This choice tempts students who vaguely recall that "res gestae" relates to excited utterances or statements made under stress. The expert's testimony about the moon's phase is not a statement made under the stress of a startling event — it is scientific opinion offered at trial. The res gestae doctrine (now largely codified as the excited utterance exception in FRE 803(2)) has nothing to do with this fact pattern. **B (original D) — "excluded as extrinsic evidence of a collateral matter":** This is the dominant trap. Students know the collateral-fact rule bars extrinsic evidence offered solely to contradict a witness on collateral points. The pull is strong because the rule is real and frequently tested. The breaker: a witness's capacity to perceive is *never* collateral — it is always material to credibility. The whole point of the expert's testimony is to show Peter could not have seen what he claimed, which goes directly to whether his identification is believable. This is a non-collateral use of extrinsic evidence, so the collateral-matter bar does not apply.

> **Gold Key:** Capacity to perceive = never collateral. The collateral-fact rule does not bar extrinsic evidence of a witness's inability to observe accurately.

**D (original C) — "excluded, because Peter was not given an opportunity to explain":** This choice tempts students who recall FRE 613(b), which requires that a witness be given an opportunity to explain or deny a prior inconsistent statement before extrinsic evidence of that statement can be introduced. But the expert's testimony is not a prior inconsistent statement — it is extrinsic evidence of a sensory deficiency. The FRE 613(b) foundation requirement applies only to proof of prior inconsistent statements, not to all forms of extrinsic impeachment. No confrontation-foundation rule applies here.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** FRE 607 provides that "any party, including the party that called the witness, may attack the witness's credibility." One established method of impeachment by the opposing party is the introduction of extrinsic evidence tending to show that the witness was incapable of perceiving the events testified about — a sensory-deficiency or capacity-based attack on credibility. *See* 27 Charles Alan Wright & Victor Gold, *Federal Practice and Procedure* §6097 (2d ed.); McCormick on Evidence §37 (8th ed.).

**Authority floor — FRE 607** (any party may attack credibility); **FRE 602** (personal knowledge required — a witness who could not see lacks personal knowledge); **FRE 401/402** (relevant evidence admissible — capacity to observe is relevant to credibility and is not collateral).

The collateral-fact rule (the *Hitchcock* rule at common law) bars extrinsic evidence only when the contradiction relates to a matter that is irrelevant to any issue in the case other than the witness's general credibility. A witness's capacity to perceive the specific events in question is not collateral — it is a "lynch pin" fact going to the reliability of the testimony itself. *See* the discussion in Wright & Gold, *Federal Practice and Procedure* §6097; *R. v. A.C.*, 2018 ONCA 333 (discussing the scope of the collateral-fact rule and its exceptions for capacity to observe).

**FRE 613(b)** does not apply here because the extrinsic evidence is not a prior inconsistent statement — it is independent evidence of a sensory deficiency. The foundation requirement of 613(b) is limited to proof of prior inconsistent statements.

**Res gestae** (answer A) is a largely archaic doctrine, now superseded by the excited utterance exception in FRE 803(2). It has no application to expert astronomical testimony offered at trial.

**Conclusion:** The credited answer (A→C) is correct. The expert's testimony is admissible to impeach Peter by showing he could not have perceived what he claimed. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original.
