### 1. Final question

Police have probable cause to believe a stolen hand-carved cedar baptism sign is hidden in Barnabas's retreat-supply van. They search the van without a warrant. In the glove box, they open a tiny prayer-oil tin too small to contain the sign and find narcotics.

Does the automobile exception justify opening the tin?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

May police using the automobile exception open a tiny container in a van when the container could not hold the large stolen item they had probable cause to find?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. The police had probable cause to search the van for a large cedar baptism sign, so the automobile exception allowed them to search places in the van where that sign could reasonably be hidden. Gold Key: automobile-exception scope follows the object of probable cause and the places or containers where that object could be found. Silver Key: match the object to the container before letting the word "vehicle" do the work. A tiny prayer-oil tin could not hold the sign, so opening it was outside the exception's scope.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A. The mobility answer is attractive because the automobile exception rests partly on a vehicle's ready mobility. The breaker is that mobility explains why a warrant is not required; it does not erase the object-container limit once officers choose where to search.

C. The never-container answer is attractive because closed containers feel like a separate privacy zone. The breaker is the Gold Key: containers may be opened under the automobile exception when they could conceal the object of probable cause.

D. The every-container answer is the dominant trap because it turns a real vehicle exception into a search-everything license. The breaker is the Silver Key: a large cedar sign cannot fit in a tiny tin, so the tin fails the object-fit test.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. The automobile exception permits a warrantless vehicle search supported by probable cause, but the search must stay within the scope of that probable cause. United States v. Ross, 456 U.S. 798 (1982), holds that when officers have probable cause to search a vehicle, they may search the vehicle and containers that may conceal the object of the search; the scope is defined by the object sought and the places where it may be found. Wyoming v. Houghton, 526 U.S. 295 (1999), repeats the same object-capability limit for passenger belongings in a car. California v. Acevedo, 500 U.S. 565 (1991), confirms that closed containers in automobiles may be searched without a warrant when the automobile-exception probable-cause rule is satisfied. Here, probable cause concerned a large stolen cedar baptism sign. A tiny prayer-oil tin could not conceal that sign. The narcotics found in the tin therefore came from a search outside the automobile exception's object-defined scope.
