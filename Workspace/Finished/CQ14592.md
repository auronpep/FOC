## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

A is correct. **Silver Key:** Police entry with the consent of someone with authority over the premises is a recognized exception to the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement. Here, Noah, the cabin owner, consented to the police entering his property to investigate a potential bear presence. Once lawfully inside, the police developed probable cause when they recognized Daniel from the bulletin. The search incident to arrest was therefore valid, and the motion to suppress must be denied. The key is that voluntary consent from an authorized person validates the entry, regardless of the guest's standing.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B is incorrect.** While Daniel, as an overnight guest, may indeed have sufficient standing under Fourth Amendment jurisprudence to contest the entry (see *Minnesota v. Olson*), the standing question is not dispositive here because the police entry was valid based on Noah's consent. The officer's lawful presence inside the cabin allowed them to observe Daniel and recognize him from the bulletin. **Silver Key:** When police have valid consent to enter, the defendant's standing does not change the lawfulness of the entry.

**C is incorrect.** Even assuming Daniel had standing to contest the entry, the critical issue is whether the police had a lawful basis to be inside the cabin. Noah's consent provided that basis. The lack of probable cause *at the time of entry* is irrelevant because the entry itself was lawful via consent. The police only needed probable cause to arrest Daniel once they saw and recognized him inside. **Silver Key:** Consent validates entry; probable cause for arrest does not need to predate the lawful entry.

**D is incorrect.** This choice overstates the legal requirement. **Gold Key:** GK-CRIMINAL-CONSENT-01: *An overnight social guest has automatic standing to challenge the lawfulness of a search of the premises where they are staying.* (See *Minnesota v. Olson*, 495 U.S. 91 (1990)). Therefore, Daniel's lack of ownership or possessory interest does not deprive him of standing to contest the search. However, even with standing, the police entry was lawful based on Noah's consent, and the motion to suppress should be denied. **Gold Key** unlocks the standing issue, but the consent exception still controls the outcome.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing Rule:** The Fourth Amendment generally requires a warrant for police entry into a home (or its curtilage, which includes a cabin on private property). However, **voluntary consent** from a person with authority over the premises is a well-established exception to the warrant requirement. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973) ("It is well settled that one of the specifically established exceptions to the requirements of both a warrant and probable cause is a search that is conducted pursuant to consent.").

**Application:** Noah, as the owner of the cabin, had authority to consent to the police entry. His consent was voluntary—he called the police for assistance and let them inside to check for a bear. The fact that the police had an additional motive (investigating a recent theft) does not vitiate the consent, so long as the consent was not coerced. *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194, 201 (2002) (consent is valid if not coerced, even if police had other motives).

Once lawfully inside, the police saw Daniel and recognized him from a bulletin, establishing probable cause for his arrest. The subsequent search of Daniel's bag was a valid search incident to arrest. *Arizona v. Gant*, 556 U.S. 332 (2009) (search incident to arrest of the arrestee's person and immediate area is valid).

**Standing Analysis:** Under *Minnesota v. Olson*, 495 U.S. 91, 96-97 (1990), an overnight guest in a home has a reasonable expectation of privacy and thus standing to challenge the lawfulness of the search. However, standing does not affect the validity of the consent-based entry. The entry was lawful, so the evidence should not be suppressed.
