# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

Martha owned a parcel of land and wanted to open a commercial bake-house on it. After negotiating, Martha hired Stephen to build a large brick bread-oven and a separate flour mill-house. Martha and Stephen signed a written contract under which Stephen agreed to start work on March 1, to finish everything except the mill-house by June 1, and to complete the mill-house by July 15. The contract price was $120,000, payable as follows: $40,000 on March 1; $40,000 upon completion of everything but the mill-house; and the final $40,000 upon completion of the mill-house. Stephen began work on March 1 after receiving $40,000 from Martha. By June 1, Stephen had finished the brick bread-oven and all other work except the mill-house, in accordance with the specifications, and Martha paid him a second $40,000. Stephen then began building the mill-house, but before he completed it he told Martha he would not finish the job. Which of the following statements is correct concerning Martha's rights against Stephen?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A builder, paid for the milestones he completed, repudiates a fixed-price staged construction contract before finishing the final structure, while the owner still holds the unpaid final installment. What may the owner recover?

- **D.** Martha may recover every payment she has made to Stephen and the reasonable cost of completing the mill-house.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**B is correct.** Stephen repudiated, so Martha is the non-breaching owner and may either rescind or sue for the standard remedy — but **either road nets to zero recovery here.**

- If Martha **rescinds**, she is freed from the contract, but rescission restitution runs both ways: Stephen gets the *reasonable value of the work he already did* in quasi-contract. He built the oven and everything but the mill-house and was paid $80,000 for it — value conferred matches money received, so Martha cannot claw those payments back.
- If Martha **does not rescind** and sues for the standard remedy, her recovery is the cost to complete the mill-house **minus the unpaid contract balance she still holds** ($40,000). She kept that final installment precisely to pay someone to finish; it offsets the completion cost.

Either way she is *made whole, not enriched.* **Gold Key:** a breach victim's recovery is cost-to-complete **minus** the unpaid contract balance, and rescission gives the builder a restitution credit for work done — no windfall, no double recovery. **Silver Key:** read the four choices as a 2×2 — (recover payments?) × (recover completion cost?); when milestone payments matched accepted work *and* the owner still holds the final balance, both axes zero out, so the "neither" corner (B) is the answer.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A — "recover the reasonable cost of completing the mill-house."** This is the choice that rewards knowing the rule: cost of completion *is* the standard measure for a builder's breach, so it feels like the textbook answer. The breaker is the money Martha never paid — she still holds the final $40,000 installment, which is deducted from any completion award; with the retained balance covering the work, the net recovery is nothing. **Gold Key:** recovery = cost to complete **minus** the unpaid contract balance.

**C — "recover every payment she has made."** It taps the instinct that a repudiating builder should hand the money back so the owner can start fresh. The breaker is mutuality of restitution: Stephen performed $80,000 of accepted work for the $80,000 he was paid, and rescission gives him a quasi-contract credit for that value — payments matched by completed, accepted work are not recoverable.

**D — "recover every payment she has made *and* the reasonable cost of completing the mill-house."** This is the punitive grab — he breached, so make him refund everything *and* pay to finish. The breaker is the bar on double recovery: stacking a full refund of accepted-work payments on top of a completion award would put Martha far ahead of where performance would have left her, which contract damages never permit.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**Tested rule (unchanged from the original):** the owner's remedy when a builder breaches a construction contract, and the netting/offset that defeats both over-recovery and clawback.

- **Standard (non-rescission) remedy:** expectation damages = the reasonable cost to complete the work, **less the unpaid contract balance** the owner avoids paying. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 347 (expectation = loss in value − cost or loss avoided) and § 348(2) (reasonable cost to complete as the measure, absent disproportion). The retained final installment is "cost avoided," so it offsets the completion cost.
- **Rescission remedy:** restitution on rescission is **mutual** — the builder recovers the reasonable value of services already rendered in quasi-contract, so payments matched by accepted performance are not clawed back; any excess of that value over payments received is a setoff in the owner's completion action.
- **No double recovery / no windfall:** contract damages place the injured party where performance would have, never ahead. Stacking a refund of accepted-work payments onto a completion award (choice D) is barred.
- **Governing-law check:** this is a construction/services contract governed by common law, **not** UCC Article 2; no goods-sale measure applies.

Credited outcome **B = original D ("neither")** is correct and **unchanged** from the source item. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are identical; only the surface story moved. `agrees_with_official_key: true`.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):** Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 347, 348(2); see also CACI No. 4531 (Owner's Damages for Breach of Construction Contract — cost to complete offset by the unpaid contract balance). No doctrinal-currency hotlist area is implicated. Authority floor met (Restatement sections).
