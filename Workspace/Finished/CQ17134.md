## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question
At a federal criminal trial, Martha is called to testify that she saw Barnabas throw a metal camping lantern at Timothy during a church picnic parking-lot scuffle. On voir dire, Martha says she was across the gravel lane, wearing her reading glasses, and could see the two men clearly under the LED lamp outside the picnic pavilion. Barnabas objects that Martha was too far away to be certain. How should the court treat the objection?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
When a lay witness has some evidence that she perceived the event, must the court exclude her because she is not certain?

### 3. Final answer choices

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. A lay witness does not need to be certain before testifying. Gold Key: for lay-witness personal knowledge, the court admits the testimony if evidence is sufficient to support a finding that the witness perceived the matter; uncertainty, distance, and perception weaknesses usually go to weight for the jury. Here, Martha's own voir dire testimony gives the court enough foundation evidence that she perceived the event, so the objection should be overruled.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
D is attractive because it turns the objection's word "certain" into a clean exclusion rule. The breaker is that FRE 602 does not require certainty; it requires evidence sufficient to support a finding of personal knowledge, so this answer overstates the admissibility threshold.

B is attractive because corroboration feels like a safer way to prove that one witness really saw what she says she saw. The breaker is that the question asks only whether Martha's testimony has enough foundation to be admitted; another witness is not required to establish her personal knowledge.

A is attractive because the judge decides admissibility, so students may think the judge must personally believe the witness before letting the testimony in. The breaker is that the judge screens for enough foundation evidence to support a finding of perception, while accuracy and credibility are left for the jury to weigh.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Evidence, Presentation of Evidence, lay-witness personal knowledge foundation. The verified outline code is `31010102`, which appears in `OUTLINE_CODES_COMPLETE.md` under `Presentation of Evidence > General Provisions > Burden of production and burden of persuasion`.

Authority floor: PASS. Federal Rule of Evidence 602 provides that a witness may testify to a matter only if evidence is introduced sufficient to support a finding that the witness has personal knowledge of the matter, and that evidence may consist of the witness's own testimony. Federal Rule of Evidence 104(b) uses the same support-a-finding formulation when relevance depends on a fact, and FRE 104(e) preserves evidence relevant to weight and credibility for the jury. See Fed. R. Evid. 602, https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_602; Fed. R. Evid. 104(b), (e), https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_104.

The credited answer is unchanged. Martha's distance and possible uncertainty do not make her testimony inadmissible if her testimony supports a finding that she perceived the event. Those concerns affect weight and credibility, not the personal-knowledge foundation.
