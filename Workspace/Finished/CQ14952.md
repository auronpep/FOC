## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Mary bought a lakeside lodge that she used for private Bible-study retreats, financing most of the price with a loan from Timothy that was secured by a mortgage on the lodge. Mary made the mortgage payments on time for several years. Later, Mary sold the lodge to Lydia, subject to Timothy's mortgage. Mary and Lydia expressly agreed that Lydia would not assume and agree to pay Mary's debt to Timothy.

The mortgage contained a due-on-sale clause stating: "If Mortgagor transfers any interest in the property without Mortgagee's prior written consent, Mortgagee may, at Mortgagee's option, declare the entire unpaid principal balance immediately due and payable."

Mary did not ask Timothy for consent. She conveyed the lodge to Lydia by deed, and the deed stated that the conveyance was "subject to a mortgage to Timothy" and listed the recording information for the mortgage. Lydia took possession and made several mortgage payments, which Timothy accepted. Now neither Mary nor Lydia has made the last three mortgage payments. Timothy has sued Lydia for the delinquent payments.

In this action, for whom should the court render judgment?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A mortgagor sells mortgaged land to a buyer who expressly does not assume the debt, the buyer later makes some payments, and the lender sues the buyer personally for missed payments. Is the buyer personally liable?

### 3. Final Answer Choices (A-D, post-shuffle)

A. Lydia, because she is not in privity of estate with Timothy.

C. Lydia, because she took the lodge subject to the mortgage but did not assume and agree to pay Mary's mortgage debt.

D. Timothy, because Mary's deed to Lydia violated the due-on-sale clause.

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**C is correct.** Lydia took the lodge **subject to** Timothy's mortgage, but she expressly did not assume and agree to pay Mary's debt. A subject-to buyer receives title burdened by the mortgage lien; the land remains available to satisfy the debt through foreclosure. But the buyer does not become personally liable for the mortgage debt merely by taking possession, accepting a deed that references the mortgage, or making voluntary payments that the mortgagee accepts.

**Gold Key:** "Subject to the mortgage" means the lien follows the land; "assumes and agrees to pay" means the buyer makes a personal promise on the debt. A money judgment against the buyer needs the personal promise.

Timothy may have remedies against Mary, and the due-on-sale clause may let Timothy accelerate the debt under the mortgage contract. Timothy may also pursue the collateral through foreclosure if the default continues. Those remedies do not turn Lydia into a personal debtor.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A.** This choice reaches the right side of the caption, so it is attractive in a "for whom" call. The breaker is the reason. The case is not decided by whether Lydia and Timothy are in privity of estate. It is decided by whether Lydia made a personal promise to pay Mary's mortgage debt. **Silver Key:** when a mortgagee sues the transferee for money, lock the call to personal liability first; a right result with the wrong legal reason is still not the best answer.

**B.** This is the dominant trap. Possession plus several accepted payments makes Lydia look like the new borrower. The breaker is the missing assumption. Lydia's possession and payment history do not override the express agreement that she would not assume and agree to pay Mary's debt. **Gold Key:** a subject-to transferee can lose the land to foreclosure, but personal liability requires assumption words or conduct that clearly undertakes the debt.

**D.** This choice sells a real clause but the wrong remedy. Mary violated the due-on-sale clause by transferring the lodge without Timothy's consent, so Timothy may have an acceleration remedy under the mortgage contract. That clause does not create a new personal debt owed by Lydia. The action is for delinquent payments against Lydia, not for acceleration against Mary or foreclosure of the lodge.

### 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Right-Answer Legal Reasoning

The source row identifies Q14952 as a Real Property / Mortgages / Transfers by mortgagor item with official key **A**. The tested rule is the distinction between a grantee who takes land **subject to** a mortgage and a grantee who **assumes** the mortgage debt.

The black-letter rule is unchanged in the transformed question. A mortgagor who remains personally liable on the note may convey the mortgaged property. If the transferee takes the property **subject to** the mortgage, the mortgage lien remains on the land, but the transferee does not become personally liable on the underlying debt. If the transferee **assumes and agrees to pay** the mortgage debt, the transferee becomes personally liable, usually as the primary obligor as between transferor and transferee, while the original mortgagor remains liable unless the mortgagee releases or novates the original debtor.

Authority check: Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages Chapter 5 covers transfers of mortgaged real estate and mortgages. Research sources identify Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages Sections 5.1 and 5.2 as the assumption / no-assumption framework for transfers with and without personal liability. The American Law Institute page confirms the Restatement and its Chapter 5 scope. HUD's FHA assumption notice states the same operational distinction: when a purchaser assumes responsibility for the debt, the purchaser is liable; if the purchaser takes title subject to the mortgage without assuming personal liability, the original borrower remains liable. For due-on-sale, 12 U.S.C. 1701j-3(b) permits lenders, subject to statutory limits, to enter into and enforce due-on-sale clauses, and it separately recognizes assumptions without treating a transfer subject to a loan as automatic personal liability.
