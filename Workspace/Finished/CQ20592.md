### 7. Right-Answer Explanation

Stephen drew a fishing knife and advanced on Lydia at the edge of a dock. That is an immediate, life-threatening confrontation with no safe exit except the water. Lydia's jump was the only available flight response — the kind of reaction any person in her position would take. The motorboat that struck her was a hazard she encountered because she was in the river because Stephen put her there.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIMINAL-FLIGHT-CAUSE-01):** When a defendant's deadly threat causes a victim to flee, and the victim is killed during that flight, the defendant is the proximate cause — as long as the victim's escape response and the type of harm were foreseeable. Stephen need not have intended the motorboat to strike Lydia. He created the threat that made her jump; the fatal consequence of that jump was foreseeable. Foreseeability — not intent to the precise mechanism — is the standard.

### 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice B — "No, because the motorboat actually struck Lydia"**

The motorboat physically ended Lydia's life — it was the last event in the causal sequence. This sells the intuitive "direct cause" or "last act" theory: whoever delivers the fatal blow is the proximate cause. The breaker: the law does not make the last physical event the automatic proximate cause. An intervening event breaks the causal chain only if it is a *superseding* cause — one that was unforeseeable. A motorboat on a navigable waterway is exactly the type of hazard one would expect when an armed attacker forces someone off a dock and into the water. The motorboat completed the chain Stephen started; it did not sever it.

*Gold Key GK-CRIMINAL-FLIGHT-CAUSE-01 kills this trap: foreseeability of the type of harm — not which event struck last — determines proximate cause.*

**Choice C — "Yes, but only if Stephen intended the motorboat to hit Lydia" (DOMINANT TRAP)**

This choice correctly identifies "Yes" but imposes a condition: Stephen must have specifically intended the motorboat to be the instrument of death. It sells a blending of mens rea reasoning into the causation analysis. A student who correctly reaches "Stephen caused this" but conflates intent with causation will stop here. The breaker: proximate cause doctrine does not require intent to the precise manner of death. Stephen intended to threaten Lydia with deadly force; the foreseeability that her escape would lead to a fatal hazard is what satisfies causation. The "only if intended the exact mechanism" condition is an invented requirement with no basis in causation law.

*Gold Key GK-CRIMINAL-FLIGHT-CAUSE-01: the stated condition is a fabricated rule. Foreseeability of the type of harm is the test — intent to the precise mechanism is not.*

**Choice D — "No, because Lydia chose to jump into the river"**

Lydia made a conscious decision to jump. This sells the autonomy theory: her own voluntary act was the cause of her entering the river, so Stephen's causal link is severed by her choice. The breaker: a victim's flight response to imminent deadly force is not a truly independent voluntary act. It is the predictable, foreseeable reaction to being confronted at close range with a knife on a dock with nowhere else to go. Autonomy-based superseding cause requires a genuinely unrelated, unforeseeable independent exercise of will — not an instinctive escape from a threat the defendant created.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Tested rule:** A defendant who creates an immediate threat causing a victim to flee is the proximate cause of the victim's death when: (1) the victim's flight response was foreseeable given the threat; and (2) the type of harm resulting from that flight was within the foreseeable scope of risk. An intervening event (here, the motorboat) breaks the chain only if it is a superseding cause — one that was unforeseeable.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer):**
- **MPC § 2.03:** Causation requires that the actual result not be "too remote or accidental" relative to the purpose or risk the defendant's conduct created. Under § 2.03(2)(b), for reckless/negligent conduct, the actual result must be within the risk of which the actor was or should have been aware.
- **Common law foreseeable-response rule:** A defendant is the proximate cause of a victim's death when the victim's flight from the defendant's deadly threat is a foreseeable response and the fatal harm occurs as a result of that flight. *See People v. Acosta*, 232 Cal. App. 3d 1375 (1991) (defendant who caused high-speed chase was proximate cause of helicopter crash that was foreseeable); *People v. Kern*, 75 N.Y.2d 638 (1990) (proximate cause found where victim's flight from defendant's conduct led to death).
- **Restatement (Third) of Torts: Physical and Emotional Harm § 29** (scope of liability): The type of harm that occurred must be within the scope of the risk that made the conduct wrongful/criminal. A boat striking someone forced into the water is within the scope of the risk created by threatening a person at a dock with a knife.

**Credited answer confirmed:** Choice A (original C) is correct under both common law and MPC. The official key is not doubted. No NEEDS_HUMAN flag.

**Issue and outcome unchanged from original:** The tested rule (foreseeable victim response does not supersede causation), legal issue (is the defendant the proximate cause?), and credited outcome (Yes) are identical to the original question.
