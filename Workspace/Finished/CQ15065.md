# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question

Lydia owned Cedar Lantern Orchard, a valuable tract of land in Jordan County. Wanting the local Bible-meal volunteers to use the land for shared fellowship work, Lydia executed a document in the form of a warranty deed. The deed was regular in every respect except that the only words identifying the grantees in both the granting clause and the habendum clause were: "the bread bakers for every Wednesday-night Bible meal in Jordan County."

The instrument was acknowledged as required by statute and promptly and properly recorded. Lydia told her lawyer Paul, but no one else, that she used that wording because she hoped those bakers would sell the orchard fruit and fund countywide fellowship meals. Nine days later, Lydia died suddenly and unexpectedly, leaving a will that devised her entire estate to Ruth.

After probate of the will became final and the administration of Lydia's estate was closed, Ruth brought an appropriate action to quiet title to Cedar Lantern Orchard and properly served as defendants each Wednesday-night Bible-meal group located in the county. The only evidence introduced consisted of Lydia's chain of title, the probated will, the recorded deed, the fact that no one except Lydia and Paul knew about the deed, and Lydia's conversation with Paul.

Does a recorded deed transfer land when the deed identifies the grantees only as a vague class of local volunteers?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. The deed fails at a threshold requirement: it does not identify the grantees with enough certainty. "The bread bakers for every Wednesday-night Bible meal in Jordan County" is a shifting and imprecise class, not an ascertainable grantee designation. Recording and acknowledgment can support execution and delivery, but they do not supply a missing grantee. Ruth therefore takes Cedar Lantern Orchard under the will.

Gold Key: A deed needs an ascertainable grantee; recording cannot cure a grantee description too vague to tell who received title.

Silver Key: When the array gives one answer about grantee identity and two answers about recording or delivery, ask which fact attacks the deed's threshold validity. Delivery evidence matters only after the deed names a grantee capable of taking.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because it reaches the same winner and uses the secrecy facts. The breaker is that the deed was promptly and properly recorded, and recording supports a delivery presumption; the problem is not delivery but the deed's failure to identify an ascertainable grantee. Gold Key: Recording may help delivery, but it does not cure a vague grantee clause.

B is attractive because its delivery statement is legally strong. The breaker is responsiveness: prima facie delivery does not answer the dispositive defect, because even a delivered deed must identify the grantees with adequate certainty.

D is attractive because recording sounds like it validates the whole document. The breaker is the overclaim. Recording can support execution or delivery presumptions, but it does not create a blanket presumption that a deed with an indefinite grantee clause transferred title.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. A deed must identify the grantor and grantee and describe the property and estate with enough certainty to transfer title. The recorded instrument here does not identify ascertainable grantees, so it does not transfer Cedar Lantern Orchard. The will beneficiary prevails.

Primary-law authority supports the split between grantee identification and delivery:

- Maryland Real Property Code section 4-101(a)(1), in the official Maryland code PDF, states that a sufficient deed contains the names of the grantor and grantee, a property description sufficient to identify the property with reasonable certainty, and the estate intended to be granted, if properly executed, acknowledged, and recorded: https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2022RS/Statute_Web/grp/grp.pdf
- Maryland Real Property Code section 4-103(a) separately provides that when a deed is executed, acknowledged, and recorded if required, validity as to execution and delivery is presumed. That presumption addresses delivery; it does not supply an ascertainable grantee: https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2022RS/Statute_Web/grp/grp.pdf
- McKeever v. Parker, 226 P.2d 425 (Okla. 1950), states the common delivery rule that delivery is essential and that recordation of a duly executed deed raises a delivery presumption. That confirms why the delivery-based answer is not the best ground for Ruth: https://law.justia.com/cases/oklahoma/supreme-court/1950/58093.html
- Cornell Wex's deed overview is consistent at a secondary level: a valid real-estate deed generally requires names of grantor and grantee, words of conveyance, adequate property description, signature, and delivery: https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/deed
