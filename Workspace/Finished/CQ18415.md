Detectives gave Daniel Miranda warnings before questioning him about a missing cash box from a relief truck after a Christian disaster-training day. ### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is correct. Daniel received Miranda warnings, but warnings are not a cure-all for a confession produced by coercive police pressure. Gold Key: voluntariness asks whether police conduct, under the totality of the circumstances, overbore the suspect's will. Here the detectives used a knowingly false promise of immediate release and case dismissal after nine hours of questioning. Silver Key: separate the Miranda-warning question from the voluntariness question. The confession is inadmissible because the false promise and prolonged questioning made it involuntary.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells the interrogation-flexibility instinct: officers may use some tactics, and not every promise or statement of leniency makes a confession involuntary. The breaker is the word "always"; a knowingly false promise of immediate release and dismissal, especially after prolonged questioning, can overbear the will.

B sells the dominant shortcut. Miranda warnings matter, but Gold Key: voluntariness remains a separate due-process screen. Warnings do not make a confession admissible when later police coercion overbears the suspect's will.

C sells an outdated physical-force picture of coercion. The breaker is that coercion can be psychological; threats, false promises, exhaustion, and pressure can make a confession involuntary even without physical harm.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: a confession can be inadmissible as involuntary when police coercion, assessed under the totality of the circumstances, overbears the suspect's will. Miranda warnings address custodial-interrogation safeguards, but a separately coerced confession still violates due process.

Lynumn v. Illinois, 372 U.S. 528 (1963), treated threats and promises by officers as coercive when they overbore the suspect's will. Mincey v. Arizona, 437 U.S. 385 (1978), confirms that use of an involuntary statement violates due process even apart from ordinary Miranda analysis. Colorado v. Connelly, 479 U.S. 157 (1986), requires coercive police activity for a due-process involuntariness claim. Arizona v. Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279 (1991), confirms that a confession can be coerced under the totality of circumstances when the government uses improper pressure. Sources checked: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/372/528/, https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/437/385/, https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/479/157/, and https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/89-839.ZO.html.

Daniel's facts satisfy the coercion requirement. The pressure came from detectives, not a private actor. They knowingly promised immediate release and dismissal, lacked authority to make that promise, and used it after nine hours of questioning while Daniel was worn down. Those facts support the official key: the confession is inadmissible because it was involuntary.
