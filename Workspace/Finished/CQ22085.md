## 1. Final Question

Ruth bought two residential lots on a privately owned desert mesa called Psalm Ridge. She planned to build two small single-family cottages on the lots. Two years after Ruth bought the property, the state legislature enacted a land-use law that barred anyone from building permanent habitable structures in state-designated desert tortoise corridors. The law was designed to reduce erosion on fragile mesa slopes and protect endangered desert tortoises during nesting season.

Ruth sued, claiming that she was entitled to compensation because the land-use law created a taking of her land. Is Ruth entitled to compensation?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A landowner buys residential lots and, after purchase, a state law bars all permanent habitable structures on those lots. Is compensation owed when the regulation removes all economically beneficial use of the land?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

**A is correct.** A land-use regulation can be a compensable taking even when the government does not physically occupy the land. Gold Key: under *Lucas*, when a regulation deprives land of all economically beneficial or productive use, the owner is generally entitled to just compensation unless background principles of property or nuisance law already barred the proposed use. The stem gives residential lots, a post-purchase ban on permanent habitable structures, and no background-principles bar. That puts the item in the total-regulatory-taking lane. Silver Key: separate "valid regulation" from "paid taking." A law can validly protect fragile land and endangered species while still requiring compensation if it wipes out all beneficial use of the affected property.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**B.** This answer sells a real takings route: physical occupation or appropriation is often enough for compensation. The breaker is that physical occupation is not the only route. Gold Key: *Lucas* also treats a total denial of economically beneficial land use as a categorical regulatory taking. A student who stops at "no physical invasion" misses the separate total-wipeout rule.

**C.** This answer is attractive because Ruth bought before the law and had a concrete building plan. The breaker is that planned use is not automatically a grandfathered nonconforming use. A nonconforming-use answer would need an existing lawful use before the new zoning restriction, not merely ownership plus intent to build.

**D.** This answer is the dominant trap. The erosion and endangered-tortoise facts make the law sound like an ordinary valid police-power regulation. The breaker is that validity does not end the compensation question. Silver Key: once the call asks whether compensation is owed, do not stop at public purpose; ask whether the regulation leaves any economically beneficial use.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule is unchanged from the source item: a land-use regulation that deprives land of all economically beneficial or productive use is a compensable taking under the Fifth Amendment, applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, unless the prohibited use was already barred by background principles of state property or nuisance law.

Authority floor: *Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council*, 505 U.S. 1003 (1992), recognizes the categorical regulatory-taking rule for land left with no economically beneficial or productive use. The Fifth Amendment Takings Clause supplies the constitutional command that private property shall not be taken for public use without just compensation. Current-law check: *Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid*, 594 U.S. ___ (2021), confirms that physical appropriation is a separate per se takings category, but it does not make physical occupation the only compensation route. *Tyler v. Hennepin County*, 598 U.S. 631 (2023), concerns surplus equity after tax foreclosure and does not alter the *Lucas* total-use-deprivation rule.

Authority links: *Lucas*, https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/505/1003/; *Cedar Point*, https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-107; *Tyler*, https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/22-166_8n59.pdf.
