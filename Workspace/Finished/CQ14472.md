Barnabas Lanternworks, a for-profit maker of devotional event equipment, contracted in writing with Lydia’s private Scripture-retreat company to sell and deliver to Lydia a portable stained-glass verse wall using a new bioluminescent glass coating. ### 2. Distilled Core Question

A seller promised finished goods using a known unperfected method; when the method failed for everyone, does the seller or buyer win?

### 3. Final answer choices

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Barnabas promised to deliver finished goods, not merely to conduct research. The stem gives the risk-allocation fact: Barnabas knew the coating was under development and not perfected when it made the contract. The later failure of that same projected method is the very risk Barnabas undertook.

**Gold Key:** Impracticability does not excuse a seller who took on the risk that a known, unperfected method would fail.

**Silver Key:** When the array gives you “seller wins because no one can build it” against “buyer wins because the seller assumed the risk,” name the axis first: **who owned the risk of the failed method?** The fact that Barnabas knowingly contracted around an unperfected coating sends the answer to Lydia.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells a commercially sympathetic idea: experimental work sounds like a promise to try hard, not a promise to deliver. The breaker is the word **only**. Nothing in the stem limits the contract to best efforts, and a finished-goods delivery promise is not automatically downgraded into a best-efforts undertaking.

**C.** This is the dominant trap. It grabs the loud fact that no maker can yet build the promised item and treats that as the whole case. **Gold Key:** the “no one can build it yet” fact does not end the analysis when the seller knowingly promised a finished product using that unperfected method. The missing move is risk allocation.

**D.** This choice reaches the buyer-favorable result with a technical-sounding shortcut. The breaker is the categorical merchant carve-out. The impracticability doctrine is not unavailable merely because the party is a merchant; the real reason Lydia wins is Barnabas’s assumed risk.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The source row tests a Contracts / Performance, Breach, and Discharge item. It gives the original seller’s knowledge of unperfected technology, the later discovery that no manufacturer could yet build the promised product reliably, answer choice D as the official key, and an explanation that the seller bore the risk because it knew the technology was not perfected when it contracted. 

The governing legal authority is UCC § 2-615. It excuses seller delay or non-delivery only when performance has become impracticable by a contingency whose nonoccurrence was a basic assumption of the contract, and it begins with the limiting phrase “Except so far as a seller may have assumed a greater obligation.” ([Legal Information Institute][1]) That authority supports the supplied key: Barnabas’s non-delivery is not excused because the risk that the experimental coating would fail was built into the bargain Barnabas chose to make.

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: seller promises goods using known unperfected technology; technology fails; seller claims impossibility/impracticability; buyer wins because seller assumed the risk.
