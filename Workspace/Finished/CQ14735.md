## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

Paul and Mary were driving home from a late-night Bible trivia tournament when they stopped at a 24-hour copy shop to pick up printed retreat handouts. Paul had offered to pay for the copies. While Paul waited near the copier, he was startled to see Mary step behind the unattended front counter, remove cash from a small till, and return to the car. Paul paid the clerk for the copies, and Mary drove away. Mary offered to reimburse Paul for the printing, but Paul declined. After the clerk discovered the missing cash, he called the police. Mary was later stopped, but she escaped with the stolen money and was never prosecuted.

If Paul is prosecuted for theft as an accomplice, should he be convicted?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

Can a passenger be convicted as an accomplice to theft when he learned of the theft only as it happened, paid for his own lawful purchase, and never intended to help the theft succeed?

### 3. Final Answer Choices

A. No, because Mary, the principal, was never prosecuted.

B. Yes, because Paul paid the clerk while he knew Mary was holding the stolen money.

C. No, because Paul had no intent to promote the commission of the offense.

D. Yes, because Paul facilitated commission of the offense by failing to make any effort to stop it.

### 5. Correct Answer

C (original key: A).

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Paul did not plan the theft, encourage it, agree to help it, or act with a purpose to make it succeed. Paying for the copies was the lawful transaction he had already offered to cover; it did not become intentional assistance to Mary's theft merely because he saw Mary holding stolen cash. Gold Key: accomplice liability needs purposeful promotion or facilitation of the crime, not just surprise, presence, silence, or after-the-fact awareness. Silver Key: because the call charges Paul as the helper, keep the axis on Paul's intent and aid instead of Mary's prosecution status.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because it points to the missing prosecution of the person who actually took the cash. Silver Key: the call charges Paul as the helper, so Mary's procedural status is not the answer axis. Modern accomplice liability does not depend on the principal being prosecuted or convicted; the decisive problem is Paul's lack of intent to help the theft.

B is attractive because Paul acted after seeing Mary with the cash, and that timing feels like participation. The breaker is that the act he performed was paying for his own copies. Gold Key: knowledge that another person has stolen property is not the same as purposeful aid in the theft.

D is attractive because doing nothing can feel like helping when a crime is happening in front of you. The breaker is that ordinary failure to stop another person's theft, without a legal duty and without purpose to help, is not the intent required for accomplice liability.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. Accomplice liability requires an act of assistance, solicitation, agreement, or attempted assistance plus the required culpable mental state. Model Penal Code section 2.06(3)(a) states the core rule in purpose terms: the person must act with the purpose of promoting or facilitating the offense. Rosemond v. United States, 572 U.S. 65 (2014), likewise treats aiding-and-abetting liability as requiring active participation with the intent to facilitate the crime's success. Model Penal Code section 2.06(7) also confirms that an accomplice may be convicted even though the principal has not been prosecuted or convicted.

Here, the theft was committed by Mary. Paul saw it as it happened, paid for the copies, declined reimbursement, and did nothing to stop Mary. Those facts do not show that Paul had the purpose of promoting or facilitating the theft. The credited answer therefore remains the no-conviction answer.
