## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Lydia does not need to prove that Stephen actually agreed to pay her. The facts point to a law-imposed restitution claim: she supplied emergency professional medical services, Stephen was unconscious the entire time, and she reasonably expected compensation when she began. Gold Key: emergency professional services supplied to an unconscious person can support reasonable-value restitution when intervention is justified and the provider expects payment; actual assent is not required. Silver Key: separate the two "Lydia wins" answers by legal source - actual conduct assent versus law-imposed restitution. The unconsciousness fact cuts actual assent; the expected-payment fact points to restitution.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the named-statute bait. A Good Samaritan statute sounds like the fact the exam writer wants you to use, but the call is not asking whether Lydia is immune from negligence liability. Gold Key: a Good Samaritan statute is a tort-immunity shield for qualifying emergency care; its mere existence does not automatically bar a reasonable-value restitution claim.

B sells the right winner through the wrong contract source, which is why it is the dominant trap. "Implied in fact" requires conduct showing actual assent, and Stephen never regained consciousness. Gold Key: emergency professional services can create restitution without actual assent, so the answer cannot call the obligation implied in fact. Silver Key: when one answer says "implied in fact" and another uses expected compensation, the clash is actual assent versus law-imposed restitution. The unconsciousness fact steers away from B.

D sells a result-based benefit trap. The law does not make reasonable compensation for emergency professional services depend on whether the patient recovered. The breaker is the reasonable value of the justified services when rendered, not a hindsight guarantee of medical success.

## 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule is implied-in-law contract / restitution for emergency professional services. Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment section 20, "Protection of Another's Life or Health," states the modern restitution rule for professional services required to protect another's life or health and measures recovery by a reasonable charge for the services. The classic common-law authority is Cotnam v. Wisdom, 83 Ark. 601, 104 S.W. 164 (1907), where surgeons who operated on an unconscious accident victim could recover reasonable compensation from the estate even though the patient never assented and died without regaining consciousness. The Cotnam court also rejected making recovery depend on successful benefit to the patient.

The Good Samaritan fact does not defeat the supplied key. Good Samaritan statutes are generally tort-immunity rules for emergency care, often limited to care rendered without compensation or expectation of compensation. An illustrative current statute, Minn. Stat. section 604A.01, subd. 2, gives immunity to a person who renders emergency care "without compensation or the expectation of compensation" and separately excludes a person receiving or expecting compensation. That confirms the statute is not a formation rule that automatically bars a restitution claim.
