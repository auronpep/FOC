### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A is incorrect.** It reaches the right outcome but gives the wrong legal reason. The acquittal is not because private attorney advice automatically excuses the crime. The acquittal follows because the believed advice shows Paul did not know he lacked privilege.

**C is incorrect.** This is the dominant trap. It states the attorney-advice rule in the abstract, but it answers the neighboring question: “Is advice of counsel a defense?” The actual call is narrower: “Did Paul have the mental state required by this statute?”

**Gold Key:** Even when attorney advice is not itself a defense, the belief produced by that advice can negate a required knowledge element.

**D is incorrect.** It swaps the statute’s subjective mental-state word for an objective negligence-style test. The statute asks whether Paul **knew** he had no privilege, not whether a reasonable person should have known the attorney was wrong.

### 8. Black-letter verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule is unchanged from the original Q14640: where an offense requires knowledge of a legal circumstance, a belief inconsistent with that knowledge can negate mens rea. The supplied official explanation confirms that the defendant’s belief that he had privilege meant he did not know he lacked privilege, so he lacked the required mental state. 

Lawyer-confirmation layer: Model Penal Code § 2.04(1)(a) states the general principle that ignorance or mistake as to fact or law is a defense when it negatives the required purpose, knowledge, belief, recklessness, or negligence. ([UMKC Law][1]) *Cheek v. United States* illustrates the related subjective point: a good-faith misunderstanding can negate a required willfulness element even if the belief is not objectively reasonable. ([Justia Law][2]) *Liparota v. United States* likewise confirms that when a statute requires knowledge that conduct is unauthorized, the government must prove that knowledge. ([Justia Law][3])
