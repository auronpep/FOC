A is correct. Common-law conspiracy is bilateral: two people must genuinely agree to the criminal plan. Barnabas never agreed to traffic stolen sound mixers; he only pretended to agree as an undercover officer. Gold Key: common-law conspiracy needs a real second guilty mind, but it does not require a separate overt act. Silver Key: once the answer array splits into Yes/No reasons, lock the jurisdiction label first. ## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law and Procedure, Inchoate Crimes, Conspiracy, and the verified outline code is `71040200`. The source rule and credited outcome are unchanged: under the common-law, bilateral approach to conspiracy, there must be at least two genuine parties to the criminal agreement. A person who agrees only with an undercover officer who is secretly trying to frustrate the crime cannot be convicted of common-law conspiracy on that pairing alone.

Primary authority supports the source key. In *Sears v. United States*, 343 F.2d 139 (5th Cir. 1965), the court recognized the bilateral point that an agreement only with a government informer secretly trying to stop the crime is not enough for conspiracy. Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/343/139/109353/. *United States v. Shabani*, 513 U.S. 10 (1994), confirms the separate common-law overt-act point: the agreement itself is the actus reus, and common law did not require a further overt act. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/513/10. The Model Penal Code's unilateral approach is the contrast, not the governing rule, because the stem expressly selects common law. No researched authority contradicts the supplied official key.
