### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**D is correct.** Lydia had authority to decide who could use the cannon during the event and handed it to Barnabas despite facts showing obvious unsafe use: visible intoxication and no experience. **Gold Key:** negligent entrustment looks to control/supply plus known or reasonably knowable risk of misuse, not formal legal title. **Silver Key:** lock the target defendant first; the call asks about Lydia’s duty, not Barnabas’s separate negligence.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells the tempting idea that once a dangerous object causes injury, the entrustor automatically pays. The breaker is the absolute language: “strictly liable” and “any injury” overstate a negligence theory and erase causation and reasonableness.

**B.** This choice sells a formal-title shortcut: only the owner sounds like the legally responsible person. **Gold Key:** negligent entrustment is a supply/control doctrine, so a person who controls access can be the relevant supplier even without title.

**C.** This choice sells the physical-actor shortcut: Barnabas fired the cannon, so Lydia seems too far away. **Silver Key:** the call is about Lydia’s duty, and “third-party conduct always cuts off duty” is an overclaim; foreseeable misuse by the person entrusted with the chattel is the very risk the doctrine targets.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The variation preserves the tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome: negligent entrustment can create direct negligence liability when a defendant supplies or permits use of a chattel to a person the defendant knows or has reason to know is likely to misuse it. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 390 describes liability for one who supplies a chattel to another whom the supplier knows or has reason to know is likely, because of inexperience or otherwise, to use it in a manner involving unreasonable risk of physical harm. ([Justia][1]) The same authority confirms that liability is not limited to the formal legal status of the transaction and turns on supplying the chattel with knowledge of likely unsafe use. ([Justia][1]) Restatement § 308 also supports the control framing: permitting a third person to use a thing under the actor’s control can be negligence when the actor knows or should know the third person is likely to use it in a way creating unreasonable risk. ([Penn State Open Resources][2])
