### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Adequacy of representation is unnecessary because the named plaintiff personally suffered the same injury as the class.**

This is the **dominant trap**. It is the most rule-precise of the three wrong answers, and it specifically targets the adequacy prong that the publisher's argument is raising. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: adequacy of representation is a separate Rule 23(a) prong, distinct from typicality and from the named plaintiff's personal injury. The named plaintiff and class counsel must "fairly protect the interests of absent class members" — that is a separate inquiry from whether the named plaintiff suffered the same injury. The two tests overlap in purpose (both screen for adequate representation) but they are separately stated in the rule and must be separately found.

**C. Numerosity and commonality are enough because the challenged policy affected hundreds of customers.**

This is the two-prong-missing trap. The student reads "hundreds of customers" and concludes that numerosity and commonality are the only relevant prongs. The breaker is that Rule 23(a) has four prongs, not two. Typicality and adequacy are also required. The size of the group satisfies numerosity (and arguably commonality), but the other two prongs are separate inquiries that the court must also find.

**D. Certification is automatic if the company used one policy, because commonality is the main Rule 23 requirement.**

This is the automatic-certification trap. The student reads "single policy" and concludes that commonality is dispositive. The breaker is that commonality is one prong, not the only prong. A single policy helps establish commonality (the legal and factual questions are common to the class), but it does not eliminate the other three prongs. Certification is never "automatic" — the court must find all four Rule 23(a) prongs and one Rule 23(b) category.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17443, a Civil Procedure item involving a Rule 23 class certification inquiry. The official key is C: the court must find numerosity, commonality, typicality, and adequacy, and the action must also fit within Rule 23(b).

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23(a) sets out four prerequisites for any class action: (1) numerosity — the class is so numerous that joinder of all members is impracticable; (2) commonality — there are questions of law or fact common to the class; (3) typicality — the claims or defenses of the representative parties are typical of the claims or defenses of the class; and (4) adequacy of representation — the representative parties will fairly and adequately protect the interests of the class. Rule 23(b) further requires that the action fit within one of three categories: (b)(1) prejudice from separate actions; (b)(2) injunctive or declaratory relief appropriate to the class as a whole; or (b)(3) common questions predominate and a class action is superior.

The Supreme Court's modern class-certification doctrine is summarized in Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338 (2011), which emphasized that commonality requires that the common questions be capable of generating common answers. The Fourth Circuit's decision in EQT Production Co. v. Adair, 764 F.3d 347 (4th Cir. 2014), provides additional structure for the typicality-and-adquacy analysis. The named plaintiff's personal suffering of the same injury establishes standing (under Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins, 578 U.S. 330 (2016), as refined in TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez, 594 U.S. 413 (2021)) and typicality, but it does not eliminate the adequacy prong — adequacy is a separate inquiry focused on the named plaintiff's and class counsel's ability to "fairly protect the interests of absent class members."

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: the court must find all four Rule 23(a) prongs and one Rule 23(b) category. Adequacy of representation is a separate prong, distinct from typicality and from the named plaintiff's personal injury, and it must be found in addition to the other three prongs.
