## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Barnabas wanted to buy Naomi's 180-acre orchard and convert it into a private wedding garden with a small Scripture-calligraphy studio. The main farmhouse was sound and would become the reception kitchen. The other outbuildings were old, and Barnabas planned to remove them after closing.

Barnabas and Naomi signed a contract selling "all real property known as Mercy Orchard, Parcel 77, consisting of 180 acres, one farmhouse, one stone equipment barn, and eight small wooden coops measuring about 60 square feet each" for $360,000, with closing scheduled 60 days later.

Three weeks after signing, Barnabas's finances collapsed and he decided not to buy. Before he told Naomi, a windstorm destroyed two of the small wooden coops. Barnabas immediately said he would not close because part of the property had been destroyed.

In a jurisdiction that has rejected equitable conversion, if Naomi sues Barnabas for damages for refusing to close, should the court find for Naomi?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

Can a seller enforce a land-sale contract with price abatement when only immaterial outbuildings are destroyed before closing?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. No, because risk of loss passed to Barnabas immediately when the contract was signed.

B. Yes, because the destroyed coops were immaterial parts of the realty and price abatement would be the proper adjustment.

C. No, because the property no longer matched the contract description.

D. No, because risk of loss from a no-fault casualty remained with Naomi until closing.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. The jurisdiction rejected equitable conversion, so Barnabas does not bear ordinary preclosing casualty risk just because he signed the contract. But the destroyed items were small, old coops he planned to remove. Gold Key: immaterial preclosing destruction does not let the buyer cancel; the contract may be enforced with price abatement. Silver Key: after spotting that seller bears risk, ask the second question: material or immaterial loss?

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because it states the equitable-conversion rule. The breaker is in the call: this jurisdiction rejected equitable conversion. The answer imports a doctrine the stem removed.

C is attractive because the contract specifically listed the coops. The trap treats any literal mismatch as cancellation. The better rule distinguishes material destruction from immaterial damage that can be handled by abatement.

D is the dominant trap. It correctly remembers that, without equitable conversion, preclosing risk generally remains with the seller. The breaker is that the loss was immaterial; that rule does not give Barnabas a walkaway right for two unwanted coops.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited outcome is unchanged: the seller may recover because the casualty affected only immaterial parts of the property and abatement is adequate. The tested issue is risk of loss in a land-sale contract where equitable conversion has been rejected.

Lawyer-confirmation authority: the Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act rule, reflected in New York General Obligations Law section 5-1311, states that when neither title nor possession has transferred, destruction of all or a material part prevents enforcement, but destruction of an immaterial part does not deprive either party of enforcement rights and calls for abatement. Source: https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GOB/5-1311. That rule confirms the supplied key's distinction between material and immaterial destruction.
