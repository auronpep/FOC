### 7. Right-Answer Explanation

**Choice B is correct.**

Evidence obtained through an unlawful search is not automatically excluded. The inevitable discovery doctrine provides that unlawfully obtained evidence is admissible if the prosecution proves by a preponderance of the evidence that the same evidence would have been discovered through a lawful, independent, standardized procedure.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIM-INEV-01):** Three elements make inevitable discovery apply: (1) a concrete lawful path existed — the written workplace-security agreement mandated inventory of all locked drawers when a supervisor was arrested; (2) the path was already in motion — the property inventory team had already been called; (3) discovery through that path was inevitable — the written policy expressly required inventorying locked drawers, leaving no officer discretion to skip them. All three elements are supplied by the stem. The cocaine would have been found regardless of the officer's premature, unlawful opening of the drawer.

**Silver Key (SK-CRIM-INEV-CHECKLIST-01):** On inevitable discovery questions, confirm all three elements in the stem before locking the answer: (1) written/standardized lawful procedure covering the exact space; (2) that procedure was already actively in motion; (3) no discretion to skip the space — inevitability, not mere possibility. When all three are present, inevitable discovery is the argument to lock.

### 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A** captures the reflex that an unlawful search means suppression. The officer did open the drawer unlawfully — that is accurate — and the fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine says evidence derived from unconstitutional conduct is excluded. But FPT is not absolute. The inevitable discovery exception allows the evidence to be admitted when the prosecution proves the same discovery would have resulted from a lawful process already underway. Stopping the analysis at the initial unlawful act misses the exception. A treats "found it unlawfully" as a rule with no exceptions; it is a rule with at least four exceptions.

**Gold Key (tested_choice A):** Inevitable discovery is one of the FPT exceptions. Unlawful discovery + standardized lawful path already in motion + written policy requiring the specific space = evidence survives suppression.

**Choice C** invents a consent requirement for inventory searches. Standardized inventory searches are an administrative caretaking exception to the warrant requirement — their validity depends on whether a standardized, written policy requires them, not on whether the individual subject agrees. Requiring consent would defeat the administrative purpose of inventory (uniformity, protection against claims of theft, documentation of property). No consent is required when a lawful standardized inventory procedure covers the search.

**Choice D** invokes a search incident to arrest theory to justify the locked-drawer search. SITA authorizes a search of the area within the arrestee's immediate control at the time of the arrest — it is about officer safety and preserving evidence of the crime of arrest, not a blank authorization to access all locked compartments of a supervisor's workspace. If Barnabas had already been moved away from the workstation before the drawer was opened, SITA does not reach the locked drawer. More critically, the stem identifies a superior argument: the inventory-inevitable-discovery path is supported by every fact in the stem, while the SITA argument is not.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIM-SITA-GANT-01, tested_choice D):** SITA is limited to the area within the arrestee's immediate control at the time of the arrest. It does not authorize searching all locked compartments of a workspace after the arrestee has been moved. When the stem provides a complete inevitable discovery path, that argument is stronger than an overbroad SITA claim.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Rule — Inevitable Discovery:** Evidence obtained through an unlawful search is admissible if the prosecution establishes by a preponderance of the evidence that it would inevitably have been discovered through an independent, standardized, lawful process. Three conditions: (1) a concrete lawful path existed; (2) that path was actively being pursued; (3) discovery through it was inevitable, not merely possible.

**Rule — Inventory Exception:** Standardized inventory searches conducted pursuant to written departmental policy are a recognized exception to the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement, justified by administrative caretaking purposes. They do not require consent, a warrant, or probable cause. Their validity turns on whether a uniform policy existed and was followed.

**Rule — SITA Limits:** Under *Arizona v. Gant*, 556 U.S. 332 (2009), a search of a vehicle incident to arrest is valid only when the arrestee is unsecured and within reaching distance of the vehicle at the time of the search, or when it is reasonable to believe the vehicle contains evidence of the crime of arrest. For non-vehicle SITA under *Chimel v. California*, 395 U.S. 752 (1969), only the area within the arrestee's immediate control at the time of the arrest is covered. A locked drawer in a workspace from which the arrestee has been removed is not within immediate control.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer):**
- *Nix v. Williams*, 467 U.S. 431 (1984) — inevitable discovery doctrine; prosecution bears preponderance burden; evidence admitted if standardized lawful procedure would have found it.
- *South Dakota v. Opperman*, 428 U.S. 364 (1976) — standardized inventory searches are an exception to the warrant requirement.
- *Colorado v. Bertine*, 479 U.S. 367 (1987) — inventory searches conducted pursuant to uniform written policy are valid; no consent required; locked containers may be included if policy so requires.
- *Florida v. Wells*, 495 U.S. 1 (1990) — inventory policy must be standardized, not left to individual officer discretion.
- *Chimel v. California*, 395 U.S. 752 (1969) — SITA limited to area within immediate control at time of arrest.
- *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963) — FPT doctrine; subject to exceptions.

#### wrong_answer_recovery_paths

**Choice A — FPT absolutism (dominant trap):**
Why picked: The officer DID open the drawer unlawfully, and the FPT doctrine says that evidence from unconstitutional searches is excluded. "Found it unlawfully = suppress" is the default rule, and it's seductive because the premise (unlawful opening) is accurate.
Skipped move: Recognizing that FPT has recognized exceptions, specifically inevitable discovery. The unlawful discovery is not the end of the analysis — it is only the beginning.
Recovery: Memorize the FPT exceptions: inevitable discovery, independent source, attenuation, good faith. When the stem shows a written policy covering the exact space and the inventory already in motion, run the inevitable discovery checklist before defaulting to suppress.

**Choice C — Consent-required myth:**
Why picked: Students who think of inventory searches as a "lesser form of search" requiring some form of permission may feel that the subject's agreement is needed, or that without consent, the search's caretaking justification fails.
Skipped move: Knowing that standardized inventory searches are a warrant exception that operates independently of consent. Their validity depends on the existence of a uniform written policy, not on individual agreement.
Recovery: Inventory searches have one validity test: does a written, standardized, non-discretionary policy exist and was it followed? Consent is irrelevant to that test.

**Choice D — SITA overbroad:**
Why picked: Students who know the SITA doctrine and see an arrest in the stem may default to "SITA = search the area" without thinking about the immediate-control limitation, especially if they're used to thinking of workplace supervisors as having authority over their own workstations.
Skipped move: Checking whether the arrested person was within reaching distance of the locked drawer at the time the drawer was opened. If he had already been moved from the workstation (as the stem implies), SITA does not reach the locked drawer.
Recovery: SITA = immediate control at time of arrest. If the person is moved before the search, the locked space is outside SITA's reach. When the stem provides a complete inevitable discovery path, that is the stronger argument.
