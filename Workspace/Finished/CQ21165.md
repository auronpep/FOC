### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. Common-law arson is the malicious burning of the dwelling of another. Gold Key: for this rule, "of another" is about habitation or possessory occupancy, not record title. Stephen has the one-year possessory dwelling interest, so the cottage is another person's dwelling for arson purposes even though Lydia owns it. Gold Key: the burning element does not require the house to be destroyed; actual charring of part of the wooden structure is enough.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A pulls hard because title ownership feels like control over the property. The breaker is that common-law arson is framed around the safety of another person's habitation, so the owner/tenant label does not give the title owner immunity when the tenant has the dwelling possession.

B sells a more dramatic mental-state requirement. The breaker is that common-law arson requires a malicious burning, not a purpose to kill the person living there. The dangerous fire is enough; a homicide purpose is not an element.

D sells an intuitive picture of "arson" as a house burned to the ground. The breaker is that the law requires an actual burning of the structure, and charring part of the wooden steps satisfies that requirement without total destruction.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged. Common-law arson is the malicious burning of the dwelling of another. The "another" element protects habitation or occupancy rather than title ownership; Colorado annotations to People ex rel. VanMeveren v. District Court, 619 P.2d 494 (Colo. 1980), state that common-law arson was about possession or occupancy rather than ownership. Cornell Wex states the common-law definition as malicious burning of another's dwelling, and CALI's criminal law text states the same habitation/occupancy framing. On the burning element, People v. LeFebre, 546 P.2d 952 (Colo. 1976), is cited in the Colorado annotations for the rule that total destruction is unnecessary when part of the structure is burned. Those authorities confirm the source key: Lydia can be guilty because Stephen's lease makes the cottage another person's dwelling for arson purposes and the cedar steps were actually charred.
