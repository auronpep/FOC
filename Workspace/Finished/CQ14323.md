## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final Question

The state of Olive sent three employees, Lydia, Stephen, and Ruth, to a city in the state of Cedar to consult with a private restoration studio about handbells for Olive's state museum exhibit on historic worship music. While acting in the course of their employment, the three Olive employees negligently loaded several heavy handbell cases onto a lift at the studio, causing the cases to fall into a public walkway and injure several Cedar residents.

The injured Cedar residents sued Lydia, Stephen, Ruth, and the state of Olive in Cedar state court for their injuries. At trial, all defendants admitted the Cedar court's jurisdiction, fully participated, and argued that Olive law limited Olive's liability to $150,000 in any tort case. The Cedar court rejected that defense and entered a final $7.2 million judgment against all defendants.

The Cedar residents then brought a proper enforcement proceeding in an appropriate Olive court. In that proceeding, Olive again argues that its liability is capped at $150,000 under Olive law. Lydia, Stephen, and Ruth can together pay only $80,000 of the judgment, so full satisfaction depends on recovering the balance from Olive.

May the Cedar residents recover the full balance of the Cedar judgment from Olive in the Olive enforcement proceeding?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A state fully litigated and lost a tort case in another state's court, then the winning plaintiffs seek to enforce the final judgment in the defendant state's own court. Can the defendant state use its own damages cap to reduce the sister-state judgment?

### 3. Final Answer Choices

C. No, because Lydia, Stephen, and Ruth were negligent and therefore were not authorized to act for Olive.

D. Yes, because a cap on damages against Olive for torts committed by its agents would violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. Olive fully participated in the Cedar action, admitted Cedar's jurisdiction, raised the damages-cap defense, lost, and let the judgment become final. The Olive proceeding is not a new merits trial about what Olive law would have done before judgment. It is an enforcement proceeding for a final sister-state judgment. Gold Key: when a sister-state judgment is final and jurisdiction was not open to collateral attack, the enforcing state must give the judgment full faith and credit; its own public policy or damages cap does not reopen the amount. Silver Key: once the stem shifts from "trial" to "enforcement proceeding," stop asking which state's tort cap is better and ask whether the second court may relitigate the judgment.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because it sounds like the defendant state should control its own treasury and tort policy in its own courts. The breaker is that the case is past the policy-choice stage: a final Cedar judgment already exists, and the Full Faith and Credit rule defeats a public-policy refusal to enforce it. Gold Key: a state-law damages cap may matter before judgment, but it cannot be used to relitigate a final sister-state judgment.

C is attractive because negligence sounds like a personal frolic rather than state action. The stem gives the breaker: Lydia, Stephen, and Ruth were acting in the course of their Olive employment, and Olive itself was a named defendant that litigated to judgment.

D is attractive because it reaches the right yes/no result and dresses the answer in a constitutional-rights frame. The breaker is the reason: ordinary damages caps against a state do not become equal-protection violations merely because they limit tort recovery, and the final-judgment enforcement rule is doing the work here.

### 9. Black-Letter Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. Article IV, Section 1 of the U.S. Constitution requires full faith and credit for sister-state judicial proceedings, and Congress implemented that command in 28 U.S.C. Section 1738, which requires state judicial proceedings to receive the same effect in every court that they have in the rendering state. Underwriters Nat'l Assurance Co. v. North Carolina Life & Accident & Health Ins. Guaranty Ass'n, 455 U.S. 691 (1982), confirms that a judgment is entitled to full faith and credit, including as to jurisdictional issues, when those issues were fully and fairly litigated and finally decided. Baker v. General Motors Corp., 522 U.S. 222 (1998), confirms there is no roving public-policy exception to full faith and credit for judgments.

Current sovereign-immunity doctrine does not change the credited answer on these facts. Franchise Tax Board of California v. Hyatt, 139 S. Ct. 1485 (2019), holds that states retain sovereign immunity from private suits in other states' courts without consent, but Olive admitted Cedar's jurisdiction, fully participated, raised the cap defense, and is now facing enforcement of a final judgment. The Olive court must enforce the full Cedar judgment, not reduce it to Olive's tort cap.
