Timothy, a 14-year-old boy of low intelligence, received his parents' permission to take the family's snowmobile out on the backcountry trails. He had had very little experience operating a snowmobile and was below the age at which the state issues a snowmobile operator's certificate. Although he did the best he could, he lost control of the machine and struck Hannah, who was snowshoeing along the trail. Hannah has brought a negligence action against Timothy. A young teen of low intelligence with very little experience, doing the best he could, loses control of a motorized vehicle and injures a bystander, who sues him for negligence. Is he judged by the lenient "reasonable child" standard, or the adult standard of care?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**D is correct.** Operating a snowmobile is a dangerous activity characteristically undertaken by adults, so Timothy is held to the **adult** standard of care — the care of a reasonably prudent snowmobile operator. The standard is **not** adjusted downward for his youth, his low intelligence, or his inexperience; the law measures him against the reasonable adult precisely because those traits put others on the trail at risk. That he "did the best he could" is irrelevant. Losing control and striking Hannah readily shows he fell below that standard, and his negligence caused her injury, so Hannah prevails.

**Gold Key:** the child standard (reasonable child of like age, intelligence, and experience) switches off and the adult standard switches on the moment a minor operates a motorized vehicle — the youth/low-intelligence/inexperience facts become decoys, not defenses.

**Silver Key:** when two choices reach the same outcome ("Yes"), credit the one whose reason is the actual legal basis for liability; D names the operative reason (adult activity → adult standard → breach), so it beats the same-outcome decoy B, which rests on a non-dispositive status.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

- **A (No — acted reasonably for a low-intelligence, inexperienced 14-year-old).** This is the answer the rule normally rewards: minors really are judged by the standard of a reasonable child of like age, intelligence, and experience, so applying that lenient yardstick feels like textbook law. The breaker is the adult-activity exception — operating a motorized vehicle flips the standard to the adult one, where low intelligence and inexperience earn no discount. **Gold Key:** a minor operating a motor vehicle is held to the adult standard; the child standard does not apply, so "reasonable for a 14-year-old" never reaches the question.
- **B (Yes — too young to be issued an operator's certificate).** It lands on the right outcome and sounds rigorous — he was legally too young to be on the machine, so of course he loses. The breaker is that being underage or unlicensed is a status, not a breach: absent a statute making it negligence per se, the failure to hold the required certificate is not itself evidence that he operated carelessly. **Silver Key:** a "Yes" that rests on a non-dispositive status (too young, unlicensed) is the decoy; the credited "Yes" names the conduct standard, not the paperwork.
- **C (No — only Timothy's parents are subject to liability).** The parents' permission and ownership make it tempting to push all liability up to them. The breaker is the word "only": the minor who negligently operates the vehicle is himself liable for the harm he causes, and the parents' potential liability does not immunize him — both can be sued.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

**Tested rule (unchanged from the original):** A child is ordinarily held to the standard of a reasonably careful person of the same age, intelligence, and experience. **Exception:** when the child engages in a dangerous activity that is characteristically undertaken by adults (operating a motor vehicle, motorboat, snowmobile, aircraft, or powered machinery), the child is held to the **adult** reasonable-person standard, with **no** downward adjustment for age, low intelligence, or inexperience.

**Why D is correct under the governing rule:** Operating a snowmobile is squarely within the adult-activities exception, so the adult standard governs. Under that objective standard, Timothy's subjective limitations (low intelligence, inexperience, sincere effort) are immaterial; the only question is whether his operation fell below that of a reasonably prudent operator, which losing control and striking a bystander shows. Hannah therefore prevails.
