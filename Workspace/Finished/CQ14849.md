### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (C)

**C is correct.** The bystander's first statement — "That reckless guy is jumping up and down on the platform" — was admitted at trial as a hearsay statement (likely as an excited utterance under FRE 803(2) or a present sense impression under FRE 801(d)(1)). The bystander is therefore a hearsay declarant. Under FRE 806, when a hearsay statement has been admitted in evidence, the declarant's credibility may be attacked by any evidence that would be admissible for that purpose if the declarant had testified as a witness. This includes impeachment by prior inconsistent statement.

The second statement — "the whole thing just gave way under him" — directly contradicts the first statement by asserting that the platform malfunctioned rather than that Peter was at fault. It is therefore admissible as a prior inconsistent statement to impeach the bystander's credibility.

However, the second statement is hearsay if offered to prove the truth of what it asserts (that the platform's bolts snapped). It was not made under oath at a proceeding, so it does not qualify as a prior inconsistent statement admissible for substantive purposes under FRE 801(d)(1)(A). No other hearsay exception applies. Accordingly, it is admissible only for impeachment, not as substantive evidence of the construction company's negligence.

> **Gold Key (FRE 806 unlock):** When a hearsay statement is admitted, the declarant is treated "in effect as a witness" for impeachment purposes — but the impeachment rules are *more* permissive for hearsay declarants than for actual witnesses. FRE 806 allows the inconsistent statement "regardless of when it occurred or whether the declarant had an opportunity to explain or deny it." This is the reverse of the usual FRE 613(b) foundation requirement.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect.** This is the dominant trap. It correctly identifies that the second statement can be used to impeach the bystander's credibility, but it overclaims by asserting that the statement is also admissible as substantive evidence to prove negligence. A prior inconsistent statement is admissible as substantive evidence under FRE 801(d)(1)(A) only if it was given under oath at a prior trial, hearing, or other proceeding, or in a deposition. The bystander's casual remark to a friend the next day was not made under oath at any proceeding. No other hearsay exception applies to the second statement. It is therefore hearsay without an exception if offered for the truth of the matter asserted, and it must be excluded for substantive purposes. The statement is admissible *only* for impeachment.

> **Gold Key:** This trap is killed by the Gold Key — FRE 806 lets you impeach the hearsay declarant, but it does not transform the impeaching statement into non-hearsay. The second statement still needs its own hearsay exception to come in substantively, and none applies here.

**B is incorrect.** This distractor exploits the misconception that only a trial witness can be impeached. The bystander was never called as a witness — she is a hearsay declarant. FRE 806 explicitly provides that a hearsay declarant's credibility may be attacked "by any evidence that would be admissible for those purposes if the declarant had testified as a witness." The rule treats the declarant as "in effect a witness" for impeachment purposes. The fact that the bystander did not take the stand is irrelevant.

> **Silver Key:** When you see a question about impeaching someone who never testified, check whether that person is a hearsay declarant. If so, FRE 806 is the governing rule, and the usual witness-centered limitations do not apply.

**D is incorrect.** This distractor applies the foundation requirement of FRE 613(b), which generally requires that a witness be given an opportunity to explain or deny a prior inconsistent statement before extrinsic evidence of that statement is admitted. However, the bystander is a hearsay declarant, not a trial witness. FRE 806 contains an explicit override: "The court may admit evidence of the declarant's inconsistent statement or conduct, regardless of when it occurred or whether the declarant had an opportunity to explain or deny it." The opportunity-to-explain requirement simply does not apply to hearsay declarants.

> **Silver Key:** FRE 613(b) foundation requirements apply to trial witnesses. FRE 806 applies to hearsay declarants — and it explicitly dispenses with the opportunity-to-explain requirement. Spot which rule governs before applying any foundation requirement.

### 9. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Authority

**Tested Rule:** Under FRE 806, when a hearsay statement has been admitted in evidence, the declarant's credibility may be attacked by any evidence admissible for that purpose if the declarant had testified as a witness. The court may admit evidence of the declarant's inconsistent statement regardless of when it occurred or whether the declarant had an opportunity to explain or deny it. However, a prior inconsistent statement not made under oath at a proceeding is admissible only for impeachment, not as substantive evidence, under FRE 801(d)(1)(A).

**Authority:**
- **FRE 806** — "When a hearsay statement ... has been admitted in evidence, the declarant's credibility may be attacked ... by any evidence that would be admissible for those purposes if the declarant had testified as a witness. The court may admit evidence of the declarant's inconsistent statement or conduct, regardless of when it occurred or whether the declarant had an opportunity to explain or deny it."
- **FRE 801(d)(1)(A)** — Prior inconsistent statement is not hearsay only if "the declarant testifies at the trial or hearing and is subject to cross-examination concerning the statement, and the statement is ... inconsistent with the declarant's testimony, and was given under oath subject to the penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, or other proceeding, or in a deposition."
- **FRE 613(b)** — Extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement by a witness is generally inadmissible unless the witness is afforded an opportunity to explain or deny the statement (the foundation requirement that FRE 806 overrides for hearsay declarants).
- **Advisory Committee Notes to FRE 806** — The declarant of a hearsay statement "is in effect a witness" and "[h]is credibility should in fairness be subject to impeachment and support as though he had in fact testified." The Notes explain that the opportunity-to-explain requirement is impractical for hearsay declarants because the inconsistent statement may be a subsequent one.
- **Cornell LII, FRE 806:** https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_806

**Verification:** The credited answer (A in the original, C in the variant) is correct. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original. The second statement is admissible for impeachment under FRE 806 but is hearsay without an exception if offered substantively. No doctrinal-currency issues — FRE 806 has not been amended on this point.

### 13. Review Truth

When a hearsay declarant's statement has been admitted at trial, FRE 806 allows the opposing party to impeach the declarant with an inconsistent statement — even if the declarant was never called as a witness and even without giving the declarant a chance to explain or deny it — but a prior inconsistent statement not made under oath at a proceeding is admissible only for impeachment, not as substantive evidence of the truth.

### 2. The 17-Section Student Case Study

#### Program Frame

This item tests the intersection of hearsay and impeachment — specifically, the rules governing when a party may impeach a hearsay declarant with a prior inconsistent statement, and the limits on using that statement for substantive purposes. The governing rule is FRE 806, which treats a hearsay declarant "in effect as a witness" for impeachment purposes but with more permissive foundation requirements than FRE 613. The item is anchor-assisted: the student needs two Gold Keys to cleanly eliminate the dominant trap and the FRE 613 misfit traps.

#### Student Access Test

A student who has learned the C3 method and the Gold/Silver Keys for this item can solve it without outside legal doctrine. The stem facts are sufficient: the bystander's first statement was admitted (making her a hearsay declarant), the second statement contradicts the first, the bystander was never called, and the second statement was a casual remark not under oath. The Gold Keys unlock the traps; the Silver Keys guide navigation.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** Peter sued a construction company for injuries he sustained when a scaffolding platform at a church-building project collapsed and he fell. At trial, after Peter rested his case, the construction company called a witness who testified that just before the collapse he had heard a bystander say to a friend, "That reckless guy is jumping up and down on the platform." Peter then offered the testimony of another witness who would testify that the next day she was with the same bystander, and that in describing the incident, the bystander told her that the platform's bolts had snapped and "the whole thing just gave way under him."

**Call:** How should the court rule with respect to this offered testimony?

**Choices:**
- A: Rule it admissible to impeach the bystander's credibility and to prove the construction company's negligence.
- B: Rule it inadmissible, because the bystander herself was not called as a witness.
- C: Rule it admissible only to impeach the bystander's credibility.
- D: Rule it inadmissible, because the bystander was given no opportunity to deny or explain her apparently inconsistent statement.

#### Distilled Core Question

A hearsay declarant's out-of-court statement was admitted at trial. The opposing party now offers a different out-of-court statement by the same declarant that contradicts the first statement. The declarant was never called as a witness. Is the second statement admissible, and if so, for what purpose?

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks how the court should rule on the admissibility of the second statement. The student should predict that the statement is admissible for some purpose (impeachment) but not for others (substantive proof), because the second statement contradicts the first but was not made under oath at a proceeding.

#### Trigger Facts

1. The bystander's first statement was admitted at trial → the bystander is a hearsay declarant.
2. The second statement contradicts the first → it is a prior inconsistent statement.
3. The bystander was never called as a witness → FRE 806, not FRE 613, governs.
4. The second statement was made the next day to a friend, not under oath → not admissible substantively under FRE 801(d)(1)(A).

#### Governing C3 Lane

**CUT → CLASH → CALL.** The item is decided at the CLASH phase: two choices (C and D) present competing views on admissibility, and the splitting fact is whether FRE 613 or FRE 806 governs impeachment of a hearsay declarant.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "Admissible to impeach and to prove negligence"**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice says the statement can be used for TWO purposes — impeachment AND proving negligence. That's a red flag. When a choice claims a statement is admissible for multiple purposes, check whether each purpose is independently supported. The second statement was a casual remark to a friend the next day — was it under oath at a proceeding? No. So it can't come in substantively under the prior inconsistent statement exemption.
2. **Student label:** Admissible for both impeachment and negligence.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would say the statement is admissible for impeachment only, because the statement doesn't meet the requirements for substantive admissibility.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 801(d)(1)(A) limits substantive prior inconsistent statements to those under oath at a proceeding. No other hearsay exception applies to a casual remark to a friend. The statement is hearsay without an exception if offered for the truth.

> **Gold Key (GK-EVIDENCE-PRIOR-INCONSIST-02):** A prior inconsistent statement is admissible as substantive evidence under FRE 801(d)(1)(A) ONLY if made under oath at a prior proceeding or deposition. This statement was not. Admissible for impeachment only.

**Choice B: "Inadmissible because bystander not called as a witness"**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice says the bystander can't be impeached because she never testified. But the bystander's statement WAS admitted at trial — she's a hearsay declarant. FRE 806 exists precisely for this situation.
2. **Student label:** Inadmissible because bystander not called.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would recognize that FRE 806 allows hearsay declarants to be impeached even though they never took the stand.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 806 explicitly provides that a hearsay declarant's credibility may be attacked as if the declarant had testified. The bystander not being called is irrelevant.

> **Gold Key (GK-EVIDENCE-HEARSAY-IMPEACH-01):** FRE 806 lets you impeach a hearsay declarant more freely than a trial witness — no foundation, no opportunity to explain required.

> **Silver Key (SK-EVIDENCE-DECLARANT-01):** When you see a question about impeaching someone who never testified, check if they are a hearsay declarant. If so, FRE 806 governs, not FRE 613.

**Choice C: "Admissible only to impeach"**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice correctly limits the statement to impeachment only. The second statement contradicts the first (impeachment use), but it wasn't under oath (no substantive use). This is the true-and-responsive answer.
2. **Student label:** Admissible only to impeach.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This IS the true-and-responsive version.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 806 permits impeachment; FRE 801(d)(1)(A) does not permit substantive use because the statement was not under oath at a proceeding.

**Choice D: "Inadmissible because no opportunity to explain or deny"**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice applies the FRE 613 foundation requirement (opportunity to explain or deny). But the bystander is a hearsay declarant, not a trial witness. FRE 806 explicitly overrides this requirement.
2. **Student label:** Inadmissible because no opportunity to explain.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A true version would recognize that FRE 806 dispenses with the opportunity-to-explain requirement for hearsay declarants.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 806 states the court may admit the inconsistent statement "regardless of when it occurred or whether the declarant had an opportunity to explain or deny it."

> **Silver Key (SK-EVIDENCE-DECLARANT-02):** FRE 613(b) foundation requirements apply to trial witnesses. FRE 806 applies to hearsay declarants — and it explicitly dispenses with the opportunity-to-explain requirement. Spot which rule governs before applying any foundation requirement.

#### Residual Answer

**C** is the residual answer. It is the only choice that is both true (the statement IS admissible for impeachment) and responsive (it correctly limits the use to impeachment only, excluding substantive use).
