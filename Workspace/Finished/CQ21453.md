### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam register)

**Gold Key:** A bill of attainder is a law that singles out a definable group and imposes a disability or punishment on them without a judicial trial, without requiring proof of individual knowledge or intent. The Bill of Attainder Clause (Art. I, § 9, cl. 3) bars such laws regardless of whether the legislature labels the penalty "criminal."

The statute here penalizes Samuel — he is a member of a defined group (past members of the Open Hand Cooperative) and receives a disability (permanent ineligibility for a category of federal employment). The critical defect is that the law applies **without regard to whether Samuel knew** about the group's illegal activities. An innocent member who joined for craft workshops is treated identically to one who knowingly aided the scheme. This is precisely the kind of legislative punishment-without-individualized-finding that the Bill of Attainder Clause prohibits. *See* United States v. Brown, 381 U.S. 437 (1965) (striking down law barring Communist Party members from union office without requiring knowledge of party's illegal aims). Choice C correctly identifies the constitutional defect.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**D (original A) — "unconstitutional, because it is an ex post facto law"**
*Attractive because:* A student who spots retroactive application may reflexively reach for the ex post facto label — it's a familiar Clause and the law clearly applies to past membership.
*Breaker:* The Ex Post Facto Clause applies only to **criminal** penalties. *Calder v. Bull*, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 386 (1798). A job-eligibility bar is a civil disability, not a criminal punishment. Calling it "ex post facto" overcriminalizes the analysis. Gold Key: Attainder, not ex post facto, is the rightClause for non-criminal legislative punishment of a defined group.

**A (original C) — "constitutional, because handling federal procurements is a privilege, not a right"**
*Attractive because:* The "privilege vs. right" distinction is a recurring bar-exam trope, and employment eligibility does sound privilege-adjacent.
*Breaker:* Even if federal procurement employment is not a "right," declaring someone ineligible for it is still a **disability**. The Constitution prohibits imposing that disability on the basis of mere group membership absent individualized findings. The privilege framing cannot save a bill of attainder. United States v. Brown, 381 U.S. at 455–56 (rejecting the argument that union office is merely a privilege the legislature can condition on any ground).

**B (original D) — "constitutional, because the federal government may protect the integrity of its procurement process"**
*Attractive because:* National-security and government-self-protection reasoning *feels* weighty, and on many MBE questions the government does have broad power to set employment qualifications. A student may reason: the government needs reliable procurement handlers, and Congress gets to decide who is trustworthy.
*Breaker:* Silver Key: When a "government interest" answer choice addresses a **different element** than the statute's actual defect, it's not responsive. The law does not ask whether the member *holds views inconsistent with* statutory entitlements — it penalizes mere *membership*, regardless of the member's own views or knowledge. The government's interest in procurement integrity cannot cure a bill of attainder: Congress may disqualify persons who **knowingly** support illegal aims, but it may not write a law that sweeps in innocent members based solely on associational status. The self-protection rationale misfits the actual legal defect.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Tested doctrine:** The Bill of Attainder Clause, Art. I, § 9, cl. 3.

**Governing rule:** A bill of attainder is a legislative act that singles out an identifiable person or group, imposes a punishment or disability, and does so without the benefit of a judicial trial. *Nixon v. Adm'r of Gen. Servs.*, 433 U.S. 425, 468 (1977). The Clause is to be given a liberal, functional construction. *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 447–49 (1965). It is not limited to death sentences; it extends to any "punishment," which includes banishment from a type of employment or deprivation of a means of livelihood. *See also* Cummings v. Missouri, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 277 (1866) (oath requirement barring former Confederates from professions is punitive); Ex parte Garland, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333 (1866) (same).

**The key distinction — knowledge:** A statute that bars members of an organization from a position is unconstitutional as a bill of attainder **if it does not require proof that the member knew of the organization's illegal purposes**. *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437 (1965). By contrast, a statute requiring individualized findings of knowing affiliation with illegal aims may survive. *American Communications Ass'n v. Douds*, 339 U.S. 382 (1950) (upholding non-Communist union officer affidavit because it targeted knowing advocacy, not mere membership).

**Ex Post Facto Clause distinction:** The Ex Post Facto Clause, Art. I, § 9, cl. 3, applies only to retroactive **criminal** legislation. *Calder v. Bull*, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 386 (1798). A civil employment bar is not criminal punishment and therefore does not qualify.

**Outcome confirmed:** The credited answer (C, original B) is correct. Research supports the official key. No doctrinal drift detected.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 9, cl. 3; United States v. Brown, 381 U.S. 437 (1965); Nixon v. Adm'r of Gen. Servs., 433 U.S. 425 (1977); Calder v. Bull, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 386 (1798).
