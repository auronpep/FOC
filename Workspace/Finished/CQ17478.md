### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Due process screening of eyewitness identification evidence is triggered by an unnecessarily suggestive identification procedure arranged by law enforcement. Here, the suggestive event was a private media broadcast. Police did not supply the photo, ask for the broadcast, or otherwise arrange the exposure. That means the constitutional due process suppression framework does not require exclusion. The defense may still attack Martha's reliability through cross-examination, jury argument, and ordinary evidentiary safeguards.

**Gold Key:** Due process identification suppression requires police-arranged suggestiveness. If the suggestive exposure came from a private actor or media source that police did not arrange, the constitutional suppression door stays closed.

**Silver Key:** In an identification-suppression call, ask first who created the suggestive exposure. If the answer is not law enforcement, cut the due-process-suppression answers that treat all suggestiveness as constitutionally identical.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A** is the dominant trap because it feels fair: a suspect-labeled booking photo can strongly influence a witness. The breaker is that due process suppression is not a general reliability filter for every suggestive circumstance. **Gold Key:** Perry limits the constitutional screening rule to suggestive circumstances arranged by law enforcement, so a private broadcast not arranged by police does not by itself create a due process violation.

**B** aims at the wrong object. The motion is not asking whether the livestream segment itself is admissible as trial evidence. The question is whether Martha's later identification must be suppressed on due process grounds. **Silver Key:** Keep the call locked on the identification procedure; an answer about admitting news coverage answers a different question.

**D** overstates the reliability point. Seeing a booking photograph may affect how persuasive the identification is, but reliability is not "always irrelevant" and the due process inquiry does not fail because courts ignore reliability in every case. The constitutional claim fails earlier here because no law-enforcement-arranged suggestive procedure created the exposure.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

**Governing authority:** *Perry v. New Hampshire*, 565 U.S. 228 (2012), holds that the Due Process Clause does not require a preliminary judicial reliability inquiry for an eyewitness identification unless the identification was procured under unnecessarily suggestive circumstances arranged by law enforcement. The Court situated that rule in the earlier identification cases, including *Neil v. Biggers*, 409 U.S. 188 (1972), and *Manson v. Brathwaite*, 432 U.S. 98 (1977), which apply once improper police arrangement is present. Later references, including *Sexton v. Beaudreaux*, 585 U.S. ___ (2018), repeat that due-process concerns arise when law enforcement uses an identification procedure that is both suggestive and unnecessary.

The transformed facts keep the legal issue and credited outcome unchanged. The witness saw a suspect-labeled image before any police lineup or photo array, and the police did not arrange, encourage, or supply the exposure. Under *Perry*, that private/media-created suggestiveness may bear on weight and credibility, but it does not require constitutional suppression. The correct answer is therefore **C**.
