## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question
Lydia lawfully divided her family's old apple orchard into 12 large homesites and recorded a plat named Psalm Ridge. The recorded plat imposed no restrictions on any of the 12 homesites. Six weeks after recording the plat, Lydia conveyed Lot 4 to Peter by a deed that contained no restriction on the lot's use. Sales then stalled. Eighteen months later, after the county amended its zoning ordinance to permit professional offices in residential zones, the market improved. During the next four months, Lydia sold and conveyed 10 of the remaining 11 homesites. In each of those 10 deeds, Lydia included the following language: "It is a term and condition of this conveyance, which shall be a covenant running with the land for the benefit of each of the 12 lots shown on the recorded Psalm Ridge plat, that for 12 years from the recording of the plat, the premises may be used only for single-family residential purposes." Peter had actual knowledge of the later deeds. Shortly after Lydia's most recent sale, while Lydia still owned one unsold homesite, Peter built a one-story cottage on Lot 4 and conveyed Lot 4 to Martha, an accountant. The deed to Martha made no reference to any use restriction. Martha applied for an appropriate certificate of occupancy to use one room of the cottage as a professional office. Lydia, on behalf of herself as owner of the unsold homesite and on behalf of the other lot owners, sued to enjoin Martha from opening the office and to impose the quoted restriction on Lot 4. Who is likely to prevail?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
A lot was sold unrestricted before a developer later began putting a residential-use covenant in other subdivision deeds. Can that later common scheme be enforced against the unrestricted lot's successor because the first buyer learned of the later restrictions?

### 3. Final answer choices

B. Lydia, because Peter's actual knowledge of the later deeds brought Lot 4 into the common residential plan.

C. Martha, because Lot 4 was conveyed to Peter without the use covenant, and the later deed to Martha also contained no such covenant.

D. Lydia, because Martha, as Peter's successor in interest, is estopped from denying that Lot 4 remains subject to the zoning rules in effect when Peter bought it.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. Lot 4 left Lydia's ownership before any use restriction attached to it. The later deeds could create a residential common plan for lots Lydia still owned or later conveyed, but they could not paste a new burden onto Peter's already-conveyed unrestricted lot merely because Peter later knew what Lydia had done. Gold Key: common-scheme restrictions are not retroactive; they must attach while the common owner still owns the land to be burdened, or through an express restriction in that parcel's own conveyance. Silver Key: separate public permission from private burden. Zoning tells whether the government allows the office; the deed/common-plan question tells whether a private restriction blocks it. No private restriction ever burdened Lot 4, so Martha prevails.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because it picks the winning party and turns the occupancy certificate into a simple trump card. The breaker is that zoning permission does not automatically override a valid private restrictive covenant; if a valid private covenant existed, the stricter private limitation could still matter. Martha wins for the deed-and-timing reason, not because zoning wipes out covenants.

B is attractive because it uses the right neighborhood of doctrine: actual notice plus a common residential plan. The breaker is timing. Gold Key: a common scheme cannot retroactively burden a lot that the common owner already conveyed free of the restriction. Peter's later knowledge is not the missing covenant.

D is attractive because old residential zoning sounds like a clean reason to stop an office use. The breaker is that old zoning is not the restriction Lydia is trying to enforce, and a later owner is governed by the current zoning rules unless some separate private covenant validly burdens the land. This choice fights the wrong battle.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Real Property, Rights in Land, Implied from common scheme, and the verified outline code is `81020501`. Under the common-plan doctrine, a reciprocal negative easement or implied equitable servitude can bind later purchasers with notice when a common owner sells lots under a uniform plan and the burden attaches while the common owner still owns the land to be burdened. The leading common-plan case `Sanborn v. McLean`, 206 N.W. 496 (Mich. 1925), explains that a reciprocal negative easement must start with a common owner, is not retroactive, and attaches to retained land through restricted sales by the common owner. The nonretroactivity point also matches `Riley v. Bear Creek Planning Committee`, 17 Cal. 3d 500 (1976), where a lot conveyed by an unrestricted deed before later recorded restrictions was not burdened by those later restrictions. Research source URLs: https://law.justia.com/cases/michigan/supreme-court/1925/223-mich-227-206-n-w-496.html and https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/3d/17/500.html. The supplied official key is consistent with those authorities: Lydia cannot impose the later common-plan restriction on Lot 4 after she already conveyed Lot 4 by an unrestricted deed, and Martha's later deed also contains no restriction. Authority floor satisfied; attorney_review: false.
