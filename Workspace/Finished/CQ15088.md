## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Trespass to chattels requires the plaintiff to show either (1) actual harm to the chattel, or (2) substantial interference with the plaintiff's use and enjoyment of the chattel for a meaningful period. Here, Lydia intentionally touched Peter's dog — she tugged on its ears and tail — but the dog was unharmed, and Peter was only inside the store briefly. There is no allegation of injury to Barnabas or that Peter was deprived of his dog's use for any substantial time. Intentional contact with a chattel, without more, does not satisfy the harm requirement. Peter has no viable trespass to chattels claim.

> **Gold Key:** *Mere intentional contact with a chattel is insufficient for trespass to chattels — the plaintiff must prove actual harm to the chattel or substantial interference with the owner's use and enjoyment of it.*

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is attractive** because students may assume that a six-year-old lacks the mental capacity to commit an intentional tort. This is a common intuition — very young children are often shielded from liability in everyday reasoning. **The breaker:** A child can form the requisite intent for an intentional tort so long as the child is old enough to intend the physical act itself (here, touching/pulling the dog). The real reason Peter's claim fails is not Lydia's age but the absence of harm to the chattel. This answer reaches the right outcome ("No") for the wrong reason.

**B is attractive** because Peter was "visibly upset" — the stem tells us he suffered emotional distress, and students may conflate the plaintiff's emotional reaction with the type of harm the tort requires. **The breaker:** Trespass to chattels protects property interests, not the owner's feelings. Emotional distress to the plaintiff does not satisfy the requirement of actual harm to the chattel or substantial interference with its use. The distress is real but legally irrelevant to this cause of action.

> **Gold Key callout:** The Gold Key above explains why emotional distress doesn't substitute for harm to the chattel — a student who knows this rule can immediately eliminate B.

**D is attractive** — and is the **dominant trap** — because Lydia clearly touched Peter's dog without permission, and students may believe that any unauthorized intentional contact with personal property automatically constitutes trespass to chattels. This is the most intuitive wrong answer: she touched it, it wasn't hers, no consent — done. **The breaker:** Intentional contact alone is not enough. The tort requires actual harm to the chattel or substantial interference with the owner's use. Here, the dog was unharmed and Peter lost no meaningful use of Barnabas. Without that harm element, the claim fails regardless of the unconsented touching.

> **Gold Key callout:** The Gold Key directly defeats this trap — mere intentional contact, without harm or substantial interference, is insufficient.

## 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Rule statement:** Trespass to chattels is an intentional tort that occurs when a defendant intentionally interferes with the plaintiff's possession or use of a chattel. Under the majority rule, where the defendant merely makes contact with the chattel (as opposed to taking it out of the plaintiff's possession), the plaintiff must prove either (a) actual harm to the chattel, or (b) substantial interference with the plaintiff's use and enjoyment of the chattel for a meaningful period. Mere intentional contact, without more, is insufficient.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 217–218 (1965). Section 217 defines trespass to chattels as an intentional interference with a chattel in the possession of another. Section 218 provides that one who commits a trespass to a chattel is subject to liability only if the interference causes harm to the chattel or deprives the possessor of its use for a substantial time. See also Wex, Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School, "Trespass to Chattels" (reviewed June 2024).

**Application to this item:** The credited answer (C / original A) is correct. Lydia intentionally touched Peter's dog, but the dog suffered no injury and Peter was not deprived of its use for any substantial period. The claim fails for lack of the required harm element.
