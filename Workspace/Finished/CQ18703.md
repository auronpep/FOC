## 1. Final Question

Esther was diagnosed with a severe personality disorder that seriously blunted her emotional grasp of moral consequences. During a private Christian camp cleanup weekend, she became fixated on Paul's old delivery van, which he used to bring hymnals and folding chairs to small Bible-study groups. Under the MPC/ALI test, can a defendant have a viable insanity claim when she knew the act was illegal in the abstract but may have lacked substantial capacity to appreciate its wrongfulness?

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. The MPC/ALI test does not stop at whether Esther could recite that arson was illegal. Gold Key: MPC/ALI asks whether, because of a mental disease or defect, the defendant lacked substantial capacity to appreciate the criminality or wrongfulness of the conduct, or to conform conduct to law. "Appreciate" is broader than abstract knowledge. Silver Key: when the stem separates "knows the rule" from "cannot appreciate wrongfulness," do not let the knowledge choice win the clash.

Esther knew the legal label in the abstract, but the facts say her disorder prevented genuine appreciation of wrongfulness. That does not guarantee acquittal; it makes the insanity claim viable under the MPC/ALI framework.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the planning-capacity trap. Planning shows Esther could think through steps, but the call is not asking whether she had zero cognitive function. The breaker is that MPC/ALI substantial incapacity can coexist with planned behavior if the mental disease or defect impaired the relevant appreciation or control capacity.

C sells the diagnosis-alone shortcut. A severe personality disorder matters only if it causes the required lack of substantial capacity. The answer overclaims by turning a possible qualifying defect into a categorical excuse.

D sells the dominant M'Naghten-style knowledge trap. The choice is attractive because Esther knew setting the van on fire was illegal. Gold Key: under MPC/ALI, abstract knowledge is not the whole test; the stronger question is whether she could appreciate the criminality or wrongfulness with substantial capacity.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification AND Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged. Under Model Penal Code section 4.01, a defendant is not responsible if, at the time of the conduct and as a result of mental disease or defect, the defendant lacks substantial capacity either to appreciate the criminality or wrongfulness of the conduct or to conform the conduct to the requirements of law. Section 4.01 also provides that "mental disease or defect" does not include an abnormality shown only by repeated criminal or antisocial conduct.

Authority used for lawyer confirmation:
- Model Penal Code section 4.01, quoted in the Department of Justice Criminal Resource Manual archive: https://www.justice.gov/archives/jm/criminal-resource-manual-636-insanity-prior-law
- Model Penal Code section 4.01 text reproduced in an Open Casebook criminal law resource: https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/981-balloberman-crim-law-casebook/resources/10.5.5-model-penal-code-sec-401/
- American Law Institute discussion of Kahler v. Kansas noting the Supreme Court's reference to Model Penal Code section 4.01: https://www.ali.org/news/articles/us-supreme-court-cites-model-penal-code-2020

The source facts and this variation both put the jurisdiction under the MPC/ALI test. Esther's abstract awareness that arson is illegal does not by itself defeat the claim because the facts separately state that her disorder prevented genuine appreciation of wrongfulness. Her planning does not defeat the claim either, because the relevant question is substantial capacity to appreciate criminality or conform conduct, not whether she could sequence the act. The claim is viable, subject to proof that the disorder qualifies and caused the required incapacity.
