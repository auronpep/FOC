## 7. Right-Answer Explanation

Common-law false imprisonment requires intentional confinement within fixed limits from which the victim has no reasonable means of escape. The critical test is not whether any physical opening exists somewhere in the structure, but whether any exit was reasonably and practically available to this victim.

**Gold Key:** A theoretical means of escape that the victim cannot practically and safely reach — due to height, physical characteristics, or the absence of needed equipment — is NOT a "reasonable means of egress" and does not defeat the confinement element of common-law false imprisonment.

The ventilation hatch was six feet above the cellar floor. Naomi stood five feet one inch tall. No ladder was inside. Reaching that hatch without equipment was not merely difficult — it was not safely achievable by this person in this situation. Because no practically usable exit existed, the confinement element is satisfied and Barnabas committed false imprisonment.

**Silver Key:** When the stem pairs a physical description of the victim (height, stature) with a theoretical exit (ceiling hatch, high window), that combination IS the CLASH axis. The question is always whether THIS person can reasonably use THAT exit — not whether the opening physically exists.

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice C — No, hatch provided a means of egress (DOMINANT TRAP):**
The pull here is immediate and powerful: the hatch was unlatched, so Naomi wasn't "completely" confined. The trap works by collapsing "an exit exists" into "a reasonable exit exists." Those are different tests. The governing rule asks whether the victim has a *reasonable* means of escape — one she can practically and safely use. A ceiling hatch six feet up, inaccessible without a ladder that isn't there, is not a reasonable exit for a person of Naomi's stature. **Gold Key:** existence of a theoretical opening is necessary but not sufficient; the exit must be practically reachable by this victim. Choice C misstates the standard as a pure existence test.

**Choice D — No, lacked intent to confine:**
The pull is sympathetic common sense: Barnabas didn't know Naomi was inside, so how could he have meant to lock her in? The trap rests on conflating "intent to confine this particular person" with "intent to perform the confining act." False imprisonment does not require specific intent to confine a known victim. Barnabas intentionally locked the door — that voluntary act is the confining act, and it satisfies the intent element even if he was mistaken about whether anyone was inside. The analytical focus on these facts is the egress question, not intent. Choice D misstates the intent element.

**Choice A — Yes, but only if aware throughout the entire period:**
The pull is a dim memory that the victim must be conscious of the confinement — and the "entire period" language sounds technical and specific. But no common-law jurisdiction requires continuous awareness for the full duration of confinement. Even the minority rule requiring contemporaneous awareness is satisfied when the victim becomes aware of the confinement, not before. Naomi knew she was locked in when she tried to leave. The "throughout the entire five-hour period" clause is a fabricated requirement that no court applies. Cut it as too much — an invented qualifier.

## 9. Black-Letter Verification

**Tested rule:** Common-law false imprisonment requires (1) an intentional act by the defendant (2) that confines the plaintiff (3) within fixed boundaries (4) with no reasonable means of escape available to the plaintiff.

**Egress element:** The Restatement (Second) of Torts § 36 provides that a person is not imprisoned if there is a "reasonable means of escape." Reasonableness is determined by whether this victim can practically and safely use the available route, accounting for the individual's physical characteristics and the available equipment. An opening positioned at a height the victim cannot safely reach without equipment that is not present is not a reasonable means of escape.

**Intent element:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 35, comment e: the intent required is the intent to perform the confining act, not specific intent to confine a particular known person. Mistaken belief that no one is present does not negate the intent element when the confining act itself (locking the door) is intentional.

**Awareness element:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 42: a plaintiff who is not conscious of the confinement at the time may still recover, but no authority requires continuous awareness for the full duration. Awareness when the plaintiff attempts to exit is sufficient.
