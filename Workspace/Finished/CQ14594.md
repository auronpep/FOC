### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** The Fourth Amendment requires both probable cause and a warrant before police may enter a private home, unless an exception applies. *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967); *U.S. Const. amend. IV*. One well-established exception is voluntary consent. When a person with authority over the premises freely permits officers to enter, the warrant requirement is satisfied. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973). Here, Lydia — a co-occupant with common authority over the home — opened the door and expressly invited the officers in. Nothing in the facts suggests coercion, deception, or duress. Under the totality of the circumstances, her consent was voluntary.

Once lawfully inside the home by virtue of Lydia's consent, the officers were in a position where the plain-view doctrine authorized seizure. The plain-view doctrine permits warrantless seizure when (1) the officer is legitimately on the premises, (2) the incriminating character of the evidence is immediately apparent, and (3) the discovery is inadvertent. *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443 (1971); *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983). The power tools with obliterated serial numbers, the shoebox of cash, and the ledger were visible through the open studio doorway and on the kitchen counter — all in plain sight from areas where the officers had a lawful right to be. Their incriminating nature was immediately apparent.

**Gold Key:** *Consent is valid if voluntary under the totality of the circumstances. Officers do NOT need to inform the consenting person of the right to refuse. Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218 (1973).*

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (Yes — fruit of illegal search of Stephen)** would trap the student who sees a prior illegal search and reflexively applies the fruit-of-the-poisonous-tree doctrine. The attraction is real: Stephen was tackled and searched outside the home, and the officers then went to the front door. But the exclusionary rule is personal — a defendant may only invoke it to vindicate *her own* Fourth Amendment rights. *Rakas v. Illinois*, 439 U.S. 128 (1978); *Jones v. United States*, 362 U.S. 257 (1960). Even if the search of Stephen was unlawful, it did not violate *Lydia's* Fourth Amendment rights, so she cannot challenge it. The fruit doctrine has no root to attach to here.

> **Gold Key:** *The exclusionary rule is personal. A motion to suppress can only be grounded on a violation of the movant's own Fourth Amendment rights — not a third party's. Rakas v. Illinois, 439 U.S. 128 (1978).*

**B (No — probable cause plus inevitable discovery via warrant)** correctly predicts the outcome (motion denied) but for the wrong reason. This is the answer that sounds right to a student who understands that probable cause existed but has not identified consent as the governing exception. The catch: with only probable cause and no warrant, entry into a home is *per se* unreasonable unless an exception applies. *Payton v. New York*, 445 U.S. 573 (1980). The "inevitable discovery / warrant" framing obscures the actual legal vehicle — consent — and on the MBE, the credited answer must identify the *correct legal basis*, not just the correct outcome.

**D (Yes — officers didn't inform Lydia of right to refuse)** exploits the student's intuition that consent must be "knowing and intelligent" — the Miranda framework. But the Supreme Court has specifically held that knowledge of the right to refuse is *one factor* in the voluntariness analysis, not a prerequisite. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973). The Fourth Amendment does not impose a "Miranda warning" requirement on consent searches.

> **Silver Key:** *When a choice says "Yes, because the police failed to tell the person [X]," check Schneckloth first. Knowledge of the right to refuse is NOT required for valid Fourth Amendment consent. The trap is importing the Miranda knowing-waiver standard into a context where only voluntariness under the totality matters.*

### 9. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Authority

**Governing rule:** Warrantless entry into a home is *per se* unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment unless a recognized exception applies. *Payton v. New York*, 445 U.S. 573 (1980); *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967). Voluntary consent by a person with common authority over the premises is one such exception. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973) (consent is valid if voluntary under the totality of the circumstances; no requirement to inform of right to refuse). The scope of consent is measured by an objective reasonableness standard — what a reasonable person would have understood by the exchange. *Florida v. Jimeno*, 500 U.S. 248 (1991).

**Plain view:** When an officer is legitimately on the premises, may seize evidence without a warrant if (1) the officer has a lawful right of access to the object, (2) the incriminating character of the object is "immediately apparent." *Horton v. California*, 496 U.S. 128 (1993) (abolishing the inadvertence requirement from Coolidge); *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443 (1971).

**Standing / personal rights:** A defendant may only challenge a search that violated *his own* Fourth Amendment rights. *Rakas v. Illinois*, 439 U.S. 128 (1978). The Fourth Amendment right is personal and cannot be vicariously asserted. *Jones v. United States*, 362 U.S. 257 (1960).

**Fruit of the poisonous tree:** Under *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963), evidence derived from an illegal search is inadmissible *as to the victim of that illegal search*. It does not transfer to a third party whose rights were not violated.

**Confirmed:** The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original. The correct answer (motion denied based on consent + plain view) is B→C. No doctrinal drift noted. Current controlling law supports this answer.

#### Question Data

**Question:**
Peter operates a rug-weaving (stolen-goods) studio from a converted garage attached to his home. He shares the house with his mother, Lydia. A neighbor has reported that Peter sells stolen power tools from the studio. Officers conduct a 2-day surveillance and observe many strangers making brief visits and leaving with packages. On day three, Stephen exits the studio carrying a taped box. An officer approaches and asks what's in the box. Stephen shoves the officer and tries to flee. Officers tackle him, search his person, and find a loaded pistol and a folded list of home addresses. Stephen is arrested.

An hour later, officers knock on the front door of Peter and Lydia's house. Lydia answers. The lead officer explains they are investigating stolen-goods trafficking and asks if they can come inside. Lydia says, "Of course — my son hasn't done anything wrong," and steps aside. Once in the living room, officers observe through the open studio doorway: power tools with serial numbers scratched off, a shoebox full of cash, and a notebook listing names and dollar amounts on the kitchen counter.

Lydia is charged as a co-conspiror in possession of stolen property and conspiracy. She moves to suppress the power tools, cash, and notebook.

**Should Lydia's motion to suppress be granted?**

A. Yes, because the officers' entry into the home was the fruit of an illegal search of Stephen, and the evidence found inside the house is tainted.

B. No, because the tip together with the pistol and address list found on Stephen provided probable cause, and the officers would inevitably have obtained a warrant.

C. No, because Lydia voluntarily consented to the officers' entry, and the evidence was in plain view.

D. Yes, because the officers did not inform Lydia that she had the right to refuse consent.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

---

**CHOICE A: Yes — fruit of illegal search of Stephen**

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| **1. C3 Signal** | A prior illegal search occurred (Stephen tackled on the street). Tempting to connect it to the home entry. But the exclusionary rule is personal — it protects the *victim* of the search. Stephen was the victim of the possible illegal search, not Lydia. The "poisonous tree" was Stephen's tree, not Lydia's. Without a personal constitutional violation, the fruit theory has no roots. |
| **2. Student Label** | "Trap: you cannot challenge someone else's Fourth Amendment violation." |
| **3. True/Responsive Version** | A true-and-responsive version would say: "Yes, because the officers violated *Lydia's* Fourth Amendment rights by entering without a warrant or exception, and the home evidence is the product of that violation." That would require showing the consent was invalid — which the facts do not support. |
| **4. Lawyer Confirmation** | *Rakas v. Illinois*, 439 U.S. 128 (1978); *Jones v. United States*, 362 U.S. 257 (1960). The exclusionary rule is personal. A defendant cannot invoke another person's Fourth Amendment rights to suppress evidence. *Gold Key GK-CRIMINAL-STANDING-01 applies.* |

---

**CHOICE B: No — probable cause, inevitable warrant**

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| **1. C3 Signal** | This answer gets the outcome right (No) but the reason wrong. It says "probable cause + inevitable warrant." On the MBE, "right answer, wrong reason" is ALWAYS incorrect. The call requires the legal basis for admissibility, not just the conclusion. Probable cause alone never justifies warrantless entry into a home. |
| **2. Student Label** | "Trap: the MBE demands the correct legal vehicle, not just the correct destination." |
| **3. True/Responsive Version** | A true-and-responsive version would say: "No, because the officers had probable cause AND a valid exception to the warrant requirement (consent)." |
| **4. Lawyer Confirmation** | *Payton v. New York*, 445 U.S. 573 (1980). Absent an exception, PC alone does not authorize home entry. The credited B answer must name the *actual* exception — consent — not hypothetical future warrants. |

---

**CHOICE C: No — consent + plain view** ✅

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| **1. C3 Signal** | Lydia — a co-occupant with common authority — voluntarily invited the officers inside. "Of course" + stepping aside = no coercion, no deception. This satisfies the consent exception to the warrant requirement. Once lawfully inside, the officers observed immediately incriminating items (tools with obliterated serial numbers, cash, ledger) through an open doorway. Consent gave them lawful presence; plain view justified seizure. |
| **2. Student Label** | "Correct answer: consent + plain view." |
| **3. True/Responsive Version** | This choice already is the true-and-responsive version. It breaks no filter. |
| **4. Lawyer Confirmation** | *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973) (consent exception); *Horton v. California*, 496 U.S. 128 (1993) (plain view seizure without inadvertence requirement); *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443 (1971); *Florida v. Jimeno*, 500 U.S. 248 (1991). |

---

**CHOICE D: Yes — officers didn't inform Lydia of right to refuse**

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| **1. C3 Signal** | This feels right because we're trained that rights come with warnings (Miranda). But Fourth Amendment consent ≠ Miranda waiver. The Miranda "knowing and intelligent" standard does not apply. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte* explicitly held that knowledge of the right to refuse is ONE factor in voluntariness analysis, but NOT a prerequisite. If the answer requires a warning to make consent valid, it's importing the wrong doctrine. |
| **2. Student Label** | "Trap: Miranda waiver standard imported into Fourth Amendment consent." |
| **3. True/Responsive Version** | A true-and-responsive version would say: "Yes, because Lydia's consent was involuntary (she was tricked, threatened, or coerced)." But the facts give no indication of involuntariness. |
| **4. Lawyer Confirmation** | *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973): "The requirement of a 'knowing and intelligent' waiver... has never been imposed on the Fourth Amendment consent-to-search doctrine." *Gold Key GK-CRIMINAL-SEARCH-CONSENT-01 applies.* |
