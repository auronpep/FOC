## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.** The statute does not merely set the price for sales inside the state. It makes access to the in-state market depend on what the seller charges in every other state during the same month.

**Gold Key:** A state may not condition in-state sales on a seller’s out-of-state prices. When the in-state sales gate is tied to prices charged elsewhere, the strongest dormant Commerce Clause objection is extraterritorial price control.

**Silver Key:** Follow the pricing lever, not the state’s label. The state says “inside our borders,” but the certification forces the seller to account for prices outside the state.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells the tempting shortcut that price rules are just ordinary local housekeeping. The breaker is the absolute move: price rules are commercial regulation, and saying they “never affect commerce” erases the very commerce the stem is about.

**B.** This choice reaches for a familiar constitutional doctrine and tries to stop there. The breaker is the call: the question asks for a dormant Commerce Clause objection, and the stem is about interstate price regulation, not Article IV privileges and immunities.

**D.** This choice starts from a real local-power instinct: a state can regulate sales inside its own market. The breaker is the jump from in-state sales to **all nationwide prices**. **Gold Key:** the state’s in-state sales power does not let it control prices charged wholly elsewhere.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The source row identifies QID 21872 as a Constitutional Law / Dormant Commerce Clause / extraterritorial-regulation item, with original answer A credited because the certification ties in-state market access to prices charged wholly elsewhere.  The controlled fields used below come from the project’s controlled-vocabulary file. 

The credited rule is current and unchanged for this variation. In **Brown-Forman**, the Court treated a same-month price-affirmation statute as direct out-of-state regulation because, once the seller posted the in-state price, it was not free to lower prices elsewhere during the relevant month. ([Justia Law][1]) In **Healy**, the Court held that a price-affirmation statute had the impermissible practical effect of controlling commercial activity wholly outside the regulating state. ([Justia Law][2]) **National Pork Producers Council v. Ross** narrowed broad “extraterritoriality” arguments, but it expressly preserved the Baldwin / Brown-Forman / Healy line as price-control or price-affirmation cases tying in-state prices to out-of-state prices. ([Justia Law][3]) **Baldwin** states the core idea: a state may not establish a price scale for use in other states and bar sale unless that scale is observed. ([Justia Law][4])
