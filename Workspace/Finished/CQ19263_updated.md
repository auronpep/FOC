## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sells the commercial-trust story: Paul gave Lydia the handbells in a transaction, so “entrusted” sounds plausible. The breaker is that the facts are not lawful custody followed by later conversion; Paul transferred ownership because of Lydia’s lie.

**C.** This choice sells the easiest visible fact: Lydia lied to get the handbells. The breaker is the signed bills of sale; the fraud produced ownership/title transfer, not possession-only transfer.

**D.** This choice sells the bad-check fact because the closed account screams fraud. The breaker is that the bad check supports intent but is not the operative present-fact representation; the operative present-fact lie is Lydia’s claimed current role.

## 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The transformed item preserves the original tested rule: false pretenses is the common-law theft crime in which the defendant obtains title to property through misrepresentation with intent to defraud; it differs from larceny by trick because larceny by trick involves possession without title. Cornell’s Wex entry states that false pretenses involves obtaining title through misrepresentation and distinguishes it from larceny by trick on the title-versus-possession axis. ([Legal Information Institute][1])

The official row states the same bar-rule distinction: false pretenses is title by knowingly false present material fact, while larceny by trick is possession-only deception; it also explains that the bad check is evidence of intent but not the operative present-fact misrepresentation.  A secondary legal source also states the common-law formulation as past-or-present fact, not a future promise alone. ([LegalClarity][2])
