### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**Gold Key:** After anticipatory repudiation, the non-breaching party has an immediate right to sue for breach. The standard measure is expectation damages — the lost profit the plaintiff would have earned had the contract been performed. But the non-breaching party also has a duty to mitigate. Expenses voluntarily incurred *after* receiving clear notice of repudiation — expenses that reasonable effort could have avoided — are not recoverable as reliance damages. The plaintiff cannot stack reliance costs on top of expectation damages when those reliance costs were avoidable post-repudiation.

**C is correct.** Lydia's expectation damages are her lost profit: $2,500. She cannot recover the $1,200 she spent on materials *after* Barnabas repudiated, because that expense was avoidable — she had clear notice the contract was dead and should have stopped spending.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is attractive** because mitigation is a real doctrine, and students who spot the post-repudiation spending may overreact and conclude the *entire* claim is forfeited. **The breaker:** the duty to mitigation only bars recovery of the *avoidable* damages — here, the $1,200 in post-repudiation materials. It does not destroy the underlying breach-of-contract claim or the expectation interest. Lydia can still recover her lost profit.

**B is attractive** (the dominant trap) because it feels fair — Lydia lost both her expected profit *and* the money she spent on materials, so adding them together seems like full compensation. **The breaker:** the $2,500 expectation award already puts Lydia in the position she would have occupied had the contract been performed. The $1,200 was spent *after* the repudiation, which Lydia could have avoided with reasonable effort. Reliance damages are not available for avoidable post-repudiation expenditures, and they certainly cannot be stacked on top of expectation damages.

**D is attractive** because restitution is a recognized contract remedy, and students may think Lydia should at least get back what she spent. **The breaker:** restitution for breach of contract requires that the non-breaching party's performance conferred a *benefit* on the breaching party. Here, the $1,200 in materials was never used — Barnabas's daughter never received a single lesson, and the materials were student-specific. No benefit was conferred. Moreover, where expectation damages are available, restitution is generally not an alternative.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification & Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** When one party to a contract commits an anticipatory repudiation, the non-breaching party may immediately treat the contract as breached and sue for damages. *Restatement (Second) of Contracts* § 250 (repudiation defined); § 251 (failure to give assurance as repudiation); § 243 (total breach).

**Measure of damages:** The standard measure is expectation damages — the loss in value of the other party's performance, plus other loss, less costs avoided. *Restatement (Second) of Contracts* § 347. Expectation damages put the promisee in the position she would have occupied had the contract been performed. *Robinson v. Harman*, 1 Ex. 850, 855 (1848).

**Mitigation (avoidability):** The injured party may not recover damages that could have been avoided by reasonable effort. *Restatement (Second) of Contracts* § 350. After clear repudiation, the non-breaching party has a duty not to increase the damages by continuing to perform or incur costs. Expenses incurred post-repudiation that the plaintiff could have avoided are not recoverable.

**Reliance as alternative:** Reliance damages under § 349 are an *alternative* to expectation damages, not additive. They are also reduced by any loss the breaching party can prove the plaintiff would have suffered had the contract been performed. Post-repudiation reliance costs that were avoidable fail the mitigation bar.

**Restitution:** Restitution under § 371-375 requires a benefit conferred on the breaching party. Where no benefit was received, and where expectation damages are available, restitution is not independently recoverable.

**Authority:** *Restatement (Second) of Contracts* §§ 250, 251, 243, 347, 349, 350. *Robinson v. Harman*, 1 Ex. 850 (1848).
