## 1. Final question - revised stem and call

Lydia runs a private Scripture-lettering supply shop. Before a weekend workshop, she asked Mary to drive a sealed cardboard banner tube to Bethany Hall, a privately rented classroom where several friends were meeting to make verse posters. Inside the tube was a small bottle of caustic etching solution, but Lydia wrapped the tube in kraft paper and did not tell Mary what was inside because she thought Mary would refuse to carry it.

Mary placed the tube in the side storage pocket of her van and started toward Bethany Hall. On the way, she saw Peter, an old Bible-study friend, walking along the road toward the same workshop, and offered him a ride. While Peter was sitting in the front passenger seat, liquid began to seep from the storage pocket and dampen Peter's canvas tote. Without saying anything to Mary, Peter reached into the pocket and lifted the wet tube away from the tote. As soon as the liquid touched Peter's hand, it burned his skin severely.

If Peter commences a negligence action against Mary in a jurisdiction that has no automobile guest statute and that applies the all-or-nothing rule of contributory negligence, which of the following would be Mary's most effective argument in defense?

A driver unknowingly carries a wrapped container with hidden caustic contents, a passenger is burned after moving it when it leaks, and the passenger sues for negligence; what is the driver's best defense?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is the best answer. Negligence turns on an unreasonable risk that Mary knew or should have known about. Lydia concealed the dangerous contents, wrapped the tube, and gave Mary no reason to think the tube would leak caustic liquid onto a passenger. **Gold Key:** if the danger is hidden from the defendant and the defendant has no reason to anticipate that kind of harm, the strongest negligence defense is lack of foreseeable unreasonable risk. **Silver Key:** when the call asks for the most effective defense, test the answer that defeats the plaintiff's negligence case before chasing plaintiff-fault defenses.

Peter's conduct may look active because he touched the tube, but the facts do not show that he knew it contained a caustic chemical or that moving a wet paper-wrapped tube was unreasonable. Mary therefore has a stronger defense in lack of foreseeable negligence than in contributory negligence or assumption of risk.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Peter was contributorily negligent in moving the wet tube.**
This is the dominant trap. It sells the visible plaintiff-action fact: Peter touched the leaking tube after the jurisdiction tells you contributory negligence is all-or-nothing. The breaker is the missing unreasonable-risk fact. **Gold Key:** contributory negligence requires conduct that falls below reasonable self-care, and the stem gives no fact showing Peter knew or should have known the wet tube was caustic. **Silver Key:** do not let the defense label in the call outrank the facts that support it.

**B. Peter was a mere licensee and was entitled only to a warning of conditions that Mary knew were dangerous.**
This sells a familiar status word. A passenger who accepts a ride sounds like a licensee in ordinary speech. The breaker is the frame. The licensee warning rule is a land-possessor rule for dangerous conditions on land, not the duty rule for a driver carrying a passenger in a van.

**D. Peter assumed the risk of injury resulting from contact with the wet tube.**
This sells the voluntary-contact story. Peter chose to lift the wet tube, so the answer feels like consent to the consequence. The breaker is the known-risk requirement. Assumption of risk requires a voluntary encounter with a known risk, and the facts do not show that Peter knew the wetness came from caustic solution.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

**Pass-1 Step 1 - tested black-letter rule.** Negligence requires conduct that creates an unreasonable risk of harm that a reasonable person in the defendant's position would recognize. Restatement (Second) of Torts section 282 defines negligence around unreasonable risk, and section 284 treats negligent conduct as an act or omission involving an unreasonable risk the actor should recognize. *Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad Co.*, 248 N.Y. 339 (1928), states the exam anchor in classic form: the reasonably perceived risk defines the duty to be obeyed. Research links checked: Restatement section 282 and section 284 summaries at https://www.criminallawweb.net/MPC/torts/torts282.htm and https://www.criminallawweb.net/web1/MPC/torts/torts284.htm; *Palsgraf* archive at https://www.nycourts.gov/reporter/archives/palsgraf_lirr.htm.

The plaintiff-fault defenses also remain unchanged. Contributory negligence requires the plaintiff's own unreasonable conduct to contribute to the harm; Restatement (Second) of Torts section 467 states the traditional complete-bar effect where contributory negligence exists. Assumption of risk requires voluntary acceptance of a known risk; Restatement (Second) of Torts section 496A gives the general doctrine, and section 496D supplies the knowledge-and-appreciation requirement. Research links checked: https://www.classlawgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/Restatement-Second-of-Torts-Section-467-Contributory-Negligence.pdf and https://www.classlawgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/Restatement-Second-of-Torts-496A-Assumption-of-Risk.pdf.

**Pass-1 Step 2 - invariant layer preserved.** The legal issue remains the most effective defense to a passenger's negligence action after the passenger is burned by hidden caustic contents in a package the driver unknowingly carried. The credited legal substance remains that the defendant could not reasonably foresee the hidden danger. The three wrong-answer mechanics remain: a land-licensee status rule misapplied to a vehicle passenger, contributory negligence without unreasonable self-risk facts, and assumption of risk without known-risk facts. The call still asks for the defendant's most effective negligence defense in a no-guest-statute, contributory-negligence jurisdiction.
