### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is correct. Self-defense in a battery action does not ask only whether Daniel was frightened or whether Paul started the fight. The privilege protects Daniel only if the force he used was no more than he actually and reasonably believed was necessary under the threat facing him. Gold Key: civil self-defense requires both a subjective belief in the need for force and an objectively reasonable belief about the amount of force used. Silver Key: when the answer set splits "actual belief," "fear," "first blow," and "reasonable belief," choose the answer that combines necessity with an objective reasonableness check.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because real fear feels like the whole point of self-defense, especially when Paul was reaching toward a knife pocket. The breaker is that fear alone does not measure whether Daniel's force was reasonably necessary.

B is attractive because it includes Daniel's actual belief and the need to protect himself from death or serious bodily harm. The breaker is the Gold Key: actual belief is only half the privilege; the amount of force must also be objectively reasonable.

C is attractive because Paul began the physical confrontation, so the answer gives Daniel a clean-sounding moral reason to hit back. The breaker is that self-defense prevents a present threat; it does not authorize retaliation merely because the other person struck first.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. This is a Torts battery item testing the self-defense privilege. Under Restatement (Second) of Torts section 63, an actor may use reasonable non-deadly force to defend against harmful or offensive contact that he reasonably believes another is about to inflict. For force intended or likely to cause death or serious bodily harm, Restatement (Second) of Torts section 65 requires a reasonable belief that the actor is in peril of death or serious bodily harm and that immediate force is necessary. The same Restatement section 63 comments also reject retaliation as a basis for the privilege once the force is not being used to stop a present threat.

Authority layer: Restatement (Second) of Torts section 63, https://advance.lexis.com/open/document/openwebdocview/-63-Self-Defense-By-Force-Not-Threatening-Death-or-Serious-Bodily-Harm/; Restatement (Second) of Torts section 65, https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/4643-tobia-torts-2022/resources/2.2.2.5-restatement-2d-65-self-defense-by-force-threatening-death-or-serious-bodily-harm/. Doctrinal-currency note: the ALI announced a 2026 Restatement Third volume on intentional torts including self-defense, https://www.ali.org/news/articles/intentional-torts-persons-now-available, but this MBE-style item tests the conventional common-law formulation that the supplied official explanation also uses: actual and objectively reasonable necessity, with no retaliation.

Because the facts let a jury find that Daniel faced a renewed, dangerous threat and used no more force than reasonably necessary, D is the finding most likely to support a defense verdict. A and B omit objective reasonableness. C mistakes prevention for retaliation.
