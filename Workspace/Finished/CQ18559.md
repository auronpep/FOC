## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** Directing executive personnel decisions — including the removal of a federal contractor from a policy role — is an official function within the outer perimeter of a high-ranking executive officer's duties. Under the separation-of-powers doctrine rooted in Nixon v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 731 (1982), the President (and by extension, high-level executive officials acting on direct orders) is entitled to absolute immunity from civil damages liability for official acts. The threshold question is whether the challenged conduct falls within the scope of official responsibility, not whether the plaintiff alleges an improper motive. Because the Secretary's direction to the agency head was an official personnel decision, the immunity applies.

> **Gold Key:** Official-act immunity turns on whether the conduct falls within the outer perimeter of official duties — NOT on the plaintiff's allegation of bad motive. If the act is official, motive is irrelevant to the immunity question.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (original D) — attractive wrong answer: delegation ≠ removal from responsibility.**

*What makes it attractive:* Students may reason that because the agency head actually executed the removal, the Secretary who merely gave the direction should not bear liability. It feels like a fairness/proximate-cause argument — the person who pulled the trigger should be the one sued.

*The breaker:* The Secretary personally directed the removal. Directing executive personnel decisions remains within the outer perimeter of official responsibility, even when the mechanical act of processing the removal is delegated to an agency head. The Secretary's personal involvement is what brings the act within the scope of official immunity.

**B (original C) — legally true but irrelevant: statute-required myth.**

*What makes it attractive:* Students know that many immunities are statutory (e.g., qualified immunity for state officers under §1983 has statutory roots). It feels logical that cabinet-level immunity would also require a specific statute.

*The breaker:* Presidential and high-level executive official-act civil immunity is a constitutional separation-of-powers doctrine, not a creature of statute. It derives from the structural need to protect the executive function from the distraction of personal damages suits. No specific immunity statute is required.

> **Gold Key callout:** Official-act immunity is constitutional, not statutory. Congress does not need to enact an immunity statute for the protection to apply.

**D (original B) — dominant trap: motive-always-defeats myth.**

*What makes it attractive:* This is the most powerful distractor because it appeals to a deep moral intuition: a person who acts for the wrong reason should not be shielded. Students will feel that "retaliation" is different from a legitimate policy disagreement, and that the law should distinguish between the two.

*The breaker:* Official-act immunity would be functionally meaningless if a plaintiff could defeat it simply by alleging improper motive. The threshold is whether the conduct falls within official responsibility — not the actor's subjective reason. This is the core holding of Nixon v. Fitzgerald.

> **Gold Key callout:** Alleging bad motive does NOT defeat official-act immunity. The test is whether the act was within the outer perimeter of official duties — motive is irrelevant.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule:** The President is entitled to absolute immunity from damages liability predicated on official acts within the outer perimeter of presidential responsibility. Nixon v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 731 (1982) (5–4 decision, Powell, J.).

**Key holding:** "The President is entitled to absolute immunity from damages liability predicated on his official acts." Id. at 749. The immunity "extends to all acts within the 'outer perimeter' of his duties of office." Id.

**Scope limitation:** This immunity applies to civil damages for official acts. It does not extend to criminal prosecution (id. at 756–57) or to suits for conduct committed before assuming the presidency (Clinton v. Jones, 520 U.S. 681 (1997)).

**Structural basis:** The immunity is rooted in the separation of powers — the need to protect the President from the distraction and personal vulnerability of civil damages suits that would impair the performance of the executive function. Id. at 751–53.

**Distractor authority:**
- B (statute myth): The immunity is constitutional, not statutory. No statute is required. Id. at 749.
- D (motive myth): The Court explicitly rejected the argument that alleging improper motive defeats immunity. The test is the outer-perimeter question, not motive. Id. at 749–50.
- A (delegation myth): Directing an agency head to carry out a personnel decision is itself an official act within the outer perimeter. The Secretary's personal direction is the operative act.
