# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final Question

On March 1, a church deacon named Paul entered into a written agreement with a skilled metalworker named Timothy, under which Timothy agreed to fabricate and deliver a custom commercial oven for the church's community bakery by May 1, for a price of $8,000, payable 10 days after delivery. While sourcing materials, Timothy discovered that the specialized refractory bricks required for the oven's interior had doubled in cost due to a supplier shortage. Timothy informed Paul of the unexpected cost increase, and on April 1, both parties signed a written modification to the March 1 agreement raising the contract price to $9,500. Timothy delivered the conforming oven on May 1, but Paul paid only $8,000 and refused to pay more. Which of the following concepts of the Uniform Commercial Code, dealing expressly with the sale of goods, best supports an action by Timothy to recover $1,500 for breach of Paul's April 1 promise?

A. Promissory estoppel.
B. Bargained-for exchange.
C. Modification of contracts without consideration.
D. Unconscionability in the formation of contracts.

## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Under UCC § 2-209(1), an agreement modifying a contract for the sale of goods needs no consideration to be binding, provided the modification is made in good faith. Here, Timothy's costs increased unexpectedly due to a supplier shortage — an honest business reason — and both parties signed the modification. The modification was proposed in good faith, not as coercion, so it is enforceable despite the absence of new consideration.

> **Gold Key:** UCC § 2-209(1) — For sale-of-goods contracts, a signed modification is binding without new consideration if made in good faith. The common-law pre-existing-duty rule does NOT apply under the UCC.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A (Promissory estoppel)** is attractive because Timothy relied on Paul's promise to pay more and went ahead with production. A student who conflates UCC modification doctrine with common-law promissory estoppel will pick this. But the call asks which UCC concept *best* supports the action — promissory estoppel is a non-UCC, common-law doctrine, and the facts present a straightforward UCC modification, not a reliance-based claim. The UCC's own modification rule is the direct path.

> **Gold Key:** Promissory estoppel is a common-law fallback for when consideration is missing — but the UCC has its own rule (§ 2-209(1)) that displaces the need for consideration entirely in good-faith modifications. When the UCC speaks directly, use it.

**B (Bargained-for exchange)** is attractive because students trained on common-law contracts instinctively look for consideration. The modification here had no new consideration from Timothy — he was already bound to deliver the oven. A student who doesn't know that the UCC eliminates the consideration requirement for good-faith modifications will think the modification fails for lack of bargained-for exchange. But UCC § 2-209(1) expressly removes this requirement.

> **Gold Key:** Under the UCC, modification without consideration is the RULE, not the exception — the common-law pre-existing-duty rule is displaced.

**D (Unconscionability in the formation of contracts)** is attractive only to a student scanning for any doctrine that might help the seller. Unscionability is a defense against enforcement of an unfair contract term — it is not a basis for enforcing a price increase. Nothing in the facts suggests the original contract or the modification was procedurally or substantively unconscionable. This doctrine is simply not in play.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** UCC § 2-209(1) — "An agreement modifying a contract within this Article needs no consideration to be binding."

**Authority:** UCC § 2-209(1) (Uniform Commercial Code, Article 2, Sales). Available at: https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-209

**Good-faith requirement:** UCC § 2-209 does not explicitly state the good-faith requirement in subsection (1), but Comment 2 to § 2-209 clarifies that "the extortion of a 'modification' without legitimate commercial reason is ineffective as a violation of the duty of good faith." The good-faith limit is also reinforced by UCC § 1-304 (formerly § 1-203), which imposes an obligation of good faith in every contract within the UCC.

**Application:** The facts state that Timothy encountered "unexpected expenses" and "specialized refractory bricks required for the oven's interior had doubled in cost due to a supplier shortage." This is a legitimate commercial reason — not extortion or bad faith. Both parties signed the modification. The modification is binding.

**Confirmed:** The tested rule (UCC modification without consideration), the legal issue (enforceability of a price-increase modification), and the credited outcome (seller can recover) are UNCHANGED from the original.
