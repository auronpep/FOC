# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question (revised stem + call)

Stephen owned a hillside vineyard that touched no public road; the only way his ox-carts could reach the market road was a fifteen-foot-wide cart path that Stephen had bought as an easement across Naomi's barley field. After several harvests, Stephen — who had started floating his grapes downriver instead — told Naomi, "I won't be needing that cart path anymore." Soon after, Stephen sold the vineyard to Philip. Philip relied on Stephen's assertion that the cart-path easement still ran with the vineyard when he bought it. On the first day Philip owned the vineyard, he drove his ox-carts onto the path, but Naomi had built a barricade across it and told Philip she would not let the carts pass without a new agreement. Philip sued Naomi for use of the easement.

**Should the court rule in Philip's favor?**

## 2. Distilled Core Question

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**C is correct.** Stephen merely *told* Naomi he would no longer need the cart path. **Gold Key:** an express easement is an interest in land; releasing or extinguishing it requires a writing that satisfies the Statute of Frauds, so a holder's bare oral statement that he "won't need it" does not end the easement. Because the easement was never extinguished, it remained appurtenant to the vineyard and passed with the land when Stephen sold to Philip. Naomi cannot force Philip into a new agreement. **Silver Key:** two choices answer "Yes" — keep the one whose reason is the operative rule (words alone don't end an easement), not the one leaning on the sympathetic fact the stem spotlighted (Philip "relied").

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A — Yes, because Philip relied on Stephen's assertion when he bought the vineyard.** The stem hands you reliance on a platter, so estoppel feels like the winning theory and it reaches the right outcome. But the easement never ended, so there was nothing for Philip's reliance to repair — the reliance is immaterial. **Gold Key:** because the oral statement did not extinguish the easement, the buyer takes the still-living easement with the land regardless of what he relied on.

**B — No, because the easement was revocable at the will of the grantor.** "Revocable at will" sounds like a real property rule, and it is — for a *license*. That is exactly the distinction being tested: revocability at the will of the grantor is the defining feature of a license, not an easement. **Gold Key:** an easement is a durable interest in land; it is not revocable at will, so labeling it revocable misstates the governing rule.

**D — No, because there is no indication that Naomi received any benefit from the sale.** It invites you to hunt for a missing element, and "she got nothing from the sale" sounds like a fairness gap. But no rule conditions an easement's survival on the servient owner profiting from the dominant owner's sale; the requirement is invented. The easement binds Naomi's land whether or not she gained anything when Stephen sold.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule (unchanged from the original):** An express easement is an interest in land. A release or voluntary extinguishment of it must satisfy the Statute of Frauds — i.e., a writing. A holder's oral statement that he no longer needs the easement, standing alone, does not terminate it. (Termination by abandonment is possible only on clear intent to abandon *plus* an overt act inconsistent with future use; mere words of non-use are not enough.) An unextinguished appurtenant easement runs with the dominant tenement and passes to a buyer of that estate.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation only):**
- Statute of Frauds — a writing is required to convey or release an interest in land; an easement is such an interest.
- Restatement (Third) of Property (Servitudes) § 7.4 (termination by abandonment, merger, and release; release requires a writing complying with the Statute of Frauds; abandonment requires intent plus conduct manifesting it). Cited as a Restatement section; no case citation is asserted.

**Confirmation:** the credited outcome (buyer wins; easement enforceable), the legal issue (whether an oral disclaimer extinguishes an easement that then passes to the buyer), and the tested rule (SoF/writing required; words alone insufficient) are identical to the source item. No element of the transform moves the credited result. Not on the doctrinal-currency hotlist; easement-termination doctrine is stable common law. Authority floor satisfied (Statute of Frauds rule + Restatement section).

- **GK-RP-LICENSE-VS-EASEMENT-02** — "Revocable at the will of the grantor" defines a license, not an easement; an easement is durable and not revocable at will. *Unlocks B.*
