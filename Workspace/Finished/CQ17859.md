### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The carrying-away element **is** satisfied. Larceny at common law requires a trespassory taking and carrying away of personal property of another with intent to permanently deprive. The asportation element is satisfied by **any** movement, however slight, that is part of the taking - moving the ornament from the open display table into the tote bag, even by two steps, is enough. The ornament need not leave the building, and no sale is required. **Gold Key:** asportation in larceny is satisfied by even minimal movement of the property; the property need not leave the building and the act of sale is not an element. **Silver Key:** when a stem gives a "small item, two steps, stopped before exit" pattern, lock the call to the asportation minimum and cut the "must leave the building" and "must have sold it" distractors first.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. No, because the property must be removed from the building.**

This is the dominant trap and the classic "larceny requires leaving the premises" misconception. The student conflates asportation with a successful shoplifting exit. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: asportation requires only minimal movement, not removal from the building. Naomi's two steps toward the exit with the ornament already in her bag are sufficient.

**B. No, because picking up merchandise in a store is always permitted until the customer leaves.**

This is the consent-based overclaim trap. The student half-remembers that stores impliedly consent to handling and pockets the "until the customer leaves" framing as the cutoff. The breaker is the consent scope: the store consents to inspection and ordinary handling, not to concealment with intent to steal. The "always permitted" framing is an overclaim; once intent to permanently deprive attaches, the consent rule is negated.

**D. No, because the clerk stopped Naomi before she sold the ornament.**

This is the "wrong element" trap. The student tries to find an incomplete-act theory and lands on "she hadn't sold it yet." The breaker is the elements of larceny: trespassory taking + carrying away + intent to permanently deprive. There is no sale element. The clerk's interception does not undo the trespassory taking and asportation that already occurred; it only affects the probability of apprehension, not the elements of the completed offense (subject to the jurisdiction's attempt/larceny distinction, which is not the issue here).

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17859, a Criminal Law item involving the asportation element of common-law larceny. The official key is A: yes, slight movement of the property satisfies asportation.

Common-law larceny requires a trespassory taking and carrying away of another's personal property with intent to permanently deprive the owner. The Department of Justice Criminal Resource Manual section 1006 states the larceny elements as wrongful taking and carrying away, absence of consent, and intent to deprive, citing United States v. Barlow, 480 F.2d 1245, 1251 (D.C. Cir. 1972): https://www.justice.gov/archives/usam/criminal-resource-manual-1006-larceny. The MBE-style black-letter asportation rule is that any movement, however slight, is enough when it is part of the taking. The property need not leave the building - moving the goods from the shelf into the bag is enough. The sale element is not in the rule: a thief who takes and carries away with intent to permanently deprive has committed larceny, regardless of whether a downstream sale ever occurs.

The "stopped by the clerk" fact is a fillip, not an element. The interception affects whether Naomi is caught and whether a jurisdiction may want to charge attempt or solicitation in addition, but the larceny elements are evaluated as of the moment of the taking and asportation, not the moment of apprehension. Naomi took the ornament, moved it into her tote bag, and turned toward the exit with intent to keep it. The asportation is complete.

The transformed outcome is identical: Naomi's asportation is satisfied by the minimal movement of slipping the ornament from the open display table into her tote bag; the carrying-away element of larceny is satisfied; the clerk's interception does not undo the offense.

## distilled_core_question

A customer slips a small item from an open display into her bag, takes two steps toward the exit, and is stopped before leaving the building. Has the carrying-away (asportation) element of larceny been satisfied?

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. No, because the property must be removed from the building.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The "must be removed" pushes the asportation rule too far; the rule requires only minimal movement.
2. **Student label:** Must leave the building trap.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Larceny by asportation would require removal from the building if the rule were about shoplifting exits; the actual rule is much lower - any movement suffices."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Common-law asportation is satisfied by any movement, however slight; the property need not leave the building. The "must be removed from the building" formulation is an extreme_of_range misstatement.

### B. No, because picking up merchandise in a store is always permitted until the customer leaves.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The "always permitted" framing is an absolute that ignores the consent-scope rule (consent covers inspection, not concealment with intent to steal).
2. **Student label:** Always permitted until exit.
3. **True/responsive version:** "A store consents to ordinary handling and inspection; that consent does not extend to concealment with intent to steal. The 'always permitted' framing is an overclaim."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Stores consent to ordinary handling and inspection; that consent does not extend to concealment with intent to steal. "Always permitted until the customer leaves" is an overclaim of the consent rule.

### C. Yes, because even slight movement of the property is enough.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Two steps with the ornament already in the tote bag is the textbook minimal asportation.
2. **Student label:** Slight movement satisfies asportation.
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Under common-law larceny, asportation is satisfied by any movement, however slight, that is part of the taking. Naomi's two steps toward the exit with the ornament in her bag satisfy asportation.

### D. No, because the clerk stopped Naomi before she sold the ornament.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Sale is a downstream disposition; the elements of larceny are trespassory taking + asportation + intent to permanently deprive.
2. **Student label:** Sale is not an element.
3. **True/responsive version:** "The clerk's interception affects apprehension and possibly attempt liability, not the elements of the completed taking."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Sale is not an element of larceny. The clerk's interception does not undo the trespassory taking and asportation that already occurred; it affects apprehension, not the offense elements.

## residual_answer

The residual is **C**. Slight movement of the property is enough; the asportation element is satisfied by Naomi's two steps toward the exit with the ornament in her bag.
