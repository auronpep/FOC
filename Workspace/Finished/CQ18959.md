## 1. Final question

Barnabas, a volunteer assistant coach for his town's youth soccer league and a regular attendee at his local church, was charged with second-degree murder. The statute defined that crime as “the unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought, but without premeditation.” Barnabas's attorney asserted a defense of insanity and called Barnabas as a witness on his own behalf. After Barnabas testified on direct examination and cross-examination, his attorney called Dr. Luke to the witness stand. Dr. Luke stated that he was a psychiatrist, had practiced for thirty years treating patients with impulse-control disorders, and had testified as an expert in numerous criminal trials. He testified, “Having observed Barnabas throughout his testimony and during the defense case, I am of the opinion that Barnabas did not have malice aforethought as the law defines it when the incident occurred.” On cross-examination, Dr. Luke admitted that he had never met or examined Barnabas before the trial and that his opinion was based entirely on what he saw and heard while sitting in the courtroom.

Which of the following would be the prosecuting attorney's most effective argument in support of a motion to exclude Dr. Luke's statement?

A: Dr. Luke never had an opportunity to examine Barnabas before forming his opinion.

B: Dr. Luke's testimony embraces the ultimate issue.

C: Determining whether Barnabas acted with malice aforethought is a matter for the jury to decide.

D: Dr. Luke based his opinion entirely on what he observed while in the courtroom.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The prosecuting attorney's motion to exclude should be granted because Dr. Luke's statement directly told the jury that Barnabas lacked the precise mental state the statute requires as an element of the crime. 

**Gold Key:** In a criminal case, while an expert may describe a defendant's mental condition, symptoms, or behavior, the expert may not state an opinion about whether the defendant did or did not have a mental state or condition that constitutes an element of the crime charged or of a defense. Those matters are for the trier of fact alone.

Dr. Luke crossed the line by naming the exact legal element ("malice aforethought as the law defines it") and giving his conclusion on it. Choice C correctly identifies that this determination belongs to the jury. The other choices identify facts that may affect how much weight the jury gives the opinion once admitted, but they do not provide a ground for excluding the opinion under the rules governing expert testimony on criminal mental-state elements.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A: Dr. Luke never had an opportunity to examine Barnabas before forming his opinion.  
This choice is attractive because it feels like basic fairness and reliability — a psychiatrist should personally examine someone before diagnosing or opining on his mental state. A student might think "how can he know without talking to him?" The breaker is that lack of a prior personal examination or treatment relationship goes only to the weight the jury should give the testimony, not to its admissibility. Experts are permitted to base opinions on hypotheticals or on observations made in the courtroom; the rules do not impose a personal-examination prerequisite that would render this opinion excludable on that ground alone.

B: Dr. Luke's testimony embraces the ultimate issue.  
This is the dominant trap. It sells the familiar (and formerly true) idea that experts are not allowed to opine on the "ultimate issue" in a case. Many students carry the old common-law prohibition or a broad memory of "experts can't decide the case for the jury." The breaker is that FRE 704(a) abolished the general ultimate-issue bar. Expert opinions are not objectionable merely because they embrace an ultimate issue. The carve-out that actually applies here is narrower: in a criminal case the expert still may not give the jury the legal conclusion on the defendant's mental state element. Choice B invokes the wrong (overbroad) prohibition.

D: Dr. Luke based his opinion entirely on what he observed while in the courtroom.  
This choice is attractive for the same "reliability" intuition as A: an expert who never met the person and only watched him in court seems to have a shaky foundation. The breaker is identical in principle — the source or basis of the expert's information (courtroom observation rather than a clinical interview) affects the persuasive weight the jury assigns to the opinion after it is admitted. It does not make the opinion inadmissible under the specific rule that prohibits experts from stating conclusions on criminal mental-state elements. The testimony is excludable for what it says (the legal conclusion), not for how the expert reached it.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged from the original question.

Governing authority: Federal Rule of Evidence 704.

FRE 704(a): "An opinion is not objectionable just because it embraces an ultimate issue."

FRE 704(b): "In a criminal case, an expert witness must not state an opinion about whether the defendant did or did not have a mental state or condition that constitutes an element of the crime charged or of a defense. Those matters are for the trier of fact alone."

The original stem and the transformed stem both present an expert who, after the defendant has testified, states a direct conclusion on the exact mental-state element ("did not have malice aforethought as our law defines it"). This is precisely the statement 704(b) prohibits in a criminal case. The jury, not the expert, must decide whether the evidence proves the mental state the statute requires.

The distractors that point to the expert's lack of prior examination or to the fact that the opinion rests on courtroom observations correctly describe the record but misstate the legal consequence: those facts bear on weight (see FRE 702 and 703 on basis and helpfulness), not on the 704(b) prohibition. The "embraces the ultimate issue" distractor reflects the pre-1984 common-law rule that 704(a) expressly abolished for most purposes; only the mental-state carve-out in 704(b) survives for criminal cases.

Research confirmation (web search of current FRE 704 text and application, Cornell LII and court-rules sources, March 2026 results): the rule text and the distinction between general ultimate-issue opinions (permitted) and criminal mental-state conclusions (prohibited for experts) remain controlling. No post-2023 amendment altered 704(b). The credited answer D (now C) is correct under the black-letter rule, and the legal issue tested (expert opinion on criminal mental-state element) is identical.
