### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The best response is **B**. The National Stolen Property Act targets the act of shipping stolen property across state lines — the use of the channels of interstate commerce. Congress's commerce power reaches the channels themselves, and Congress may keep harmful or stolen goods from moving through those channels, regardless of whether the original taking was in-state. **Gold Key:** the channels-of-commerce power depends on Congress's authority over the channels and instrumentalities of interstate commerce, not on the commercial character of the underlying goods; the in-state origin of the stolen property is therefore not a defense to a charge of knowingly shipping it across state lines. The Supreme Court has long recognized this channel-of-commerce authority alongside the broader Commerce Clause cases. *See Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States*, 379 U.S. 241 (1964); *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824); cf. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 558–559 (1995) (recognizing channels-of-commerce as a separate category of Commerce Clause authority, distinct from regulation of non-economic activity).

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Congress lacks power unless the theft itself happened in multiple states.**

This is a wrong-rule cut. The Commerce Clause, including the channels-of-commerce power, is not conditioned on the multi-state character of the underlying offense. The regulated act is the interstate shipment, which is itself a use of the channels of interstate commerce. The original taking's in-state character is irrelevant to Congress's authority to prohibit the use of those channels to move stolen property across state lines.

**C. Congress may regulate only if the defendant personally owns a shipping company.**

This is a wrong-rule cut. The National Stolen Property Act reaches any person who knowingly ships stolen property across state lines, whether or not the defendant owns a shipping company. The statute targets the act of using the channels, not the defendant's occupational status. The "must own a shipping company" framing misstates who the statute can reach.

**D. Congress lacks power because stolen goods are outside commerce.**

This is the dominant trap. The student hears "stolen goods" and "outside commerce" and assumes Congress cannot reach them. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: the channels-of-commerce power reaches the channels themselves, not the goods in them. Even if stolen goods have no clean commercial title, Congress can still regulate their movement through the channels of interstate commerce. The "outside commerce" framing misstates the channels-of-commerce power; the inquiry is whether the statute regulates the use of a channel, not whether the goods are commercially clean.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17728, a Constitutional Law item involving a federal statute that prohibits knowingly shipping stolen property across state lines, a defendant who stole property in one state and shipped it to a buyer in another, and an argument that the in-state theft is outside federal reach. The official key is A: Congress may regulate use of interstate channels to move stolen goods.

The tested doctrine is the channels-of-commerce component of the Commerce Clause. The Commerce Clause empowers Congress "[t]o regulate Commerce . . . among the several States." U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3. The Supreme Court has long recognized that this power includes the authority to regulate the channels and instrumentalities of interstate commerce — for example, the national highways, waterways, railroads, and air routes — and to keep harmful or unlawful articles from moving through those channels. *See Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States*, 379 U.S. 241 (1964) (upholding Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 as applied to a motel that served interstate travelers); *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824) (the foundational Commerce Clause case, recognizing federal authority over interstate navigation). In *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 558–559 (1995), the Court identified three broad categories of Commerce Clause regulation: (1) the channels of interstate commerce; (2) the instrumentalities of interstate commerce; and (3) activities that substantially affect interstate commerce. The first category — channels of interstate commerce — includes the authority to keep harmful or stolen articles from moving through those channels, even if the underlying property's chain of title is not clean. The National Stolen Property Act, 18 U.S.C. § 2314, is a paradigmatic channels-of-commerce statute: it prohibits knowingly transporting stolen property across state lines, and it is a long-standing exercise of Congress's commerce power.

The defendant's argument that the in-state theft is outside federal reach confuses the underlying offense (the taking, which is governed by state law) with the regulated act (the interstate shipment, which uses a federal channel). The regulated act is the use of the channels of interstate commerce to move stolen property; the in-state origin of the property is not a defense to the federal charge. The transformed outcome is identical to the original: the best response is that Congress may regulate the use of interstate channels to move stolen goods.
