## Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The credited answer and the tested rule are unchanged from the source row. Common-law arson requires (1) a malicious (2) burning (3) of the dwelling house (4) of another. The critical element here is "burning." At common law, "burning" means that the fire must actually ignite and char or consume some portion of the wood fibers of the structure itself. A slight charring, no matter how small, is traditionally sufficient. Mere smoking, scorching, blackening, or surface discoloration of the wood — without any actual consumption or charring of the fibers — is insufficient. 

This rule is confirmed across multiple authorities describing the common-law definition:

- The common law required an actual burning or ignition of some part of the building, at least to the extent of charring the wood. A mere smoking, scorching, or discoloration of the wood was insufficient. (Legal-historical summaries of common-law arson.)
- Historically, the legal definition of arson was quite stringent with regards to what constituted 'burning.' The common law required the material of the structure to be charred, not just scorched or discolored by heat.
- At common law, "burning" required ignition or charring of the material itself, not just its surface. Soot or blackening from smoke alone was insufficient; there needed to be a change in the substance's identity through combustion.
- Common law arson required that some part of the structure be actually burned or charred (as opposed to mere blackening or smoke damage). The slightest charring of the fibers or structural components typically suffices, whereas superficial scorching or sooting on the surface may not.
