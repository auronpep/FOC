## 6. Right-Answer Explanation (exam register)

**Silver Key: call_focus** — The call asks for the *best effect* of Mary's belief, not whether she is guilty at all. Three options describe homicide convictions at different levels; one describes acquittal. The credited answer lies between full acquittal (too high) and murder or involuntary manslaughter (too low) — the same call-reading move eliminates the extremes and lands on the middle path.

**Gold Key: imperfect-self-defense gateway** — In jurisdictions that recognize imperfect self-defense, an honest but unreasonable belief in the need for deadly force does not justify the killing (that requires a reasonable belief), but it mitigates murder to voluntary manslaughter. The key to this doctrine is that only *one* of the two self-defense elements (honest belief) is present, not both. This unlocks the answer: voluntary manslaughter is a partial defense / mitigation theory, not a full justification.

Mary honestly believed Hannah was drawing a pistol — that satisfies the subjective (honest) belief prong. But no reasonable person would have mistaken a tissue reach for a gun draw — the objective (reasonable) prong fails. In a jurisdiction recognizing imperfect self-defense, honest-but-unreasonable belief removes malice, reducing murder to voluntary manslaughter. Since Mary intentionally used deadly force and killed, the result is voluntary manslaughter (an intentional killing under mitigating circumstances), not involuntary manslaughter (unintentional killing through negligence).

## 7. Wrong-Answer Explanations

### A — Involuntary manslaughter (because Mary made a negligent mistake)

**What makes it attractive:** The student recognizes Mary's belief was unreasonable and labels it "negligent." From negligence, the student reaches for the lowest homicide form — involuntary manslaughter. The surface logic ("unreasonable = negligent = involuntary") feels clean.

**The breaker:** **Silver Key: call_focus** — The call asks for the *best effect* of Mary's belief. An honest belief in the need for deadly force, even when unreasonable in a jurisdiction recognizing imperfect self-defense, mitigates murder to *voluntary* manslaughter — an intentional killing under mitigating circumstances. Mary intentionally drew and fired her handgun; this is not an unintentional (involuntary) killing. The negligent-mistake reasoning conflates the unreasonableness of the belief with the mens rea of the killing, which are separate. (Filter: NOT_TRUE — flat_misstatement of the law.)

### B — Murder with no mitigation (because an unreasonable belief is legally irrelevant)

**What makes it attractive:** The student knows that unreasonable beliefs normally do not negate intent or reduce culpability. The strong-sounding absolute ("legally irrelevant everywhere") invites agreement from students who want a tough-on-crime answer.

**The breaker:** **Gold Key: imperfect-self-defense gateway** — The stem explicitly says "the jurisdiction recognizes imperfect self-defense." That is the exception to the general rule. Imperfect self-defense gives legal relevance to an honest but unreasonable deadly-force belief — it does not justify but it *mitigates*. The "everywhere" in the choice overclaims: it pretends no jurisdiction makes any exception, which contradicts the stem's premise. (Filter: NOT_TRUE — tiered_absolute overclaim.)

### D — Complete acquittal (because Mary honestly feared deadly harm)

**What makes it attractive:** This is the dominant trap because it invokes the intuitive fairness response: "She genuinely believed she was in danger — she shouldn't be punished." The honest belief feels like it should be enough.

**The breaker:** Complete self-defense requires BOTH an honest belief AND a reasonable belief in the need for deadly force. Honest alone is not enough for a full justification. Mary's belief was unreasonable (a reasonable person would have seen the tissue). The jurisdiction's imperfect self-defense doctrine gives effect to the honest prong, but only as a *partial* mitigation — reducing murder to voluntary manslaughter, not creating a complete acquittal. (Filter: NOT_TRUE — half_truth: honest belief is relevant but missing the necessary reasonableness element for full acquittal.)

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Tested rule:** In jurisdictions recognizing imperfect self-defense, an honest but unreasonable belief in the need to use deadly force in self-defense reduces murder to voluntary manslaughter. The rule is a partial defense/mitigation, not a full justification.

**Authority:** The common law doctrine of imperfect self-defense is recognized in many U.S. jurisdictions (e.g., California, Pennsylvania, Illinois) as a mitigation that removes malice aforethought. The rule is typically judge-made but may be codified. See, e.g., *People v. Flannel*, 25 Cal.3d 668 (1980) (California recognizes imperfect self-defense as a mitigation reducing murder to voluntary manslaughter). See also MPC § 210.3(1)(b) (extreme mental or emotional disturbance as a mitigation — the Model Penal Code's analog).

**Stability:** This rule remains good law in recognizing jurisdictions. The credited outcome (voluntary manslaughter via imperfect self-defense) is UNCHANGED from the original.

## question_data

**Stem:** The jurisdiction recognizes imperfect self-defense. After several tense but nonviolent disagreements about hymn selection during choir practice, Mary saw Hannah reach quickly into her purse during a heated exchange. Mary honestly believed Hannah was drawing a small pistol, but a reasonable person standing in Mary's position would have seen that Hannah was reaching for a tissue. Mary drew her concealed handgun and shot Hannah, killing her.

**Call:** What is the best effect of Mary's belief?

**Choices:**
- **A:** Involuntary manslaughter, because Mary acted on a negligent mistake.
- **B:** Murder with no mitigation, because an unreasonable belief is legally irrelevant.
- **C:** Voluntary manslaughter, because the jurisdiction recognizes imperfect self-defense for honest but unreasonable deadly-force beliefs.
- **D:** Complete acquittal, because Mary honestly feared deadly harm.

This is a classic call that expects the student to: (1) know that the jurisdiction recognizes imperfect self-defense; (2) recognize that Mary's belief satisfies the honest prong but not the reasonable prong; (3) conclude the effect is mitigation from murder to voluntary manslaughter. Prediction: most students will be drawn to D (acquittal) because it feels fair — she genuinely feared for her life. The correct answer is C.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### Choice A — Involuntary manslaughter (negligent mistake)

**Part 1: Student-accessible C3 signal:**
The stem says Mary "drew her concealed handgun and shot Hannah." Drawing and firing a handgun at another person is an intentional act. Even if the *belief* was negligent (unreasonable), the *killing act* was not negligent — it was a deliberate choice to use deadly force. Involuntary manslaughter covers unintentional killings (criminal negligence, misdemeanor-manslaughter). The honest belief in self-defense, even unreasonable, does not transform an intentional killing into an unintentional one.

**Part 2: Student label:**
Wrong theory — conflates the unreasonableness of the belief with the mens rea of the killing.

**Part 3: What a true/responsive version would look like:**
"If Mary had accidentally fired her gun while startled, or if she had not intended to shoot, involuntary manslaughter might apply." But the stem says she drew and shot — intentional.

**Part 4: Lawyer confirmation:**
Voluntary manslaughter is an intentional killing under mitigating circumstances, including imperfect self-defense. Involuntary manslaughter (criminal negligence or unlawful-act homicide) is unintentional. Mary intentionally killed. The correct outcome involves the intentional-killing framework. MPC § 210.3 vs. § 210.4.

### Choice B — Murder with no mitigation (unreasonable belief is legally irrelevant)

**Part 1: Student-accessible C3 signal:**
The stem's FIRST sentence says "The jurisdiction recognizes imperfect self-defense." That is a fact given to the student. Choice B says "an unreasonable belief is legally irrelevant everywhere." That claim is directly contradicted by the stem's premise. A student who reads the stem and then reads B can see the contradiction: the stem says the jurisdiction DOES give legal relevance to unreasonable beliefs (through imperfect self-defense), while B says it's "legally irrelevant everywhere."

**Part 2: Student label:**
Absolute overclaim that ignores the stem's established exception.

**Part 3: What a true/responsive version would look like:**
"Murder, because the jurisdiction applies imperfect self-defense only when deadly force was actually threatened — not when the threat was mistaken." This would be a harder variant, but it's not what B says. B says "legally irrelevant everywhere," which is disproven by the first sentence.

**Part 4: Lawyer confirmation:**
Imperfect self-defense is a well-recognized common law doctrine that gives legal relevance to an honest but unreasonable deadly-force belief, reducing murder to voluntary manslaughter. B's "legally irrelevant everywhere" is a false absolute statement.

### Choice C — Voluntary manslaughter (imperfect self-defense) ★ CORRECT

**Part 1: Student-accessible C3 signal:**
**Silver Key: call_focus** — The call asks for the "best effect" of Mary's belief. C directly matches the doctrine announced in the first stem sentence: the jurisdiction recognizes imperfect self-defense, which specifically addresses honest but unreasonable deadly-force beliefs. Mary's belief is honest (she believed Hannah was drawing a pistol) but unreasonable (reasonable person would have seen the tissue). That's the exact trigger for imperfect self-defense → voluntary manslaughter.

**Part 2: Student label:**
Correct answer — voluntary manslaughter via imperfect self-defense mitigation.

**Part 3: What a true/responsive version would look like:**
C is the correct answer; no alternative formulation needed.

**Part 4: Lawyer confirmation:**
**Gold Key: imperfect-self-defense gateway** — The doctrine is: An honest but unreasonable belief in the need for deadly force reduces murder to voluntary manslaughter in jurisdictions recognizing imperfect self-defense. *People v. Flannel*, 25 Cal.3d 668 (1980). Mary's belief was honest (she genuinely feared a pistol) but unreasonable (a reasonable person would have seen a tissue). The jurisdiction recognizes imperfect self-defense. Result: voluntary manslaughter.

### Choice D — Complete acquittal (honest fear)

**Part 1: Student-accessible C3 signal:**
Full self-defense requires BOTH an honest belief AND a reasonable belief. The stem tells us Mary's belief was unreasonable (a reasonable person would have seen the tissue). So full self-defense cannot apply — that would require two prongs, and only one is satisfied. The most the law gives for a one-prong case in this jurisdiction is imperfect self-defense, which mitigates but does not acquit.

**Part 2: Student label:**
Dominant trap — overclaims honest belief as a complete defense.

**Part 3: What a true/responsive version would look like:**
"If Mary's belief had been both honest AND reasonable — if a reasonable person would also have believed Hannah was drawing a pistol — then complete acquittal via self-defense would be available." But the stem says the belief was unreasonable.

**Part 4: Lawyer confirmation:**
**Gold Key: imperfect-self-defense gateway** — Complete self-defense is a full justification requiring both subjective and objective reasonableness. *People v. Goetz*, 68 N.Y.2d 96 (1986) (New York's justification defense requires both subjective and reasonable belief). Mary satisfies only the subjective prong — she honestly feared harm. But her fear was objectively unreasonable. In a jurisdiction recognizing imperfect self-defense, the honest prong alone does not justify; it only mitigates murder to voluntary manslaughter. *People v. Flannel*, 25 Cal.3d 668 (1980).
