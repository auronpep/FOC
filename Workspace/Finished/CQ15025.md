## 1. Final question

Lydia once owned two neighboring parcels, Cedar Field and Olive Grove. Cedar Field fronted on a rutted public lane, while Olive Grove fronted on Apostle Avenue, a paved state highway. Eighteen years ago, Lydia conveyed Cedar Field to Paul “together with a right-of-way 18 feet wide along the north edge of Olive Grove to Apostle Avenue.” At that time, Cedar Field was improved with a private Bible-verse miniature golf course with eight holes. Twelve years ago, Lydia died. Her will devised Olive Grove “to Paul for life, remainder to Ruth.” Four years ago, Paul executed an instrument in the proper form of a deed, purporting to convey Cedar Field and Olive Grove to Timothy in fee simple. Timothy then expanded the course to ten holes. Six months ago, Paul died and Ruth took possession of Olive Grove. Ruth brought an appropriate action to enjoin Timothy from using the right-of-way. In this action, who should prevail?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A landowner grants a parcel together with an express right-of-way over retained land, later the grantee holds only a life estate in the servient parcel, and then the grantee purports to convey both parcels. After the life tenant dies, does the buyer of the dominant parcel keep using the right-of-way?

## 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct. Gold Key:** an express easement appurtenant is tied to the dominant parcel and passes with that parcel to successors unless a valid termination event cuts it off. Lydia expressly granted Cedar Field a right-of-way over Olive Grove. Paul later conveyed Cedar Field to Timothy, so Timothy received the benefit of that express right-of-way. Paul’s temporary life estate in Olive Grove did not destroy that right. Timothy’s modest expansion of the same private miniature golf use also does not change the source of the easement. Ruth therefore should not enjoin Timothy from using the right-of-way.

**Silver Key:** sort the array by source of the path right. B uses the source that matches the deed. C gives Timothy the right winner for the wrong source.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Ruth, because Timothy has overburdened the easement.**
The expansion fact makes this tempting: an eight-hole Bible-verse course became a ten-hole course, so increased traffic feels like the issue. The breaker is that the same dominant parcel is still using the same right-of-way for the same general access purpose. **Gold Key:** normal development of the benefited parcel does not equal overburden unless the use becomes unreasonable or outside the easement’s scope.

**C. Timothy, because he has an easement by necessity.**
This choice is attractive because it picks Timothy and uses a familiar easement label. The breaker is source. The facts give an express right-of-way, and Cedar Field also fronts a public lane. **Gold Key:** necessity is not a convenience label; it requires lack of legally sufficient access, not merely a worse road.

**D. Ruth, because merger extinguished the easement.**
This is the dominant trap. Paul seemed to control both pieces at once: he owned Cedar Field and held a life estate in Olive Grove. The breaker is title quality. **Gold Key:** merger requires complete, coextensive ownership of the dominant and servient estates. A fee in Cedar Field plus only a life estate in Olive Grove is not enough.

## 8. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The original tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are preserved: an express easement appurtenant was granted over one parcel for the benefit of another, and the buyer of the dominant parcel keeps the easement unless it was terminated or exceeded in scope. The source row’s official explanation identifies D as correct because the granted easement ran with the land to the friend, and rejects merger, overburden, and necessity on the same mechanics. 

Authority floor: the Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes is an appropriate servitudes authority; the ALI describes the Restatement volume as covering creation, interpretation, succession to benefits and burdens, modification, termination, and enforcement of servitudes. ([American Law Institute][1]) Appurtenant easements are attached to the land and transfer automatically with the benefited land; ordinary changes in use may be allowed for reasonable development of the dominant estate. ([Justia][2]) Merger requires unity of title or ownership that is coextensive in quality and circumstance; Mularoni v. Bing cites Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 7.5 comment a for the merger principle. ([Justia Law][3]) An implied easement by necessity requires unity of ownership and necessity at severance, traditionally strict landlocked necessity; it does not fit a parcel with public-road access. ([Legal Information Institute][4])
