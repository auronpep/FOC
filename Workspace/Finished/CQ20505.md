## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

**Tested black-letter rule.** Under Fed. R. Civ. P. 65(b)(1), a court may issue a temporary restraining order without written or oral notice to the adverse party only if: (A) specific facts in an affidavit or a verified complaint clearly show that immediate and irreparable injury, loss, or damage will result to the movant before the adverse party can be heard in opposition; and (B) the movant's attorney certifies in writing any efforts made to give notice and the reasons why it should not be required. Additionally, Fed. R. Civ. P. 65(c) provides that the court may issue a preliminary injunction or a temporary restraining order only if the movant gives security in an amount the court considers proper to pay the costs and damages sustained by any party found to have been wrongfully enjoined or restrained.

There is no requirement that the applicant have first moved for or been granted a preliminary injunction. A TRO is frequently the first step, used precisely to maintain the status quo until a noticed preliminary injunction hearing (which always requires notice) can be held. A preliminary injunction cannot be issued ex parte.

The source row's official explanation matches exactly: a party can request a TRO when injury will occur before the grant of a preliminary injunction; the correct order is TRO first, preliminary injunction second; and a preliminary injunction cannot be issued without notice.
