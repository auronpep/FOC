### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. Ruth gave the Lydia Players permission to use the hall temporarily, without charge, and without a stated term. That arrangement did not give the troupe a leasehold right to remake the building's structure on its own terms. ### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. An occupant who receives oral permission to occupy land or a building with no stated duration and no periodic rent does not hold a fixed-term lease. At common law, that arrangement is treated as a tenancy at will, or at minimum revocable permission, unless the facts show a more durable leasehold. A tenancy at will is terminable by either party, and the owner may condition continued occupancy. That supports the credited answer: the Lydia Players had not entered into a lease that gave them a right to make permanent structural alterations.

Authority checked:

- Restatement (Second) of Property: Landlord and Tenant section 1.6, comments a and b, as quoted and applied in `Hefner v. Napoleon`, confirms that a tenancy at will is terminable at either party's will and is presumed where no duration is stated and no periodic rent is reserved or paid. Source: https://cnmilaw.org/pdf/superior/93-12-09-CV93-0007.pdf
- The same decision separately discusses license doctrine and cites Restatement of Property, Servitudes sections 512 and 519 for the rule that a license arises by consent to use another's land and is generally revocable. Source: https://cnmilaw.org/pdf/superior/93-12-09-CV93-0007.pdf
- Secondary cross-check: an open property casebook summarizes the same common-law category: a tenancy at will has no fixed duration and may be ended by either party. Source: https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/510-open-source-property/as-printable-html/18/
