### 1. Final Question

In 2003, Hannah conveyed a farm to "my sons Timothy and Stephen as joint tenants and not as tenants in common, with full right of survivorship." Timothy, the older son, died in 2008, leaving a will that devised "all my interest in the farm to my daughter Lydia for life, then to Lydia's daughters for life, then to all children of Lydia's daughters whenever born." In 2016, Mary contracted to sell the farm to Esther, promising to convey marketable title. When Mary tendered a general warranty deed on the date title was to close, Esther refused to accept it on the ground that Mary's title was unmarketable. In an appropriate action by Mary against Esther for damages resulting from Esther's breach of contract, should the court rule in Mary's favor?

### 7. Right-Answer Explanation

**C is correct.** Timothy and Stephen were joint tenants with right of survivorship. **Gold Key (JT Survivorship):** A joint tenant cannot devise their interest by will. At the moment of Timothy's death in 2008, his interest in the farm passed automatically to Stephen by right of survivorship — not through Timothy's will. Timothy's will was legally ineffective as to the farm. Stephen then held the entire farm in fee simple absolute as the sole owner. When Stephen died in 2011, he held a devisable fee simple — so his will effectively passed the farm to Philip. Philip held fee simple absolute, and his quitclaim deed to Mary conveyed that fee simple to her. **Gold Key (Quitclaim Conveys Actual Interest):** A quitclaim deed conveys the grantor's actual interest — whatever it is. Philip held clean fee simple absolute, so Mary received clean fee simple absolute. The only potential challenge to Mary's title would come from persons claiming under Timothy's will, but those claims have no reasonable chance of success because the will was legally inoperative. Title is marketable. Esther's refusal was a breach. **Silver Key (Array):** This is a two-Yes / two-No array — decide which direction is correct first. Survivorship makes the devise ineffective → title is clean → direction is Yes.

### 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A — No, because the devise in Timothy's will gave interests in the farm to Lydia, Lydia's daughters, and any children of Lydia's daughters.**
A is the dominant trap. Timothy's will is elaborate — it strings out life estates through a daughter, then granddaughters, then great-grandchildren. A student who reads that will sees an army of potential claimants and instinctively thinks "title cloud." The cognitive move is: if these people could assert a claim, a reasonable buyer wouldn't accept this title. But the premise is fatally incomplete: **Gold Key (JT Survivorship):** Because Timothy and Stephen were joint tenants, Timothy's interest passed to Stephen by survivorship the moment Timothy died — Timothy had no interest left to devise. The will attempted to convey what Timothy no longer held. Persons claiming under the will have no reasonable chance of success in court, so the title is not clouded.

**B — No, because a title derived from a quitclaim deed is unmarketable.**
B articulates one of the most persistent property-law myths: that a quitclaim deed signals defective title. A student who associates quitclaims with distressed or uncertain ownership sees "quitclaim deed" in the chain and concludes the title must be shaky. **Gold Key (Quitclaim Conveys Actual Interest):** A quitclaim deed conveys whatever interest the grantor holds — neither more nor less. Philip held fee simple absolute (by valid devise from Stephen, who was sole owner by JT survivorship). Philip's quitclaim to Mary conveyed fee simple absolute. The instrument's form has no bearing on the quality of what is conveyed; only the grantor's actual interest determines that.

**D — Yes, because Mary's tender of a general warranty deed gave Esther sufficient protection, even if Mary's title was unmarketable.**
D reaches the right direction (Yes) but for a legally wrong reason. The cognitive move is: a general warranty deed gives the buyer recourse against the seller for any title defect — so even if title is bad, the buyer is protected by the covenants. This misstates the law. **Silver Key (Deed Form After Marketability):** The general warranty deed doesn't establish whether title is marketable; marketability is assessed on its own, before the deed's protective covenants matter. If title were actually unmarketable, Esther would not be required to purchase a lawsuit — she wouldn't be obligated to accept any deed. Because title IS marketable (for the reason in C), D's hypothetical ("even if title is unmarketable") is both legally wrong and factually beside the point.

### 9. Black-Letter Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Tested rules:**

**Joint tenancy survivorship and devise:** At common law and under the Restatement (Third) of Property: Wills and Other Donative Transfers § 26, a joint tenant cannot pass their interest by will. The right of survivorship means that on a joint tenant's death, the interest passes automatically and instantaneously to the surviving joint tenant(s). The deceased joint tenant has no devisable interest. See also 4 Thompson on Real Property § 31.06 (2d ed.); Tenhet v. Boswell, 554 P.2d 330 (Cal. 1976) (well-known leading case on JT survivorship preventing testamentary transfer).

**Marketable title:** Title is marketable if a reasonable buyer, fully informed of the facts and their legal significance, would be willing to accept it. Title is not marketable if there is a reasonable doubt about a third party's ability to assert a valid claim. Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 7.2 cmt. b; see generally Conklin v. Davi, 388 A.2d 598 (N.J. 1978). Because Timothy's will was legally ineffective, the potential claimants under it have no reasonable basis for a successful lawsuit — the title is marketable.

**Quitclaim deeds:** A quitclaim deed operates to convey the grantor's present interest in the property, whatever that interest may be. It carries no covenants and makes no representations about title quality, but it does convey the grantor's full actual interest if the grantor holds one. Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 4.1 cmt. c. A quitclaim from a holder of fee simple absolute conveys fee simple absolute.

**General warranty deed and marketable title:** A contract promising marketable title requires the seller to produce title a reasonable buyer would accept, not merely a deed with strong covenants. If title is unmarketable, a buyer is not required to accept any deed, even a general warranty deed, because the buyer should not be forced to take a litigation risk. Lohmeyer v. Bower, 227 P.2d 102 (Kan. 1951) (classic marketable title case).

**Key agreement:** Official key (A → now C) is confirmed correct. No disagreement; no NEEDS_HUMAN flag.

#### 8. choice_by_choice_walkthrough

**Choice A — No, because Timothy's will gave interests to Lydia, Lydia's daughters, and any children of Lydia's daughters.**

1. *Student-accessible C3 signal:* Timothy's will is the most intimidating part of this fact pattern. Life estate for Lydia, then life estates for all of Lydia's daughters, then fee simple to all their children "whenever born" — that's potentially many people who could assert claims. The cognitive move is: multiple potential claimants = title cloud = unmarketable. **Silver Key (Will Trap):** Before reading the will's provisions, ask whether Timothy was a joint tenant. He was. **Gold Key (JT Survivorship):** JT survivorship made Timothy's will legally inoperative as to the farm. Timothy's interest passed to Stephen at the moment of Timothy's death, before the will could operate. The elaborate devise language is a nullity — it attempted to convey what Timothy no longer held. The potential claimants under the will have no reasonable chance of success.
2. *Student label:* Title cloud illusion — dominant trap (half_truth).
3. *True/responsive version would look like:* "No, because Lydia, her daughters, and their children hold valid interests conveyed by a court-recognized devise from Timothy, creating a reasonable basis for adverse claims."
4. *Lawyer confirmation:* A joint tenant cannot devise their interest by will; survivorship is instantaneous at death and superior to the will. Tenhet v. Boswell, 554 P.2d 330 (Cal. 1976). Restatement (Third) of Property: Wills and Other Donative Transfers § 26. **Gold Key (JT Survivorship):** the will was legally inoperative and created no title cloud.

**Choice B — No, because a title derived from a quitclaim deed is unmarketable.**

1. *Student-accessible C3 signal:* B articulates one of the most persistent property myths: quitclaim deed = uncertain or defective title. Students who associate quitclaim with distressed situations see "Philip quitclaimed" and think the chain is suspect. But the deed form doesn't determine the grantor's actual interest. **Gold Key (Quitclaim Conveys Actual Interest):** A quitclaim deed conveys whatever the grantor holds. Philip held fee simple absolute (by valid devise from Stephen, who was sole owner by survivorship). Mary received fee simple absolute.
2. *Student label:* Quitclaim myth — backwards.
3. *True/responsive version would look like:* "No, because Philip's quitclaim deed conveyed only a partial interest or a clouded interest, making the title defective."
4. *Lawyer confirmation:* A quitclaim deed conveys the grantor's present interest, nothing more and nothing less. Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 4.1 cmt. c. If the grantor holds fee simple absolute, the quitclaim conveys fee simple absolute. **Gold Key (Quitclaim Conveys Actual Interest)** kills B.

**Choice C — Yes, because Stephen became the sole owner of the farm upon Timothy's death in 2008. ← CORRECT**

1. *Student-accessible C3 signal:* C gives the legal basis for why the chain is clean: JT survivorship. Timothy's death in 2008 transferred his interest to Stephen automatically. Stephen then held fee simple absolute — a fully devisable estate. Stephen's will to Philip was effective. Philip's quitclaim to Mary conveyed fee simple absolute. Timothy's will was inoperative and creates no viable claim.
2. *Student label:* Correct — JT survivorship → clean chain.
3. *True/responsive version:* This IS the true and responsive answer. The JT survivorship rule, properly applied, confirms the chain of title is clean and the title is marketable.
4. *Lawyer confirmation:* JT survivorship: Tenhet v. Boswell, 554 P.2d 330 (Cal. 1976). Marketable title standard: Lohmeyer v. Bower, 227 P.2d 102 (Kan. 1951). **Gold Keys (JT Survivorship + Quitclaim)** both confirm.

**Choice D — Yes, because Mary's tender of a general warranty deed gave Esther sufficient protection, even if title is unmarketable.**

1. *Student-accessible C3 signal:* D reaches the right direction (Yes) but for the wrong reason. The cognitive move is: a general warranty deed gives the buyer covenants — if anything goes wrong with title, the buyer has recourse against the seller. So even if title is shaky, the buyer is "protected." But that's not how marketable title works. **Silver Key (Deed Form After Marketability):** If title is unmarketable, Esther should not have to accept a lawsuit even with a GWD — she shouldn't be compelled to buy litigation risk. The deed form doesn't establish whether title is marketable; that's evaluated independently. And once title IS marketable (via JT survivorship), D's hypothetical ("even if title is unmarketable") is beside the point.
2. *Student label:* GWD cures all — flat misstatement.
3. *True/responsive version would look like:* "Yes, because Mary holds marketable title and her tender of a general warranty deed satisfied her contractual obligation."
4. *Lawyer confirmation:* A buyer contracting for marketable title is not required to accept any deed if title is unmarketable. Lohmeyer v. Bower, 227 P.2d 102 (Kan. 1951). The seller's obligation is to produce marketable title, not merely to tender a deed with strong covenants.
