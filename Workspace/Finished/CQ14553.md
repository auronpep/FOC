## 1. Final question

Lydia owned a private Bible-library with a glass atrium and a painted Gospel mural. Peter, a specialist in suspending oversized Christian art, signed a written contract with Lydia to hoist and mount a heavy bronze dove-and-olive-branch chandelier in the atrium.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is the best answer.

**Gold Key:** Delegation is generally permitted, even to an equally skilled delegate, unless the other party has a substantial interest in the original promisor’s performance or control. A clause that leaves Lydia bearing physical-damage risk during the installation gives her a concrete reason to insist on Peter’s own control of the work.

**Silver Key:** Sort the clauses by function. Timing clauses, remedy clauses, and extra-work writing clauses do not answer a consent-to-delegate question unless they affect who controls performance or who bears performance risk.

The physical-damage waiver is the clause that makes performer identity matter. If Barnabas damages the atrium or mural during installation, Lydia may be exposed to the loss because Peter’s contract removes Peter’s liability for that damage. That risk supports Lydia’s argument that she has a substantial interest in Peter, not Barnabas, performing or controlling the installation.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. The per-day late-performance damages clause.**
This clause is attractive because it sounds like protection against bad performance. The breaker is clause function: a late-performance remedy answers what happens if the job is late, not whether Lydia must accept a substitute performer. It does not connect to Peter’s personal control of the installation.

**B. The signed-writing clause for later additional decorative work.**
This is the dominant trap. It sells the idea that any later takeover needs signed consent because it uses formal signed-writing language. **Silver Key:** read what the clause is about before treating it as a consent clause. It governs later additional decorative work, not delegation of the original chandelier installation. **Gold Key:** the delegation issue turns on substantial interest in Peter’s performance or control; a change-order clause for extra work does not create that interest.

**D. The “time is of the essence” clause.**
This clause is attractive because it is forceful and familiar. The breaker is the call: Lydia is not arguing that Barnabas will be late. She is arguing that Peter’s duties are not delegable without consent. A timing clause makes punctual performance important; it does not make an equally skilled replacement performer unacceptable.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The source row supplies QID 14553, the Contracts / Third-Party Rights classification, selection data, original choices, official key, and official explanations.  The verified outline placement is `53070300`, listed under Contracts → Third-Party Rights → Delegation of Duties. 

The governing rule is unchanged. A contractual duty may generally be delegated, but delegation is improper when the obligee has a substantial interest in having the original promisor perform or control the promised acts. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 318 states that an obligor generally may delegate performance, but a promise requires performance by a particular person to the extent the obligee has a substantial interest in that person’s performance or control; it also preserves the delegating obligor’s liability absent obligee assent. ([LexisNexis][1]) UCC § 2-210 uses the same substantial-interest formulation for delegation in Article 2 contracts. ([Legal Information Institute][2])

Here, the physical-damage waiver is the only clause that supplies the substantial-interest argument. Lydia bears the physical-damage exposure if the installation harms her Bible-library or atrium. Even though Barnabas is equally skilled, Lydia can argue that she bargained for Peter’s own control because the contract places installation-damage risk on her. The credited outcome is therefore unchanged.
