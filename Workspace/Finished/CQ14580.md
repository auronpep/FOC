## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct. Gold Key:** When officers lawfully encounter a suspect with the target contraband and have probable cause to make a custodial arrest, they may search the suspect's person incident to that arrest. The search does not need a second warrant, a separate weapon-specific reason, formal arrest words before the search, or Miranda warnings before physical evidence on the person can be seized.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged. The Fourth Amendment permits a warrantless search of the arrestee's person incident to a lawful custodial arrest. The Supreme Court in *United States v. Robinson*, 414 U.S. 218 (1973), treats a full search of the person after a lawful custodial arrest as a categorical search-incident rule. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/414/218/

*Chimel v. California*, 395 U.S. 752 (1969), supplies the standard rationale: after an arrest, officers may search the person arrested and the area within immediate control to remove weapons and preserve evidence. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/395/752/

*Rawlings v. Kentucky*, 448 U.S. 98 (1980), confirms that where probable cause to arrest exists and formal arrest follows quickly, the search may be upheld as incident to the arrest even if exact sequencing is not mechanically perfect. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/448/98

The bank key is therefore verified. The officers had a valid warrant to search for the keyboard case and fentanyl. They found Peter holding the target case. That gave probable cause for a custodial arrest. The lockblade knife and 9mm pistol were found on Peter's person during the arrest-linked search. The correct suppression result is denial because the search was incident to a lawful arrest.

The entry-execution trap does not change the answer. The source facts supply an announced warrant entry and danger/drug-destruction facts. In any event, *Hudson v. Michigan*, 547 U.S. 586 (2006), holds that a knock-and-announce violation does not automatically require suppression of evidence found under a valid warrant. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/547/586/
