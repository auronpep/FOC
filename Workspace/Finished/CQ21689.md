## Final Question

Detectives investigating a fatal van crash learned that the brake line on Mary's privately owned choir van had been cut after an evening hymn rehearsal. They brought Lydia to the station and, as part of an intentional department policy, questioned her for 80 minutes without giving Miranda warnings until she admitted that she had planned the brake-line sabotage. The detectives then gave Lydia Miranda warnings, obtained her signed waiver, and resumed questioning. They reminded Lydia of her earlier admissions and asked her to repeat and confirm them for the written record. Lydia repeated the confession. Lydia moves to suppress the post-warning confession.

Should the post-warning confession be suppressed?

## Distilled Core Question

Police intentionally question first without Miranda warnings, get a confession, warn later, and use the first confession to get the suspect to repeat it; is the repeated post-warning confession admissible?

## Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. The decisive fact is not merely that there were two interviews. The record says the department intentionally used a question-first, warning-second sequence, then used the earlier unwarned admission to get Lydia to repeat the confession after warnings. That is the *Seibert* problem. The later warning does not function as a real fresh choice when it is used to formalize the confession the officers deliberately extracted first.

Gold Key: *Elstad* covers an unwarned-first/warned-later sequence when the first failure to warn is not a calculated Miranda workaround; *Seibert* controls when the two-step sequence is deliberate and designed to undermine the warning.

Silver Key: When an answer pair fights over *Elstad* versus deliberate two-step interrogation, do not stop at "warnings were later given"; make the clash turn on why the first warning failure happened.

## Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because it grabs the clean formal acts: warnings were given, and Lydia signed a waiver. The breaker is that formal warnings and a written waiver do not cure a deliberate question-first tactic when the second interview exploits the first confession.

C is attractive because the answer reaches the right suppression result and sounds protective of Miranda. The breaker is its absolute word "every." *Elstad* rejects automatic taint for every unwarned-first sequence; suppression here depends on the deliberate two-step tactic.

D is the dominant trap because it names a real Supreme Court case from the same lane. Gold Key: *Elstad* is not a safe harbor for a calculated question-first plan.

## Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The governing issue is unchanged: whether a post-warning confession must be suppressed when police intentionally use a two-step interrogation strategy to obtain an unwarned confession first, then administer Miranda warnings and elicit a repeated confession.

The credited outcome is unchanged. Suppression is required. In *Missouri v. Seibert*, 542 U.S. 600 (2004), the Court held that a post-warning confession obtained through a deliberate question-first interrogation strategy could be inadmissible because the midstream warnings did not effectively advise the suspect of a real choice. Justice Kennedy's controlling concurrence applies the suppression rule when officers deliberately use the two-step technique in a calculated way to undermine Miranda, absent curative measures. The LOC U.S. Reports PDF is here: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep542/usrep542600/usrep542600.pdf.

The adjacent rule is *Oregon v. Elstad*, 470 U.S. 298 (1985), which held that a later warned confession is not automatically suppressed merely because of an earlier unwarned but voluntary statement. The LOC U.S. Reports PDF is here: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep470/usrep470298/usrep470298.pdf. This source confirms why C is too broad and D is nonresponsive to the deliberate-policy fact.
