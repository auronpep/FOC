## 7. Full right-answer explanation — exam register

Silver Key: Treat the choices as a 2×2 charge grid. First ask whether larceny exists; then ask whether conspiracy exists.

Gold Key: Larceny needs another person’s property and a trespassory taking. Conspiracy needs a real agreement by two people who both intend the target crime.

Ruth owned the lantern and told Martha to take it. That removes the trespassory-taking element of larceny. Ruth also knew the lantern was hers and was joking, so she did not intend the lantern to be stolen. That removes the genuine criminal agreement needed for conspiracy. The residual answer is C: neither larceny nor conspiracy.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

### A. Conspiracy only

The pull: Martha and Ruth appeared to plan the taking together, and Ruth even staged a distraction.

Breaker: conspiracy needs two people who both intend the target crime. Ruth knew the lantern was hers and was joking, so she lacked the specific intent to commit larceny. There is no conspiracy.

### B. Larceny only

The pull: Martha physically took the lantern and left with it, which makes the larceny label feel natural.

Breaker: larceny needs a trespassory taking of another person’s property. Because Ruth owned the lantern and consented to Martha taking it, the taking did not violate Ruth’s ownership rights. There is no larceny.

### D. Larceny and conspiracy

The pull: this answer combines the two surface facts that feel most incriminating: Martha moved the lantern, and Ruth helped create the diversion.

Breaker: the same ownership and joke facts defeat both charges. Owner consent defeats larceny; Ruth’s lack of criminal intent defeats conspiracy. The compound answer overclaims.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

Legal layer only: the item tests common-law larceny and conspiracy.

- Larceny: at common law, larceny is the trespassory taking and carrying away of personal property of another with intent to permanently deprive the owner. Cornell Wex describes larceny as a common-law crime involving illegal taking of another’s property with intent to deprive the owner. Because Ruth owned the lantern and consented to the taking, the taking was not trespassory as to the owner.
- Conspiracy: Cornell Wex defines conspiracy as an agreement between two or more people to commit an illegal act, with intent to achieve the agreement’s goal. 18 U.S.C. § 371 likewise requires “two or more persons” to conspire and an act to effect the object. Ruth knew the lantern was hers and was joking; she did not intend the target crime. Under the bilateral-agreement framing used by the supplied official key, there is no conspiracy.
- Attempt / impossibility: the question asks completed larceny and conspiracy, not attempt. The impossibility-adjacent fact is relevant only to show why the completed offenses fail.
