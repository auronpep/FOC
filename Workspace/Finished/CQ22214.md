## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Hearsay is an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted (FRE 801(c)). Here, Barnabas is not offering the customer's statement to prove that the merchant actually *is* dangerous or has *actually* threatened to kill people. He's offering it to show that he heard the warning, which caused him to fear the merchant — making his use of force in self-defense reasonable. Because the statement is offered for a non-hearsay purpose (its effect on the listener), it is not hearsay at all and is admissible without needing to satisfy any hearsay exception.

**Gold Key: GK-EVID-NON-HEARSAY-01** — An out-of-court statement offered to prove its effect on the listener, rather than the truth of the matter asserted, is not hearsay under FRE 801(c) and requires no hearsay exception to be admissible.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

### Choice A: "No, because the statement does not meet the applicable hearsay exception."

This is the **dominant trap**. A student who classifies the customer's statement as hearsay will immediately start scanning for an applicable exception — present sense impression, excited utterance, state of mind — and when none clearly fits, will conclude the statement is inadmissible. The error is the threshold classification: the statement is not hearsay in the first place because it's not offered for its truth. The Gold Key (`GK-EVID-NON-HEARSAY-01`) defeats this trap: always ask *why* the statement is being offered before asking whether a hearsay exception applies.

### Choice B: "No, because there is no indication that the customer is unavailable to testify."

This choice tempts a student who knows that some hearsay exceptions require unavailability (e.g., former testimony, dying declaration). The student reasons: "This might be hearsay, and since the customer isn't shown to be unavailable, the exception fails." But this analysis is doubly wrong — the statement isn't hearsay, and availability is therefore irrelevant. The Gold Key (`GK-EVID-NON-HEARSAY-01`) blocks this: availability only matters for hearsay exceptions that require unavailability, and this statement isn't hearsay at all.

### Choice D: "Yes, because Barnabas is defending himself against a criminal charge."

This choice offers the right outcome (Yes) for the wrong reason. A student may think that the criminal-defense context relaxes the hearsay rule — that defendants get a break. ## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification

**Rule:** Federal Rule of Evidence 801(c) — Hearsay Definition.

> "Hearsay" is a statement that: (1) the declarant does not make while testifying at the current trial or hearing; and (2) a party offers in evidence to prove the truth of the matter asserted in the statement.

**Key principle — Effect on Listener (Non-Hearsay Purpose):** An out-of-court statement offered for a purpose other than proving the truth of the matter asserted — such as proving the statement was made, or proving its effect on the listener's state of mind — is not hearsay under FRE 801(c) and is admissible without satisfying any hearsay exception. *See United States v. Parry*, 649 F.2d 292, 294–95 (5th Cir. 1981) (statement offered to show defendant's state of mind, not for truth, is not hearsay); *United States v. Cruz-Diaz*, 550 F.3d 169, 176 (1st Cir. 2008) (same).

**Application:** Barnabas offers the customer's warning not to prove the merchant is actually dangerous, but to show Barnabas heard the warning and therefore reasonably feared the merchant when the confrontation occurred. This is a classic non-hearsay use — effect on the listener. The statement is admissible.

**Choice A: "No, because the statement does not meet the applicable hearsay exception."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This choice assumes the statement IS hearsay and then finds no exception. Before evaluating exceptions, ask: is this hearsay? The statement is offered to show Barnabas's fear, not to prove the merchant is actually dangerous. Not hearsay → no exception needed.

2. **Student label:** The hearsay-exception trap — skips the threshold classification.

4. **Lawyer confirmation:** FRE 801(c) defines hearsay as a statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted. A statement offered for a non-hearsay purpose is not hearsay. *United States v. Parry*, 649 F.2d 292 (5th Cir. 1981).

   > **Gold Key GK-EVID-NON-HEARSAY-01:** An out-of-court statement offered to prove its effect on the listener — rather than the truth of the matter asserted — is not hearsay under FRE 801(c) and requires no hearsay exception.

**Choice B: "No, because there is no indication that the customer is unavailable to testify."**
