## 1. Final Question

On July 3, Daniel, who ran a private Christian Scripture-quiz league, and Lydia, owner of a print shop, entered into a contract under which Lydia would deliver all of the spiral-bound study workbooks Daniel required for the league's October tournament. The contract provided that delivery would begin on October 1. Daniel had no reason to doubt Lydia's ability to print and deliver the workbooks, but he wanted a written promise that she was still on schedule. On August 1, Daniel sent Lydia a letter demanding adequate assurance that she could meet the October 1 delivery date. Lydia refused to provide the assurance. Daniel immediately ordered the workbooks from another printer.

If Lydia sues Daniel for breach of contract, is Lydia likely to prevail?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

If a requirements buyer with no reasonable grounds for insecurity demands adequate assurance and covers when the seller refuses, who breached?

## 3. Final Answer Choices

A. Yes, because Daniel's demand for assurance was unauthorized unless the contract expressly gave him that right.

B. Yes, because Lydia's refusal to provide assurance was not a repudiation when Daniel had no reasonable grounds for insecurity.

C. No, because a contract for all workbooks Daniel required failed for lack of a definite quantity.

D. No, because Lydia anticipatorily repudiated the contract when she refused Daniel's demand for adequate assurance.

## 4. Correct Answer

B (original key D).

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. Daniel had no reasonable basis to fear that Lydia would miss the October 1 delivery. Gold Key: a refusal to give adequate assurance becomes repudiation only after a justified demand, and the demand is justified only when the demanding party has reasonable grounds for insecurity. Silver Key: split the surviving answers on Daniel's reason for demanding assurance before judging Lydia's refusal. Because Daniel had no such reason, Lydia did not repudiate by refusing, and Daniel breached by ordering the workbooks elsewhere.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A. The attraction is the phrase "expressly gave him that right"; it makes Daniel's assurance letter sound like an extra-contract demand, so Lydia's lawsuit result feels right. The breaker is that adequate assurance is an implied UCC protection, not a right that must be written into the contract. Lydia wins because Daniel lacked reasonable grounds for insecurity, not because the contract omitted an assurance clause.

C. The attraction is the missing fixed number of workbooks. The breaker is that a requirements term is a valid quantity term for a sale of goods when it measures the buyer's good-faith requirements. This answer points at formation, but the tested problem is Daniel's unjustified assurance demand and early cover.

D. The dominant trap is that Lydia refused Daniel's assurance demand, which sounds like classic anticipatory repudiation language. Gold Key: refusal to assure is repudiation only after a justified demand. Because the stem says Daniel had no reason to doubt Lydia's performance, his demand never crossed that threshold.

## 8. Full Black-Letter Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged. UCC 2-609 allows a party to a sales contract to demand adequate assurance only when reasonable grounds for insecurity arise, and a failure to provide assurance is repudiation only after a justified demand. Between merchants, the reasonableness of the grounds and adequacy of assurance are judged by commercial standards. See D.C. Code 28:2-609, an enacted UCC 2-609 provision: https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/28%3A2-609 and Cornell LII UCC 2-609: https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-609.

Daniel had no reason to doubt Lydia's ability to perform. His written demand therefore was not justified, and Lydia's refusal was not an anticipatory repudiation. Daniel's immediate order from another printer breached the contract.

The requirements quantity attack also fails. UCC 2-306 validates output and requirements terms by measuring quantity by good-faith output or requirements, subject to limits on unreasonably disproportionate quantities. See D.C. Code 28:2-306: https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/28%3A2-306 and Cornell LII UCC 2-306: https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-306.

Authority floor: UCC 2-609 and UCC 2-306. Attorney review: false.
