### 7. Full right-answer explanation
C is correct. Lydia did the legally operative act when she handed the deed to Stephen for Daniel and used present-transfer words: "I want Daniel to have the cottage. Please take this deed for him." Gold Key: deed delivery turns on the grantor's intent to make a present transfer, and delivery to someone acting for the grantee counts. Silver Key: in a delivery question, identify the first event that legally made title pass; later recording and later death are bait events unless the handoff lacked present intent.

Daniel did not need to know about the deed before Lydia died because acceptance of a beneficial conveyance is presumed. Stephen's later recording strengthened the proof trail, but the transfer did not wait for recording.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because recording feels like the official public act and it happened before Lydia died. The breaker is that recording is evidence of delivery, not the delivery event when the grantor has already handed the deed to someone for the grantee with present-transfer intent. Silver Key: do not let the later courthouse event replace the earlier title-passing handoff.

B is attractive because the stem spotlights two witnesses and says the jurisdiction also requires two witnesses for a will. The breaker is that the deed did not operate as a will. Lydia used a deed and delivered it with present-transfer intent, so the title question is resolved by deed delivery, not by testamentary formalities.

D is attractive because Lydia was gravely ill and died soon after the handoff. The breaker is that gift-causa-mortis doctrine is the wrong tool for a real-property deed, and the death did not complete the transfer. Gold Key: a deed delivered with present-transfer intent works as an inter vivos conveyance; it is not a deathbed personal-property gift.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Real Property, Titles, Deeds, and Conveyancing, Conveyance by Deed, Delivery and acceptance, and the verified outline code is `84040203`. The black-letter rule is that a deed becomes operative through delivery and acceptance. Delivery depends on the grantor's intent that the deed presently pass an interest, and delivery may be made to the grantee or to a third person holding for the grantee if the grantor does not retain control. Acceptance of a beneficial deed is presumed.

Representative authority confirms the rule. *Sweeney v. Sweeney*, Supreme Court of Connecticut, Mar. 6, 1940, states the delivery inquiry in terms of intent to pass title and recognizes presumed acceptance for a beneficial deed. Source: https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/59147ef9add7b04934459aa3. *Rosengrant v. Rosengrant*, 629 P.2d 800 (Okla. Civ. App. 1981), distinguishes a failed death-substitute deed where the grantor retained control, while also citing the rule that third-party delivery is effective when the grantor intends to part with control. Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/oklahoma/court-of-appeals-civil/1981/5128.html. *Havens v. Schoen*, 108 Mich. App. 758, 310 N.W.2d 870 (1981), treats recording as a presumption/evidence point rather than the only way delivery occurs and notes presumed acceptance of a beneficial deed. Source: https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/59149192add7b0493458aab7. Cornell Wex separately summarizes the gift-causa-mortis limitation to personal property. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/gift_causa_mortis.

Those authorities confirm the official key. Lydia did not retain a right to retrieve the deed, did not tell Stephen to wait until death, and did not use testamentary language. She handed the deed to Daniel's friend for Daniel with words of present donative intent. That completed delivery before death. Daniel's acceptance was presumed because receiving the cottage was beneficial, and his later possession confirmed acceptance. Recording was not the explanation for Daniel's title, gift causa mortis did not apply to land, and the deed was not a will.

## review_truth
A deed transfers title when the grantor intends a present conveyance and delivers it to the grantee or someone acting for the grantee; recording is only evidence of delivery, and acceptance of a beneficial deed is presumed.
