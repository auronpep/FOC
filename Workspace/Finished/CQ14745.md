## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Paul planned to rob the cash trailer at a private Christian music festival. He promised Timothy one-third of the cash if Timothy would wait behind the arena in a van and drive him away after the robbery. Timothy agreed, but only after Paul promised that no one would be hurt.

Before the festival opened, Paul went to Lydia's outdoor-supply shop. He told Lydia that he needed a small handgun to scare the cashier during the trailer robbery. Lydia said that Paul was foolish to reveal the plan and that the price would be much higher because she was taking the risk of supplying the weapon for the robbery. She sold him the handgun for four times its regular price.

During the robbery, Paul pointed the handgun at the cashier and demanded the cash. The handgun fired accidentally, killing a festival attendee. Timothy then drove Paul away in the van.

In addition to Paul, who could properly be convicted of murder in the death of the attendee at common law?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

Who else is liable for a felony-murder death when one helper agreed to drive the getaway vehicle and another knowingly sold the weapon at a profit because it would be used in the robbery?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. Neither Timothy nor Lydia.

B. Only Lydia.

C. Both Timothy and Lydia.

D. Only Timothy.

### 5. Correct answer

C. Both Timothy and Lydia. Original key: A.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. Timothy agreed to help Paul rob the trailer by driving the getaway van. A killing during an armed robbery is a foreseeable act in furtherance of that robbery, even when Timothy asked Paul not to hurt anyone. Gold Key: a co-conspirator is responsible for reasonably foreseeable acts committed by another conspirator in furtherance of the agreed crime.

Lydia is also liable. She did more than make an ordinary sale with vague suspicion. She knew the handgun was for the robbery and raised the price because of that criminal use. Gold Key: knowledge plus a purposeful stake in the venture can make the seller an accomplice. Silver Key: treat the answer choices as a two-person liability grid; once Timothy is yes and Lydia is yes, the only responsive answer is both.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A. Neither Timothy nor Lydia. This choice feels clean if the accidental shot is treated as Paul's private act. The breaker is that both liability paths are active: Timothy is tied to the robbery through the getaway agreement, and Lydia is tied to the robbery by knowingly supplying the weapon at a premium because of its planned criminal use.

B. Only Lydia. This choice notices Lydia's unusually direct assistance but drops Timothy's conspiracy role. Timothy's "do not hurt anyone" condition does not withdraw him from the robbery plan. The foreseeable killing happened during the felony he agreed to help complete.

D. Only Timothy. This is the dominant trap. It correctly sees that Timothy can be liable for the foreseeable killing during the robbery, but it treats Lydia as a neutral merchant. Gold Key: a seller who knows the criminal plan and takes a special profit from supplying the means can be treated as intentionally aiding the venture.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The credited outcome is unchanged. At common law, robbery can support felony murder when a death occurs during the felony. A participant in a conspiracy may be liable for a co-conspirator's reasonably foreseeable act committed in furtherance of the conspiracy. Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640 (1946), states the federal co-conspirator liability principle in that form. Timothy agreed to drive the getaway van for a share of the robbery proceeds, so the armed robbery and accidental killing were foreseeable incidents of the plan.

Accomplice liability requires intentional assistance or encouragement of the principal offense. 18 U.S.C. section 2 reflects the federal aiding-and-abetting frame, and Rosemond v. United States, 572 U.S. 65 (2014), describes aiding and abetting as participation with the intent to facilitate the offense. Direct Sales Co. v. United States, 319 U.S. 703 (1943), supports treating a seller's knowing, profit-driven stake in an unlawful venture as evidence of intent to further it. Lydia knew the gun would be used in the robbery and charged extra because of that use, so the transformed seller keeps the original accomplice-liability result.
