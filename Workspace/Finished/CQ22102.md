## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**B is correct.** The lease gives no fixed duration. It does not say "for one year," "month to month," or any other definite term or recurring period. That points to a tenancy at will. **Gold Key:** under the majority rule, if a lease with no fixed duration says only the landlord may terminate at will, courts generally imply a matching right in the tenant; otherwise the landlord could keep the tenant bound indefinitely. **Silver Key:** the strongest trap repeats the contract language, but the call asks for the legal tenancy type under the majority rule, not just the literal wording.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A - A tenancy at will that may be terminated only by Martha.** This is the best-looking trap because it quotes the lease's one-sided termination language. The breaker is the majority-rule overlay. **Gold Key:** an at-will lease is not usually made one-way against the tenant; courts imply that either party may terminate.

**C - A periodic tenancy based on Daniel's rental payments.** Periodic tenancy is a familiar category, and rental payments can sometimes help identify a recurring period. But this stem does not give a recurring payment period as the measure of the term. The lease instead says the occupancy continues until Martha decides to end it.

**D - A term-of-years tenancy with an automatic ending when Martha gives the word.** "Automatic ending" sounds plausible because the lease describes what happens after Martha's decision. But a term of years needs a fixed, ascertainable duration from the start. Martha's future discretionary decision is not a fixed end date.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule:** A tenancy at will is a leasehold with no fixed duration that is terminable at the will of the parties. A term of years requires a fixed, ascertainable ending point. A periodic tenancy continues from period to period, usually tied to a stated rental period, until proper notice. Under the majority rule for a lease that purports to be terminable only at the landlord's will and contains no fixed duration, courts generally treat the lease as a tenancy at will terminable by either party, avoiding an indefinite one-sided restraint on the tenant.

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Property, Landlord and Tenant, addresses the nature and duration of landlord-tenant relationships in Chapter 1 and is the appropriate Restatement authority class for leasehold categories. Public landlord-tenant references likewise state the core black-letter rule: an estate at will has no designated duration and is terminable at the unilateral decision of either party; a lease for years has a definite period fixed in advance; a periodic tenancy continues by recurring periods. California Department of Real Estate, Reference Book ch. 9, "Landlord and Tenant," states those categories in exam-ready terms. Massachusetts materials discussing tenancies at will similarly describe the defining feature as termination at the will of either landlord or tenant, subject to statutory notice rules.

**Confirmation:** The credited outcome is unchanged from the source: the tenant has a tenancy at will terminable by either landlord or tenant. The legal issue is unchanged: how to classify a lease with no fixed duration and a purported landlord-only termination clause in a majority-rule jurisdiction. The dominant trap is unchanged: following the literal landlord-only language instead of the majority rule. Not on the doctrinal-currency hotlist. Authority floor satisfied by Restatement authority and state/common-law category references.
