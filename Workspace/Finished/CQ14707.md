Ruth and her sister Lydia returned to the United States from a Scripture-art market in Belize. At U.S. Customs, officers found packets of fentanyl powder hidden under false bottoms in separate wooden hymnbook display boxes that each sister was carrying. Both sisters were arrested. Ruth and Lydia were indicted for conspiracy to import fentanyl. They were tried separately. At Ruth's trial, after the government introduced the evidence above and rested, Ruth's lawyer moved for a judgment of acquittal based on insufficient evidence. Should the court grant the motion?

Two sisters return through U.S. Customs with hidden controlled substances in separate containers, and one says she warned the other about too many inspectors. Is that enough evidence to send conspiracy to the jury?

### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. The court should not grant the motion. The hidden fentanyl in both sisters' separate boxes plus Ruth's statement that she had warned Lydia about "too many inspectors" gives the government enough circumstantial evidence for a rational jury to infer an agreement to import the fentanyl. Gold Key: conspiracy turns on agreement, and agreement can be proven by circumstantial facts rather than a signed plan or a confession from every participant. Silver Key: on an insufficiency motion after the government rests, ask whether a rational jury could infer the missing element, not whether the judge would personally convict.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because it sees the obvious shared possession and reaches the same bottom-line "No." The breaker is that possession is not the conspiracy element; the missing element is agreement. Gold Key: joint possession alone is not enough, but Ruth's statement about warning Lydia supplies the agreement inference that the possession answer skips.

B is attractive because Ruth talked to officers and that can sound like cooperation. The breaker is that a post-arrest statement is not an effective withdrawal from a completed conspiracy for this sufficiency motion, and it also does not erase evidence that an agreement already existed.

D is attractive because separate containers can make the case look like two parallel possession crimes. The breaker is the word "only"; Ruth's own statement adds a planning conversation with Lydia, so a rational jury could find more than parallel possession. Silver Key: do not stop at the possession snapshot when the call is asking whether the agreement issue can go to the jury.

### 9. Full Black-Letter Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. A conspiracy requires an agreement to commit an unlawful objective, and the agreement may be proven through circumstantial evidence. The object here is importing a controlled substance; 21 U.S.C. section 963 punishes attempts and conspiracies to commit controlled-substance import/export offenses, and 21 U.S.C. section 952 makes unauthorized importation of covered controlled substances unlawful. Iannelli v. United States, 420 U.S. 770 (1975), describes conspiracy as an inchoate offense whose essence is an agreement to commit an unlawful act. Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307 (1979), supplies the sufficiency frame: evidence is sufficient when, viewed favorably to the prosecution, a rational trier of fact could find the elements beyond a reasonable doubt.

Under those authorities, the government did not have to prove the conspiracy by direct confession from Lydia. Ruth's statement that she warned Lydia about the number of inspectors, combined with both sisters carrying hidden fentanyl through Customs, supports a rational inference of agreement. Mere possession would not be enough by itself, but the statement makes the agreement question one for the jury. The court should deny acquittal.
