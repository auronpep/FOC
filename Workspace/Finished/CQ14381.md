## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question
Barnabas, a wholesaler of handbound Scripture journals, and Lydia, who runs a private Bible-study gift shop, entered into a written contract for Lydia to buy a shipment of journals. They used the same one-page order form they had used for several earlier journal orders. The form stated that payment was due 45 days after delivery and that the writing was the complete and exclusive statement of the parties' agreement. On several previous orders, Lydia had paid within 8 days of delivery and deducted 7% from the invoice, and Barnabas had accepted the payment without objection. This time, after Lydia paid within 8 days and deducted 7%, Barnabas objected because the leather covers on this shipment left him with a smaller margin. If Barnabas sues Lydia for breach of contract, may Lydia introduce evidence that the 7% early-payment discount was part of the agreement?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. The earlier journal orders created a course of dealing: repeated conduct between these same parties in earlier transactions. Gold Key: under the UCC, course-of-dealing evidence can explain or supplement a final sale-of-goods writing even when the writing says it is complete and exclusive. Silver Key: when the answer array splits between merger clause, later objection, modification, and course of dealing, lock onto the repeated previous transactions before choosing a parol-evidence answer. Lydia is not trying to prove a brand-new oral term that contradicts the writing; she is using the parties' prior pattern to show what their agreement meant. The evidence is admissible.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is the dominant trap. It is attractive because the form uses strong merger language, and students often treat "complete and exclusive" as the end of every parol-evidence question. The breaker is the UCC exception: Gold Key says course-of-dealing evidence may still explain or supplement a final writing, so the merger clause does not by itself exclude the prior-discount pattern.

C is attractive because the UCC really does relax the consideration requirement for good-faith modifications in sales of goods. The breaker is that modification is the wrong lane: Lydia is not arguing that the parties changed this contract after formation; she is offering the prior course of dealing to explain or supplement the contract's payment term.

D is attractive because Barnabas did object on this transaction, and the choice makes that objection sound timely and decisive. The breaker is timing and issue fit: the prior accepted discount pattern already supplies the course-of-dealing evidence, and Barnabas's objection after Lydia acted under that pattern does not make the evidence inadmissible.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. This remains a Contracts/UCC parol-evidence item about whether course-of-dealing evidence may be introduced despite a final writing with a complete-and-exclusive clause.

UCC section 2-202 provides that a final written expression may not be contradicted by prior or contemporaneous agreements, but it may be explained or supplemented by course of dealing, usage of trade, or course of performance. UCC section 1-303(b) defines course of dealing as a sequence of conduct in previous transactions between the parties that establishes a common basis for interpreting their conduct, and section 1-303(d) makes course of dealing relevant to ascertain, give particular meaning to, supplement, or qualify the agreement. See UCC section 2-202, https://www.law.cornell.edu/ucc/2/2-202; D.C. Code sections 28:2-202 and 28:1-303, https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/28%3A2-202 and https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/28%3A1-303.

Here, the repeated prior early-payment discounts on earlier journal orders are previous transactions between Barnabas and Lydia. The evidence explains or supplements the payment arrangement rather than proving an unrelated later modification. The complete-and-exclusive clause would block inconsistent additional terms, but it does not bar this course-of-dealing evidence under the UCC. The official key agrees with current black-letter law, so the bank-validation verdict is PASS.
