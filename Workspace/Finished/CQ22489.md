### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Promissory estoppel starts with a promise. Mary cried out for emergency help, but she did not say she would pay Peter, promise a reward, name compensation, or make any other commitment from which payment could reasonably be inferred.

**Gold Key:** Promissory estoppel can enforce a promise that induced reliance; it does not supply the missing promise itself. A general plea for rescue may invite action, but invitation is not the same thing as a payment commitment.

**Silver Key:** When the call says promissory estoppel, check the first word before everything else: was there a promise? If the facts give only distress words and no commitment language, the threshold promise gate decides the item before damages, restitution, or consideration.

Peter did a brave and helpful thing. But the legal theory he chose is not restitution or unjust enrichment; it is promissory estoppel. Without an express or reasonably implied promise by Mary to pay him, there is nothing for the doctrine to enforce.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. There was no consideration for any promise Mary made.** This is the dominant trap because it uses the default contract reflex: no consideration, no enforcement. The breaker is the Gold Key. Promissory estoppel is the doctrine that can enforce certain relied-on promises despite the absence of consideration, so the lack of consideration is not Mary's best defense to a promissory-estoppel claim.

**C. The value of Stephen's safety is too speculative.** This choice is attractive because a rescue of a person feels hard to price. The breaker is that promissory estoppel does not begin by pricing the value received by the alleged promisor. The claim fails at the threshold promise gate; even if a remedy were available, the focus would not be the abstract value of Stephen's safety.

**D. Peter was an officious intermeddler.** This choice sounds lawyerly because volunteers ordinarily cannot force payment for unwanted benefits. The breaker is the visible stem fact: Mary cried for someone to help, and Stephen was in immediate danger. Peter was responding to a requested emergency rescue, not forcing an unwanted benefit on Mary in a situation that called for no action.

### 8. Black-letter verification and legal reasoning

**Pass-1 step (1) - black-letter rule tested.** The item tests the threshold promise requirement in promissory estoppel. Reliance can substitute for consideration only when there is a promise to enforce.

**Lawyer-confirmation authority.** Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90 supplies the promissory-estoppel rule: a promise expected to induce action or forbearance, and that does induce it, may be binding when enforcement is needed to avoid injustice. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 2 supplies the promise concept: the alleged promisor must manifest an intention that justifies understanding a commitment has been made. The American Law Institute identifies the Restatement (Second) of Contracts as its authoritative Contracts publication, and H2O reproduces the relevant Restatement sections with permission. **Application.** Mary made no payment commitment. Her words asked for rescue, not compensation. Because promissory estoppel requires a promise before reliance matters, Mary's strongest defense is that no promise of payment can reasonably be inferred from the cry for help.
