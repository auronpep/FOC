### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Gold Key: burglary requires unlawful entry with intent to commit a felony or crime inside; when the intended felony is larceny, a person who honestly believes the property is hers or that she is authorized to take it lacks intent to steal. Lydia entered Naomi's shed intending to take Naomi's harp by deception. Timothy believed Lydia's authorization story, so he lacked theft intent. Naomi broke into Lydia's house, but she was recovering her own harp and lacked intent to steal Lydia's property.

Silver Key: do not count entries; count intent at each entry. The answer array is a person-list trap, and only Lydia has both an entry and the needed criminal intent.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because Naomi used a tool to force open Lydia's house, which looks like the most burglary-like act. The breaker is intent: Naomi entered to recover her own harp, so she lacked the larceny intent needed for the burglary target crime.

C is attractive because every named person entered or helped with an entry connected to the harp. Gold Key: burglary does not attach just because a person entered a structure; the person must enter with the required criminal intent. Timothy and Naomi both lack that intent.

D is attractive because Timothy opened the shed, climbed up, and handed down the harp while Lydia left with it. Silver Key: the physical taker is not automatically the burglar; the array turns on the person's belief at entry. Timothy honestly believed Lydia had lent the harp and was authorized to retrieve it.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged. Burglary requires unlawful or unprivileged entry into a building or structure with intent to commit a crime inside; the source jurisdiction keeps the common-law intent requirement while broadening covered structures and removing nighttime. Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575 (1990), states the generic burglary formulation as unlawful or unprivileged entry into a building or structure with intent to commit a crime. When the intended crime is larceny, common-law larceny requires taking another's personal property with intent to permanently deprive the owner. People v. Navarro, 99 Cal. App. 3d Supp. 1 (1979), reflects the claim-of-right principle that an honest belief in a right to take property negates the specific intent required for theft, even if the belief is mistaken.

Lydia entered Naomi's shed with intent to take Naomi's harp. Timothy lacked intent to steal because he honestly believed Lydia had lent the harp and could retrieve it. Naomi lacked intent to steal because the harp was hers and she reclaimed it. Only Lydia is guilty of burglary.
