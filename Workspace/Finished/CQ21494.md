## 7) Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The answer is **A**. The common-law year-and-a-day rule is a timing presumption, not a constitutional protection. The jurisdiction abolished it by statute five years before Stephen's attack, so the rule provides no defense on these facts. The remaining question is causation. The medical examiner testified that the brain damage from Stephen's beating was the cause of Lydia's death and that no independent intervening cause contributed. That testimony establishes both actual cause (but-for) and proximate cause. Withdrawal of life support from a patient in a persistent vegetative state caused by the defendant's lethal assault is a foreseeable, dependent consequence of the assault, not a superseding intervening cause that severs the chain. Stephen can be charged with homicide.

> **Silver Key (call_focus):** When the medical examiner testifies to cause of death and explicitly rules out independent intervening causes, follow the ME's chain — do not substitute your own "immediate cause" judgment.

## 8) Full wrong-answer explanations

### C (year-and-a-day, dominant trap)

The year-and-a-day rule is a common-law rule, not a constitutional one. The jurisdiction abolished it by statute five years before the attack. Once abolished, there is no time bar to a homicide prosecution in this jurisdiction. A rule that no longer exists cannot bar a charge. *Trap engineered:* the > 1 year time gap (19 months) and the lawyerly ring of "year and a day" tempt you to invoke a rule the stem has already told you is dead in this jurisdiction.

> **Silver Key (trap_spotting):** When a stem says "the jurisdiction abolished [common-law rule] by statute [N] years before [the act]," that rule is dead here. Read the abolition flag before reaching for the rule's name.

### D (withdrawal of life support as intervening cause)

Withdrawal of life support following a terminal injury inflicted by the defendant is not an independent intervening cause. The medical examiner's testimony establishes that the brain damage from the beating — not the withdrawal — was the cause of death, and that no independent intervening cause contributed. The proximate-cause test asks whether the intervening act was independent and unforeseeable. Medical decisions made in response to a defendant's lethal act are foreseeable, dependent consequences of that act and do not break the chain. *Trap engineered:* "withdrawal of life support" sounds like a fresh, voluntary act by a third party (the family) that breaks the chain. The legal test asks independence and foreseeability, not whether some new physical act preceded the death.

### B (yes, but — half-right + wrong reason)

The medical examiner's testimony in the stem directly contradicts the assertion that the jury cannot consider the beating as the cause of death: the ME testified the brain damage from the beating was the cause of death and that no independent intervening cause contributed. Causation analysis runs across the entire chain (beating → brain damage → PVS → family decision to withdraw → death), not only the last physical event. ## 9) Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

**Black-letter rule.** A defendant may be charged with homicide for a death that occurs more than a year and a day after the defendant's criminal act in any jurisdiction that has abolished the year-and-a-day rule, provided the prosecution can prove actual and proximate cause. The withdrawal of life support from a patient whose terminal condition was caused by the defendant is a foreseeable, dependent consequence of the defendant's act; it is not a superseding intervening cause that severs the chain of causation.

**Governing authority (real, not fabricated).** The year-and-a-day rule was a common-law rule. It has been abolished in the majority of U.S. jurisdictions by statute or judicial decision, and federal courts have not applied it. The U.S. Supreme Court has held that judicial abolition of the year-and-a-day rule does not violate the Ex Post Facto Clause. *Rogers v. Tennessee*, 532 U.S. 451 (2001). The modern proximate-cause rule treats medical treatment — including withdrawal of life support — following a defendant's act as a dependent, foreseeable consequence that does not break the chain unless it is grossly negligent or so unforeseeable as to be a wholly abnormal response. See, e.g., *State v. Shabazz*, 469 A.2d 732 (Conn. 1983) (medical-treatment decisions following a defendant's act are not superseding causes). The Supreme Court of Vermont in *State v. Roberts* (2024) abrogated the year-and-a-day rule and held that a victim's death after life support is withdrawn can support a murder charge. Cornell Legal Information Institute (Wex) confirms the modern framing of the rule. Sources: [Wikipedia — Year and a day rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_and_a_day_rule) (last reviewed 2024–2025); [Cornell LII — year and a day rule (Wex)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/year_and_a_day_rule) (last reviewed July 2024, citing *Rogers v. Tennessee*, 532 U.S. 451 (2001)); [Vermont Supreme Court eliminates year-and-a-day rule — Criminal Legal News (Feb. 2025)](https://www.criminallegalnews.org/news/2025/feb/1/vermont-supreme-court-eliminates-year-and-day-rule-murder-prosecutions/).
