## 1. Final question — stem + call

At a regional harvest fair, Lydia sells wine under her federally registered "Vine & Branch" mark from a corner booth. Stephen, a rival vintner, sets up the adjoining booth, pours his own bottles under a nearly identical "Vine & Branch" label, and — in the same afternoon, at the same booth — talks Lydia's exclusive distributor (who is browsing the fair) into tearing up her standing distribution contract on the spot. Lydia sues Stephen in federal court, pleading one federal Lanham Act trademark-infringement claim and one supplemental state-law tortious-interference claim arising from the same fair incident. Stephen answers the interference claim with a state-law competition-privilege defense. Lydia argues Erie is irrelevant because the court's original jurisdiction rests on the federal trademark claim.

**Call:** Is Erie irrelevant to the state-law claim?

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**A is credited.** The source of a claim, not the jurisdictional doorway the case walked through, decides what law governs it. Lydia is in federal court on a federal trademark claim, but her interference claim is a creature of state law, and a federal court hearing a state-law claim decides it under state substantive law — including Stephen's state competition privilege. **Gold Key:** Erie is not diversity-only; it reaches *any* state-law claim a federal court hears, including a supplemental claim riding along with a federal question, because supplemental jurisdiction is about the court's *power* to hear the claim, not about converting it into federal law. **Silver Key:** with two "No" answers on the board, take the one that states the rule cleanly (A) over the one that bolts on an unrequired condition (B) when the stem supplies no facts triggering that condition.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations (one per distractor)

- **C (dominant trap) — "Yes, because federal-question jurisdiction makes every claim federal."** It hands you the plaintiff's own framing back as if jurisdiction were a dye that stains every claim federal — the case is "in federal court on a federal question," so why would state law touch any of it? The breaker: supplemental jurisdiction gives the court *power* over the state claim without changing the claim's source; the interference claim stays a state-law claim governed by state substantive law. **Gold Key:** Erie reaches every state-law claim a federal court hears, not only diversity cases — federal-question entry does not federalize a tag-along state claim.

- **B — "No, but only if all parties are diverse."** It reaches the right top-line ("No, Erie applies") and then quietly attaches a diversity admission ticket, trading on the muscle memory that Erie *is* the diversity doctrine. The breaker: Erie governs because the claim is state-law, full stop — diversity is one way state-law claims reach federal court, not a precondition for applying state substantive law to a supplemental one. **Silver Key:** when two answers share the same conclusion and one adds a condition the stem never raises, cut the conditioned answer.

- **D — "Yes, because state defenses never apply in federal court."** It leans on a tidy "federal court means federal rules all the way down" instinct, sweeping state defenses off the table. The breaker is the word that overreaches: "never." Stephen's state-law competition privilege is part of the state substantive law the federal court must apply to the state claim, so state defenses plainly do apply here.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

**Tested rule (unchanged from original):** A federal court applies state substantive law to a state-law claim it adjudicates, regardless of whether subject-matter jurisdiction over the action rests on diversity or on a federal question with a supplemental state claim. Erie governs the *state-law* claim; only the source of the claim, not the jurisdictional basis for the case, controls the choice between state and federal rules of decision.

**Affirmative reasoning for A:** *Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), directs federal courts to apply state substantive law to state-created rights. *United Mine Workers v. Gibbs*, 383 U.S. 715 (1966) — itself a federal-question case carrying a pendent state contract-interference claim — confirms that once a federal court exercises jurisdiction over a state-law claim it decides that claim under state substantive law. Supplemental jurisdiction (28 U.S.C. § 1367) supplies the *power* to hear the related state claim ("same case or controversy"/"common nucleus of operative fact") but does not convert the claim into federal law. *Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Elec. Mfg. Co.*, 313 U.S. 487 (1941), adds that the federal court applies the forum state's choice-of-law rules. A state-law privilege (here, the competition privilege to tortious interference) is substantive and must be applied. The credited outcome — Erie is *not* irrelevant to the supplemental state claim — is therefore correct and unchanged.

**Authority floor:** *Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938); *United Mine Workers v. Gibbs*, 383 U.S. 715 (1966); 28 U.S.C. § 1367; *Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Elec. Mfg. Co.*, 313 U.S. 487 (1941). All are leading authority / a federal statute and stand for the propositions cited. **Doctrinal currency:** stable; the Erie/supplemental-jurisdiction framework is not on the hotlist and has not been displaced. **Verdict:** clean PASS on the authority line; no attorney-review flag required.
