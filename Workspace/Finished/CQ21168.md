### 7. Full right-answer explanation
A is correct. Larceny requires the specific intent to take the personal property of *another* — the so-called *animus furandi*, the intent to steal. When a defendant sincerely, even if unreasonably, believes she has a legal right to the *specific* property she takes, that belief is incompatible with the intent to steal another's property. The common-law claim-of-right doctrine therefore negates larcenous intent even where the belief is mistaken and the taking is civilly wrongful. The honest, subjective good-faith belief in a right to the item is what matters; the belief need not be reasonable, and the defendant need not be correct.

**Gold Key:** The claim-of-right defense is *subjective* and turns on the defendant's sincere belief in a right to the *specific* property taken — the belief does not have to be reasonable, and the defendant does not have to be right.

The taking is still a civil trespass or conversion — Lydia may have to return the clock or pay damages — but the criminal larceny charge requires a felonious taking, and the sincere belief in a right to the specific clock defeats that element.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
**B is attractive** because it sounds like plain common sense: she took it without asking, so she stole it. The breaker is that larceny is a *specific-intent* crime; the trespassory taking alone is not enough. Gold Key: the claim-of-right defense is subjective and turns on sincere belief in a right to the *specific* property taken — belief need not be reasonable, and the defendant need not be right. Once you see Lydia's sincere (if mistaken) belief, the criminal larcenous-intent element collapses, even though the taking remains civilly wrongful.

**C is attractive** because grandfather clocks are heavy, often tall, and students sometimes confuse "big and immovable-looking" with "real property." The breaker is that the larceny issue is *intent*, not the property classification. Even if Lydia had taken a piece of real property, claim of right would still negate the intent element for any larceny-style charge, and in any event a grandfather clock — even a seven-foot tall one — is universally treated as personal property because it is not attached to the realty. **Gold Key:** claim of right is subjective and turns on sincere belief in a right to the *specific* property taken; the property-classification debate is a red herring on this call.

**D is attractive** because it sounds rigorous: "you can't just *believe* a thing is yours and walk off with it; your belief has to be legally correct." The breaker is that the common-law rule is *not* an objective-correctness rule. Gold Key: claim of right is subjective; the belief does not have to be reasonable, and the defendant does not have to be right. The mistaken-but-sincere belief is enough to defeat larcenous intent, even though it does not defeat a civil suit.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested rule is unchanged: a defendant who honestly believes she has a right to the *specific* property taken generally lacks the larcenous intent required for larceny. This is the common-law *claim-of-right* doctrine, and it is reflected in the Model Penal Code's general approach to theft and codified in many state statutes. See, e.g., N.Y. Penal Law § 155.15 ("In any prosecution for larceny committed by trespassory taking or embezzlement, it is an affirmative defense that the property was appropriated under a claim of right made in good faith."); Model Penal Code § 223.1(3) (consolidating theft offenses and preserving a claim-of-right principle that an honest belief in a right to the property defeats the culpability required for *theft*). The leading common-law statement is that claim of right is subjective — belief must be sincere but need not be reasonable. A grandfather clock, even a tall antique one, is personal property because it is not annexed to the realty, so a larceny analysis of Lydia's taking is doctrinally proper. The source row's official key (the claim-of-right answer) is preserved, the legal issue is unchanged, and the credited outcome is the same: no larceny.
