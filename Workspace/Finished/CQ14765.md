## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. Ruth's pending arson charge and the prosecutor's promised dismissal give Ruth a concrete reason to testify in a way that helps the prosecution. Gold Key: a promise of leniency to a prosecution witness is classic bias impeachment. Silver Key: ask why the promise is being used. Daniel is not using the promise to prove that the prosecutor will actually dismiss Ruth's charge; he is using it to show Ruth's motive to shade her testimony.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells the hearsay-purpose trap. The promise is an out-of-court statement, but Daniel is not offering it for the truth of the dismissal promise. The breaker is the purpose of offer: the statement shows Ruth's bias and interest in pleasing the prosecution.

C sells the dominant policy trap. Negotiated criminal resolutions may be encouraged, but that policy does not let the prosecution hide a cooperating witness's motive to testify favorably. The breaker is that the evidence targets Ruth's credibility, not the general validity of plea bargaining.

D sells the party-opponent shortcut. The prosecutor's promise helps explain admissibility, but not because it is a Rule 801(d)(2) admission by an opposing party. The breaker is that the government is not treated as a party-opponent for this defendant's use of the prosecutor's statement in the way a civil party's agent would be; the clean route is bias impeachment.

## 9. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: evidence that a prosecution witness expects leniency or dismissal in exchange for testimony is admissible to show bias or motive to testify favorably. The prosecutor's promise is not offered for its truth. It is offered to show the witness's state of mind and interest in the outcome, so the hearsay objection does not block the impeachment use.

United States v. Abel, 469 U.S. 45 (1984), recognizes impeachment by showing bias under the Federal Rules of Evidence. Davis v. Alaska, 415 U.S. 308 (1974), protects defense cross-examination that exposes a prosecution witness's possible bias from vulnerable criminal status. Delaware v. Van Arsdall, 475 U.S. 673 (1986), likewise involved restricted cross-examination aimed at showing witness bias from dismissal of a pending charge. FRE 607 permits any party to attack a witness's credibility. FRE 801(c) limits hearsay to statements offered for the truth of the matter asserted, and FRE 801(d)(2) is not the needed theory here. FRE 410 protects plea-related material against specified uses against the plea participant, but it does not erase a defendant's ability to expose a prosecution witness's motive to testify favorably. Sources checked: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/415/308/, https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/475/673/, https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-supreme-court/469/45.html, https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_801, https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_410, and https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2025-02/federal-rules-of-evidence-dec-1-2024_0.pdf.

Because Ruth's hoped-for dismissal depends on helping the prosecution, Daniel may use the prosecutor's promise to impeach Ruth for bias. The admissibility theory is bias impeachment, not party-opponent admission and not hearsay exception.
