### 7. Full right-answer explanation

D is correct. Martha wrongfully took the page-marker, but common-law larceny requires intent to permanently deprive at the moment of the taking. She intended to return it in a week or two, so the required larcenous intent was missing. Gold Key: actual later loss is not a substitute for intent at the taking. Silver Key: lock the mental-state snapshot before analyzing later-causation facts.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because Professor Daniel ultimately lost the marker for good. The breaker is the timing of intent: larceny asks whether Martha meant to permanently deprive him when she took it, not whether later events created permanent loss.

B reaches the acquittal result but sells the wrong path. The backpack theft does not operate as a superseding-cause defense; Martha is acquitted because she lacked permanent-deprivation intent at the taking.

C is attractive because foreseeability sounds like a clean causation answer. Gold Key: foreseeability cannot replace the specific intent element; even a foreseeable later loss does not turn a temporary-use taking into common-law larceny.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested issue is unchanged: common-law larceny requires intent to permanently deprive at the time of the taking. The credited outcome is unchanged: acquittal because Martha intended to return the property in a week or two.

For the lawyer-confirmation layer, DOJ Criminal Resource Manual section 1639 states that larceny requires wrongful taking and carrying away, personal property of another, lack of consent, and intent to deprive, citing United States v. Barlow, 480 F.2d 1245, 1251 (D.C. Cir. 1972), and then notes that larceny requires intent to permanently deprive, citing Ailsworth v. United States, 448 F.2d 439, 442 (9th Cir. 1971): https://www.justice.gov/archives/usam/criminal-resource-manual-1639-definition-steal-or-purloin. DOJ Criminal Resource Manual section 1655 likewise distinguishes larceny from temporary misappropriation and states that larceny requires intent to permanently deprive: https://www.justice.gov/archives/jm/criminal-resource-manual-1655-protection-government-property-element-intent. State v. Jackson, 101 Ariz. 399 (1966), states the same theft-intent distinction: grand theft requires specific intent to permanently deprive, while temporary use is a separate mental state: https://law.justia.com/cases/arizona/supreme-court/1966/1593-0.html.

Applied here, Martha did not intend to keep the page-marker or make Daniel lose it permanently. She intended to return it in a week or two. The later theft from her backpack caused actual permanent loss, but it did not create the missing larceny intent retroactively. The causation choices therefore do not defeat the credited acquittal.
