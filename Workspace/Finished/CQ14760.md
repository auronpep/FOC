## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

Barnabas's statement was made out of court and is offered to prove the truth of
what it asserts — that Silas reversed the forklift without warning and caused the
injury. That is the definition of hearsay, and it comes in only through an
exception. Walk the two exceptions the array dangles. **Medical diagnosis or
treatment (A)** covers what helps the doctor treat the patient — symptoms,
history, the general cause of the harm — but **Gold Key:** statements assigning
fault or naming the wrongdoer are not reasonably pertinent to treatment, so
Barnabas's accusation against Silas falls outside it. **Excited utterance (B)**
needs a statement made while still under the stress of the startling event;
**Silver Key:** the clock kills it — three hours is long enough that the
excitement has passed, especially for Barnabas, who was a bystander and not the
person injured. No other exception fits. The statement is hearsay not within any
exception, so **C** is correct.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

- **A. Yes, as a statement made for purposes of medical diagnosis or treatment.**
  This sells the instinct that anything a worried family member tells the ER
  doctor rides in on the treatment exception — and the exception genuinely does
  reach a bystander's account of symptoms and general cause. The breaker is scope:
  **Gold Key:** the exception covers only what is reasonably pertinent to
  diagnosis or treatment, and a statement pinning blame on Silas does nothing to
  treat Stephen. Fault is for the jury, not the doctor.
- **B. Yes, as an excited utterance.** This trades on the image of an upset brother
  blurting out what he saw — the emotional register feels like an excited
  utterance. The breaker is timing: the statement came three hours later, after
  the stress of the event had subsided, so the spontaneity the exception requires
  is gone. **Silver Key:** when a "Yes, excited utterance" choice appears, find the
  delay before you credit it.
- **D. No, because it relates an opinion.** This reaches the right outcome by the
  wrong road, which is its pull — the answer feels safe because it also says "No."
  The breaker is that there is no rule excluding hearsay merely because it contains
  an opinion; opinion-laden out-of-court statements come in whenever an exception
  applies. The statement fails for want of an exception, not because it is an
  opinion.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

Hearsay is an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter
asserted and is inadmissible unless an exception applies (FRE 801(c), 802).
Barnabas's statement is offered to prove how the accident happened and that Silas
was at fault — classic hearsay.

- **FRE 803(4) (medical diagnosis or treatment):** admits statements describing
  medical history, symptoms, or the *general cause* of a condition when made for
  and reasonably pertinent to diagnosis or treatment, and bystander/family
  statements can qualify. But the Advisory Committee Note and settled application
  hold that statements of **fault or the identity of the wrongdoer** are not
  reasonably pertinent to treatment and are therefore excluded. Barnabas's
  accusation against Silas is exactly such a fault statement.
- **FRE 803(2) (excited utterance):** requires that the declarant still be under
  the stress of excitement caused by the startling event. A statement made roughly
  three hours later, by a bystander, does not satisfy the contemporaneous-stress
  requirement.

No exception reaches the statement, so it is inadmissible hearsay. The tested rule
(hearsay; scope of 803(4) and 803(2)), the legal issue, and the credited outcome
(inadmissible — no exception) are **unchanged** from the original. Verdict: **PASS**
on authority (rule numbers + Advisory Committee Note to FRE 803(4)); no
case-citation risk taken.
