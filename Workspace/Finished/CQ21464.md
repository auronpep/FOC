## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question
At a private Christian calligraphy conference, two separate grab-and-run incidents happen outside the hotel.

**Incident 1:** Lydia is leaving with a leather devotional satchel hanging from her shoulder. Stephen rushes up beside her, grabs the strap, and pulls. Lydia clamps down on the strap and pulls back; Stephen jerks so hard that the satchel tears free, Lydia is twisted sideways, and she falls to the pavement. Stephen runs off with the satchel.

**Incident 2:** Naomi is leaving with a slim Bible-cover pouch dangling loosely from two fingers. Barnabas jogs past, lifts the pouch from Naomi's relaxed hand in one clean motion, and is gone before Naomi reacts. Naomi feels no tug, is not hurt, and never has a chance to pull back.

Under common law, which statement is most accurate?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
When one thief yanks property away after the victim resists, and another thief makes a clean surprise snatch before any resistance, which taking is common-law robbery?

C. Neither is guilty of robbery because grab-and-run takings from a person's hand or strap are always larceny from the person rather than robbery.

D. Both Stephen and Barnabas are guilty of robbery because each took property directly from another person by physical force.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
A is correct. Stephen used more than the bare effort needed to take property: Lydia resisted, and Stephen used extra force to overcome that resistance and pull the satchel away. Barnabas did not overcome resistance, injure Naomi, threaten her, or use force beyond the clean surprise taking itself. Gold Key: common-law robbery needs larceny plus force or intimidation, and a purse-style snatch crosses the robbery line when the thief uses force that overcomes resistance, causes injury, or otherwise goes beyond the bare taking. Silver Key: compare the two incidents on one axis only: resistance overcome. Stephen is on the robbery side of that line; Barnabas is on the larceny-from-the-person side under the traditional rule.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
B is attractive because it grabs a real larceny element: Barnabas intended to permanently deprive Naomi of the pouch. The breaker is that intent to keep the property does not supply the extra robbery element. Gold Key: the upgrade from larceny to robbery comes from force or intimidation beyond the bare taking, not from the intent element shared by both crimes.

C is attractive because it remembers that quick snatches can be larceny from the person and then turns that memory into a clean all-or-nothing rule. The breaker is the word "always." When the victim resists and the thief uses force to break that resistance, the taking can be robbery.

D is the dominant trap because it sounds like the simplest summary: both thieves physically took property from a person. The breaker is that the physical contact needed to remove the item is not enough by itself under the traditional common-law line. Silver Key: do not count "from the person" or "physical grab" twice; the robbery upgrade asks whether the thief overcame resistance or created fear.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains the common-law line between robbery and larceny from the person in a snatching scenario, and the credited outcome remains unchanged: the resisted, forceful taking is robbery; the clean, unresisted surprise snatch may be only larceny from the person. The verified outline code is `73030400`, Crimes against Property > Robbery. The source row listed `73030100` for larceny, but the deepest listed node covering the rule actually tested is robbery.

At common law, robbery is larceny from the person or presence of another accomplished by force or intimidation. The Supreme Court's discussion in *Stokeling v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 544 (2019), describes the common-law rule as requiring force sufficient to overcome resistance and notes the distinction between a taking that overcomes resistance and a taking that does not. Source: https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/18pdf/17-5554_4gdj.pdf. The purse-snatching line is also illustrated by *State v. Sein*, 124 N.J. 209, 590 A.2d 665 (1991), which explains that the predominant rule treats a simple sudden snatching without resistance, injury, or struggle as insufficient for robbery, while force used to wrest property away can satisfy the force element. Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/new-jersey/supreme-court/1991/124-n-j-209-1.html. Those authorities confirm the official key and do not create a key disagreement.
