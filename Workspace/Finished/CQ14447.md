**B is correct.** The answer names the real promissory-estoppel gate: reliance on a future gift promise matters only if enforcement is needed to avoid injustice.

**Gold Key:** A future gift promise can be enforceable by promissory estoppel, but only through the actual reliance gates: expected reliance, actual reliance, and injustice. No “equivalent loss” gate and no “benefit to promisor” gate is part of the rule.

**Silver Key:** Read the array as a gate list. Three choices either delete the reliance exception or invent extra gates. B is the only choice that names the real gate.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because it borrows a familiar contracts idea: legal enforcement often looks for something flowing back to the promisor. The breaker is the Gold Key: promissory estoppel does not require the promisee’s reliance to confer an economic benefit on the promisor.

**C.** This is the dominant trap. It is attractive because it states the ordinary baseline: a future gift promise usually lacks bargain consideration. The breaker is that the worded rule is too absolute once reliance appears; some relied-on gift promises can be enforceable under promissory estoppel.

**D.** This choice is attractive because it turns reliance into a damages-style matching exercise. The breaker is that promissory estoppel asks whether enforcement is needed to avoid injustice, not whether the promised gift is substantially equivalent to the promisee’s reliance loss.
