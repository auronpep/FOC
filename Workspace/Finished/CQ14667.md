### 7. Full right-answer explanation
A is correct. Daniel's act of pointing a loaded firearm at Stephen and pulling the trigger posed a great threat of serious bodily injury, and, because it resulted in Stephen's death, Daniel should be convicted of murder in the second degree. Murder in the second degree is murder with malice aforethought, done without premeditation and deliberation. Daniel's actions were done with reckless indifference to an unjustifiably high risk to human life and will support a finding of malice aforethought. Gold Key: in a Russian-roulette-type fact pattern, the act of pointing a loaded firearm at another and pulling the trigger is the textbook "depraved heart" — implied malice supports second-degree murder even when the defendant did not intend to kill. Silver Key: run the gates in order — (1) intent to kill (absent here) kills the premeditated first-degree branch; (2) the predicate-felony independence gate (assault that kills the victim merges, so it does not supply an independent felony for felony murder) kills the other first-degree branch; (3) only second-degree murder remains, and consent or assumption of risk is no defense to criminal homicide. That is why A is the proper residual answer.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
B is attractive because it echoes a familiar civil-law idea — that a willing participant cannot recover for an injury they assumed. That intuition collapses the moment the question turns criminal. Criminal homicide is prosecuted by the sovereign, not by the victim; voluntary consent (and the related assumption-of-risk idea) is not a defense to causing another person's death. The breach is the public-policy barrier to letting one person consent to their own killing, and Silver Key: the consent frame answers a civil battery question, not a homicide question. The most serious proper charge still runs through murder analysis.

C is attractive because it bundles two classical theories of first-degree murder (premeditation and felony murder) in one choice, which can make a student feel the choice is "covered." The breach has two parts. First, "intentional and premeditated" requires a true intent to kill, and Daniel had no such intent — the whole point of the dare is that the player does not know which chamber will fire. Second, the felony-murder branch fails on the merger doctrine: the predicate "assault with a deadly weapon" is the very act that caused the death and merges into the homicide, so it is not an independent felony and cannot support felony murder. With both branches of first-degree murder defeated, the choice cannot be the most serious proper charge.

D is attractive because it feels like the right amount of criminal liability for a "dare" — high enough to punish, low enough to recognize that the defendant was only "playing." The breach is the threshold of recklessness: manslaughter requires a killing caused by gross negligence, but Daniel's act went well past that. Pointing a loaded firearm at another person's head and pulling the trigger shows a conscious, callous disregard for human life — the "depraved heart" that supplies the implied malice required for murder. Gold Key: a finding of mere gross negligence cannot capture conduct that creates a grave risk of death with conscious indifference; that conduct is depraved-heart murder, not manslaughter.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law, Homicide, Murder, and the verified outline code is `74020101`. Murder at common law is the unlawful killing of another with malice aforethought; malice can be express (intent to kill) or implied. Implied malice is supplied by conduct showing "a depraved heart" — a wicked, evil, or inhuman state of mind, a callous disregard for human life, and conduct creating a grave and unjustifiable risk of death. The leading authority is *Commonwealth v. Malone*, 354 Pa. 180, 47 A.2d 445 (1946), in which the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania affirmed a second-degree murder conviction where a teenager, without intent to kill, killed a companion by playing "Russian poker": "[w]hen an individual commits an act of gross recklessness for which he must reasonably anticipate that death to another is likely to result, he exhibits that 'wickedness of disposition; hardness of heart; cruelty; recklessness of consequences and a mind regardless of social duty' which proved that there was at that time in him 'that state or frame of mind termed malice'." The "playing Russian roulette" example is in turn treated by treatises and Model Penal Code commentary as a paradigm of depraved-heart murder. The felony-murder branch is defeated by the merger doctrine: an assault with a deadly weapon that results in death is not an independent felony, so it cannot support felony murder (see *People v. Sarun Chun*, 203 P.3d 425 (Cal. 2009); LII Wex, "felony murder rule"). And consent or assumption of risk is not a defense to criminal homicide because the State, not the victim, prosecutes homicide. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the original; the surface story now lives in a Christian men's retreat instead of a secular party.
