# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question

At a privately run Christian youth-theater studio, a lighting platform collapsed during rehearsal for a Bible-pageant scene. Lydia pulled Ruth, Naomi's adult daughter, clear of the platform and kept her breathing until paramedics arrived. Ruth survived.

After hearing what Lydia had done, Naomi signed a note that stated: "In consideration of Lydia's saving Ruth's life, I promise to pay Lydia $700." Naomi died six weeks after signing the note. Lydia submitted a $700 claim to the administrator of Naomi's estate, but the administrator denied it.

Is the fact that Lydia saved Ruth's life sufficient consideration for Naomi's promise to pay Lydia $700?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

A person promises to pay for an emergency rescue of her adult daughter after the rescue has already happened. Is that past rescue consideration for the later promise?

## 3. Final answer choices

B. No, because Naomi did not ask Lydia to save Ruth.

C. Yes, because the rescue is recited as consideration in the note Naomi signed.

D. No, because the value of Lydia's life-saving service to Naomi was speculative.

## 5. Correct answer

## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**B is correct.** Consideration requires a bargained-for exchange. Lydia's rescue happened before Naomi made any promise, and Naomi did not request the rescue as the price of a promise. **Gold Key:** a past service is not consideration for a later promise unless the promisor requested that service as part of the exchange before it happened. **Silver Key:** when the stem gives a noble past act and a later promise, ask who asked for the act before performance. If the promise comes only after the act, gratitude is not the bargain.

Naomi's later note may show gratitude, moral obligation, or an attempt to memorialize a debt. It does not turn the earlier rescue into consideration for Naomi's promise.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This answer is attractive because Lydia's rescue saved Naomi's daughter, so the benefit feels obvious and weighty. The breaker is that a benefit, even a deeply important one, is not consideration unless it was sought by the promisor in exchange for the promise. **Gold Key:** material benefit can be a separate enforcement theory in narrow settings, but it does not make a past, unrequested service bargained-for consideration. **Silver Key:** do not let the emotional force of the benefit answer the exchange question.

**C.** This answer sells the formal signed-note trap. The note uses the word "consideration," but a recital cannot manufacture the exchange element. The legal question is whether Lydia saved Ruth because Naomi asked for that performance in return for Naomi's promise. She did not.

**D.** This answer reaches the right "No" result through the wrong reason. A service can be consideration even if its dollar value is hard to measure; the law does not require precise equivalence or easy valuation. The defect here is not speculative value. The defect is timing and request: Lydia's service came first, and Naomi's promise came later.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

The tested rule and credited outcome are preserved: a past, unrequested service is not consideration for a later promise, even when the service was beneficial and even when the later note calls it consideration.

The source row supplied outline code **55020400** for implied-in-fact contracts. The Contracts outline places the tested rule under **55020201 - Formation > Consideration > Bargain and exchange**, which is the verified code used throughout this file. The source code is recorded only as provenance in the Question YAML.

Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 71 supplies the governing consideration rule: a performance or return promise must be bargained for, meaning it is sought by the promisor in exchange for the promise and given by the promisee in exchange for it. A later expression of gratitude does not satisfy that exchange requirement. The classic moral-obligation case, *Mills v. Wyman*, 20 Mass. (3 Pick.) 207 (1825), fits the same exam lane: care was furnished first, the promisor's promise came later, and the later promise was not supported by consideration. Restatement section 86 recognizes a narrow material-benefit enforcement path in the absence of consideration, but that doctrine does not change the answer to this call, which asks whether the past rescue itself is sufficient consideration.

Naomi did not seek Lydia's rescue as the price of Naomi's promise. Lydia acted before Naomi promised to pay. The rescue therefore cannot be the bargained-for consideration for Naomi's later promise. Research does not contradict the supplied official key.
