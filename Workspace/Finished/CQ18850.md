### 1. Final question

Daniel was convicted in state court of burglary after breaking into Lydia's private Bible bookstore after closing. At trial, the judge gave a reasonable-doubt instruction that Daniel later argued could be read two ways. Daniel raised that instruction issue on direct appeal, and the state supreme court affirmed, holding that the instruction was proper. Daniel did not file a petition for certiorari in the United States Supreme Court.

Daniel is now serving his sentence and files a federal habeas petition under 28 U.S.C. 2254, claiming that the instruction violated due process. Was the district court correct to deny habeas relief?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

A is correct. Daniel's jury-instruction claim was adjudicated on the merits by the state supreme court. Under AEDPA, a federal habeas court may not grant relief just because it would have analyzed the constitutional issue differently. The federal court may grant relief only if the state-court decision was contrary to clearly established Supreme Court law or involved an objectively unreasonable application of that law. The district judge found error, but not objective unreasonableness. That finding leaves AEDPA deference in place, so denial was correct.

Gold Key: On federal habeas after a state merits adjudication, "wrong" is not enough. The state court must be contrary to, or objectively unreasonable under, clearly established Supreme Court law.

Silver Key: When the stem separates "incorrect" from "unreasonable," lock the axis before reading the choices. AEDPA asks unreasonable, not merely wrong.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested issue is federal habeas review of a state conviction after a state-court merits adjudication. The corrected and verified outline code is `75110400`, Post-Trial Rights > Writ of Habeas Corpus. Under 28 U.S.C. 2254(d), a federal court may not grant habeas relief on a claim adjudicated on the merits in state court unless the state decision was contrary to, or involved an unreasonable application of, clearly established federal law as determined by the Supreme Court, or rested on an unreasonable determination of facts. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/2254. In Williams v. Taylor, 529 U.S. 362 (2000), the Supreme Court explained the "contrary to" and "unreasonable application" clauses. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/98-8384.ZS.html. In Woodford v. Visciotti, 537 U.S. 19 (2002), the Court reiterated that an unreasonable application is different from an incorrect application. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-137.ZPC.html.

The exhaustion distractor is also wrong. Section 2254 requires exhaustion of available state remedies, and O'Sullivan v. Boerckel, 526 U.S. 838 (1999), frames exhaustion around presentation through the state's ordinary appellate review process. See https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-2048.ZS.html. A petition for certiorari to the United States Supreme Court is not an available state remedy and is not required before Section 2254 review.
