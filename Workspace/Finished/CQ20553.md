## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Mary rented a street-level unit from Daniel for $4,800 a month to operate a Scripture-art print shop. The lease also gave Mary the right to exclusive occupancy of a locked rear garage bay for packing and shipment storage. ### 7. Full right-answer explanation

B is correct. Daniel physically kept Mary out of an exclusive part of the leased premises by leaving his own materials in the garage bay for months. That is an actual partial eviction by the landlord. Gold Key: when the landlord actually excludes the tenant from part of the leased premises, the tenant may stay in the rest and withhold all rent while the exclusion continues; the rent is not apportioned in the landlord's favor. Silver Key: because the call asks what the landlord recovers, treat each dollar answer as a remedy formula, then ask whether the formula is full rent, cleanup cost, proportional rent, or no rent.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A looks attractive because Mary kept using the storefront. The trap treats continued possession of the unblocked space as if it keeps the whole rent obligation alive. The breaker is the Gold Key: actual partial eviction by the landlord lets the tenant remain in the rest and still suspend rent while the exclusion continues.

C looks attractive because the blocked garage bay sounds like a cleanup problem. That moves to the wrong remedy. Daniel is suing for rent, and the rent consequence of landlord-created actual partial eviction is not a deduction measured by what it would cost Mary to clear Daniel's property.

D is the dominant trap because it feels fair to subtract only the value of the garage bay. The trap is a half-right apportionment instinct. The breaker is the actual partial eviction rule: when the landlord wrongfully excludes the tenant from part of the leased premises, the landlord cannot apportion his own wrong into a partial rent recovery.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule is actual partial eviction by the landlord. When the landlord physically excludes the tenant from part of the leased premises, the tenant's obligation to pay rent is suspended while the exclusion continues, even if the tenant remains in possession of the rest. Under the traditional common-law rule, the landlord does not recover a prorated portion of rent for the unblocked portion.

Authority: Kuschinsky v. Flanigan, 170 Mich. 245, 247-48 (1912), and Ravet v. Garelick, 221 Mich. 70, 72 (1922), are cited by the Michigan Courts landlord-tenant benchbook for the rules that partial eviction caused by the landlord abates rent, that the tenant need not vacate the remaining premises, and that no apportionment is required. See Michigan Courts, Residential Landlord-Tenant Law Benchbook, Nonpayment Claims, https://www.courts.michigan.gov/4a4eb6/siteassets/publications/benchbooks/lltbb/lltbbresponsivehtml5.zip/LLTBB/Ch_5_Defenses/Nonpayment_Claims.htm. Giraud v. Milovich, 85 P.2d 182 (Cal. Ct. App. 1938), likewise states the traditional no-apportionment rule for partial eviction by the landlord; a commercial leasing CLE reproduces and discusses that rule at https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/events/2022/10/icsc-article.pdf.

The transformed facts keep the legal issue and credited outcome unchanged. Daniel, the landlord, physically blocks an exclusive part of the leased premises with his own materials. Mary remains in the unblocked portion and withholds the next month's rent. Under actual partial eviction, Daniel recovers $0.
