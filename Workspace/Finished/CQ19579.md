## 6. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

The objection should have been overruled. When a defendant raises the defense of entrapment, the prosecution is permitted to introduce evidence of the defendant's prior similar acts to rebut the claim by showing that the defendant was predisposed to commit the crime. 

**Gold Key:** When the defendant claims entrapment, prior similar acts are admissible under FRE 404(b) to prove the defendant was predisposed (a non-propensity purpose), not to show bad character or that he acted in conformity with it on this occasion.

The evidence here was not offered to prove that Timothy has a bad character and therefore sold heroin on the charged occasion. It was offered to show that he was already willing to sell heroin before the undercover officer approached him. Because the defendant raised entrapment, the prior sales to Peter, John, and Lydia are relevant to the predisposition element of that defense. The trial court should have overruled the objection.

## 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A (New A — original D):** This choice is attractive because it correctly states that past conduct can sometimes be relevant, and it feels like a direct response to the facts (he did it before, so he probably did it this time). It is defeated because it invites the exact propensity inference that FRE 404(b)(1) forbids: using the prior sales to prove that Timothy engaged in criminal behavior on this particular occasion. Even though the facts are similar, the prosecution cannot offer the prior acts for the purpose of showing "he is the kind of person who sells drugs."

**C (New C — original A):** This choice is attractive because students know the general rule that the prosecution cannot attack a criminal defendant's character with prior bad acts. It is defeated because it is an overbroad absolute ("character evidence is not admissible") that ignores the explicit non-propensity exception in FRE 404(b)(2). When entrapment is raised, predisposition is legitimately in issue, and prior similar acts may be admitted for that specific purpose.

**D (New D — original B — dominant trap):** This choice is attractive because it tracks the precise language of the character-evidence prohibition and correctly notes that the prior acts were unconvicted. It is the dominant trap because it stops at the general rule without asking whether a permitted non-propensity purpose exists in this case. The prosecution is not using the prior sales to prove Timothy's character; it is using them to show he was already predisposed when the officer gave him the opportunity. The "unconvicted bad acts" prohibition does not apply when the evidence is offered for a proper 404(b)(2) purpose such as rebutting entrapment.

## 8. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited answer is correct and the tested rule, legal issue, and outcome are identical to the source question.

Governing authority: FRE 404(b). FRE 404(b)(1) prohibits evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts to prove a person's character in order to show action in conformity therewith on a particular occasion. FRE 404(b)(2) expressly permits such evidence when offered for another purpose, such as proof of motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, or absence of mistake or accident (the list is illustrative, not exhaustive).

When a defendant raises an entrapment defense, the subjective test (federal rule and the test followed on the MBE) asks whether the defendant was predisposed to commit the crime before the government agent approached him. See Sorrells v. United States, 287 U.S. 435 (1932); Sherman v. United States, 356 U.S. 369 (1958); Jacobson v. United States, 503 U.S. 540 (1992). Once the defendant raises entrapment, the prosecution may introduce evidence of the defendant's prior similar criminal conduct to prove predisposition and thereby rebut the defense. The prior acts are not offered to prove "he has a bad character and therefore did it again"; they are offered to show the defendant's state of mind (predisposition) at the time the government provided the opportunity. This is a classic non-propensity purpose under 404(b)(2).
