## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

The jurisdiction applies only the M'Naghten test. Timothy cannot shoplift his way into a different insanity standard just because his preferred test (Durham) is broader. A defendant is bound by whatever insanity framework the legislature or courts of that jurisdiction have adopted. Because M'Naghten controls, the court asks one narrow question: Did the defendant know the nature of the act and know it was wrong? Timothy did both — he knew he was taking store property and knew it was illegal. The Durham "product of mental disease" language is unavailable in this jurisdiction, so the argument gets rejected on that basis alone (and also fails on the merits under M'Naghten).

**Gold Key:** The insanity defense test is jurisdiction-specific. A defendant cannot invoke Durham's "product" language unless the jurisdiction has adopted it. The adopted test controls — full stop.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A (originally B):** This is the trap for students who think "broader = automatically available." Durham IS broader than M'Naghten — that is just true as a factual matter. But broadness means nothing if the jurisdiction has not adopted it. The cognitive move this choice sells is: bigger safety net = better for the defendant. The breaker: the jurisdiction chose its test; the defendant does not get to swap in a friendlier one at trial. The correct answer (D) cites this **Gold Key**: the adopted test controls.

**Choice B (originally A, DOMINANT TRAP):** This choice mirrors Timothy's own argument almost word-for-word — that his crime was a "product" of mental disease, so Durham should apply. It is the most attractive wrong answer because it sounds compassionate and tracks the causal language the defendant used. The cognitive move: mental illness caused the crime → insanity test should apply. The breaker: the causal link might exist, but the test Timothy wants is not available in this jurisdiction. M'Naghten is the only game in town, and under M'Naghten, knowing the act is wrong defeats the defense regardless of causation. Choice D steers off this trap via the **Gold Key** jurisdictional-adoption rule.

**Choice C (originally D):** This choice lands on the right outcome (reject) but for the wrong reason — it says insanity is categorically unavailable for theft. That is a wrong element: insanity CAN apply to theft if the jurisdictional test is met. The cognitive move: theft is a property crime → no insanity defense. The breaker: the reason for rejection here is that Timothy used the wrong legal test, not that the subject matter bars the defense.

## 8. Black-Letter Law Verification

**Governing rule:** The insanity defense is a common-law and statutory framework that varies by jurisdiction. Each jurisdiction selects its own test — M'Naghten (right/wrong test), irresistible impulse, Durham (product test), or the MPC substantial-capacity test (Model Penal Code § 4.01). A defendant is bound by the test adopted by the jurisdiction; a defendant cannot invoke a test the jurisdiction has not adopted.

**Authority:** Model Penal Code § 4.01; Clark v. Arizona, 548 U.S. 735 (2006) (states have broad latitude in defining the insanity defense and may retain M'Naghten without constitutional violation); M'Naghten's Case, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843) (the defendant must not have known the nature and quality of the act, or that it was wrong); Durham v. United States, 214 F.2d 862 (D.C. Cir. 1954) (adopting the product test — not binding outside jurisdictions that adopted it).

**Application:** Timothy's jurisdiction adopted only M'Naghten. He knew he was taking property belonging to another and knew it was illegal. His Durham argument fails because Durham is not the adopted test. The credited answer is unchanged. Legal issue, tested rule, and outcome are all preserved.
