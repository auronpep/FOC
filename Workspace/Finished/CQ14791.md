### 1. Final question

At a federal civil trial for professional malpractice, Peter claimed that Martha, an electrical engineer, had negligently specified undersized motors for the rotating Bethlehem-star display at Peter’s private Nativity-themed mini-golf course.

Peter called Daniel, an expert in stage machinery. Daniel based his opinion solely on his own professional experience, but when asked, he also said that *Lydia’s Handbook of Sacred Stage Motors* was a reliable authority in the field and consistent with his views. On cross-examination, Martha’s lawyer asked Daniel whether he and Lydia were ever wrong. Daniel answered, “Only the Lord is perfect.” The lawyer asked no further questions.

Martha later called Hannah, her own expert, and asked, “Do you accept Lydia’s handbook as reliable?” Hannah answered, “It once was, but it is now badly out of date.” Peter requested that the jury be allowed to examine the handbook and judge for itself the handbook’s reliability.

Should the court allow the jury to examine the handbook?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A party asks to let the jury examine an entire learned treatise after experts discuss its reliability. May the court send the publication itself to the jury?

### 3. Final answer choices

A. No, because Peter’s expert did not rely on the handbook in forming his opinion but instead relied on his own professional experience.

B. Yes, because the jury decides the weight and credibility to give written and oral evidence.

C. Yes, because an expert has testified that the handbook is a reliable authority.

D. No, because the jury may consider only statements from the handbook that are read to it by counsel or by a witness.

### 5. Correct answer

### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**D is correct.** The request is not to read a specific learned-treatise statement to the jury. The request is to let the jury examine the handbook itself. That is the wrong object under the learned-treatise rule.

**Gold Key:** Under FRE 803(18), a reliable learned-treatise statement may be read into evidence, but the publication itself is not received as an exhibit.

**Silver Key:** In a learned-treatise array, lock the object: **statement read aloud** versus **publication handed to the jury**. The rule may allow the first while forbidding the second.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This is the dominant trap because it reaches the right “No” result with a wrong prerequisite. The breaker is that the rule does not require Peter’s expert to have relied on the handbook; a learned-treatise statement can also be called to an expert’s attention on cross-examination if the reliability foundation is otherwise met. **Gold Key:** the exhibit limit, not Peter’s expert’s reliance, decides the jury-examination request.

**B.** This choice sells a familiar jury-role principle. The breaker is the layer: the court decides whether the publication may be received at all, and the jury’s weighing role does not override an evidence rule that bars the publication itself from being an exhibit.

**C.** This choice treats reliability as the last step. The breaker is that reliability may open the door to reading qualifying statements, but it does not permit the whole handbook to go to the jury.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

FRE 803(18) covers statements in learned treatises, periodicals, or pamphlets when the statement is called to an expert’s attention on cross-examination or relied on by the expert on direct, and the publication is established as reliable authority. If admitted, the statement may be read into evidence but not received as an exhibit. ([Legal Information Institute][1])

The transformed question preserves the tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome: the jury may not examine the learned-treatise publication itself. The right answer remains the one that distinguishes **read statements** from **received publication**.
