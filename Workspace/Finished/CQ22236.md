## PASS-1 Transform Report

### 1. Final question
Naomi was pulling a folding orchard wagon manufactured by Lydia's company when the wagon crossed a shallow driveway lip and Naomi lost control. After the incident, the wagon's tow bar was found severely bent. Naomi claimed the tow bar bent during ordinary pulling and caused the loss of control, so she sued Lydia's company for personal injuries on the ground that the tow bar was defectively designed and not strong enough for normal use. Lydia's company denied any defect and claimed Naomi yanked the wagon into a stone planter and that the tow bar did not bend until that impact. During trial, the judge gave the jury a special instruction about the uncertainty of negative evidence. Lydia's company then called Timothy, its vice president in charge of customer safety. Timothy testified that he had held that job for seven years, that the company had sold more than 12,000 identical wagons during that time, and that he had never heard of a tow bar bending during ordinary pulling. If Naomi's attorney objects, should Timothy's testimony be admitted?

### 6. Full right-answer explanation
Choice B states the foundation fact that matters. `Gold Key:` no-prior-accident or no-prior-failure evidence may be admitted as circumstantial proof against defect or causation if the proponent shows substantially similar use and a witness or recordkeeping system that likely would have captured prior complaints. Timothy is the person to whom product-failure complaints would have been reported, so his role supplies the key foundation. `Silver Key:` when one answer identifies the complaint-capturing witness and the rival no-answer only says some incidents might have gone unreported, treat the rival as a weight objection, not an admissibility rule.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations
**A.** This is the dominant trap because it sounds sensible that some failures may never have been reported. The breaker is that the possibility of underreporting attacks weight, not the threshold admissibility decision, once the proponent has shown a witness who likely would have received complaints.

**C.** This sells a broad mistrust of negative evidence, as though testimony about the nonexistence of prior failures can never be used. `Gold Key:` the doctrine is not a per se ban; the real question is whether the proponent laid the required foundation for similar use and complaint awareness.

**D.** This sounds procedurally serious because the judge gave a cautionary instruction about the uncertainty of negative evidence. The breaker is that such an instruction may help the jury evaluate the testimony, but it is not the legal basis that makes the testimony admissible.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification with authority
The credited answer remains sound. Under FRE 401 and FRE 402, evidence is admissible if it has any tendency to make a consequential fact more or less probable and no other rule excludes it. In `Forrest v. Beloit Corp.`, 424 F.3d 344 (3d Cir. 2005), the Third Circuit explained that absence-of-prior-accident evidence can be relevant to absence of defect or causation, but that courts demand a proper foundation because under FRE 403 the evidence can mislead a jury if the proponent cannot show substantially similar products or circumstances and a basis for knowing about prior incidents. The court described the foundation requirement as a tool for the Rule 403 balancing inquiry and noted that such evidence may be admitted when that showing is made. Here, the row gives the key foundation fact: Timothy was the person to whom product-failure complaints would have been reported. Under the MBE's framing, the sale of more than 12,000 identical units during ordinary use also supplies enough similarity for the answer. The objection that some failures might not have been reported does not create a categorical rule of exclusion; that point goes to weight once the basic foundation is laid. Authority checked against FRE 401, FRE 402, FRE 403, and `Forrest v. Beloit Corp.`, 424 F.3d 344 (3d Cir. 2005). Sources: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_401, https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_402, https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_403, https://www2.ca3.uscourts.gov/opinarch/042184p.pdf.
