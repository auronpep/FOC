## 7. Full right-answer explanation

**C is correct.** Lydia intended to sell MDMA, believed the tablets were MDMA, divided them for resale, and sold one envelope to an undercover officer. **Gold Key:** factual impossibility is not a defense to attempt when the actor meant to commit the target crime and took a substantial step under the facts as the actor believed them. If Lydia's belief had been true, her sale would have been a drug sale. Barnabas is different. He knew the tablets were not MDMA, so he lacked the intent to sell MDMA. **Silver Key:** in a two-actor attempt array, grade each actor separately for purpose plus believed facts; the same fake tablets can convict one actor and clear the other.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This is the dominant trap. It sees the fake tablets and turns the whole case into "no real drugs, no attempt." The breaker is Lydia's belief and purpose. **Gold Key:** factual impossibility does not defeat attempt when the crime would have occurred under the facts as the actor believed them.

**B.** This choice rides Barnabas's supplier role. A supplier sounds more criminal than the person caught with one envelope, but the mental states run the other way. Barnabas knew the tablets were harmless candy, so he did not intend to sell MDMA. Lydia believed she was selling MDMA and took the sale step.

**D.** This choice gets Lydia right and then overextends the result to Barnabas. Supplying the pouch and taking money from Lydia may be suspicious, but attempt liability for selling MDMA requires the purpose to sell MDMA. Barnabas knew there was no MDMA, so the needed target-crime intent is missing.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The source item tests criminal attempt and impossibility. The credited outcome is preserved: the actor who believed the contraband facts and completed a sale step is guilty of attempt; the actor who knew the goods were fake lacks the intent to attempt sale of the drug.

The governing anchor is Model Penal Code section 5.01. Section 5.01(1)(a) covers a person who purposely engages in conduct that would constitute the crime if the attendant circumstances were as that person believed them to be, and section 5.01(1)(c) covers a substantial step under the actor's believed circumstances. The MPC explanatory note rejects impossibility as a defense by focusing on the actor's believed circumstances rather than actual facts. Source: [Model Penal Code section 5.01 text in Vermont legislative materials](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2018/WorkGroups/House%20Judiciary/Bills/S.267/S.267~Matthew%20Valerio~Model%20Penal%20Code%205.01%20Full%20Document~4-18-2018.pdf).

Federal pattern-instruction commentary likewise states that attempt under the MPC turns on an act that is a substantial step under the circumstances as the defendant believes them, and that factual impossibility is not a defense. Source: [District of Massachusetts Pattern Criminal Jury Instructions, Attempt](https://www.mad.uscourts.gov/resources/pattern2003/html/patt2uwk.htm). The same believed-circumstances principle appears in New York attempt law as discussed in *Dlugash*, where impossibility does not bar attempt if the crime could have been committed had the facts been as the defendant believed. Source: [*Dlugash v. People of State of NY*, 476 F. Supp. 921](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp/476/921/1378705/).

Applied here, Lydia has the attempt mens rea and actus reus: she intended to sell MDMA and performed the sale step. The tablets being vitamins is factual impossibility, not a defense. Barnabas lacks the attempt mens rea for sale of MDMA because he knew the tablets were not MDMA. The supplied official key is confirmed. No doctrinal-currency hotlist issue is triggered.
