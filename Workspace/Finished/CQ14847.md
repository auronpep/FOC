## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning for the Right Answer

The tested rule is Federal Rule of Evidence 606(b). During an inquiry into the validity of a verdict, Rule 606(b)(1) bars juror testimony about statements or incidents during deliberations, the effect of anything on a juror's vote, and any juror's mental processes concerning the verdict. Rule 606(b)(2) then allows juror testimony about whether extraneous prejudicial information was improperly brought to the jury's attention, whether an outside influence was improperly brought to bear on a juror, or whether a mistake was made in entering the verdict on the verdict form.

The official key is consistent with the rule. A, C, and D all depend on a juror explaining internal reasons for the vote: illness and hurry, misunderstanding instructions, or relying on stricken trial testimony. Those are barred under Rule 606(b)(1). B is different because the juror learned prejudicial information from a court clerk about other fraud accusations against Daniel. That information came from outside the admitted trial record and from outside the jury's mental process, so the juror may testify to the objective fact that it reached him. Given the content is highly prejudicial and case-specific, a new trial would be most likely on B.
