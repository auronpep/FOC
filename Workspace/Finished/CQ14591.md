## 7. Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

The troopers acted in objectively reasonable reliance on the computer report indicating a valid warrant. Under the good faith exception to the exclusionary rule, evidence obtained during a search incident to an arrest based on a warrant that police reasonably believed to be valid is admissible, even if the warrant was later found to have been quashed due to a clerical error. *See Herring v. United States*, 555 U.S. 135 (2009). The arrest was treated as lawful, and the search of Ruth's person incident to that lawful arrest was valid. The fentanyl is admissible. **Silver Key:** When the fact pattern involves a computer error and a warrant that turns out to be invalid, look for the good faith exception — it's the bridge between the clerical mistake and the admissibility of the evidence.

**Gold Key:** The good faith exception applies when police act in objectively reasonable reliance on a facially valid warrant that is later found to be defective due to isolated negligence (e.g., a clerk's failure to update a database). The exclusionary rule is meant to deter deliberate, reckless, or systemic police misconduct — not to punish isolated record-keeping errors.

## 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A — Yes, because there was no lawful arrest that could be made on the basis of the warrant.** The attraction: the warrant WAS actually quashed, so a student might think the arrest was unlawful and the evidence must be suppressed. The breaker: the good faith exception exists precisely for this situation. When police reasonably rely on a computer report showing a valid warrant, the arrest is treated as lawful for Fourth Amendment purposes, even if the warrant was actually invalid due to a clerical error. The Supreme Court held in *Herring v. United States* that isolated negligence in record-keeping does not trigger the exclusionary rule.

**C — Yes, because there was no probable cause or reasonable suspicion to believe that the driver possessed drugs.** The attraction: students often think that every search requires independent probable cause for the specific item found. The breaker: when a search is incident to a lawful arrest, no separate probable cause or reasonable suspicion for the contraband is needed. The search-incident-to-arrest exception authorizes a full search of the arrestee's person regardless of whether there was reason to believe drugs would be found. The doctrine is about officer safety and evidence preservation, not about suspicion of a particular crime.

**D — No, because police may lawfully search a driver incident to a valid traffic stop. (Dominant Trap)** The attraction: students know that traffic stops allow some police action and may conflate the scope of a traffic stop with the scope of a search incident to arrest. The breaker: a traffic stop, without more, does NOT authorize a full search of the driver's person. A traffic stop permits a brief detention and a pat-down for weapons only if there is reasonable suspicion the driver is armed. Here, the search was justified not by the traffic stop but by the custodial arrest on the warrant — a search incident to a lawful arrest authorizes a full search of the person. The legal basis matters: traffic-stop search authority and search-incident-to-arrest authority are different doctrines with different scopes.

## 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Authority:** *Herring v. United States*, 555 U.S. 135 (2009). The Supreme Court held that the good faith exception to the exclusionary rule applies when police arrest a person in reasonable reliance on a warrant that is later found to have been recalled due to a negligent clerical error. The Court reasoned that the exclusionary rule deters deliberate, reckless, or systemic police misconduct — not isolated negligence in record-keeping. Because the arresting officers acted in objectively reasonable reliance on the computer report, the arrest was lawful for Fourth Amendment purposes, and the search incident to that arrest was valid.

**Search incident to arrest:** Under *Chimel v. California*, 395 U.S. 752 (1969), a lawful custodial arrest authorizes a warrantless search of the arrestee's person and the area within immediate control. The justifications are officer safety and prevention of evidence destruction. Here, Ruth was lawfully arrested (via the good faith exception), and the search of her person was contemporaneous with the arrest.

**The good faith exception:** *United States v. Leon*, 468 U.S. 897 (1984) (good faith reliance on a defective search warrant); *Herring v. United States*, 555 U.S. 135 (2009) (good faith reliance on a defective arrest warrant due to computer error). The exception applies because the troopers' reliance on the computer report was objectively reasonable, and the error was caused by isolated negligence, not systemic misconduct.
