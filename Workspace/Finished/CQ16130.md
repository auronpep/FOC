## 9. Black-Letter Legal Verification

**Governing rule:** Vicarious criminal liability imposed on an employer for an employee's strict-liability regulatory offense during the course of employment is constitutionally permissible under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments when the penalty is limited to a fine. *See* **United States v. Park**, 421 U.S. 658 (1975) (Responsible Corporate Officer Doctrine — corporate officer held liable for regulatory violation without personal fault); **United States v. Dotterweich**, 320 U.S. 277 (1943) (corporation and its agent liable under FDCA for shipping adulterated products regardless of personal knowledge or intent); **United States v. Wiesenfeld Warehouse Co.**, 376 U.S. 86 (1964) (strict-liability public welfare offenses do not violate due process).

**Constitutional limit:** Courts have recognized that imposing *imprisonment* on an employer solely on the basis of an employee's strict-liability crime may violate due process. *See, e.g.*, **State v. Guminga**, 395 N.W.2d 344 (Minn. 1986) (Minnesota Supreme Court held that criminal liability without fault for a fine-only offense was permissible, but noted the constitutional line at imprisonment). The distinction between fine and imprisonment is the critical constitutional boundary.

**Application:** The statute here: (1) imposes liability on the employer for the employee's act of selling broadheads to a minor; (2) requires the sale occur during the course of employment (satisfied — Lydia was working the counter Barnabas assigned); (3) eliminates knowledge of age as a defense (strict liability); (4) punishes the employer only with a misdemeanor fine not exceeding $250. This falls squarely within the constitutionally permissible zone. The employer's guilt does not depend on presence, negligence, or knowledge.

**Authority verification:** The Responsible Corporate Officer Doctrine (*Park*, *Dotterweich*) and the broader public-welfare strict-liability framework confirm that Congress and state legislatures may impose vicarious criminal liability for regulatory offenses without violating due process, particularly when the penalty is a fine. The statute's elimination of the knowledge defense is consistent with the strict-liability nature of public-welfare offenses. *See also* Model Penal Code § 2.07(1)(a) (corporate liability for agent's conduct within scope of employment).
