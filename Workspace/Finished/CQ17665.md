## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question

Hannah owned a private riverside tract that she planned to divide into small retreat lots. She first deeded Parcel 1 to Peter. Peter's deed contained no land-use restriction, and at that time Hannah had recorded no declaration and had made no representation that the tract would follow a common residential plan.

Eighteen months later, Hannah recorded a declaration stating that every parcel in the tract could be used only for private residences. Hannah then deeded Parcel 2 to Ruth, and Ruth's deed referred to the recorded declaration. Peter later began building a small Bible-map print shop on Parcel 1.

Ruth sues Peter to enforce the residential-use restriction against Parcel 1. Which is the best result?

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A buyer took an unrestricted lot before any declaration, restriction, or common-plan representation existed; can a later buyer enforce a later-recorded residential restriction against that earlier lot?

### 6. Full right-answer explanation

B is the best answer. **Gold Key:** a private land-use burden must attach to the burdened parcel through an effective restriction, common plan, representation, or other recognized servitude source before that burden can be enforced; a later unilateral declaration does not retroactively burden a parcel already conveyed free of the restriction. Peter took Parcel 1 when there was no deed restriction, no recorded declaration, and no representation of a common residential plan. Ruth bought later under a declaration that reached Parcel 2, but that later event did not place a new burden on Parcel 1.

**Silver Key:** lock the time of attachment before you ask about notice. Notice matters only if there is a restriction or implied plan to notice; it does not create the burden by itself.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice is attractive because common-plan servitudes can sometimes bind lots without identical deed language. The breaker is the timing and source problem: one later restricted parcel does not automatically reach backward and place a common scheme on a parcel already sold without any restriction or plan.

**C. Dominant trap.** This choice is attractive because record notice often decides whether a later purchaser is bound by a land-use servitude. The breaker is the Gold Key: later notice cannot create the servitude burden that was missing when Peter took title.

**D.** This choice is attractive because residential use sounds consistent with the later declaration and with the tract's retreat-lot character. The breaker is the private-law source problem: zoning or land-use preferences do not create a private restrictive covenant enforceable by Ruth against Peter.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested issue remains whether an equitable servitude or restrictive covenant burden attached to the earlier-conveyed lot. The transformed item preserves the source row's rule, legal issue, trap structure, and credited outcome: the later buyer cannot enforce the later residential restriction against the earlier buyer because the burden was not attached when the earlier buyer took title.

The governing rule is grounded in the creation limits for servitudes and implied reciprocal negative easements. In **Sanborn v. McLean**, 206 N.W. 496 (Mich. 1925), the court explained that reciprocal negative easements must start with a common owner and are not retroactive; they arise, if at all, from restrictions imposed while the burdened land is still in the common owner's hands. Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/michigan/supreme-court/1925/223-mich-227-206-n-w-496.html

The same timing logic appears in modern restrictive-covenant doctrine. The Texas Supreme Court, applying **Evans v. Pollock**, described implied reciprocal negative easements as a narrow exception that applies when a developer sells lots with substantially uniform restrictions under a development plan and some retained or later-sold lots without restrictions are taken with notice of existing restrictions. The opinion also cites Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes section 1.1 for the general definition of a servitude and distinguishes Restatement section 2.10 servitude-by-estoppel theories that require representation and reliance. Source: https://www.txcourts.gov/media/1458724/220733.pdf

Those authorities confirm the supplied official key. Hannah had not yet recorded a declaration, inserted restrictions in deeds, or made common-plan representations when Peter took Parcel 1. Later recording and Peter's later knowledge could not retroactively create the missing burden. `bank_validation_verdict: PASS`.
