## 1. Final question (revised stem + call)

Martha keeps the books for a regional grain cooperative. An officer arrested her for embezzling the proceeds of the cooperative's harvest sales and, while walking her across the loading yard to the cruiser, asked without warnings, "Do you know what brings us here?" Martha answered, "The money I took from the grain accounts." At the courthouse, a different investigator gave full Miranda warnings. Martha signed a waiver and gave a detailed confession. The trial court finds the first question was an inadvertent slip, not a deliberate two-step strategy.

**Must the later warned confession be suppressed because of the earlier unwarned statement?**

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

The first statement was voluntary, and the trial court found the failure to warn was an inadvertent slip — not a deliberate question-first tactic. A voluntary unwarned statement does not, by itself, poison a later confession that follows full warnings and a knowing, written waiver. **Gold Key:** an *inadvertent* failure to warn followed by a voluntary statement does not taint a later, properly warned and waived confession; the stricter two-step bar fires only on a *deliberate* strategy to undermine Miranda. **Silver Key:** the call asks only whether the *later* confession is suppressed — keep your eye on the second statement, not the first. With valid warnings, a signed waiver, and a detailed station confession, the later confession is admissible. **B** is correct.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

- **A (dominant trap).** It rewards a student who remembers that police "questioning first and warning later" can trigger a suppression bar, and treats that bar as automatic. The word "always... whenever" is the tell: the two-step bar is not categorical — it targets *deliberate* tactics, and the stem hands you the opposite finding ("an inadvertent slip, not a deliberate two-step strategy"). **Gold Key:** the two-step bar applies only to a deliberate question-first strategy; an inadvertent lapse leaves the warned confession admissible.
- **C.** It feels safe because it reaches the correct bottom line ("No") and sounds like a real Miranda point about case-in-chief admissibility. **Silver Key:** the call asks only whether the *later confession* must be suppressed — C argues about the *first statement's* admissibility, a different question; worse, its premise is false (a voluntary unwarned custodial statement is excluded from the case-in-chief, usable only to impeach), so it cannot be the breaker.
- **D.** It sells the familiar "fruit of the poisonous tree" intuition — one tainted statement spreads to everything after it. The absolute "automatically taints every statement that follows" ignores that Miranda's fruits doctrine does not extend to a later, voluntary, fully warned and waived confession; the stem's signed waiver defeats the blanket claim.

## 9. Full black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

**Tested rule (unchanged from original):** Whether a later, Mirandized and waived confession must be suppressed because an earlier custodial statement was taken without warnings.

**Governing authority (lawyer-confirmation layer):**
- *Oregon v. Elstad*, 470 U.S. 298 (1985): A simple failure to administer Miranda warnings, where the unwarned statement was voluntary, does not require suppression of a subsequent statement given after warnings; Miranda's "fruit of the poisonous tree" reach is limited, and a later voluntary, warned statement is admissible.
- *Missouri v. Seibert*, 542 U.S. 600 (2004): When officers use a *deliberate* "question-first, warn-later" two-step strategy calculated to undermine Miranda, the warned confession is inadmissible absent curative measures. Justice Kennedy's controlling concurrence keys the bar to the *deliberateness* of the two-step.
- *Harris v. New York*, 401 U.S. 222 (1971) (lawyer-confirmation only): a voluntary unwarned statement is inadmissible in the case-in-chief but may be used to impeach — confirming choice C's premise is false.

**Application:** The trial court's factual finding of an *inadvertent* lapse (not a deliberate two-step) places the case squarely under *Elstad*, not *Seibert*. The first statement was voluntary; full warnings, a signed waiver, and a detailed confession followed. The later confession is admissible. Credited outcome (No / admissible) is **UNCHANGED** from the original. **Doctrinal currency:** Elstad (1985) and Seibert (2004) remain controlling; this item is not on the currency hotlist. *Vega v. Tekoh* (2022) addressed § 1983 liability, not the suppression framework, and does not disturb Elstad/Seibert.
