### 7. Full right-answer explanation

**A is correct.** The surplus does not jump straight to Ruth just because the foreclosure sale produced extra money. Peter Bank holds a valid judgment lien against Ruth's real property, and Ruth still owes Peter Bank $2,400. The surplus is first used to satisfy that lien. Once Peter Bank is paid in full, the remaining $1,300 belongs to Ruth as the owner of the equity. **Gold Key:** foreclosure surplus follows valid remaining liens before the owner, but a lienholder is capped at the unpaid debt. **Silver Key:** read a surplus-distribution answer in two passes: remove anyone already paid, then cap every remaining claimant at the amount still owed.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**B.** This choice is attractive because "surplus" often sounds like money that belongs to the owner. The breaker is the unpaid judgment lien. **Gold Key:** the owner receives the remainder only after valid lien claims attached to the foreclosed property or its substitute proceeds are satisfied. Ruth cannot take the full $3,700 while Peter Bank still has a $2,400 lien claim.

**C.** This is the dominant trap. It correctly notices that Peter Bank is the unpaid lien creditor, then lets priority swallow the entire fund. **Gold Key:** foreclosure surplus follows valid remaining liens before the owner, but a lienholder is capped at the unpaid debt. Peter Bank is owed $2,400, so the remaining $1,300 goes to Ruth.

**D.** This choice treats Lydia Finance as if the foreclosing lender keeps sharing in the surplus after its own debt has been paid. The stem says Lydia Finance's debt and foreclosure costs were already satisfied before the surplus existed. **Silver Key:** remove already-paid parties before you start distributing the remaining fund. Lydia Finance gets nothing more from the surplus.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: foreclosure surplus is distributed to valid lienholders according to priority before the owner of the equity receives any remainder, and no creditor takes more than the debt still owed.

Legal authority supports the credited result. Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages sec. 7.4 supplies the direct foreclosure-surplus rule. The Kansas Supreme Court in *Fidelity Bank v. King* looked to that section and treated excess foreclosure proceeds as payable through lien priority before any debtor remainder. Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/kansas/supreme-court/2006/92410.html. *Bank of America, N.A. v. BA Mortgage, LLC* applied the same Restatement rule and held that a lien claimant with priority to the surplus takes before the holder of the equity. Source: https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/591475bcadd7b049343b57f5. Arizona's foreclosure-priority statute, A.R.S. sec. 33-727(B), as applied in *Vista v. Millan / Patterson*, also directs excess funds to other liens in their order and confirms that a satisfied lien does not keep taking from the surplus. Source: https://www.azcourts.gov/Portals/0/OpinionFiles/Div1/2019/CV%2018-0609.pdf.

On these facts, Lydia Finance has already been paid from its foreclosure. Peter Bank's docketed judgment lien remains unpaid in the amount of $2,400. The $3,700 surplus therefore pays Peter Bank $2,400 first, and the remaining $1,300 goes to Ruth.
