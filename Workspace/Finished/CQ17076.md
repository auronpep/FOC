### 1. Final question
Esther walked into a county sheriff's office carrying a receipt from a private Christian bookstore and said she wanted to confess to stealing the store's locked box of Scripture memory prizes. A deputy gave Miranda warnings, Esther said she understood, and she calmly described taking the box after the store closed. Weeks later, a psychiatrist concluded that Esther was experiencing delusions that made her feel commanded to confess. The deputies had not threatened, tricked, promised leniency, or pressed her to speak. Is Esther's confession involuntary under due process?

If a suspect voluntarily comes to police, receives warnings, and confesses while mentally ill, but police do not coerce the suspect, is the confession involuntary under due process?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. Due process voluntariness asks whether government coercion overbore the suspect's will. Esther's mental condition matters as background, but the stem gives no police threats, tricks, promises, pressure, or exploitation. Gold Key: mental illness alone does not make a confession involuntary under the Due Process Clause; there must be coercive police conduct. Silver Key: sort the pressure source first. If the only pressure comes from inside the suspect's mind, the due-process involuntariness answer is no.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
D is attractive because it notices the vivid fact that Esther was experiencing delusions and treats irrational confession as the same thing as involuntary confession. The breaker is the Gold Key: due-process involuntariness requires coercive police conduct, so an internal compulsion by itself does not carry the legal test.

A is attractive because it sounds protective and categorical: if police later learn the suspect was mentally ill, they should not have listened at all. The breaker is that due process has no automatic refusal rule for every confession by a mentally ill person; the missing element is police coercion.

C is attractive because Miranda warnings are a familiar confession safeguard and the stem says the deputy gave them. The breaker is that warnings do not erase every voluntariness objection; if police coercion had overborne the suspect's will, Miranda warnings alone would not automatically cure that problem.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue is criminal procedure, specifically voluntary statements under `75080101`. The source row's `73030400` robbery label is a classification defect because the call tests a suspect's due-process challenge to a confession, not the elements of robbery.

The credited outcome is confirmed by *Colorado v. Connelly*, 479 U.S. 157 (1986), under the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause. The Court held that coercive police activity is a necessary predicate for finding a confession involuntary under due process; mental condition may matter to the totality inquiry, but mental illness by itself and apart from official coercion does not dispose of constitutional voluntariness. The official key also fits the current rule: Esther initiated contact, received warnings, calmly confessed, and the officers did not threaten, trick, promise, pressure, or exploit her.
