### 7. Right-Answer Explanation (exam-register)

**C is correct.** Fourth Amendment standing requires a reasonable expectation of privacy in the area searched or the items seized. Naomi has two independent bases. First, an overnight guest has a recognized reasonable expectation of privacy in the host's home — society acknowledges that overnight guests share in the privacy of the space where they sleep. Second, Naomi retained a direct privacy interest in her own duffel bag regardless of where it sat. Either basis alone suffices; together they are unambiguous. The warrantless entry and search were Fourth Amendment events Naomi can challenge.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIMINAL-4A-OVERNIGHT-01):** An overnight guest has a reasonable expectation of privacy in the host's home even without ownership or lease rights. The Fourth Amendment standing test is Katz reasonable-expectation-of-privacy, not property law. Society recognizes that people who sleep somewhere overnight have a legitimate privacy interest there.

**Silver Key (SK-CRIMINAL-4A-STANDING-01):** Whenever a "no standing" choice grounds its denial in lack of property ownership or tenancy, flag it immediately — the test is Katz, not property law. Any choice that conditions standing on ownership or a lease misstates the controlling standard.

### 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**D** (dominant trap): Refusing standing because Naomi did not own or rent the farmhouse is intuitive — after all, it is Peter's property, not hers. The trap borrows from property law concepts that have no role in the Fourth Amendment standing analysis. Fourth Amendment standing asks whether the defendant had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the searched area, not whether she held title or a lease. Overnight guests in a host's home satisfy the privacy-interest test as a recognized category regardless of their property status. **Gold Key (GK-CRIMINAL-4A-OVERNIGHT-01):** The Katz reasonable-expectation test displaced property-based Fourth Amendment analysis. Lack of ownership or tenancy does not defeat standing for an overnight guest.

**B**: The word "never" condemns this choice on its face. Courts have long recognized that some guests — specifically overnight guests — do have a reasonable expectation of privacy in the host's home. B is an overclaim that sweeps too broadly and eliminates a recognized protected category. If the word were "usually" or "ordinarily," it would be a closer call, but "never" is absolute and wrong.

**A**: The reasoning here is backwards. Ownership of the contraband found during a search is not the basis for Fourth Amendment standing. The relevant inquiry is whether the defendant had a privacy interest in the searched space or container — not whether she owned the items that happened to be inside. Narcotics ownership does not supply a privacy interest in the area; overnight guest status and personal-bag ownership do. By grounding standing solely in narcotics ownership, A identifies the wrong element entirely.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification

The Fourth Amendment standing doctrine requires a "reasonable expectation of privacy" in the area searched or the items seized. *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967). A defendant lacks standing to challenge a search if she had no such expectation. *Rakas v. Illinois*, 439 U.S. 128 (1978).

The overnight guest rule: In *Minnesota v. Olson*, 495 U.S. 91 (1990), the Supreme Court held that an overnight guest has a reasonable expectation of privacy in the host's home, giving the guest standing to challenge a warrantless entry. The Court reasoned that staying overnight is a longstanding social custom and that hosts and guests alike understand the privacy of the space shared. Naomi's overnight stay gives her that same recognized interest.

Additionally, Naomi has an independent, direct privacy interest in her own duffel bag. *United States v. Jacobsen*, 466 U.S. 109 (1984) (owner of searched item has obvious privacy interest in it). The combination is airtight.

The official key (standing exists) is confirmed. The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from Q17168.
