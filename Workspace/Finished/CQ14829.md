### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. Daniel is not merely objecting to a routine foundation point; he is contesting the admissibility of an alleged confession based on missing Miranda warnings. Gold Key: Federal Rule of Evidence 104(c) makes confession-admissibility hearings a mandatory outside-the-jury hearing category. Silver Key: classify the preliminary question before applying discretion. Once the preliminary question involves a confession, the court must conduct the hearing so the jury cannot hear it.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A sells the dominant discretion trap. Courts often have practical discretion about how to handle preliminary evidentiary matters, but the confession category is carved out. The breaker is that this is not a generic foundation hearing; it is a hearing on the admissibility of a confession.

B sells a true hearsay instinct in the wrong lane. A defendant's own statement offered by the prosecution may qualify as an opposing-party statement, but that does not answer whether the statement was obtained in violation of Miranda or whether the preliminary hearing must occur outside the jury's hearing.

D sells a made-up formalism. The law does not require a confession to be signed before it can be used in a criminal case. The real admissibility issue is whether Miranda was satisfied, and the hearing on that issue must be outside the jury's hearing.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The tested rule and credited outcome are unchanged: when the preliminary question involves the admissibility of a confession, the court must conduct the hearing so the jury cannot hear it. The defendant's Miranda objection gives the court a real confession-admissibility question, and Rule 104(c) controls the hearing location.

Federal Rule of Evidence 104(c)(1) states that the court must conduct a preliminary-question hearing so the jury cannot hear it if the hearing involves the admissibility of a confession. The Advisory Committee material likewise treats confession admissibility as the mandatory out-of-jury category. Miranda v. Arizona supplies the underlying constitutional rule that custodial-interrogation statements generally require warnings and waiver before use in the prosecution's case. Rule 801(d)(2) explains why a party's own statement is not hearsay when offered against that party, but that hearsay classification does not displace the constitutional admissibility screen or Rule 104(c)'s hearing procedure. Sources checked: https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2025-02/federal-rules-of-evidence-dec-1-2024_0.pdf, https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_104, https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/384/436/, and https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_801.

Because Daniel contends that no Miranda warnings were given before an oral confession following arrest and questioning, the court should grant the request for a hearing outside the jury's hearing. The court does not yet decide in front of the jury whether the confession is admissible.
