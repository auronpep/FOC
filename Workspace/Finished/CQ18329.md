## 1. Final Question

Daniel was arrested after investigators tied him to a scheme involving forged purchase-order forms sent to a private Christian bookshop. A federal grand jury issued a subpoena requiring Daniel to do three things: (1) copy a short paragraph in his own handwriting for comparison with the forms, (2) provide a blood sample for DNA testing on an envelope seal, and (3) appear before the grand jury and answer questions about what he knew when the forms were prepared. Daniel refuses all three demands, asserting his Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination.

If the government moves to compel, which refusal is protected by the Fifth Amendment?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

Which compelled act is protected by the Fifth Amendment: giving handwriting, giving blood, or answering questions about knowledge?

## 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is correct. The Fifth Amendment privilege protects Daniel from being compelled to give testimonial or communicative answers about what he knew. The grand jury question asks for the contents of his mind. The handwriting exemplar and blood sample are different. They use Daniel's physical characteristics as evidence, not his compelled testimony.

Gold Key: The Fifth Amendment protects testimonial communication, not the compelled display or extraction of physical characteristics.

Silver Key: Sort each demand by what the government is taking: mind-content answers are protected; body/identity evidence is not protected by the Fifth Amendment.

## 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because it feels invasive and rights-protective. The breaker is that the call is Fifth Amendment self-incrimination, not bodily-integrity search doctrine. Gold Key: blood is physical evidence, so the Fifth Amendment privilege does not protect the refusal.

C is attractive because all three demands could help the prosecution. The breaker is the overclaim: the Fifth Amendment does not protect every incriminating disclosure, only compelled testimonial communication. A handwriting sample and a blood sample can be compelled under the Fifth Amendment analysis.

D is attractive because handwriting looks expressive. The breaker is the comparison purpose: the government is using the physical characteristics of the writing, not Daniel's thoughts or message. Gold Key: a handwriting exemplar is treated as identifying physical evidence, not testimonial communication.

## 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The source row's legal issue and credited outcome are preserved: the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination protects compelled testimonial or communicative evidence, but it does not protect compelled production of physical characteristics or biological evidence.

The governing authority supports the same result. In Schmerber v. California, 384 U.S. 757 (1966), the Supreme Court treated a compelled blood sample as physical evidence rather than compelled testimonial communication for Fifth Amendment purposes. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/384/757/

In Gilbert v. California, 388 U.S. 263 (1967), the Court held that a handwriting exemplar is an identifying physical characteristic outside the Fifth Amendment privilege. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/388/263/

United States v. Hubbell, 530 U.S. 27 (2000), confirms the testimonial boundary: the Fifth Amendment protects compelled communications that make a person a witness against himself. Source: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/530/27/

Applied here, Daniel may be compelled to provide handwriting and blood because those demands obtain physical evidence. He may not be compelled, absent immunity, to answer grand jury questions about his knowledge of the forged documents because that demand seeks testimonial content. The transformed item does not change the rule, issue, or credited outcome.
