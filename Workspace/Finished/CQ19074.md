### 1. Final question
Ruth has a diagnosed compulsion disorder. She knows that opening the drain valve on a privately owned mobile baptismal-tank trailer is illegal and morally wrong, and she understands that the rushing water will flood and ruin the owner's audio equipment stored beside it. During an episode, she feels an uncontrollable urge and opens the valve after the lot is closed; the water destroys the equipment. The jurisdiction applies only the M'Naghten test, with no irresistible-impulse supplement.

Does Ruth have a successful insanity defense?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
Does pure M'Naghten excuse a defendant who knew what she was doing and knew it was wrong, but could not control the impulse to do it?

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. Pure M'Naghten asks whether the defendant, because of mental disease or defect, lacked the cognitive capacity to know the nature and quality of the act or to know that the act was wrong. Ruth knew what opening the valve would do, and she knew the act was illegal and morally wrong. Gold Key: M'Naghten is a knowledge test, not a self-control test, unless the jurisdiction adds an irresistible-impulse supplement. Silver Key: when the stem expressly says the jurisdiction uses only M'Naghten, lock onto what the defendant knew and do not let compulsion facts supply a separate defense.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because a diagnosed compulsion disorder sounds like the fact that should trigger legal insanity. The breaker is that diagnosis alone does not answer the legal test; Ruth still knew the nature of the act and knew it was wrong.

C is attractive because the ruined equipment makes the crime look like a property-only offense, and the answer reaches the same "no" result. The breaker is the categorical word "unavailable." Insanity can apply to property crimes when the jurisdiction's insanity test is satisfied; Ruth loses because she does not satisfy pure M'Naghten.

D is the dominant trap. It is attractive because the stem says Ruth felt an uncontrollable urge, and that fact would matter in a jurisdiction using an irresistible-impulse supplement. Gold Key: pure M'Naghten does not include inability to control conduct. The breaker is that the stem removes that supplement and gives the cognitive facts that defeat the defense.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Criminal Law and Procedure, General Principles, Defenses, Excuse Defenses, Responsibility, with verified outline code `72060101`. The source rule is the M'Naghten insanity test: a defendant is not excused unless, because of disease of the mind, the defendant did not know the nature and quality of the act or did not know the act was wrong. The leading authority is M'Naghten's Case, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), identified in the University of Minnesota Law Library classic cases collection at https://lawlibrarycollections.umn.edu/classic-cases-criminal-mnaghten-rule. The Cornell Wex insanity-defense overview distinguishes M'Naghten's cognitive focus from the irresistible-impulse test's volitional focus at https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/insanity_defense. Under the supplied pure-M'Naghten jurisdiction, Ruth's inability to resist does not establish insanity because she knew the physical nature and wrongfulness of her act. The credited outcome is unchanged from the original: no successful insanity defense.
