The policy was enacted because of a concern about the adverse visual effects of clutter and paper waste from flyers posted on the library bulletin boards. ### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**C is correct.** Under *Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission*, 447 U.S. 557 (1980), a government regulation of truthful, non-misleading commercial speech must (1) serve a substantial government interest, (2) directly advance that interest, and (3) be no more extensive than necessary to serve that interest. The fourth prong — the "reasonable fit" requirement as refined by *Board of Trustees of the State University of New York v. Fox*, 492 U.S. 469 (1989) — does not demand the least restrictive means, but it does require a reasonable fit between the government's interest and the means chosen.

Here, reducing lobby clutter and paper waste may be a substantial interest. However, removing only bulletin-board postings that are entirely commercial advertisements is not a reasonable fit. Only 15 of 150 bulletin-board slots across the library system were affected — just ten percent — and there is no evidence that commercial-ad flyers generate more clutter or paper waste per posting than the non-commercial flyers that remain. The policy therefore sweeps more broadly (or more narrowly) than necessary to achieve the board's stated goal, and it fails the reasonable-fit requirement of the *Central Hudson* test.

**Silver Key:** When you see a commercial-speech question, lock the *Central Hudson* framework first — substantial interest, directly advances, reasonable fit. Then check whether the answer choice names the correct tier. If it says "rationally related," it is cutting the test too low. If it says "compelling interest / necessary," it is pushing the test too high. The right tier is intermediate scrutiny with a reasonable-fit tail.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is incorrect** because it states the wrong level of scrutiny. Commercial speech is not subject to mere rational-basis review. The applicable test is the intermediate-scrutiny framework from *Central Hudson*: the regulation must directly advance a substantial government interest and be a reasonable fit. A student who picks A is likely defaulting to the lowest tier of review because the government interest (reducing clutter) sounds reasonable — but the standard is higher than rational basis for commercial speech.

**B is incorrect** because it also states the wrong test — this time pushing too high. It invokes strict scrutiny ("compelling interest" and "necessary to vindicate"), which is the standard for content-based restrictions on fully protected speech, not for commercial-speech regulations. A student who picks B may be conflating commercial speech with political speech, or may assume that any ban on speech triggers strict scrutiny. The *Central Hudson* intermediate standard applies instead.

**C is incorrect** because it reaches the correct outcome (unconstitutional) but for the wrong reason. The *Central Hudson* test does not require the government to use the least restrictive means — that is strict-scrutiny language. Under *Fox*, the government must show only a reasonable fit between its interest and the means chosen. A student who picks C correctly senses the policy is too narrow to work but reaches for the wrong legal vocabulary. The problem is not that a less restrictive alternative exists; it is that the chosen means do not reasonably fit the stated end.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** *Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission of New York*, 447 U.S. 557 (1980), as refined by *Board of Trustees of the State University of New York v. Fox*, 492 U.S. 469 (1989).

**The four-step *Central Hudson* test for commercial-speech regulations:**

1. **Is the speech commercial, and does it concern a lawful activity that is not misleading or fraudulent?** If the speech is misleading or concerns unlawful activity, it receives no First Amendment protection and may be banned outright. If it is truthful and lawful, proceed.

2. **Does the regulation serve a substantial government interest?** The government must identify a genuine, significant interest — not a pretext.

3. **Does the regulation directly advance that interest?** The government must show that the restriction actually furthers the interest asserted, not merely that it might do so in the abstract.

4. **Is the regulation no more extensive than necessary to serve that interest (reasonable fit)?** Per *Fox*, this does not require the least restrictive means. It requires a "reasonable fit" between the government's ends and the means chosen to accomplish those ends. The regulation must be narrowly tailored, but not perfectly so.

**Application to this item:**

- The library board's policy targets commercial advertisements for for-profit services — classic commercial speech under *Virginia State Board of Pharmacy v. Virginia Citizens Consumer Council*, 425 U.S. 748 (1976) (speech that "does no more than propose a commercial transaction").
- Reducing visual clutter and paper waste in public library lobbies is a substantial government interest. *See Metromedia, Inc. v. City of San Diego*, 453 U.S. 490 (1981) (aesthetics can be a substantial interest).
- The policy directly advances that interest in a general sense — removing some commercial flyers reduces some clutter.
- However, the policy is **not a reasonable fit**. Only 15 of 150 bulletin-board slots across the system were cleared — a mere 10%. There is no evidence that the removed commercial-ad flyers created more clutter per posting than the remaining 135 slots of non-commercial postings. The policy therefore does not bear a reasonable relationship to the board's stated goal. *See Rubin v. Coors Brewing Co.*, 514 U.S. 476 (1995) (a ban that addresses only a small fraction of the problem is not a reasonable fit).

**Conclusion:** The policy is unconstitutional because it fails the reasonable-fit prong of *Central Hudson*. The credited answer (C in the new lettering, D in the original) is correct.

**Authority:** *Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. PSC*, 447 U.S. 557 (1980); *Bd. of Tr. of State Univ. of N.Y. v. Fox*, 492 U.S. 469 (1989); *Rubin v. Coors Brewing Co.*, 514 U.S. 476 (1995).

**Doctrinal-currency check:** The *Central Hudson* framework remains the controlling test for commercial-speech regulations. No doctrinal-currency hotlist item overrides it. The 2023 FRE amendments and recent Supreme Court decisions (*303 Creative*, *Jack Daniel's*) do not alter the *Central Hudson* intermediate-scrutiny framework for truthful, non-misleading commercial speech.

### 2. The 17-section student case study

#### Program Frame

This question tests the **Central Hudson** intermediate-scrutiny framework for commercial speech. The case study will walk you through a government policy that targets only commercial advertisements on public bulletin boards. Your job is to apply the four-part *Central Hudson* test and determine whether the policy survives — focusing on the "reasonable-fit" prong that trips up most students.

#### Student Access Test

Before we begin, confirm you can answer these from memory:

1. What tier of scrutiny applies to truthful, non-misleading commercial speech? *(Intermediate — not rational basis, not strict scrutiny.)*
2. What are the four prongs of *Central Hudson*? *(Lawful/not misleading; substantial interest; directly advances; reasonable fit.)*
3. What does "reasonable fit" mean — is it the same as "least restrictive means"? *(No — it is a lower bar. The government must show a reasonable fit, not the single best option.)*

If you hesitated on any of these, review the Central Hudson framework card before continuing.

#### Question Data

**Stem:** A public library board enacted a policy removing from its branch lobbies all bulletin-board postings that consist entirely of commercial advertisements for for-profit services. The policy was enacted because of a concern about the adverse visual effects of clutter and paper waste from flyers posted on the library bulletin boards. However, the library board continued to allow postings for community events, church announcements, charitable notices, and other non-commercial matters on the same bulletin boards. As a result of the policy, 15 of the 150 bulletin-board slots across the library system were cleared.

**Call:** Is this library-board policy constitutional?

**Choices:**
- **A.** Yes, because regulations of commercial speech are subject only to the requirement that they be rationally related to a legitimate state goal, and that requirement is satisfied here.
- **B.** Yes, because the library board has a compelling interest in protecting the appearance of its lobbies and reducing paper waste, and such a ban is necessary to vindicate this interest.
- **C.** No, because it does not constitute the least restrictive means with which to reduce clutter and paper waste in the library lobbies.
- **D.** No, because there is not a reasonable fit between the legitimate interest of the library board in reducing clutter and paper waste and the means it chose to advance that interest.

#### Distilled Core Question

A public library board removes bulletin-board postings that are entirely commercial advertisements, citing lobby clutter and paper waste. It keeps all non-commercial postings. Only 15 of 150 slots are affected, and there is no evidence that commercial-ad flyers create more clutter per posting than other flyers. Is the policy constitutional?

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks whether the policy is **constitutional** — a classic First Amendment commercial-speech question. Before reading the choices, predict: the policy regulates commercial speech, so *Central Hudson* intermediate scrutiny applies. The government interest (reducing clutter) may be substantial, but the means must directly advance that interest and be a reasonable fit. Only 10% of slots are removed, with no evidence commercial flyers cause more clutter. Prediction: **unconstitutional — not a reasonable fit.**

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A**
1. **C3 signal:** The choice says "rationally related to a legitimate state goal." That is the rational-basis test — the lowest tier of scrutiny.
2. **Student label:** Wrong tier — rational basis is too low for commercial speech.
3. **True/responsive version:** A correct version would say "directly advances a substantial government interest in a way that is reasonably tailored to achieve that objective."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Commercial speech is subject to intermediate scrutiny under *Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. PSC*, 447 U.S. 557 (1980), not rational-basis review. This choice misstates the governing standard.

**Choice B**
1. **C3 signal:** The choice says "compelling interest" and "necessary to vindicate." That is strict scrutiny — the highest tier.
2. **Student label:** Wrong tier — strict scrutiny is too high for commercial speech.
3. **True/responsive version:** A correct version would say "the regulation directly advances a substantial government interest and is a reasonable fit."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Strict scrutiny applies to content-based restrictions on political speech, not commercial-speech regulations. *Central Hudson* intermediate scrutiny governs here.

**Choice C**
1. **C3 signal:** The choice says "least restrictive means." That phrase belongs to strict scrutiny, not intermediate scrutiny.
2. **Student label:** Right outcome, wrong reason — "least restrictive means" is strict-scrutiny language.
3. **True/responsive version:** A correct version would say "there is not a reasonable fit between the government interest and the means chosen."
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Under *Bd. of Tr. of State Univ. of N.Y. v. Fox*, 492 U.S. 469 (1989), the fourth prong of *Central Hudson* requires a reasonable fit, not the least restrictive means.

**Choice D**
1. **C3 signal:** The choice says "not a reasonable fit." That is the correct *Central Hudson* standard.
2. **Student label:** Correct — no reasonable fit between means and end.
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice is already correct as written.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Correct. Removing only 10% of slots without evidence that commercial flyers cause disproportionately more clutter fails the reasonable-fit requirement. *See Rubin v. Coors Brewing Co.*, 514 U.S. 476 (1995).

After cutting A (wrong tier — rational basis) and B (wrong tier — strict scrutiny), and resolving the clash between C (least restrictive means) and D (reasonable fit) in favor of D, the residual is:

**The policy fails the reasonable-fit prong of *Central Hudson* because removing only 10% of bulletin-board slots, without evidence that the removed postings cause more clutter, is not a reasonable fit between the board's interest and the means chosen. Choice D is correct.**
