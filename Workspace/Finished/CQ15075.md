### 1. Final Question

Martha owned Bethany Orchard in fee simple of record on March 4. That morning, Lydia, who ran a hymn-printing shop, loaned Martha $73,000, and Martha mortgaged Bethany Orchard to Lydia as security for the loan. Lydia recorded the mortgage on March 17.

### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is the best answer. The statute has two locks for Timothy: he needed to take without notice and he needed to record before Lydia. Timothy cleared the notice lock when he bought, but he did not clear the recording lock because Lydia recorded on March 17 and Timothy recorded on March 24.

Gold Key: In a race-notice recording act, a later buyer defeats an earlier unrecorded interest only if the later buyer both takes without notice and records first.

Silver Key: When an answer gives you an innocent buyer, check the recording date before you reward the buyer.

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A sells an equity-sounding story: Lydia waited, so she should lose priority. The breaker is that the statute does not punish every recording delay. It asks who qualifies under the notice-and-first-recording formula, and Lydia recorded before Timothy.

C sells a first-in-time shortcut: Lydia's mortgage came before Timothy's deed, so the deed must be subject to it. The breaker is the word "necessarily." A recording act can change ordinary first-in-time priority, so Lydia wins for the statutory recording reason, not because Timothy's later deed was automatically subject to the mortgage in every possible version of the facts.

D is the dominant trap. It rewards Timothy for paying value and lacking notice before Lydia recorded. That feels like the innocent-buyer rule, but it stops one step early. Gold Key: a race-notice buyer must also record first. Timothy did not, so the mortgage remains good against Bethany Orchard.

### 8. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, issue, and credited outcome are unchanged: this is a Real Property priority question under a race-notice recording act. The supplied statute controls. It protects a subsequent purchaser for value without notice only if that purchaser first records. Timothy bought without notice, but Lydia recorded the mortgage before Timothy recorded the deed. Timothy therefore cannot use the recording act to defeat Lydia's mortgage, and Lydia's mortgage is good against Bethany Orchard.
