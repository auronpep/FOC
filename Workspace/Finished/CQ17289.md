### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Hannah is likely to establish fraud. The elements of common-law fraud are (1) a false representation of a material fact, (2) knowledge of falsity (scienter), (3) intent to induce reliance, (4) reasonable reliance, and (5) damages. The facts support each: Paul falsely told Hannah there were "no known structural problems" when he knew about the termite damage in the joists; he covered the joists to induce reliance; Hannah reasonably relied on the seller's affirmative statement; and she suffered damages by paying full price. **Gold Key:** active concealment of a known material defect, combined with an affirmative misstatement, is actionable fraud; it is not the case that fraud can never be based on concealment, nor that every property defect is fraud, nor that economic damages are unrecoverable in fraud.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**C. Yes, because every defect in sold property is fraud.**

This is the dominant trap. The overclaim is attractive because the everyday concept of fraud is "I lied about the product," and a student who half-remembers that fraud requires the specific elements reaches for the broad form. The breaker is the **Gold Key**: fraud requires the specific elements — falsity, scienter, intent, reliance, damages — and not every property defect satisfies all of them. A latent defect the seller did not know about is not fraud.

**A. No, because the buyer's damages are economic.**

The economic-loss trap. The student remembers the negligence-bar-on-pure-economic-loss doctrine and imports it into fraud. The breaker is that fraud is a recognized business tort, and economic damages are exactly what fraud is designed to compensate.

**D. No, because fraud can never be based on concealment.**

The mislabel trap. The student treats "concealment" as a forbidden fraud theory, missing the fact that the seller both concealed AND made an affirmative misstatement. The breaker is that the misstatement alone satisfies the falsity element, and active concealment of a known material defect is independently actionable.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 17289, a Torts item on common-law fraud (misrepresentation). The official key is A: the buyer is likely to establish fraud because the seller concealed and misstated a known material fact to induce reliance.

Common-law fraud (also called intentional misrepresentation or deceit) requires a false representation of a material past or present fact, made with knowledge of its falsity or reckless disregard for the truth, with intent to induce reliance, on which the plaintiff reasonably relies, causing damages. The Restatement (Second) of Torts § 525 codifies the prima facie case: "One who fraudulently makes a material misrepresentation of fact, opinion, intention, or law for the purpose of inducing another to act or to refrain from action in reliance upon it in a business transaction is liable to the other in tort for any pecuniary loss caused by the other's reliance." Section 527 addresses active concealment: "One who actively conceals a material fact with knowledge of the fact and an intent to induce reliance is subject to the same liability to the other as though he had stated the nonexistence of the fact." Section 549 addresses the economic-loss point: fraudulent misrepresentation is a recognized business tort and economic damages are recoverable. The seller's affirmative misstatement ("no known structural problems") satisfies the falsity element; his prior knowledge of the termite damage satisfies scienter; his covering of the joists shows intent to induce reliance; Hannah's reliance was reasonable; and her damages (paying full price for a house with a serious structural defect) are recoverable. Fraud is established.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: the buyer is likely to establish fraud because the seller concealed and misstated a known material fact to induce reliance.
