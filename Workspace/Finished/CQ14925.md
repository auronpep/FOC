## Final question

Sister Lydia, a widowed member of the small Grace Fellowship congregation in the rural community of Grace Hollow, owned a modest two-bedroom cottage on the edge of town. She leased the cottage by written lease to Barnabas, a young Bible college student, for a term of two years ending on the last day of a recent month. The lease called for $700 in monthly rent. Barnabas occupied the cottage and paid the rent for the first 15 months of the lease term. When he received a call to assist in planting a new church in a distant city, he arranged for Timothy, a trusted brother from the church's young men's Bible study, to take over the cottage. Without consulting Sister Lydia (who was away visiting grandchildren for an extended period), Barnabas signed a simple handwritten note transferring to Timothy his "full remaining lease rights" for the last nine months of the term. Timothy moved in and made the next four monthly $700 rent payments directly to Sister Lydia's mailbox. For the final five months of the lease term, no rent was paid by anyone, and Timothy moved out with three months left of the lease term when his apprenticeship situation changed. Sister Lydia did not learn of the default and the vacancy until she returned after the lease term had ended. She has sued Barnabas and Timothy, jointly and severally, for $3,500 for the last five months' rent. What is the likely outcome of the lawsuit?

## Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

C is correct. Barnabas transferred the cottage to Timothy for the entire remaining nine months of the lease term by an informal writing that assigned his full remaining rights. This was an effective assignment of the leasehold. Because the assignment covered the entire remainder of the term, Timothy stepped into privity of estate with Sister Lydia for the duration of that assigned interest and is liable for rent during the period the estate ran with him. Barnabas, however, was never released by Sister Lydia. The original lease created privity of contract between Barnabas and Sister Lydia that survives the assignment. A landlord's mere acceptance of rent from an assignee does not constitute a release or novation of the original tenant's contractual liability. Therefore both are jointly and severally liable for the full $3,500 in unpaid rent.

**Silver Key (navigates the two-answer tension):** When the stem says the transfer was for "the remaining nine months" (the entire balance of the term) with no reservation of any reversionary interest, treat it as an assignment that shifts privity of estate to the new occupant while the original contract liability stays with the first tenant unless the landlord explicitly releases it. Do not assume "anyone else living there must be a sublessee."

## Full wrong-answer explanations

A: This is the dominant trap. It is attractive because students often assume that any time a tenant lets someone else live in the premises and pay rent it must be a sublease (retaining a reversion), so only the original tenant can be reached by the landlord. The breaker is the stem fact that Barnabas transferred his "full remaining lease rights" for the last nine months—the entire balance of the term—with no reversion retained. That is the definition of an assignment, which creates privity of estate in Timothy for the assigned period.

B: This choice sells the plausible but incorrect idea that privity of estate "follows possession only" and automatically ends when the assignee moves out, leaving the original tenant on the hook only for the tail end. The breaker is that once a full-term assignment occurs, the assignee's privity of estate lasts for the entire assigned term regardless of when he physically vacates; the original tenant's contract liability is not limited to the post-vacation period.

D: This choice is attractive because it feels equitable: if the landlord took money from Timothy without complaint, she should be stuck with him as the only tenant. The breaker is that acceptance of rent from an assignee does not release the original tenant from the express contractual obligations in the written lease. Release requires an agreement (novation) that the landlord never gave.

## Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer

The credited outcome is unchanged from the original: both the original tenant and the assignee are liable when the transfer is an assignment of the entire remaining term and the landlord has not granted a release.

Governing rule: At common law, an assignment of a leasehold for the entire remaining term transfers the tenant's estate to the assignee, creating privity of estate between the landlord and the assignee for the duration of the assigned term. The assignee is liable on all covenants that touch and concern the land (including the covenant to pay rent) that run with the estate during the time the assignee holds it. The original tenant, however, remains liable on the express covenants in the lease contract under privity of contract. The landlord's acceptance of rent from the assignee, without more, does not constitute a release or novation of the original tenant. See Restatement (Second) of Property: Landlord and Tenant § 16.1 (assignee liable to landlord on covenants running with the land during assigned term); see also the common-law rule illustrated in cases such as Baynton v. Morgan (1888) and standard landlord-tenant hornbooks (e.g., the original lessee remains a surety for the rent after assignment unless the landlord agrees otherwise).

The stem facts establish an assignment (full remaining term transferred, no reversion retained) rather than a sublease (which would require the original tenant to retain a reversionary interest, even one day). No facts show a release. Therefore A (original) / C (new) is correct, and the distractors that assume sublease status, automatic release by rent acceptance, or possession-time limits on estate privity are incorrect. The tested black-letter rule, the legal issue (assignment vs. sublease consequences for privity), and the credited outcome are identical to the source question.
