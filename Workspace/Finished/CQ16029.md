### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

B is the best answer. The call asks about Lydia's liability for murder after she used deadly force. The decisive fact is not the stolen wristbands. The decisive fact is Timothy's apparent life-threatening attack: he pressed a hard object into her ribs, claimed it was a knife, and threatened to cut her throat. A person may use deadly force in self-defense when she reasonably believes she faces imminent death or serious bodily harm.

Gold Key: Apparent deadly force can justify deadly self-defense if the belief is reasonable, even when the weapon later turns out to be fake or absent.

Silver Key: Lock the call on person-defense before reading the goods facts. Once the threat is to Lydia's life, the stolen wristbands are bait unless the answer ties them to lawful arrest, provocation, or a dispositive self-defense limitation.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because it punishes the illegal conduct in the opening sentence. The breaker is that committing a separate nonviolent crime does not by itself erase self-defense against a private deadly threat. Gold Key: the privilege can still exist unless the defendant provoked the deadly attack, was resisting a lawful arrest, or otherwise falls into a recognized self-defense limitation.

C is attractive because the stolen wristbands feel like the thing Lydia was protecting. The breaker is the call/fact mismatch: Timothy threatened Lydia's body, not just her property interest. Silver Key: cut a property-defense answer when the stem's danger fact points to death or serious bodily harm to the person.

D is attractive because it notices the reveal that Timothy had no knife. The breaker is that self-defense turns on a reasonable belief at the moment of force, not perfect hindsight. Gold Key: an apparent deadly weapon can be enough when the belief is reasonable under the circumstances.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source item. The issue is self-defense in a murder prosecution where the defendant was committing a separate crime but reasonably believed the decedent was threatening deadly force. The credited answer remains that the defendant should be found not guilty if the belief that her life was in danger was reasonable.

Lawyer-confirmation authority: Model Penal Code section 3.04(1) recognizes force in self-protection when the actor believes force is immediately necessary against unlawful force, and section 3.04(2)(b) treats deadly force as justified only when the actor believes it necessary to protect against death, serious bodily harm, kidnapping, or forcible sexual intercourse. Section 3.04(2)(a)(i) separately addresses resistance to known peace-officer arrest, which is not this fact pattern. Source reviewed: H2O/Open Casebook, "Model Penal Code section 3.04," https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/3553-balloberman-crim-law-casebook-third-edition/resources/10.1.3-model-penal-code-section-304/.

Nothing in the research contradicts the supplied key. The private attacker was not making a lawful arrest. Lydia's illegal sale of stolen wristbands does not convert a private death threat into force she must absorb. The right answer remains not guilty if her belief of imminent deadly danger was reasonable.
