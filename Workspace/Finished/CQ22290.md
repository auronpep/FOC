### 6. Full Right-Answer Explanation

The motion should be denied because Timothy's admission was volunteered, not the product of interrogation. His request for counsel shut down police questioning, but it did not turn every later custodial interaction into interrogation. The later deputy asked a basic comfort-and-logistics question: whether Timothy needed water or a blanket. That kind of neutral check-in is not reasonably likely to draw out an incriminating response, so Timothy's outburst remains admissible.

Gold Key: After a suspect invokes counsel, the police must stop interrogation, not all conversation. A volunteered statement remains admissible unless the police use express questioning or its functional equivalent to draw out guilt.

Silver Key: Once you see "asked for a lawyer," do not auto-suppress. Ask one more question: did the police restart interrogation, or did the suspect simply blurt something out?

### 7. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

#### A

This choice feels careful because it focuses on the hour-long gap and the officer's motives, which sounds like the sort of fact courts might care about. But the breaker is that intentional delay is not the issue here; the admissibility question turns on whether Timothy's statement came from interrogation, and this answer never solves that.

#### B

This choice sells the common myth that invoking counsel requires the police to produce a lawyer on the spot before anything else can happen. The breaker is that the rule is narrower: questioning must stop until counsel is present, but there is no separate requirement that the police instantly place counsel in the room before every later custodial exchange.

#### D

This choice is attractive because "two-step interrogation" is a real Miranda doctrine, and the stem has two officers and a time break. The breaker is that two-step doctrine needs questioning designed to work around Miranda, and this record has no first confession, no unwarned interrogation, and no later effort to repeat one. Gold Key: if the later words are just a neutral welfare check, the doctrine never turns on.

### 8. Full Black-Letter-Law Verification And Legal Reasoning For The Right Answer

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row. This is still a Fifth Amendment Miranda question about whether a post-invocation statement must be suppressed when it follows a neutral custodial interaction rather than interrogation.

Authority floor:

- U.S. Const. amend. V
- Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966)
- Edwards v. Arizona, 451 U.S. 477 (1981)
- Rhode Island v. Innis, 446 U.S. 291 (1980)
- Missouri v. Seibert, 542 U.S. 600 (2004)

Why the credited answer is correct:

Miranda covers custodial interrogation, and Miranda itself says volunteered statements are not barred by the Fifth Amendment. After a suspect asks for counsel, Edwards requires interrogation to cease until counsel is present. But Innis defines interrogation as express questioning or its functional equivalent: words or actions the police should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. A neutral welfare question like "Do you need water or a blanket?" does not fit that standard on these facts, so Timothy's admission is admissible as a volunteered statement.

Why the main trap fails:

Seibert addresses a deliberate question-first, warn-later tactic. That doctrine does not apply here because there was no prewarning interrogation, no first confession, and no deliberate attempt to recreate a confession after warnings. The presence of a second officer and a later statement does not by itself create a two-step problem.
