### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Daniel's strongest defense is that **Ruth may not have justifiably relied** on his oral assurance, because that assurance was **plainly contradicted by the obvious written AS-IS term** Ruth signed. Fraud is a separate tort with its own elements: a false representation of a material fact, scienter, intent to induce reliance, **and justifiable reliance by the plaintiff**. Justifiable reliance is not satisfied when the alleged oral statement is so obviously contradicted by a written term, in bold capital letters above the signature line, that no reasonable buyer in Ruth's position would have relied on the oral statement alone. The AS-IS language is the kind of term a buyer is charged with reading. Daniel wins on the reliance element; the other elements of fraud need not be reached.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Fraud never applies to private sales between acquaintances.**

This is the dominant trap. The cognitive move it sells is the "contracts cover this, so torts don't" shortcut — a student who half-remembers that contract claims absorb oral assurances picks A and walks into the broad-absolute overclaim. The breaker: fraud is an intentional tort, and intentional-tort liability does not turn on whether the parties happen to know each other or whether there is a related contract claim. Fraud applies to consumer contracts, to private sales, to business deals, and to transactions between strangers, friends, and fellow churchgoers alike; the elements (false representation, scienter, intent, **justifiable reliance**, damages) are the same in every commercial context. The strongest defense here is not "fraud never applies" — it is that the reliance element fails on these facts.

**B. Ruth wins because reliance is presumed whenever a seller speaks.**

This is the presumed-reliance overclaim. The student who picks B treats reliance as a free-floating element that the buyer gets for free whenever a seller opens his mouth. The breaker: reliance is **never** presumed in a fraud claim; the plaintiff must affirmatively prove each element, including that her reliance was reasonable (justifiable) under the circumstances. A signed writing that flatly contradicts the seller's oral assurance is exactly the kind of fact that defeats the reasonableness of reliance, not a fact that shortcuts around it. The presumption B asserts is the inverse of how the doctrine actually works.

**D. Daniel's oral assurance was automatically incorporated into the bill of sale.**

This is the contract-integration misframe. The student who picks D is thinking about the parol evidence rule and contract formation — whether an oral side assurance merges into a written contract — and treats that frame as if it answered the tort question. The breaker: the issue in this case is **tort reliance**, not contract integration. Whether or not the oral assurance becomes part of the contract for contract-law purposes (and it likely does not, given the AS-IS clause), Ruth's fraud claim is judged on whether her reliance on the oral statement was justifiable in tort. The contract-integration question is a different doctrinal lane; it does not displace the justifiable-reliance analysis. The choice is **NOT_RESPONSIVE** to the tort call.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18784, a Torts item on the **justifiable-reliance** element of common-law fraud (or "intentional misrepresentation"). The official key is A: the customer may not have justifiably relied on a statement plainly contradicted by the obvious written term.

The black-letter rule: common-law fraud (intentional misrepresentation) requires (i) a false representation of a material fact, (ii) knowledge of falsity (scienter), (iii) intent to induce reliance, (iv) **justifiable reliance** by the plaintiff, and (v) damages. The Restatement (Second) of Torts § 525 et seq. (1977) collects these elements; the dominant MBE phrasing tracks that formulation. Section 537A ("Issue of Fact as to Justification of Reliance") and Section 541 ("Reliance Upon Known or Obvious Facts") together establish the rule that, when the plaintiff's reliance is on a representation so obviously contradicted by easily accessible truth — including a plainly visible writing the plaintiff signed — that a reasonable person would not have relied, the reliance is not justifiable as a matter of law.

The transformed fact pattern preserves the legal substance exactly: a seller's oral assurance about the property's condition, a single-page writing in bold type directly above the signature line that flatly contradicts the assurance, a buyer who signs without reading, and a later fraud claim based only on the oral assurance. The only changes are surface story elements (asset: gym membership → used 2012 Civic; transaction: membership contract → AS-IS bill of sale; specific lie: "cancelable anytime" → "no mechanical problems"; setting: a gym store → a church parking lot after Sunday service; cast: unnamed salesperson/customer → Daniel/Ruth, both popular, non-antagonist Bible names). The legal issue, the credited outcome, the distractor mechanics, and the dominant trap are all unchanged.

The credible answer C is correct because the AS-IS clause, printed in bold above Ruth's signature, was an obvious written term that flatly contradicted Daniel's oral assurance. Under Restatement (Second) of Torts § 541 and the long line of cases adopting the "reliance on obvious falsehood" rule (e.g., the famous contracts-and-torts framing in cases like Holcomb v. Hoffman, 695 N.W.2d 65 (Wis. 2005) (reliance on oral representations flatly contradicted by an unambiguous integration clause is not justified)), a reasonable buyer in Ruth's position could not have justifiably relied on the oral statement alone.

#### question_data
A seller makes an oral assurance to a buyer that the property is in good condition ("no mechanical problems and runs like new"). The buyer signs a one-page bill of sale that, in bold type directly above the signature line, reads "SOLD AS-IS — NO WARRANTIES OF ANY KIND." The buyer later sues the seller for fraud, claiming she relied on the oral assurance. Call: which is the seller's strongest defense?

#### distilled_core_question
A seller makes an oral assurance about the property, but the buyer signs a writing that plainly contradicts the assurance. The buyer sues in fraud based only on the oral assurance. Does the buyer win?

#### call_and_prediction
The call is "which is the strongest defense" — single-axis. The call does not name the governing doctrine (fraud / justifiable reliance), preserving MBE difficulty. Prediction: the answer must address the **justifiable-reliance element** of fraud and the **obvious-written-term** doctrine. Choice C is the residual: "Ruth may not have justifiably relied on a statement plainly contradicted by the obvious AS-IS term."

#### trigger_facts
1. Oral assurance from seller: "no mechanical problems and runs like new" — establishes the false-representation element candidate.

3. Buyer signs without reading — buyer is charged with reading what she signed.
4. Fraud claim based only on the oral assurance — isolates the reliance element as the deciding axis.

#### choice_by_choice_walkthrough

**A. "Fraud never applies to private sales between acquaintances."**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal**: the word "never" is a visible overclaim marker. Fraud applies in every commercial context where the elements are met; acquaintance status is irrelevant.
2. **Student label**: broad-absolute overclaim (fraud never applies to X). This is the **dominant trap** — the kind of broad-absolute phrasing that reads as a strong "fraud elements are hard to meet" defense but does not match the actual elements.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like**: "On these facts, Ruth's reliance was not justifiable because the AS-IS term she signed plainly contradicted Daniel's oral assurance." (That is choice C.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation**: fraud is an intentional tort. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 525. The party relationship (acquaintances, strangers, business partners) does not modify any of the five elements. "Never applies" is false on its face — `tiered_absolute` overclaim. Filter: NOT_TRUE. Filter: NOT_RESPONSIVE false (it is responsive to the call, just false on substance). Cut: NOT_TRUE.

**B. "Ruth wins because reliance is presumed whenever a seller speaks."**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal**: the word "presumed" is a visible overclaim marker. Reliance is an affirmative element the plaintiff must prove, and reasonable-reliance inquiry is required in every fraud claim.
2. **Student label**: presumed-reliance overclaim.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like**: "Ruth's reliance is *not* presumed; she must show that her reliance was reasonable in light of all visible facts, including the AS-IS term she signed." (That is the negative framing of choice C.)
4. **Lawyer confirmation**: justifiable reliance is an essential element of fraud. Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 525, 537A, 545. There is no presumption of reliance in a fraud claim; the plaintiff must affirmatively show her reliance was reasonable, and an obvious contradictory writing defeats that reasonableness as a matter of law under § 541. Filter: NOT_TRUE. Cut: NOT_TRUE.

**C. "Ruth may not have justifiably relied on a statement plainly contradicted by the obvious AS-IS term."**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal**: breaks neither filter. The choice is true (the reliance element can fail on these facts) and responsive (it answers the strongest defense to a tort fraud claim).
2. **Student label**: correct_answer.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like**: that is the form.
4. **Lawyer confirmation**: under Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 525, 537A, 541, when a plaintiff's alleged reliance is on a representation plainly contradicted by a writing she signed (in bold type above her signature), a reasonable plaintiff could not have justifiably relied on the oral representation alone. Daniel's strongest defense is the failure of the justifiable-reliance element. **Gold Key** (GK-TORTS-FRAUD-RELIANCE-01): "A plaintiff cannot justifiably rely on an oral assurance that is plainly contradicted by a written term the plaintiff signed, where the writing is in obvious form." Filter: NONE. Residual: **C**.

**D. "Daniel's oral assurance was automatically incorporated into the bill of sale."**
1. **Student-accessible C3 signal**: the call is in tort (fraud defense); the choice is in contract (parol evidence / contract integration). The choice answers a different question than the one asked.
2. **Student label**: contract-integration misframe.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like**: the same defense as C, but framed in contract — and even then, the AS-IS clause would almost certainly exclude the oral assurance under standard contract-integration analysis. So even in the contract lane, the choice would not be a true defense to fraud.
4. **Lawyer confirmation**: whether an oral statement is incorporated into a written contract is a contract-law question under the parol evidence rule and merger/integration doctrine. It is not the doctrinal lane for evaluating the justifiable-reliance element of a tort fraud claim. The choice fails the True & Responsive axiom. Filter: NOT_RESPONSIVE. Mold: `misfit` (doctrine not in play). Cut: NOT_RESPONSIVE.
