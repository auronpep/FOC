# CQ19345 — Daniel's Crop Field *(The Pesticide Applicator)*

### 1. Final Question

Daniel held a valid pesticide-application license issued by the state. A state regulation prohibited applying any pesticide on food-producing crops unless the pesticide batch had been inspected and certified by a state agricultural officer. For seven years Daniel had purchased pesticide concentrate from a single supplier who consistently delivered certified batches. Without Daniel's knowledge, the supplier's state certification had lapsed before the latest delivery, making that shipment uncertified. Daniel applied the pesticide to a field of leafy greens intended for retail sale. Several consumers who purchased and ate the greens became ill.

Daniel is charged under a strict-liability agricultural-safety statute that makes it a misdemeanor to "apply uninspected pesticide on food-producing crops." Which of the following best describes the effect of Daniel's reasonable, good-faith reliance on his supplier?

---

### 2. Distilled Core Question

A licensed pesticide applicator unknowingly applies an uncertified pesticide batch after his longtime trusted supplier's certification lapses. He is charged under a strict liability statute. Does his good-faith reliance on his supplier provide a defense?

**D.** Daniel is guilty only if he knew or should have known that the supplier's certification had lapsed.

### 7. Right-Answer Explanation

**Choice B is correct.**

The fact pattern announces that the statute is a **strict liability** offense. That label does the legal work: strict liability means the prohibited act alone triggers criminal liability. No mental state, no care standard, no reliance history can be a defense.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIMINAL-STRICT-LIABILITY-01):** Public welfare regulatory statutes carry strict liability when they (1) are silent on mens rea, (2) cover a public-health subject, and (3) impose only a minor (misdemeanor) penalty. All three markers appear here. Once the statute is labeled strict liability, the defendant's good faith, diligence, or reliance on a third party's certification status is legally irrelevant. The act of applying uncertified pesticide — Daniel's conduct — is what triggers liability, full stop.

Daniel applied pesticide that was, in fact, uncertified. That is the prohibited act. The statute requires nothing more.

### 8. Wrong-Answer Explanations

**Choice A — causal deflection to supplier (7% predicted):**
The supplier's certification lapse feels like the "real" cause of the problem — that cognitive move is what the choice exploits. It invites students to apply a causation/fault framework: if the upstream actor's failure caused the violation, the downstream actor isn't responsible. But strict liability doesn't operate through fault or proximate cause: it attaches to the **regulated actor's prohibited conduct** (applying uncertified pesticide), not to who created the uncertified condition. Whether the supplier is at fault is a separate question. The supplier and Daniel may both face independent liability; their responsibilities don't cancel each other out.

**Gold Key:** Strict liability attaches to the act, not to fault. Third-party causation of the underlying regulatory condition does not negate the liability of the actor who committed the prohibited conduct.

**Choice C — good faith + reasonable care [DOMINANT TRAP] (22% predicted):**
This is the most attractive wrong answer because it mirrors everyday fairness: someone who used the same supplier for seven years, always received certified product, and had no reason to suspect a problem shouldn't be a criminal. That intuition is entirely reasonable in ordinary life — but strict liability is designed precisely to override it. The legislature determined that the public health risk from uninspected pesticides on food crops is serious enough that licensed applicators must guarantee compliance, not merely exercise reasonable care. Good faith and diligence are not defenses.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIMINAL-STRICT-LIABILITY-01):** Under strict liability, good faith and reasonable care are not defenses. The legislature placed a compliance guarantee — not a diligence obligation — on the regulated actor. Seven years of clean compliance history is irrelevant to the act-alone trigger.

**Choice D — "knew or should have known" negligence floor (13% predicted):**
This choice hedges toward a negligence standard — "he should be guilty only if he should have caught this." The phrase "knew or should have known" is a negligence formulation, not a strict liability one. Applying it here would transform the statute from strict liability into a negligence offense, contradicting the legislature's explicit choice. When the statute says "strict liability," courts cannot add a mens rea element by construction — even a minimal negligence floor — that the legislature deliberately omitted. The act alone triggers liability.

**Gold Key (GK-CRIMINAL-STRICT-LIABILITY-01):** "Knew or should have known" is a negligence standard. Strict liability eliminates all mens rea — including negligence. Reading in a "should have known" threshold rewrites the statute and contradicts the legislature's text.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Rule tested:** Public welfare offenses impose strict liability. Where a statute (1) is silent on mens rea, (2) regulates activity affecting public health and safety, and (3) imposes a minor penalty, courts apply the *Morissette* framework to recognize it as a strict liability offense. No mental state — not knowledge, intent, recklessness, or negligence — is required for conviction, and no reliance-based defense is available.

**Legal authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only — not the student path):**

- *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250 (1922): Supreme Court upheld strict liability for narcotics sales under a public welfare regulatory statute despite the defendant's claim of ignorance of the drug's illegal status. Foundation of the doctrine.
- *United States v. Dotterweich*, 320 U.S. 277 (1943): Extended strict liability to corporate officers in regulated industries; officer liable despite no personal knowledge of or participation in the violation.
- *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952): Foundational taxonomy. The Court distinguishes public welfare regulatory offenses (strict liability appropriate) from malum in se traditional crimes (mens rea required). Identifies the conditions for strict liability: statute silent on mens rea, public welfare/regulatory subject, relatively minor penalty. Warns that strict liability is a narrow exception, not a general rule.
- *United States v. Park*, 421 U.S. 658 (1975): Corporate officer convicted under strict liability food-safety statute could not escape via good faith compliance efforts or reliance on subordinates. The Court explicitly rejected reliance-based defenses.

**Confirmation:** The rule tested, the legal issue (strict liability public welfare offense; effect of good-faith supplier reliance), and the credited outcome (guilty; reliance is no defense) are identical to the original Q19345. The transform substitutes pesticide application (agriculture) for shellfish serving (restaurant). The regulatory structure, the lapsed certification, the reliance on a longtime supplier, the strict liability misdemeanor frame, and all three distractor mechanics are preserved exactly.

**Doctrinal currency check:** Public welfare strict liability is a stable, long-established doctrine. None of the hotlist flags (Establishment Clause, FRE 702, FRE 106, Second Amendment, Dobbs, Bruen, etc.) apply. No currency concern.
