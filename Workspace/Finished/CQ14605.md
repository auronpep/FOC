### 6. Full right-answer explanation

**B** is the proper instruction. It ties the jury’s reasonable doubt to the fact the alibi actually contests: whether Daniel was present at the place and time of the charged crime.

**Gold Key:** An alibi is not the defendant’s proof burden. It attacks presence or identity, so the prosecution still must prove presence beyond a reasonable doubt.

**Silver Key:** In an alibi-instruction array, scan the target of the doubt. The clean target is presence at the scene, not defendant’s proof, defendant’s evidence, or a jury-screening gate.

### 7. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A.** This choice sounds procedurally careful because it asks the jury to screen whether the alibi issue has been raised. The trap is the invented gate: the witness’s alibi testimony is evidence on presence, and the jury does not first impose a special production threshold before considering it.

**C.** This choice is attractive because it uses the words “reasonable doubt” and “not guilty.” **Silver Key:** the target is wrong. The instruction makes Daniel’s evidence the engine of the doubt, instead of asking whether the state proved Daniel’s presence at the scene.

**D.** This is the dominant trap. It sounds like ordinary defense-burden language and even uses the lower civil standard. **Gold Key:** alibi is not an affirmative-defense burden here; it negates presence, so the prosecution keeps the burden beyond a reasonable doubt.

### 8. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The source row gives QID 14605, Criminal Law, Constitutional Protection of Accused Persons, official key C, and a supplied explanation that alibi evidence negates an element and leaves the burden on the prosecution.  

The governing principle is current and matches the supplied key. **In re Winship** holds that due process protects the accused against conviction except on proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the charged crime. ([Justia Law][1]) The Ninth Circuit’s Model Criminal Jury Instruction 5.1 on alibi states that the government has the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was present at the time and place of the crime, that the defendant has no burden to prove alibi, and that reasonable doubt about presence requires acquittal. ([Ninth Circuit Court][2]) California’s current CALCRIM alibi instruction states the same operational rule and identifies as error an instruction requiring alibi to be proved by a preponderance of the evidence. ([Justia][3])
