## 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

C is correct. Stephen's no-earnest-money theory fails because the signed agreement contains exchanged promises: Naomi promised to convey the parcel, and Stephen promised to pay the price. Those promises are enough consideration. The missing deposit does not erase the bargain or let Stephen walk away from closing.

Gold Key: A land-sale contract does not need an earnest-money deposit to have consideration. The seller's promise to convey and the buyer's promise to pay are exchanged promises.

Silver Key: In a "wins because" call, grade the because-clause. The answer must explain why the contract is enforceable despite the no-deposit objection, not merely name a land-sale doctrine or a recorded fact.

## 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

A is attractive because equitable conversion is a real land-sale doctrine and sounds like the kind of equity rule that could support specific performance. The breaker is the call. Stephen is not arguing about risk of loss, title passage, or the buyer's equitable ownership; he is arguing that no earnest money means no consideration. Silver Key: the because-clause has to answer the consideration objection, and equitable conversion does not.

B is attractive because the stem deliberately includes the recorded agreement and makes the recording lawful. The breaker is that recording gives notice and can matter for priority against outsiders. It is not the reason Stephen's exchanged promise to buy is supported by consideration.

D is attractive because Stephen did use the parcel before closing, so the choice gives the eye a concrete act to grab. The breaker is that his Saturday use was permissive and free; it was not the promised purchase price, and there was already a signed writing. Gold Key: the bargain is supported by exchanged promises, so the case does not need a substitute performance theory.

## 9. Full Black-Letter Verification And Legal Reasoning

The tested rule is consideration for a land-sale contract. Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 17, ordinary contract formation requires a bargain with mutual assent and consideration. Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 71, consideration may be a bargained-for performance or a return promise, and a return promise counts when it is sought and given in exchange for the other promise. See the Restatement text reproduced in CALI's Contracts Doctrine, Theory and Practice, https://verkerkecontractsone.lawbooks.cali.org/chapter/the-consideration-doctrine/. The American Law Institute identifies the Restatement (Second) of Contracts as covering formation and consideration, https://www.ali.org/publications/restatement-law-second/contracts.

Here, Naomi promised to sell and Stephen promised to buy for $142,000. That exchange of promises supplies consideration even though no earnest-money deposit was required. Specific performance is a discretionary contract remedy for breach under Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 357, subject to adequacy and certainty limits in sections 359 and 362. See the Restatement specific-performance sections reproduced by H2O, https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/4201-contracts-2022/resources/1.8.5-restatement-2d-sections-on-specific-performance/. Part performance is a statute-of-frauds reliance doctrine for land-transfer contracts under Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 129, not a replacement for the consideration analysis here. See https://matthewminer.name/law/outlines/1L/2nd%2BSemester/LAW%2B506-002%2BContracts%2BII/R2C%2B%C2%A7%2B129.

The legal issue and credited outcome are unchanged: an earnest-money deposit is not required when the land-sale agreement is supported by exchanged promises. The credited answer remains the general contract consideration rule.
