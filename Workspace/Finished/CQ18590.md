## A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

### 1. Final question
Mary sues Barnabas for defamation after the launch of her private Bible-memory board game. Mary offers a screenshot of a one-star online marketplace review accusing her of stealing artwork and lying to customers. The reviewer's display name is "BarnabasTruth," but Mary offers no evidence that Barnabas created or controlled the marketplace account, that the account used Barnabas's photograph or email address, that the review contained information distinctive to Barnabas, or that Barnabas ever acknowledged the review. Barnabas objects. How should the court rule?

### 2. Distilled Core Question
When a party offers a screenshot of a damaging online review and ties it to the opponent only by a similar display name, has the party authenticated the review as the opponent's statement?

### 3. Final answer choices
A. Admit the screenshot because accusations in the review are relevant to Mary's defamation claim.

B. Exclude the screenshot because Mary has not produced evidence sufficient to support a finding that Barnabas wrote or adopted the review.

C. Admit the screenshot because a display name using Barnabas's name is always enough to authenticate an online review.

D. Exclude the screenshot only if Barnabas proves that someone else created the marketplace account.

### 7. Full right-answer explanation
B is correct. The screenshot may matter to the defamation case, but Mary first has to connect the review to Barnabas. A similar display name, standing alone, does not show that Barnabas created, controlled, wrote, or adopted the account or the review. Gold Key: the proponent of digital evidence must offer enough evidence for a reasonable juror to find that the item is what the proponent claims it is. Silver Key: when the objection is authentication, do not let a relevance answer sneak past the missing foundation.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations
A is attractive because the accusation in the review sounds central to a defamation suit. The breaker is that relevance does not answer the authentication objection; a relevant screenshot still needs a foundation tying it to Barnabas.

C is attractive because the account name looks like a quick identity shortcut. The breaker is the word "always": a self-labeling display name is not automatically enough without supporting circumstances such as control, distinctive content, a photograph, account records, or acknowledgment.

D is the dominant trap because it reaches the right exclude result but flips the burden. Barnabas does not have to prove that an impostor made the account before Mary lays a foundation; Gold Key: Mary, as the proponent, must produce evidence sufficient to support a finding of authorship or adoption.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification AND legal reasoning for the right answer
The tested issue remains Evidence, authentication of digital evidence, and the credited outcome remains exclusion for lack of foundation. The verified outline code used for the completed item is `32020700` because the deepest listed local node covering the FRE 901 issue is "Relevance > Authentication and Identification"; the source row listed `32020200` for laying a foundation, but the local outline map separately lists authentication at `32020700`.

Federal Rule of Evidence 901(a) requires the proponent to produce evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is. Rule 901(b)(1) and 901(b)(4) illustrate common paths: witness knowledge and distinctive characteristics taken with the circumstances. For digital-account authorship, the same foundation problem applies. In *United States v. Vayner*, 769 F.3d 125 (2d Cir. 2014), the Second Circuit held that admitting a social-networking profile was error when the proponent lacked sufficient evidence that the defendant created or controlled it. Sources checked: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_901 and https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/united-states-v-vayner-885228839.
