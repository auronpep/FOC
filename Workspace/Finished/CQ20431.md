# A) PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question — stem + call

After a federal smuggling verdict against Daniel, juror Hannah signs an affidavit stating two things: first, that during a deliberation break a courthouse security guard told several jurors that Daniel "had been out on bail for the same thing just last year"; second, that Hannah personally voted to convict because she thought the judge had instructed that the defense must prove Daniel innocent, when in fact she had the burden of proof backwards. Daniel moves for a new trial and offers Hannah's affidavit. How should the court treat the affidavit?

## 2. Distilled Core Question

After a verdict, a juror's affidavit reports (1) an outsider's prejudicial comment that reached the jury and (2) the juror's own misunderstanding of an instruction. Which part, if either, may the court consider on a motion for a new trial?

- **B.** Consider only Hannah's statement about misunderstanding the instruction, because it directly explains her vote.

- **D.** Exclude the entire affidavit because jurors may never testify about anything once a verdict is returned.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

C is correct. On an inquiry into a verdict's validity, the line is internal versus external. A juror **may** testify that **extraneous prejudicial information** was brought to the jury's attention or that an **outside influence** was improperly applied. The security guard's remark — that Daniel was out on bail "for the same thing last year" — is outside information injected from a non-juror, so that part comes in. But a juror may **not** testify about deliberations, the effect of anything on any juror's vote, or any juror's **mental processes**. Hannah's claim that she got the burden of proof backwards is a pure mental-process matter, so that part stays out. C is the only choice that splits the affidavit on the correct internal/external seam. **Gold Key:** outside information / outside influence = admissible; deliberations + mental processes = barred (FRE 606(b)). **Silver Key:** when an affidavit bundles one external fact with one internal fact, refuse the all-in / all-out choices and look for the choice that *splits* them.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

- **A (dominant trap).** It feels right because the whole motion *is* an attack on the verdict's validity, so "we're testing validity, therefore consider everything" reads like the natural rule. The breaker: the validity inquiry is exactly the setting where FRE 606(b) operates, and within it the mental-process bar still stands — challenging the verdict does not dissolve the internal/external line. **Gold Key:** outside influence is admissible, mental processes are not, *even during* a validity challenge.
- **B.** It attracts because Hannah's misunderstanding "directly explains her vote," which sounds like the most probative, on-point evidence available. The breaker: the reason a juror voted as she did is the paradigm of barred mental-process testimony — its directness is what the rule forbids, not what saves it. This choice runs the rule **backwards**, admitting the one item the rule was written to exclude.
- **D.** It attracts by stating the bar in clean, absolute form — jurors stay silent after a verdict — which matches the strong general instinct that deliberations are sealed. The breaker: the bar is not absolute; the extraneous-information and outside-influence carve-outs exist precisely so genuinely external taint can be reached. "Never... anything" overclaims past the exception.

## 9. Black-letter verification + legal reasoning (lawyer-confirmation layer)

Governing authority: **FRE 606(b)**. Rule 606(b)(1) bars a juror, on an inquiry into the validity of a verdict, from testifying about any statement made or incident that occurred during deliberations, the effect of anything on that or another juror's vote, or any juror's mental processes concerning the verdict. Rule 606(b)(2) supplies the exceptions: a juror **may** testify about whether (A) extraneous prejudicial information was improperly brought to the jury's attention, (B) an outside influence was improperly brought to bear, or (C) a mistake was made in entering the verdict on the verdict form. The guard's comment is classic extraneous prejudicial information / outside influence (606(b)(2)(A)–(B)) → admissible; Hannah's misapprehension of the burden-of-proof instruction is a mental process concerning the verdict (606(b)(1)) → barred. Supporting cases: *Tanner v. United States*, 483 U.S. 107 (1987) (juror intoxication/competency is internal, not an "outside influence"); *Warger v. Shauers*, 574 U.S. 40 (2014) (606(b) bars juror affidavits about deliberations even to show dishonesty at voir dire). *Peña-Rodriguez v. Colorado*, 580 U.S. 206 (2017) recognizes a narrow constitutional exception for clear statements of **racial animus** — not implicated here (no racial-bias facts), so it does not move the outcome. Credited outcome (consider the external statement, exclude the mental-process statement) is **unchanged** from the original. Tested rule, legal issue, and credited result all confirmed. `attorney_review`: not required for the rule statement; rule-number authority floor satisfied.
