### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation

The correct answer is **C**. A quitclaim deed is the weakest form of deed. It conveys only whatever interest the grantor holds at the time of execution—nothing more. Unlike a general warranty deed (which contains the six covenants of title, including the covenant of seisin and the covenant against encumbrances) or even a special warranty deed, a quitclaim deed contains **no covenants or warranties of title whatsoever**. The grantor does not promise that the title is good, does not promise that there are no encumbrances, and does not promise that the grantor even owns the property.

Here, Lydia conveyed the parcel to Samuel by quitclaim deed. When the court declared Samuel's interest inferior to Deborah's (because Deborah, as the prior grantee for value without notice under the recording statute, had superior title), Samuel received nothing of value. But because Lydia's deed to Samuel was a quitclaim, Lydia made no promises about the quality of the title. Samuel took the property "as is," with whatever defects existed. Samuel cannot recover damages from Lydia for the defective title.

This result holds regardless of whether Lydia knew the title was defective (eliminating D) and regardless of whether Lydia gave value for the property (eliminating A). The structural absence of covenants in a quitclaim deed is the complete defense.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A. "Samuel, because Lydia did not give value for the parcel."**

*Why it's attractive:* Students know that the recording statute protects "subsequent purchasers for value." They may reason that because Lydia received the property as a gift from Barnabas (not for value), she is somehow disqualified from conveying valid title, or that her lack of consideration somehow taints the chain. The word "value" triggers a recording-act reflex.

*The breaker:* Whether Lydia gave value for the parcel is irrelevant to her liability on the deed she delivered to Samuel. A conveyance of real property does not require consideration to be valid. A grantor can validly convey whatever interest the grantor holds, whether the grantor purchased the property or received it as a gift. The issue is not whether Lydia gave value—it is what covenants, if any, her deed to Samuel contained. Because it was a quitclaim deed, it contained none.

**B. "Samuel, if a reasonable inspection of the parcel by Lydia would have revealed that Deborah was in possession."** *(Dominant Trap)*

*Why it's attractive:* This distractor sounds like it is about inquiry notice—a concept students know well from the recording acts. If Lydia had inspected and seen Deborah's barn and olive press, she would have had notice of Deborah's prior claim. Students may conflate the recording-act inquiry-notice doctrine with a general duty of care owed by a grantor to a grantee. The distractor is especially seductive because it contains a kernel of truth: a buyer who fails to inspect *can* lose recording-act protection. But that doctrine applies to the *buyer's* priority, not to the *grantor's* liability on the deed.

*The breaker:* A quitclaim deed does not imply any promise by the grantor to act reasonably, to investigate the title, or to inspect the property. The grantor of a quitclaim deed conveys "as is"—whatever the grantor's interest may be, with all its defects, known and unknown. No duty of reasonable inspection is implied. Lydia's failure to inspect does not create liability to Samuel.

**D. "the Lydia, unless at the time she conveyed the parcel to Samuel, Lydia was aware that Deborah was in possession of the land."**

*Why it's attractive:* Students may reason that if Lydia *knew* Deborah was on the property and still conveyed to Samuel, Lydia should be liable—either for fraud or for some implied warranty. The distractor appeals to a moral intuition: a seller who knows about a defect should not be able to hide behind the form of the deed.

*The breaker:* A quitclaim deed contains no covenants of title—period. This is true even if the grantor knew the title was defective at the time of the conveyance. The very purpose of a quitclaim deed is to transfer whatever interest the grantor has without any guarantee. The grantor's knowledge of a defect does not create liability where no covenant was made. If the parties wanted warranties, they should have used a warranty deed.

### 9. Black-Letter-Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Governing rule:** A quitclaim deed conveys only the grantor's present interest in the property, if any, and carries no covenants or warranties of title. The grantor is not liable to the grantee for defects in the title, regardless of the grantor's knowledge of those defects or the grantor's failure to investigate.

**Authority:** Under the common law of real property, a quitclaim deed is distinguished from a warranty deed by the absence of covenants. A general warranty deed contains six traditional covenants: (1) covenant of seisin, (2) covenant of right to convey, (3) covenant against encumbrances, (4) covenant of quiet enjoyment, (5) covenant of warranty, and (6) covenant of further assurances. A quitclaim deed contains none of these. *See* 23 Am. Jur. 2d Deeds § 62; *see also* Ohio State Bar Association, "Quitclaim Deed Transfers Property Without Ownership Guarantee" (Law You Can Use, Sept. 4, 2015) ("A quitclaim deed offers no warranties or guarantees that the owner has good title or ownership, but simply conveys whatever interest exists when the deed is executed and delivered.").

The recording statute in the hypothetical (a notice statute) determines priority between competing claimants—it does not impose liability on a grantor who conveys by quitclaim. The court's ruling that Samuel's interest is inferior to Deborah's is a recording-act priority determination. It does not change the fact that Lydia's deed to Samuel carried no warranties.

#### Distilled Core Question

A grantor conveys land to a grantee using a quitclaim deed. The grantee's title turns out to be worthless. Can the grantee recover damages from the grantor?

#### Call and Prediction

The call asks who wins in a damages action by a quitgrantee against a quitclaim grantor. Before looking at the choices, a student should predict: "If the deed is a quitclaim, the grantor made no promises about title, so the grantee probably can't recover." This prediction points directly to an answer stating the grantor wins because the deed had no covenants.

#### Trigger Facts

1. **Deborah bought first, took possession, didn't record** → Establishes prior unrecorded deed; open notorious possession (but recording statute, not adverse possession, is the priority mechanism here)
2. **Ezekiel sold same parcel to Barnabas who recorded** → Subsequent purchaser for value who recorded under the notice statute
3. **Barnabas gifted to Lydia who recorded** → Lydia is a donee; her lack of value is a distractor trigger
4. **Lydia sold to Samuel by quitclaim deed** → **THE KEY FACT**: the conveyance at issue is a quitclaim deed
5. **Court declared Samuel's inferior to Deborah's** → Establishes Samuel has worthless title, giving him standing to sue Lydia

#### Governing C3 Lane

**Lane:** Deed-type structural analysis. The item turns on the legal effect of a quitclaim deed versus other deed types. The student must recall that a quitclaim deed contains no covenants of title and therefore creates no liability for the grantor when the title proves defective.

#### Choice-by-Choice Walkthrough

**Choice A: "Samuel, because Lydia did not give value for the parcel."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** The call asks about Lydia's liability on her deed to Samuel. The issue is what covenants Lydia's deed contained—not whether Lydia gave value for the property she received. Consideration is irrelevant to the validity of a conveyance and to the scope of deed covenants.
2. **Student label:** The "value" trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice stating that Samuel can recover because Lydia's deed contained an implied covenant of title—but a quitclaim deed contains no such covenant.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** A conveyance of real property does not require consideration to be valid. A grantor can convey whatever interest the grantor holds, whether purchased or received as a gift. The issue is the type of deed, not the grantor's consideration. **Filter broken: NOT_RESPONSIVE** (wrong element).

**Choice B: "Samuel, if a reasonable inspection of the parcel by Lydia would have revealed that Deborah was in possession."** *(Dominant Trap)*

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** A quitclaim deed contains no implied covenants—including no implied promise to inspect. The grantor conveys "as is." No duty of reasonable care is implied in a quitclaim conveyance. The recording-act inquiry-notice doctrine applies to a buyer's priority, not to a grantor's liability on the deed.
2. **Student label:** The "inspection duty" trap (dominant).
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice stating that Samuel can recover because Lydia had a duty to investigate the title before conveying—but no such duty exists in a quitclaim conveyance.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** A quitclaim deed does not imply any warranty or covenant of title. No duty of inspection is imposed on the grantor. The recording-act inquiry-notice doctrine applies to a buyer's priority, not to a grantor's liability on the deed. **Filter broken: NOT_TRUE** (fabricated rule—no inspection duty exists in a quitclaim).

**Choice C: "the Lydia, since her conveyance to Samuel contained no covenants of title."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** A quitclaim deed conveys only the grantor's present interest with no covenants of title. The grantor is not liable for defective title. This is the structural core of quitclaim-deed law.
2. **Student label:** The correct answer.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** This choice IS the true-and-responsive answer—it correctly identifies that the quitclaim deed's lack of covenants shields the grantor from liability.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** Under common law, a quitclaim deed contains no covenants of title—no seisin, no against encumbrances, no quiet enjoyment, no warranty, no further assurances. The grantor is not liable for defects in the title, regardless of knowledge or failure to inspect. **No filter broken. This is the credited answer.**

**Choice D: "the Lydia, unless at the time she conveyed the parcel to Samuel, Lydia was aware that Deborah was in possession of the land."**

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** A quitclaim deed's lack of covenants is absolute. The grantor's knowledge of a defect does not create liability where no covenant was made. The "unless" clause tries to make knowledge the trigger—but knowledge is irrelevant when no covenant exists.
2. **Student label:** The "knowledge" trap.
3. **What a true/responsive version would look like:** A choice stating that Samuel can recover because Lydia knew the title was defective—but knowledge does not create an implied warranty in a quitclaim conveyance.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The absence of covenants in a quitclaim deed applies even when the grantor knows the title is defective. The very purpose of a quitclaim is to transfer whatever interest the grantor has without any guarantee. Knowledge does not create an implied warranty. **Filter broken: NOT_TRUE** (half-truth distortion—knowledge is irrelevant, not a liability trigger).
