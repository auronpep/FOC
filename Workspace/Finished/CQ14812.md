### 7. Full right-answer explanation

C is correct. The recording may be used to show that John's trial testimony conflicts with what John previously said, so it is admissible for impeachment. It may not be used as substantive evidence that Lydia knew about the hidden crack because John's recorded statement was not made under oath at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition. Gold Key: an unsworn prior inconsistent statement by a testifying witness can impeach, but it is not substantive nonhearsay under Rule 801(d)(1)(A). Silver Key: when the array gives "only to impeach" against "prove the fact and impeach," the purpose of the offer is the axis.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

A is attractive because it spots the hearsay danger and stops there. The breaker is purpose: if the recording is used only to show the clash between John's two accounts, it is not being used to prove that Lydia's admission was true, so the hearsay objection does not bar impeachment use.

B is attractive because a secret phone recording feels invasive. The breaker is frame: this is an evidence question in a private civil suit, not a constitutional exclusion question based on government action or a privileged communication.

D is the dominant trap. It correctly sees that the recording can impeach John, then lets that limited use spill into proof that Lydia knew about the hidden crack. Gold Key: without the oath-and-proceeding safeguard in Rule 801(d)(1)(A), John's prior inconsistent statement does not become substantive evidence. Silver Key: the word "and" overclaims; the surviving answer limits the recording to impeachment.

### 9. Full black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning

The tested rule, legal issue, and credited outcome are unchanged from the source row. The issue is whether a witness's prior inconsistent statement, recorded outside court and not under oath in a formal proceeding, may be admitted for substantive truth or only for impeachment.

Under Federal Rule of Evidence 801(c), hearsay is an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 801(d)(1)(A), a prior inconsistent statement by a testifying declarant is substantive nonhearsay only if it was given under oath subject to penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition. Under current Federal Rule of Evidence 613(b), extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement generally requires the witness to have an opportunity to explain or deny it and the adverse party an opportunity to examine the witness about it; the variant places John still on the stand and after denial, so the impeachment foundation is satisfied for this exam fact pattern.

Applied here, the recording has two layers. Lydia's alleged admission could be a party-opponent statement if properly offered against Lydia, but John's out-of-court recounting is still a witness prior statement. Because John's recorded statement was not under oath in a qualifying proceeding, it cannot prove the hidden repair. It can still impeach John by showing the inconsistency between his trial testimony and his earlier account. The correct answer is therefore admissible only to impeach.
