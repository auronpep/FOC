### 7. Full Right-Answer Explanation (Exam Register)

**B is correct.** This is a design-defect claim under strict products liability. The plaintiff must show that the product was defectively designed — typically through a risk-utility balancing test demonstrating that the foreseeable risks of harm outweighed the product's utility, or that a reasonable alternative design existed. Here, the facts state the archery set was "as safe as possible, consistent with its recreational purpose, and its benefits exceeded its risks." Under the risk-utility framework, when the manufacturer shows the product was optimally designed and its social utility outweighs its risks, the design is not defective. Daniel's injury resulted from Peter's reckless misuse — firing an arrow at a person — which was not a reasonably foreseeable use of a beginner target-archery set. Because the product was not defectively designed, ArkCo is not strictly liable.

> **Gold Key:** In a strict liability design-defect case, if the product was as safe as possible for its intended purpose and its benefits outweigh its risks, the design is not defective — even if the product can cause injury when misused in an unforeseeable way.

### 8. Full Wrong-Answer Explanations

**A is attractive** because it feels right that the teenager who fired the arrow caused the injury. Students instinctively look for the "real" cause and think the manufacturer should be off the hook. **But the breaker is:** the third-party negligence is not part of the plaintiff's case in chief for strict products liability. Peter's careless act may be relevant to damages (comparative fault in some jurisdictions), but it does not defeat the threshold question of whether the product was defectively designed. If the design were defective, ArkCo's strict liability would stand regardless of Peter's conduct. *(Gold Key: The other actor's negligence does not cut off a manufacturer's strict liability for a defective product.)*

**C is attractive** because the stem explicitly tells you there was no warning, and arrows causing serious harm seems like something a manufacturer should warn about. Students see the missing warning and jump to failure-to-warn liability. **But the breaker is:** a duty to warn exists only when the danger is non-obvious, the manufacturer knows of it, and the danger is present during intended or reasonably foreseeable use. Here, the danger of firing arrows at people is obvious, and using a target-archery set to shoot at a person is not a reasonably foreseeable use. The failure-to-warn theory fails because the misuse was not foreseeable. *(This is the dominant trap — the distractor that most students will pick because the absence of a warning feels like the manufacturer's clear failure.)*

**D is attractive** because archery involves projectiles, and students may think manufacturing weapons-like products is "abnormally dangerous." **But the breaker is:** abnormally dangerous activities apply to landowners/occupiers conducting ultra-hazardous activities on land (blasting, storing explosives), not to the commercial sale of ordinary consumer products. This is a products liability question, and the abnormally dangerous activity doctrine is simply the wrong legal framework.

### 9. Black-Letter Law Verification and Legal Reasoning

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Torts § 402A (1965); Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability § 2(b) (1998).

Under § 402A, a commercial seller is strictly liable for physical harm caused by a product in a "defective condition unreasonably dangerous to the user or consumer." For design defects, courts apply either the consumer-expectations test or the risk-utility balancing test. Under the risk-utility framework (adopted by the Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability § 2(b)), a product is defectively designed when "the foreseeable risks of harm posed by the product could have been reduced or avoided by the adoption of a reasonable alternative design ... and the omission of the alternative design renders the product not reasonably safe."

Here, the facts establish that the archery set was "as safe as possible" and its "benefits exceeded its risks." This means the manufacturer satisfied the risk-utility test — the product was not defectively designed. The injury resulted from an unforeseeable misuse (firing at a person), not from a design flaw.

For failure to warn: a manufacturer has a duty to warn of non-obvious dangers that are present during intended or reasonably foreseeable use. *See* Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability § 2(c). Using a target-archery set to shoot at a person is not a reasonably foreseeable use, so no duty to warn of that danger arises.

For abnormally dangerous activities: this doctrine applies to activities on land (e.g., blasting, storing toxic chemicals), not to the sale of ordinary consumer products. *See* Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 519–520.

For third-party negligence: under strict liability, the negligence of a third party does not defeat the plaintiff's case in chief. It may be relevant to comparative fault apportionment but is not a defense to the existence of a defect. *See* Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability § 17.

#### Gold Key(s)

> **Gold Key (GK-TORTS-DESIGN-01):** In a strict liability design-defect case, if the product was as safe as possible for its intended purpose and its benefits outweigh its risks, the design is not defective — even if the product can cause injury when misused in an unforeseeable way. *(Authority: Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability § 2(b).)*

> **Gold Key (GK-TORTS-3PARTY-01):** A third party's negligence does not defeat a strict products liability claim in the plaintiff's case in chief — it may affect damages through comparative fault, but it does not determine whether the product was defective. *(Authority: Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability § 17.)*
