### 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam-register)

Naomi is **not** guilty of receiving stolen property. The common-law offense (and the offense as defined in modern statutes modeled on the Model Penal Code) requires that the defendant **know or believe** the property was stolen at the time she **receives control** of it. The required knowledge must exist at receipt; later discovery that the property was stolen does not retroactively supply the receipt-time mens rea. Naomi paid a normal price, bought the quilt from a person she had known and trusted for years, and saw nothing about the transaction that suggested the quilt was stolen. She had no knowledge or belief that the quilt was stolen when she received control of it at the craft fair. The next day's confession gave her knowledge, but that knowledge came too late to be the receipt-time mens rea. The element is missing; the offense is not made out. **Gold Key:** receipt-time knowledge is the dispositive element; the offense is not made out by the fact that property was stolen before the defendant received it, by retroactive imputation of later knowledge, or by limiting the offense to cash. **Silver Key:** when the call is "guilty of receiving stolen property?" with a prompt-return fact pattern, the first move is to test whether the defendant had knowledge or belief at the moment of receipt - if not, the offense fails on the mens rea element and the rest of the analysis is unnecessary.

### 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A. Yes, because the quilt was stolen before Naomi bought it.**

This is the dominant trap. The student reasons that the quilt was stolen, that Naomi received it, and that the offense is therefore made out. The student skips the **knowledge or belief at receipt** element. The fact that the property was stolen before the defendant received it is a necessary but not sufficient condition; the common-law and modern-statutory offense also requires that the defendant know or believe it was stolen **at the time of receipt**. Naomi had no such knowledge when she bought the quilt at the fair. The breaker is the **Gold Key:** receipt-time knowledge is the dispositive element; the offense is not made out by the fact that property was stolen before the defendant received it.

**D. Yes, because later knowledge always relates back to the purchase.**

This is the retroactive-mens-rea trap. The student treats later knowledge as if it could supply the missing receipt-time mens rea. The offense is not made out by retroactive imputation; the mens rea must exist at the moment the defendant receives control of the property. The student who picks this is reasoning from a "should have known" or "would have known" framework, not from the actual knowledge-or-belief-at-receipt standard. The breaker is the **Gold Key:** the receipt-time knowledge is the dispositive element; later knowledge does not relate back.

**C. No, because receiving stolen property applies only to cash.**

This is the wrong-subject-matter trap. The student reads "stolen property" too narrowly and concludes that the offense covers only cash, not tangible personal property like a quilt. The common-law offense (and modern statutes) covers all personal property capable of being stolen - including quilts, cameras, livestock, vehicles, and electronics. The student who picks this misstates the scope of the offense. The breaker is the **Silver Key:** receiving stolen property covers any stolen personal property, not just cash; the dispositive question is the defendant's knowledge or belief at receipt, not the type of property involved.

### 9. Black-letter-law verification and legal reasoning for the right answer

The supplied row tests QID 18477, a Criminal Law item on the mens rea element of receiving stolen property. The official key is C: the required knowledge did not exist when Mina received the camera.

The common-law offense of receiving stolen property requires that the defendant (1) receive control of stolen property, (2) with knowledge or belief that the property was stolen at the time of receipt, (3) with the intent to deprive the owner of the property permanently. Modern statutes modeled on the Model Penal Code codify the same elements. Model Penal Code section 223.6 defines the offense as receiving "movable property" that the actor "knows is stolen" or "believes is probably stolen," with the purpose of depriving the owner permanently. The knowledge or belief must exist at the time the defendant takes possession or control of the property; later knowledge does not retroactively supply the missing mens rea. See Wayne R. LaFave, *Substantive Criminal Law* section 19.6 (3d ed.) (collecting authorities on the receipt-time knowledge requirement).

The leading trap is the **stolen-before-purchase** misconception: students reason that because the property was stolen, anyone who received it must be guilty. The error is that the common-law and modern-statutory offense also requires knowledge or belief at the time of receipt - the fact that the property was stolen is necessary but not sufficient. A good-faith purchaser who has no reason to know or believe the property was stolen does not commit the offense even if the property turns out to have been stolen.

The second trap is the **retroactive-mens-rea** misconception: students reason that later knowledge "relates back" to the purchase. The mens rea must exist at the time of receipt; later knowledge does not retroactively supply it.

The third trap is the **wrong-subject-matter** misconception: students reason that the offense applies only to cash. The offense covers all personal property capable of being stolen; cameras, quilts, livestock, vehicles, and electronics are all within its scope.

The transformed outcome is identical to the original: Naomi is not guilty of receiving stolen property because she lacked knowledge or belief at the time of receipt, and her prompt return of the quilt to the police reinforces the absence of any continuing mens rea or depravity-of-conduct argument.

### 2. The 17-section student case study

## program_frame

This is a Criminal Law question on the mens rea element of receiving stolen property. The buyer (Naomi) purchased a hand-stitched quilt at a church Christmas craft fair from a fellow church member (Ruth) she had known and trusted for years. Nothing about the transaction suggested the quilt was stolen. The next day, Ruth confessed that she had stolen the fabric she used to make the quilt. Naomi immediately took the quilt to the police. The call is the guilty-or-not question, and the dispositive element is knowledge or belief at the time the defendant receives control of the property.

## student_access_test

A student without the Gold Key sits in `anchor_assisted` territory on the receipt-time-knowledge pivot, but the answer is settled by the mens rea doctrine.

## choice_by_choice_walkthrough

### A. Yes, because the quilt was stolen before Naomi bought it.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the stolen-before-purchase dominant trap. The student reasons that the quilt was stolen, that Naomi received it, and that the offense is therefore made out. The student skips the knowledge or belief at receipt element. The Gold Key tells them: receipt-time knowledge is the dispositive element; the offense is not made out by the fact that property was stolen before the defendant received it.
2. **Student label:** Stolen-before-purchase (dominant trap).
3. **True/responsive version:** "Yes, because the quilt was stolen before Naomi bought it" MIGHT be true if the offense were made out by the fact-of-receipt alone. It is not; the common-law and modern-statutory offense also requires knowledge or belief at the time of receipt.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The common-law offense and modern statutes require that the defendant know or believe the property was stolen at the time she receives control of it. A good-faith purchaser who has no reason to know or believe the property was stolen does not commit the offense even if the property turns out to have been stolen. Model Penal Code section 223.6.

### B. No, because she lacked knowledge when she received control of the quilt and promptly returned it after learning.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the residual answer. The Gold Key resolves to receipt-time knowledge: Naomi had no such knowledge at the fair, and the next-day confession came too late.
2. **Student label:** Residual answer (no receipt-time knowledge).
3. **True/responsive version:** This choice already states it.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The common-law and modern-statutory offense requires knowledge or belief at the time the defendant receives control of the property. Naomi had no such knowledge when she received the quilt at the fair; the next-day confession came too late. The element is missing; the offense is not made out. The prompt return to police reinforces the absence of any continuing mens rea or depravity-of-conduct argument.

### C. No, because receiving stolen property applies only to cash.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** Misfit / wrong-subject-matter. The student reads "stolen property" too narrowly and concludes that the offense covers only cash, not tangible personal property like a quilt. The Silver Key tells them: receiving stolen property covers all stolen personal property, not just cash; the dispositive question is the defendant's knowledge or belief at receipt, not the type of property.
2. **Student label:** Cash-only (wrong-subject-matter).
3. **True/responsive version:** "No, because receiving stolen property applies only to cash" MIGHT be true if the offense were limited to currency. It is not; the offense covers all stolen personal property, including quilts, cameras, livestock, vehicles, and electronics.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The common-law offense and modern statutes cover all personal property capable of being stolen. The offense is not limited to cash.

### D. Yes, because later knowledge always relates back to the purchase.

1. **Student-accessible C3 signal:** This is the retroactive-mens-rea distractor. The student treats later knowledge as if it could supply the missing receipt-time mens rea. The offense is not made out by retroactive imputation. The Gold Key tells them: the mens rea must exist at the moment of receipt; later knowledge does not relate back.
2. **Student label:** Retroactive-mens-rea.
3. **True/responsive version:** "Yes, because later knowledge always relates back to the purchase" MIGHT be true if the mens rea could be supplied retroactively. It cannot; the offense requires knowledge or belief at the time of receipt.
4. **Lawyer confirmation:** The mens rea must exist at the time the defendant takes possession or control of the property; later knowledge does not retroactively supply it. Wayne R. LaFave, *Substantive Criminal Law* section 19.6 (3d ed.).
