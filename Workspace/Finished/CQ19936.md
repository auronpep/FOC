# PASS-1 TRANSFORM REPORT

## 1. Final question (revised stem + call)

At a federal civil trial over a defective irrigation pump sold for a hillside
vineyard, the plaintiff calls Naomi as its first witness. Before Naomi is asked
a single question, opposing counsel Barnabas rises and says, "Your Honor, I move
to strike any nonresponsive answer Naomi may give." The judge has not yet heard
Naomi say anything and has no reason to believe she will volunteer inadmissible
information.

**How should the court rule?**

## 2. Distilled Core Question

Opposing counsel moves to strike a witness's nonresponsive answers *before the
witness has answered anything*, with no sign she will say something improper.
What does the court do with a motion to strike aimed at testimony that does not
yet exist?

## 3. Final answer choices (A–D, post-shuffle)

**A.** Grant the motion, because counsel may strike answers before they are given.

**B.** Grant the motion only if Naomi is a hostile witness.

**C.** Deny the motion as premature, while retaining authority to control the
witness and strike any improper answer if one occurs.

**D.** Deny the motion, because nonresponsive answers are never subject to court
control.

## 7. Full right-answer explanation (exam register)

**C is correct.** A motion to strike operates on evidence that already exists in
the record — it asks the court to remove an answer that has been given. Here no
answer has been given; Naomi has not been asked anything. A motion to strike
testimony that does not yet exist is premature, so the court denies it. Critically,
denying it costs the moving party nothing: the court keeps full authority to
control the mode and order of examination, instruct the witness, and strike any
genuinely nonresponsive or improper answer the moment one actually occurs. The
right answer pairs the correct disposition (deny — premature) with the correct
reason it does no harm (the court's retained control), which is exactly why it
beats every "grant" option.

## 8. Full wrong-answer explanations

**A (dominant trap).** This reads like efficient lawyering — get ahead of a
problem witness by securing a ruling before she can blurt something out. The pull
is the fantasy of a preemptive strike. The breaker: there is nothing in the record
to strike yet. A motion to strike acts on an answer that already exists; you cannot
strike a sentence the witness has not spoken. The relief simply has no object.

**B.** Hostility is a real and tested evidence concept (it unlocks leading
questions on direct), so importing it here feels lawyerly and rule-grounded. The
pull is a true doctrine dropped into the wrong slot. The breaker: a witness's
hostility has nothing to do with *when* a motion to strike is ripe. The motion is
premature whether the witness is friendly, neutral, or hostile, so conditioning
relief on hostility answers a different question.

**D.** This one over-corrects off the right instinct (that you cannot strike
nothing) into the opposite error. The pull is the absolute word "never," which
feels safely categorical. The breaker: courts plainly *do* control nonresponsive
answers — they instruct witnesses and strike improper answers after they occur.
"Never subject to court control" overstates a real limit into a false absolute.

## 9. Black-letter-law verification and right-answer legal reasoning

A motion to strike is a post-testimony remedy: it presupposes that inadmissible or
nonresponsive matter has already entered the record and asks the court to remove it.
Until an answer exists, there is nothing to strike, so the motion is premature. The
trial court's power to manage the witness comes from its general authority over the
mode and order of examining witnesses and presenting evidence, exercised when an
actual problem arises — including instructing the witness and striking a
nonresponsive answer after it is given. Denying the premature motion therefore does
not surrender any control; it simply defers the remedy to the moment a real answer
triggers it.

**Authority (lawyer-confirmation layer only):** FRE 611(a) (court's reasonable
control over the mode and order of examining witnesses and presenting evidence);
FRE 103(a)(1) (timely objection/motion to strike as the mechanism for preserving
an evidentiary ruling, by its terms keyed to evidence that has been admitted).
Both are rule-number authorities; the credited outcome is unchanged from the
original. No doctrinal-currency hotlist item is implicated. Verdict: **PASS**.
